Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

Objective: Hospitalized patients with treatment-resistant depression need rapid treatments, yet conventional brain stimulation protocols are prolonged. Given limited sham-controlled inpatient evidence, the authors examined whether accelerated intermittent theta-burst stimulation (iTBS) improves depressive symptoms when added to routine inpatient treatment.

Methods: In this double-blind, sham-controlled trial, inpatients aged 18-65 years with unipolar treatment-resistant depression were randomized to active or sham accelerated iTBS (3 sessions/day, 10 weekdays; 54,000 pulses) targeting an individualized left dorsolateral prefrontal site. Primary and secondary outcomes were change over the 2-week stimulation phase in Montgomery-Åsberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS) and daily Beck Depression Inventory-II (BDI-II) scores. Follow-up was 6 weeks after treatment.

Results: Of 57 randomized patients, 51 initiated and completed stimulation (active, n=27; sham, n=24); 42 (82%) had psychiatric comorbidity. MADRS scores decreased more with active than sham stimulation (-3.54 points/week; 95% CI, -5.53 to -1.55; Cohen's d=-0.94; model-estimated reductions, 12.06 vs 4.98 points). The effect remained significant after adjustment for the active group's higher baseline score (d=-0.77; 95% CI, -1.32 to -0.22) and in sensitivity analyses. BDI-II scores changed in the same direction (-0.23 points/day), but not significantly when individual rates of change were modeled (95% CI, -0.57 to 0.10). During follow-up, improvements were largely maintained in both groups; the baseline-adjusted week-8 difference was not significant. No serious adverse events occurred.

Conclusions: Accelerated iTBS provided added benefit beyond routine inpatient care, robustly reducing clinician-rated depressive symptoms over 2 weeks; self-rated changes were directionally consistent but not robust. Multicenter trials should determine durability and optimal implementation.

Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a leading cause of disability worldwide and major contributor to premature mortality and economic burden (1). A substantial proportion of patients fail to achieve adequate symptom relief with first-line pharmacotherapy and psychotherapy, resulting in treatmentresistant depression (TRD), which is associated with prolonged suffering, greater functional impairment, and repeated health-care utilization including repeated hospitalizations compared to treatment-responsive patients (2). In inpatient psychiatric settings, depressive episodes are often more severe and clinically urgent, making rapid and effective interventions especially important. Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) has emerged as a safe and well-tolerated treatment option, and has shown antidepressant efficacy in TRD (3). Yet, two central issues limit the translation of rTMS into routine inpatient care. First, standard 4-to-6week rTMS courses can span most or all of an inpatient stay, whereas acute settings call for rapid stabilization. Second, most sham-controlled rTMS evidence has been generated in outpatient populations (4, 5). A comprehensive meta-analysis identified only a small subset of exclusively hospitalized samples and found no superior antidepressant effect (6). In contrast, naturalistic inpatient studies suggest that rTMS may be clinically useful under routine-care inpatient conditions (7) (8) (9). This divergence between controlled trial outcomes and observational clinical data has been widely discussed (10, 11) and underscores the need for prospective, sham-controlled studies conducted in naturalistic hospital environments to determine the clinical utility of rTMS as an augmentation strategy for TRD.

To address the challenge of prolonged treatment duration and optimize efficacy for severe TRD, accelerated rTMS protocols delivering multiple sessions per day over a condensed timeframe have gained momentum, especially following the Stanford Neuromodulation Therapy (12, 13) trials. These trials achieved high rapid remission rates with 10 daily sessions over five days. However, translating such intensive regimens into widely accessible standard public healthcare systems presents major logistical and economic challenges. Protocols requiring approximately 10 hours of daily clinical management are difficult to integrate into routine clinical workflows and, in inpatient settings, limit participation in the multimodal therapeutic program, which combines pharmacotherapy, psychotherapy, and group, occupational, creative, and exercise therapies. Because active rTMS has outperformed sham when added to psychological or other nonpharmacological treatments (14), stimulation may complement this program, provided that accelerated protocols are compatible with the length of an inpatient stay and the daily therapeutic schedule.

The present randomized, double-blind, sham-controlled trial evaluated an accelerated intermittent theta-burst stimulation (iTBS) protocol delivering three daily sessions over 10 weekdays as an augmentation strategy for hospitalized patients with TRD.

Crucially, the trial was conducted under naturalistic inpatient conditions, permitting typical psychiatric comorbidities and concurrent medication adjustments within broader multimodal care, while the shamcontrolled design allowed rTMS-specific effects to be distinguished from symptom improvement related to routine inpatient care. We hypothesized that a clinically implementable accelerated iTBS protocol would provide added antidepressant benefit beyond ongoing inpatient treatment.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a double-blind, randomized, sham-controlled trial. The broader trial protocol, which included further outcomes and patient groups, was prospectively registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04832750?term=NCT04832750&rank=1 and the analytical plan for the current investigation on the Open Science Framework (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/6EMSU). The analysis plan was registered on November 7, 2024, after recruitment and before unblinding (December 2024); protocol, plan, and deviations are provided in Supplement 1. Procedures complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and were approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the University of Oldenburg [2020-144].

Participants

Based on an a priori power analysis (Supplement 2), we targeted 50 participants and planned to recruit 60. Participants were recruited via clinical referrals and community outreach at the Department of Psychiatry, University of Oldenburg located at the Karl-Jaspers-Klinik, Bad Zwischenahn, Germany, from May 2021 to May 2024 (last follow-up, August 2024). Inclusion criteria were age 18 to 65 years, a current moderate-to-severe major depressive episode (Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-5), and a TRD (≥1 failed adequate antidepressant trial during the current episode). To maintain a clinically representative inpatient sample, psychiatric comorbidities were permitted excluding acute suicidality and, as a precaution for the high-dose accelerated protocol, current substance dependence and psychotic or bipolar disorder. Suicidality led to exclusion only when it precluded safe participation, as judged clinically without a rating-scale cutoff; no screened patient was excluded for this reason.

Further exclusion criteria comprised standard neurological, MRI or rTMS (15) safety contraindications.

All participants provided written informed consent after receiving a complete description of the study.

Randomization and Blinding

Participants were randomly assigned 1:1 to active or sham stimulation using a computer-generated sequence of permuted blocks, applied unchanged throughout recruitment. To conceal allocation, the sequence was held by a researcher with no other role in the study, and stimulation was delivered with identical, code-labelled active and sham coils. Participants, raters, stimulation staff, ward clinicians, and investigators were blind to allocation. Blinding was assessed with treatment guesses, summarized with the Bang blinding index, guess confidence, and session tolerability ratings (Supplement 2); guesses were collected after the 6-week follow-up and before unblinding.

Intervention

Neuronavigated accelerated iTBS was delivered with the Apollo TMS system (MAG & More) at 90% resting motor threshold: 1,800 pulses/session, 3 afternoon sessions/day separated by 50-minute intervals for 10 weekdays (30 sessions; 54,000 pulses). Participants underwent structural and restingstate MRI for individualized connectivity-guided target selection of the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) cluster most anticorrelated with the subgenual anterior cingulate cortex independent of allocation, following Cash et al. (16) (Supplement 2). Post hoc quality control identified a templateorientation mismatch in the targeting pipeline, resulting in mean differences of 8.6 mm (active) and 10.4 mm (sham) between original and corrected target coordinates. These differences were unrelated to MADRS improvement (Supplement 2). Stimulation was added to routine multimodal inpatient treatment, which included pharmacotherapy, individual and group psychotherapy, and occupational, creative, and exercise therapies. All participants had access to the same range of inpatient treatment options provided by clinicians blind to allocation throughout hospitalization. Benzodiazepines and bupropion were prohibited during the stimulation phase (15, 17).

Clinical outcomes

The primary outcome was change in clinician-rated depression severity assessed with the Montgomery-Åsberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS) (18). The secondary outcome was change in self-reported Beck Depression Inventory-II (BDI-II) (19). The assessment obtained immediately before the first iTBS session served as treatment baseline. MADRS interviews were conducted at 7-day intervals: baseline, mid-treatment, and post-treatment. To capture high-resolution symptom trajectories, BDI-II was administered daily before the first iTBS session and at post-treatment. Both outcomes were reassessed by telephone at 6-week follow-up.

Baseline variables encompassed demographic factors (age, self-reported sex and gender identity, employment), illness chronicity (total illness duration, duration of the current episode, chronic MDD), treatment-resistance indices (Maudsley Staging Method (20), MSM; number of antidepressant and augmentation trials, and hospitalizations), and clinical variables, including comorbidities (SCID-5 (21, 22) and medical records).

Safety and side effects

Adverse events and common side effects, including headache, nausea, discomfort, and fatigue, were recorded on each treatment day irrespective of attributed cause.

Statistical analysis

Efficacy analyses included all participants who initiated stimulation (modified intention-to-treat population, n=51); because none discontinued, this equaled the preregistered per-protocol set, and the preregistered intention-to-treat sensitivity analysis for ≥5 discontinuations was not triggered. A post hoc sensitivity analysis included all 57 randomized participants; because the 6 non-initiators had no MADRS ratings, including at baseline, their values were multiply imputed (Supplement 2). All P values were 2-sided; confirmatory P values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate procedure and reported as PFDR.

Confirmatory analyses

MADRS scores were analyzed using latent growth models (LGMs) across three time points. For BDI-II, preregistered LGMs were estimated but showed inadequate fit for the daily data; BDI-II trajectories were therefore analyzed using linear mixed models (LMMs), which are less strict and more parsimonious, offering benefits when working with small samples (23). Models followed the preregistered sequence: linear and non-parametric time functions were compared first, treatment group was added next; prespecified covariates (age, sex, chronic MDD) and current episode duration as moderator were examined separately. Because random-intercept models assume a common rate of change for all patients in a group, all BDI-II models were fitted both with random intercepts and with additional random slopes for time; both versions are reported. Final models were selected based on fit, parsimony, and information criteria. Non-preregistered sensitivity models adjusted the MADRS slope for baseline severity, medication changes during stimulation, or rater (Supplement 2). Missing data were handled under missing-at-random assumptions using robust maximum likelihood for LGMs.

LMMs used maximum likelihood for fit indices and model comparisons and restricted maximum likelihood for parameter estimates. Effect-size and model-fit details are in Supplement 2.

Exploratory analyses

Deviating from the preregistration due to poor LGM fit and baseline severity differences in MADRS, follow-up trajectories were analyzed using a piecewise LMM with a knot at Week 2, separating acute treatment and follow-up phases. For the baseline-adjusted week-8 difference, the group difference at baseline was constrained to zero; further sensitivity analyses are described in Supplement 2.

Clinical response (≥50% symptom reduction) and remission (MADRS<10 (24); BDI-II<13 (25)) rates

were analyzed using Chi-square or Fisher's exact tests, with odds ratios (OR) and respective CIs.

Additional non-preregistered baseline-adjusted Firth logistic regression sensitivity analyses were conducted for MADRS outcomes to account for baseline differences. Additional moderation analyses of predefined demographic, clinical/psychological, and social baseline variables are reported in Supplement 2.

All analyses were performed in R (version 4.3.1) using lavaan for

LGMs and lme4 for LMMs. Additional methodological details are provided in Supplement 2.

Results

Demographics

A total of 128 patients were assessed for eligibility, 57 were randomized; 51 patients received and completed assigned intervention (sham: n=24; active: n=27). Six randomized patients (3 per group) did not start stimulation (reasons in Figure 1); none discontinued thereafter. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The final sample represented a clinically complex inpatient TRD sample, with long illness and current-episode durations, multiple prior treatment failures, moderate-to-severe treatment resistance, and frequent psychiatric comorbidity. All participants had at least two failed adequate trials in the current episode, meeting common TRD criteria (2). Overall, 42 participants (82%) had at least one comorbid psychiatric diagnosis. Baseline characteristics, comorbidities, concomitant medication classes, and medication changes were comparable between groups (Table 1; Tables S1 and S2); none received electroconvulsive therapy or (es)ketamine.

Treatment-baseline MADRS scores were higher in the active than in the sham group (mean±SD, 28.4±7.1 vs 23.1±8.1); thus baseline-adjusted sensitivity analyses are reported below.

Primary outcome

Observed MADRS trajectories and percentage changes across the acute treatment and follow-up phases are illustrated in Figure 2A-B. Overall, MADRS scores decreased by -4.40 points/week (95% CI, -5.53 to -3.28; z=-7.68, PFDR<.001), corresponding to a model-estimated reduction of -8.81 points over the acute treatment phase (Table 2).

Adding treatment allocation yielded a significant time-by-group interaction. MADRS scores decreased more steeply with active iTBS than sham (β=-3.54 points/week; 95% CI, -5.53 to -1.55; z=-3.49, PFDR<.001), corresponding to a model-estimated between-group difference of -7.08 points over the acute treatment phase and a large effect (Cohen's d=-0.94). Adding baseline MADRS severity as a sensitivity covariate did not significantly influence symptom change (β=-0.12, z=-1.18, P=.24), and the treatment effect remained significant (β=-2.92 points/week; 95% CI, -4.99 to -0.85; z=-2.76, P=.006; Cohen's d=-0.77). Moreover, the effect remained significant in all further sensitivity analyses: week-2 analyses of covariance and mixed models for repeated measures, inclusion of all 57 randomized participants by multiple imputation, and adjustment for medication changes during stimulation or for rater (Table S5).

Secondary outcome

Observed daily BDI-II trajectories and percentage changes are shown in Figure 2C-D. Across the full cohort, BDI-II scores decreased by -0.31 points/day (95% CI, -0.40 to -0.21; t(499.0)=-6.62, PFDR<.001), corresponding to a model-estimated reduction of -3.05 points over the acute treatment phase. This decrease remained significant with random slopes (Table 2).

Adding treatment allocation, BDI-II scores decreased more steeply with active iTBS than with sham (β=-0.23 points/day; Cohen's d=-0.22), corresponding to a model-estimated between-group difference of -2.33 points over the 10-day acute treatment phase. The difference was significant in the random-intercept model (95% CI, -0.41 to -0.05; t(498.0)=-2.53, PFDR=.02) but not in the randomslope model (95% CI, -0.57 to 0.10; t(48.0)=-1.40, PFDR=.34; Table 2), which accounts for the considerable variation of individual rates of change (SD, 0.52 points/day; χ²(1:2)=66.66, P<.001).

Covariates and moderation

For both outcomes, male participants had lower baseline scores (MADRS, β=-4.66; z=-2.65, P=.008; BDI-II, β=-7.70; t(47.0)=-2.62, P=.01), whereas age and sex were not associated with change.

Chronic MDD was associated with higher baseline MADRS scores (β=5.44; z=1.97, P=.048) and, in the random-intercept model only, with a steeper BDI-II decrease (β=-0.24 points/day; t(497.0)=-2.64, P=.009; random slope, t(47.0)=-1.46, P=.15). Current episode duration, a preregistered moderator, was not associated with symptom change and did not moderate the treatment effect on either outcome (all PFDR≥.12). In the final MADRS model, active treatment allocation remained the only significant predictor of change (β=-3.60 points/week; 95% CI, -5.60 to -1.60; z=-3.52, P<.001; Figure S2; Supplement 3, Tables S6 and S7).

Exploratory outcomes

Follow-up trajectories

Improvements achieved during stimulation were largely maintained in both groups: from week 2 to the 6-week follow-up, MADRS scores remained stable and BDI-II scores decreased slightly, with no group difference in the rate of change (MADRS, t(139.6)=1.14, P=.26; BDI-II, t(497.5)=0.43, P=.67; Table 2). Despite higher baseline MADRS scores in the active group, both groups had the same observed mean score at week 8 (18.5). After baseline adjustment, the week-8 difference favored active stimulation in all analyses, including those addressing missing follow-up data, but none was significant (piecewise model, -3.0 points; 95% CI, -6.8 to 0.8; t(169.7)=-1.56, P=.12; Table S5).

Response and remission rates

Clinical response (≥50% symptom reduction) and remission (MADRS<10; BDI-II<13) were evaluated as exploratory acute-phase endpoints (Figure 3; Table S3). The active iTBS group demonstrated a significantly higher MADRS response rate compared to sham (11/26 [42.3%] vs 3/23 [13.0%]; OR=4.73; 95% CI, 1.01 to 31.09; Fisher exact test, P=.03), while numerically but not statistically higher MADRS remission rate (7/26 [26.9%] vs 3/24 [12.5%]; OR=2.53; 95% CI, 0.49 to 17.36; P=.29), BDI-II response (4/27 [14.8%] vs 1/22 [4.5%]; OR=3.57; 95% CI, 0.32 to 188.07; P=.36), or BDI-II remission (7/27 [25.9%] vs 5/22 [22.7%]; OR=1.19; 95% CI, 0.27 to 5.68; χ²(1)=0.07, P=.80).

In baseline-adjusted logistic sensitivity models for MADRS outcomes, active iTBS was associated with higher odds of response (OR=8.53; 95% CI, 1.88 to 57.40; χ²(1)=8.22, P=.004) and remission (OR=5.73; 95% CI, 1.11 to 44.73; χ²(1)=4.41, P=.04; Table S3).

Safety and Blinding

No serious adverse events occurred, and no seizures, syncope, (hypo)manic symptoms, treatmentemergent suicidal ideation, or cognitive complaints were observed. Headache (sham, 10 of 24; active, 15 of 27), fatigue (10 vs 8), discomfort (6 vs 7), and nausea (5 vs 4) did not differ significantly between groups (Table S4).

Of 38 participants who provided guesses, 24 (63%) guessed correctly (Fisher exact test, P=.12); Bang blinding indices were 0.20 (95% CI, -0.23 to 0.63) for active and 0.33 (95% CI, -0.10 to 0.77) for sham stimulation. Confidence did not differ between correct and incorrect guesses, and tolerability neither differed between groups nor predicted guesses (Supplement 2).

Discussion

In this randomized, double-blind, sham-controlled trial, accelerated, high-dose iTBS delivered as three daily sessions over 10 weekdays produced steeper acute symptom reduction than sham stimulation when added to ongoing multimodal inpatient care in hospitalized patients with TRD. During this acute stimulation phase, active iTBS resulted in a model-estimated 12.06-point reduction on the primary clinician-rated MADRS, compared with a 4.98-point reduction in the sham group, corresponding to a large between-group treatment effect. Self-reported BDI-II scores changed in the same direction, but this smaller effect was not robust to between-patient variation in rates of change. Improvements were largely maintained through follow-up in both groups; the baseline-adjusted advantage of active stimulation at week 8 was smaller and not significant, so a lasting between-group benefit remains to be established.

The higher baseline MADRS score in the active group raises the possibility of regression to the mean. However, inclusion did not depend on a MADRS threshold, baseline severity was not significantly associated with subsequent change, and baseline adjustment reduced the 2-week difference by 1.23 points (26). Adjustment also did not weaken the response and remission results, and BDI-II trajectories, without baseline imbalance, pointed in the same direction.

These findings help address a major limitation of the rTMS evidence base in TRD: sham-controlled data from hospitalized patients remain sparse, and prior meta-analytic work did not identify significant antidepressant effects within the small subset of exclusively inpatient samples (6). Naturalistic inpatient studies have nevertheless reported clinically meaningful improvement under routine-care conditions (7, 9) and one large inpatient cohort exhibited shorter hospitalization with twice-daily compared to standard rTMS (8). The present trial therefore adds randomized sham-controlled evidence to the limited and predominantly naturalistic inpatient literature.

The clinical relevance should be interpreted considering the cohort's clinical complexity, reflected in protracted depressive episodes, high treatment resistance, and frequent psychiatric comorbidity. This profile is clinically important as depressive and anxiety symptom severity, treatment resistance, prior treatment failures, chronicity, psychiatric admissions, and functional status predicted poorer antidepressant outcomes following rTMS-based treatments (27, 28). Current episode duration, examined as a preregistered moderator, however, did not modify MADRS or BDI-II treatment trajectories in the present trial. Evidence for the impact of psychiatric comorbidity beyond anxiety remains mixed: broader antidepressant-treatment data indicate poorer outcomes with psychiatric comorbidities (29), whereas TMS-specific studies suggest patients with comorbid anxiety, trauma-and stressor-related, personality, or somatic symptom disorders can still experience meaningful improvement, with inconsistent evidence on whether these comorbidities attenuate antidepressant response (30, 31). Recent accelerated rTMS studies identified largely overlapping predictor profiles (32). Thus, demonstrating a meaningful treatment effect despite the cohort's substantial clinical complexity and frequent psychiatric comorbidities reinforces the utility of accelerated rTMS in inpatient TRD samples.

Accelerated rTMS protocols address a practical limitation of conventional several-week courses by condensing treatment schedules through multiple daily sessions. Meta-analytic evidence supports rapid symptom reduction across accelerated rTMS approaches, while optimal implementation remains unresolved (4, 33). Stanford Neuromodulation Therapy studies exemplify the most intensive end of the accelerated-treatment spectrum, delivering 50 sessions over five days, and have highlighted emergency and inpatient settings as potential applications (12, 13). For routine multimodal inpatient care, however, feasibility depends not only on shortening treatment courses but also on integrating stimulation alongside psychotherapy, other ward-based interventions, medication management, and daily clinical care. The present protocol thus represents a pragmatic middle-ground schedule with three daily iTBS sessions over 10 weekdays, broadly consistent with sham-controlled outpatient evidence supporting a similar 3-session/day accelerated approach in TRD (34).

Several factors may explain the smaller, less robust self-reported effect. Clinician ratings yield on average larger effects than self-reports, also with masked raters (35, 36) and in a large shamcontrolled rTMS trial (37). The MADRS was designed to be sensitive to change (18), whereas the BDI-II emphasizes self-evaluative symptoms that correlate only moderately with it (38). Moreover, in this largely naturalistic sample with frequent comorbidity, self-ratings may diverge from clinician ratings, as shown for personality disorders (39).

The exploratory response and remission analyses provide categorical translation of the continuous symptom trajectories. Active stimulation yielded higher MADRS response and numerically but not statistically higher MADRS remission than sham. Baseline-adjusted sensitivity analyses supported higher odds of both response and remission, with uncertainty reflected in wide confidence intervals.

The active-arm MADRS response rate falls within the wide range reported in a recent meta-regression of accelerated protocols, which found response rates of 19.4% to 90.5% and average response and remission rates of 52.2% and 32.5%, respectively, amid heterogeneity (40). Direct comparisons, however, are limited by differences in study designs, stimulation protocols, outcome definitions, and inpatient status. The accelerated schedule was well tolerated, with no serious adverse events observed and comparable side-effect rates across groups.

The findings are strengthened by the double-blind, sham-controlled design, a representative comorbid inpatient sample, the integration of stimulation into routine multimodal inpatient care, and complete adherence after treatment initiation. Several limitations, however, should be considered. First, the single-site design and modest sample size limit precision, particularly for categorical endpoints, moderator analyses, and follow-up comparisons. Second, this integration, including medication adjustments and ward-based co-interventions, means that the findings reflect additive clinical value during hospitalization rather than standalone efficacy under stable-medication conditions; although medication patterns were comparable between groups, contributions of concurrent treatments cannot be separated. Third, the bundled protocol cannot isolate the contributions of accelerated scheduling, pulse dose, intersession interval, or individualized connectivity-guided targeting (33, 40).

In conclusion, this randomized sham-controlled trial provides evidence that an accelerated iTBS protocol can produce added antidepressant benefit when integrated into routine multimodal inpatient care for hospitalized patients with TRD. The effect was clearest for clinician-rated depressive symptoms and occurred in a clinically complex cohort characterized by chronicity, high treatment resistance, and substantial psychiatric comorbidity.
