How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: This module has not been validated. All checks in the codebook_check module have unknown error rates. Carefully evaluate the output of this module. You can help improve this module by reporting an issue on GitHub.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

Abstract

Objective: To describe and evaluate a framework for using a large language model (LLM) as the primary coder in health content analysis, and to propose reporting items for LLMassisted studies, because no reporting standard exists.

Methods: An LLM coded 6,982 Instagram and TikTok posts using a human-written, humancalibrated instrument. The framework has six components: human authorship of the instrument; two-round human calibration; source discipline; quote-anchored coding; verification in a representative and an outcome-enriched sample, blinded in both directions; and per-variable reliability reporting against a prespecified threshold, with the consequence of failure fixed in advance. Agreement was measured with Gwet's AC1, prespecified because the outcome distribution was heavily skewed.

Results: Agreement between the two human coders on the primary outcome rose from AC1 0.456 to 0.977 after two ambiguous rules were rewritten. LLM-human agreement was 0.847 1.

1. Introduction

Content analysis of social media is commonly used to estimate how much of the information circulating on a platform is accurate, to characterise the forms that inaccurate information takes, and to identify the audiences it targets. Its value depends on scale: a prevalence estimate drawn from two hundred posts describes those posts, whereas one drawn from several thousand begins to describe an information environment.

Human coding does not reach that scale. Each post requires judgements about whether a claim is present, whether it is accurate and against what evidence, so a trained coder manages a few hundred posts a week. The literature reflects this constraint: a 2025 systematic review of physical activity misinformation found that most included studies examined YouTube or small TikTok samples [1]. The limitation is one of capacity, not rigour.

Large language models (LLMs) are being adopted to relieve this constraint, and adoption has outpaced reporting standards. Content analysis has no equivalent of STROBE or studies, typically tens to low hundreds; the work is finite and automation saves time. In largescale content analysis, the alternative is not slower human coding of the same corpus but a much smaller study, and a smaller study answers a different question. Where an effect is concentrated in a subgroup, or where a stratified design is needed to separate an exposure from an artefact of data collection, a corpus reduced fivefold may not answer the question at all. This does not justify unverified automation. The pertinent question is not whether LLM coding matches human coding (on several variables reported below, it did not), but whether an LLM-coded study can be made auditable: whether a reader can see what the coder got right, what it got wrong, in which direction it erred and how the study approached this.

A second problem is that an LLM coder is not reproducible in the standard computational sense. The same prompt applied to the same posts need not return identical codes, and model versions are withdrawn and replaced faster than the publication cycle. A framework for this method therefore cannot promise reproducibility. What it can offer is a measured, per-variable account of agreement with independent human coding.

3. Methods

>Methods

3.1 Setting

The framework was developed and applied within a cross-sectional content analysis of exercise misinformation in fitness content directed at women on Instagram and TikTok, which included a nested sub-study of content from creators who self-identified as an ethnic minority. The parent study collected 6,982 posts between March and June 2026 through two collection routes (manual and automated) and is reported separately [4]. This article concerns the coding method only.

The parent study was not pre-registered. A versioned protocol with dated amendments is deposited [5]. The reliability statistic, its threshold, and the primary analysis model were fixed before any results were seen.

3.2 The framework

The framework has six components, summarised in Fig. 1.

Component 1: human authorship of the instrument. The lead author (IS) wrote the coding instrument before any LLM-assisted coding. No variable definition, decision rule or boundary ruling was drafted, revised or adjudicated by an LLM at any point. The standard against which the LLM coder is judged is therefore a human one, and a failure to meet it on a given variable is interpretable rather than circular.

Component 2: two-round human calibration with diagnostic rule revision.

The instrument was tested by two human coders: the lead author (IS) and a second coder (AS), who had no part in writing it. Both coded a calibration sample independently, and agreement was computed. Disagreements were treated as diagnostic; each was examined to establish whether it arose from inconsistent application of a rule that determined an answer, or from a rule that did not. In the latter case, the rule was rewritten, and agreement was recomputed on a fresh sample, following the iterative codebook development described by Hruschka et al. [6]. Both the initial and final figures are reported.

Component 3: source discipline.

Each variable could be coded only from its declared source fields, and this restriction was stated in the coding prompt as well as in the protocol.

The primary outcome was coded from caption and on-screen text only, never from hashtags or creator biographies; professional status from biography text only; and ethnic minority selfidentification from explicit self-statements in text only. No variable was coded from images, video, or usernames. This rule was adopted first because inferring ethnicity from photographs or names is ethically indefensible, and was then applied to every variable for consistency.

Component 4: quote-anchored coding.

The exact wording of the claim on which a code rested had to be recorded in a notes field before the code was assigned, so that the textual basis for each decision preceded the judgement rather than being reconstructed afterwards.

Every coding decision can therefore be traced to, and checked against, the text that produced it. An explicit "not applicable" code was required instead of a blank, because a blank conditional variable is not a zero; recording no rhetorical features for a post without a claim asserts something the coder did not assess. Where the rules did not cover a case, the coder applied the nearest written rule, recorded the alternative code considered and set an ambiguity flag, rather than resolving the boundary. Resolving new boundaries partway through a sample changes the instrument mid-sample and invalidates reliability computed across the whole.

Component 5: dual-sample human verification with two-sided blinding.

The LLM coded the full corpus in a fresh session for each batch, working in sub-batches with a validation check after each. Its coding was then verified against human coding in two samples. For the primary outcome, the two samples were analysed separately and never combined: • Set A, drawn to be representative of the corpus (N = 150).

This sample supports the reliability claim for the dataset and is the only one of the two that can detect misinformation the LLM missed.

• Set B, enriched for the outcome (N = 50). Set B was drawn from posts the LLM had coded as containing misinformation (Yes or Mixed); it assesses whether LLMidentified positives withstand human scrutiny. It cannot detect false negatives and should not be read as a general agreement estimate.

Blinding operated in both directions. The LLM was blinded to all human codes: every reliability or verification sample was coded in a session containing no human codes, reliability results or reconciliation discussion, because exposure to any of these would invalidate the estimate. The verifying human coder received Sets A and B interleaved in random order, with the set identifier withheld, and coded without sight of the LLM codes.

Conditional variables are defined only for posts coded as containing misinformation, so they were assessed on the posts that both coders coded as misinformation across Sets A and B (n = 57); Set B supplied most of these posts. Female targeting was also estimated across both sets, as a secondary estimate (see Section 4.5).

The authors considered and rejected using the LLM to pre-filter posts before human coding (for example, removing posts it judged to contain no assessable claim). Pre-filtering would be circular, because it would conceal the coder's errors at exactly the judgement boundary where they are most likely.

Component 6: per-variable reliability reporting against a prespecified threshold.

Agreement was computed and reported separately for every coded variable, including those that fell short. A threshold of AC1 ≥ 0.80 was fixed in advance and applied to the point estimate, with the confidence interval reported. Variables below the threshold were moved to exploratory reporting, and analyses resting on them were reported as exploratory or withdrawn. The consequence of failure was therefore set before the results were known.

3.3 Model and configuration

Coding used Claude Opus 5 (Anthropic) at high reasoning effort, accessed through the Claude app with the setting that allows conversations to be used for model training switched off, between late May and late August 2026. Each batch was coded in a fresh session rather than a continuing one, to prevent context from earlier batches influencing later ones and to enforce the blinding rule above.

Because LLM outputs can vary between runs and model versions are withdrawn, computational reproducibility is not claimed for this coder. The claim made instead is empirical agreement with independent human coding, reported for each variable. The full prompt scaffold is deposited with the machine-readable codebook [5]. In summary, the scaffold supplied the protocol, the log of boundary rulings, the coding quick reference and the adjudicated calibration examples; stated the source restriction for each variable; required the exact claim wording before each code; required an explicit "not applicable" code; instructed the coder to flag rather than resolve uncovered boundaries; and coded in validated sub-batches.

Where the protocol text and the adjudicated calibration examples appeared to conflict, the calibration examples took precedence, because they record decisions already made about those boundary cases.

3.4 Reliability statistic

Gwet's AC1 [7] was prespecified as the primary agreement statistic and fixed before results were seen, to prevent post hoc selection. The reason is the marginal distribution of the outcome. Where one category predominates (many posts contained no assessable claim in this sample), Cohen's kappa can return low values despite high observed agreement, because its chance correction assumes coders guess in proportion to the observed marginal proportions. This is the kappa paradox: a property of skewed marginals rather than poor coding.

The difference is material at the levels relevant to this design. For creator professional status in Set A, 85.3% observed agreement gave AC1 0.825 but kappa 0.550; in the first human calibration round, 58.0% observed agreement gave AC1 0.456 and kappa 0.408.

Cohen's kappa was computed alongside AC1 throughout and is reported in the deposited materials for comparison with earlier studies. AC1 variance was estimated with the linearisation estimator [7]. The AC1 implementation was written for this study, validated against published figures and deposited [5].

3.5 Subgroup performance

Because LLMs are trained predominantly on sources from high-income, predominantly English-speaking countries, poorer performance on content from minority creators is plausible and has been raised in guidance on AI in health research [3]. The authors tested this rather than assuming it in either direction, by computing agreement separately for posts from creators who self-identified as an ethnic minority and for all other posts. The general rule the authors followed is to stratify the reliability sample on any subgroup variable that carries an inferential claim.

A post was placed in the subgroup if either coder flagged the creator as self-identifying as an ethnic minority, and in the comparison group if neither did; agreement was computed across Sets A and B for the primary outcome and for female targeting.

3.6 Independent reimplementation

All analyses were conducted in jamovi 2.7.37 [8] and independently reimplemented in Python (pandas, SciPy, statsmodels), with exact agreement required before any result was reported.

3.7 Open materials

The coded dataset, annotated analysis script, machine-readable codebook with per-variable reliability, prompt scaffold, causal model, AC1 implementation and versioned protocol are deposited [5]. The public dataset is anonymised and contains no raw platform text, creator handles, exact dates or engagement counts.

4. Results

>Results

4.1 Reliability by variable

Agreement for every coded variable is shown in Table 1 and Fig. 2.

4.2 Instrument calibration

At first calibration (N = 100), agreement between the two human coders on the primary outcome was AC1 0.456. Examination of the disagreements showed that they arose from two rules that did not determine an answer in the disputed cases, rather than from inconsistent application of rules that did. The largest single cause was source leakage; in some cases, the primary outcome was being coded from hashtags rather than from caption and on-screen text, producing systematic false positives. Both rules were rewritten, and the source restriction was made explicit for each variable. On a fresh sample of 60 posts (57 after all coders excluded the same three non-English posts), the same two coders reached AC1 0.977 (95% CI 0.931 to 1.000). The human pair met the threshold on every variable except exercise topic (0.795, 95% CI 0.671 to 0.920).

This interpretation is crucial to the logic of the framework. If the first figure had been read as evidence of unreliable coders, the response would have been retraining, and the ambiguity in the rules would have carried into the full corpus. There it would have been inherited by the LLM coder, which applies a written rule far more consistently than a human does and would therefore have propagated the ambiguity uniformly and invisibly. Low initial agreement between human coders is more usefully read as a property of the instrument than of the coders.

4.3 LLM pilot

Before the full corpus was coded, the LLM coded the same 60-post sample blind, using the initial prompt scaffold. Agreement on the primary outcome was below the threshold: 0.772 (95% CI 0.634 to 0.909) against IS and 0.795 (0.665 to 0.925) against AS, falling to 0.457 and 0.533 on the 19 posts carrying a claim. The LLM coded Mixed less often than either human. The adjudicated calibration example set was built to address this boundary, and the full corpus was coded with it. The pilot figures are superseded and are reported for completeness; the verification below applies to the final coding.

4.4 LLM verification

Agreement between the LLM and the human verifier on the primary outcome exceeded the threshold in the representative sample (Set A, AC1 0.847, 95% CI 0.781 to 0.914). Set B returned 0.868 (0.756 to 0.980), indicating that LLM-identified positives largely withstood human scrutiny; by design, it carries no information about missed cases. In Set A, the other universal variables met the threshold: female targeting (0.817), ethnic minority selfidentification (0.867), professional status (0.825), monetisation (0.853) and quality score (weighted AC2 0.923; n = 46). Exercise topic did not (0.782, 95% CI 0.709 to 0.855).

Of the eight conditional variables, assessed on the 57 posts that both coders coded as misinformation, three met the threshold: misuse of scientific reference (0.960), pseudoscientific language (0.904) and quick fix (0.870). Five did not: number of misinformation categories (0.613), misinformation category at six levels (0.609), absolute claim (0.479), commercial prompt (0.404) and guaranteed results (0.378). Confidence intervals for every variable are given in Table 1.

4.5 Reliability at the level of analysis

Two variables were collapsed post hoc to the binary distinction that their analyses actually test; both are labelled as post hoc throughout. Misinformation category rose from 0.609 at six levels to 0.803 (95% CI 0.650 to 0.956) for aesthetic claims versus all other categories. Female targeting was examined as any targeting versus none.

Female targeting is also reported across Sets A and B combined (N = 200) as a secondary estimate. Set B was selected on the LLM's misinformation code, not its targeting code, so combining the samples adds precision without conditioning on the variable being assessed; the Set A estimate (0.817) carries the reliability claim. Combined, agreement was 0.847 (95% CI 0.786 to 0.909) at three levels and 0.911 (0.857 to 0.966; 95.0% observed agreement) for any targeting versus none, with the non-English posts coded N/A counted as not targeted.

The collapse is informative, not merely convenient. For female targeting, disagreement arose almost entirely from the LLM classifying implicit targeting as explicit (12 of 30 posts coded as implicit by the human coder), whereas the explicit and no-targeting categories almost perfectly agreed. The level at which a variable is reliable therefore depends on the boundary being drawn. A multi-level variable can sit near the threshold overall, while the individual levels a study utilises for detailed analysis sit comfortably above it. Reporting reliability at the level of analysis, rather than the level of the codebook, avoids both discarding a usable measure and trusting an unusable distinction.

4.6 Variables coded reliably by the LLM

The pattern of success and failure is interpretable rather than random, and is the most transferable empirical finding of this study. The variables that met the threshold ask whether a specified textual feature is present: a reference to science, pseudo-scientific vocabulary, a promise of speed. The variables that fell short ask for a graded judgement about persuasive intent, or about which of several competing categories is most salient: whether a claim is absolute rather than merely strong, whether a post prompts a commercial action rather than merely mentioning a product, or which of six misinformation categories predominates. The LLM handled feature detection well and graded judgement poorly. This has a direct implication for instrument design in LLM-assisted content analysis.

Variables should be specified as feature-presence tests wherever the construct allows, and constructs that unavoidably require graded judgement should be expected to need human coding or to be reported as exploratory. Female targeting illustrates both points: whether a gendered term of address is present is a feature-presence test and was coded reliably, whereas whether a post is directed at women without saying so is a graded, inferential judgement and was not. The pattern is visible only because failing variables were reported, alongside the collapsed contrasts.

4.7 Consequences of failure

The five conditional variables below the threshold were moved to exploratory reporting under the rule fixed in advance. In the parent study, this affected two of four secondary aims: the analyses of misinformation categories and of rhetorical features are reported as exploratory, and the distribution of misinformation categories is explicitly not reported as a measured prevalence. Exercise topic, a universal variable, also fell below the threshold in Set A (0.782) and is used in the parent study only as an adjustment covariate and in descriptive comparisons, which are labelled exploratory.

4.8 Direction of disagreement

Disagreement on the primary outcome was not symmetrical. In Set A, the human coder coded 21 posts as containing misinformation and the LLM 10. All 11 disagreements about whether misinformation was present ran the same way: the human identified misinformation that the LLM did not. Remaining measurement error therefore runs towards under-detection, and prevalence estimates in the parent study are likely to be underestimates rather than estimates with error of unknown direction.

This could be reported only because the verification design facilitated it. An agreement statistic alone has no direction: AC1 0.847 is consistent with the LLM either over-calling or under-calling, and the two have opposite consequences for how a prevalence estimate should be read. It can also be reported only from Set A, which is why the two samples must be analysed separately.

4.9 Subgroup performance

Agreement on the primary outcome was similar for posts from creators who self-identified as an ethnic minority (AC1 0.848, 95% CI 0.748 to 0.949; n = 65) and for other posts (0.833, 0.756 to 0.910; n = 126). Agreement on female targeting was not: 0.744 (0.609 to 0.879) in the subgroup, below the threshold, against 0.902 (0.839 to 0.965) for other posts. The shortfall lay in separating implicit from explicit targeting. Of the 11 subgroup posts that the human coder coded as implicitly targeted, the LLM coded 8 as explicit and 2 as implicit, whereas agreement on any targeting versus none held (0.889, 0.780 to 0.997). The split between implicit and explicit targeting among ethnic minority creators in the parent study is therefore not reliably measured. The LLM also under-detected self-disclosed minority identity: of 65 posts the human coder flagged, the LLM coded 15 as not self-identifying, so the flagged subgroup is conservative. The test covers only creators who disclosed an identity in text, and does not address performance on content from minority creators in general.

4.10 Independent reimplementation

Reimplementation reproduced every reported result exactly, with one exception. The first Python implementation of the primary regression excluded rows with a missing value on any predictor (n = 2,365), whereas the jamovi implementation retained missingness as a separate category (n = 2,447). Both were arithmetically correct, but odds ratios differed by up to 5%.

The divergence was traced to an unstated analytical decision rather than to computation and was resolved by adopting one convention explicitly in both environments (complete-case analysis, excluding posts coded N/A on a model predictor). This failure mode is worth highlighting because it is the one that dual implementation identifies: not arithmetic error, but a specification choice that neither implementation had been required to state. It has the same structure as the instrument failure in the first calibration round: an underspecified rule, applied consistently but differently by two competent agents.

5. Discussion

>Discussion

5.1 Principal contribution

The framework does not make LLM coding equivalent to human coding; on five of eight conditional variables it was clearly not. What it does is make the limitations visible, bounded and consequential: visible because agreement is reported for every variable, including those that fall short; bounded because the direction of remaining error on the primary outcome is known; and consequential because the handling of failing variables was fixed before the results were seen. This is a lower bar than validity, but a more achievable one. A reader of a study reported in this way does not have to trust the coder. They can see which variables to trust, which to discount, and in which direction the remaining error runs.

5.2 Reproducibility, and what replaces it

An LLM coder is not directly reproducible. Identical inputs need not return identical outputs; models are versioned, deprecated and withdrawn faster than papers are published; and a study coded on one model version cannot be rerun on it indefinitely. A framework that implied otherwise would promise something it cannot deliver.

What can be offered instead is a measured agreement claim for each variable against independent human coding, together with the deposited instrument, prompt scaffold and codebook. These allow another team to apply the same instrument with a different coder, human, or model, and compare the results. This is a weaker claim than computational reproducibility, but a stronger one than is currently available.

5.3 Relation to existing guidance

The Cochrane position that no step of a rapid review should be fully automated [2] is not contradicted here, because no step was fully automated. The instrument was written by a human, verification was carried out by a human, and the rule for handling failure was set by the authors in advance. The LLM applied a human-written instrument at a scale humans cannot reach, under human audit.

Where this analysis diverges from a strict reading is in the implied comparison. The Cochrane statement itself cites evidence of substantial error in human data extraction, with rates of up to 50% [9], and the human coders here began at AC1 0.456 on the primary outcome. Treating human coding as the reliable baseline and LLM coding as a deviation from it describes neither accurately. The realistic comparison is between an LLM-coded corpus of 6,982 posts with per-variable verification and a human-coded corpus of 1,200 posts with reliability reported on a subsample. The second is not confidently more reliable, and it does not answer the same question.

5.4 Proposed reporting items

Table 2 lists items the authors suggest should be reported in any content analysis that uses an LLM as a coder. They derive from the framework above and from the failure modes it exposed. The list has not been validated and is offered as a starting point for consensus work, not as a standard. Items 14, 15 and 16 carry the greatest significance. Reporting only the variables that met the threshold, reporting agreement without stating what would have happened had it been lower, or reporting a direction-free statistic for an estimate that will be read as a prevalence each leaves readers unable to judge how far the results can be trusted.

5.5 Scope of the framework

The framework does not establish that the LLM's codes are correct, only that they agree with a human coder's to a measured degree in a sample. Agreement is not accuracy; both coders may be wrong in the same direction against an external standard. It offers no remedy for the variables the coder could not handle; graded judgements about persuasive intent were lacking here, and the framework detects the failure without rectifying this. It is also restricted to written text. Content carried in images, video or audio was outside the coding scheme, which limits what any reliability figure reported here can signify.

5.6 Limitations

The verification samples are modest (N = 150 and N = 50), and the figures for conditional variables rest on 57 posts. Confidence intervals around AC1 at these sample sizes are wide, so the ordering of variables above and below the threshold should not be over-interpreted; the threshold crossings are the interpretable result.

The second coder did not write the instrument and coded the verification samples blind to the LLM codes and to sample membership, but they were not independent of the instrument's development. Their disagreements at first calibration identified the two underspecified rules, and the instrument was revised in response. This is the iterative method working as intended [6], not a methodological error, but it limits what the final figure signifies. AC1 0.977 should be read as agreement reached by the development pair on an instrument refined using disagreements, not as an estimate of what the deposited instrument would achieve with a new coder. The latter is the quantity relevant to transferability, and this study does not measure it. The same coder also provided both the human-human and the LLM-human comparisons, so those figures are not independent of each other.

The subgroup analysis captures only creators who disclosed an ethnic minority identity in text. This conservative rule under-identifies the subgroup and leaves performance on content from undisclosed minority creators untested.

The framework was developed and applied within a single study, in one content domain, with one model. Its transferability to other settings is untested.

6. Conclusions

An LLM can serve as the primary coder in a health content analysis at a scale human coding cannot reach, and the resulting study can be made auditable. The conditions are that the instrument is written by humans before coding is automated; that verification is designed to reveal the direction of error, not only its size; that reliability is reported for every variable, including those that fall short; and that the consequence of falling short is fixed before results are known.

Computational reproducibility is not available and should not be claimed. Reported as described here, an LLM-coded study asks readers to check rather than to trust. That is a reasonable request, and the current absence of a reporting standard makes it hard to meet.

The framework applies to studies in which an LLM codes written text in health content, such as post captions and on-screen text, and a human-written instrument and an independent human verifier are available.
