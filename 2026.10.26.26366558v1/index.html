Power Analysis Check

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

Research on vaping prevention messages has predominantly examined adolescents' perceptions of and responses and the research exploring how experts' perspectives is limited.

Expert knowledge is crucial for conceptualising the risk phenomenon and its key causal mechanisms, uncertainties, and issues that are relevant to address in evidence-based communication strategies. This study therefore explored how professionals and experts conceptualise and understand adolescent vaping behaviour, including its perceived causes and drivers.

Semi-structured interviews were conducted with a purposive sample of 12 experts from medical research, addiction science, public health prevention, and adolescent-oriented practice in Norway. Data collection and analysis were informed by the mental models approach to risk communication.

The analysis identified an expert-derived mental model/influence diagram of adolescent vaping behaviour. Across expert domains, vaping was understood as a multifactorial phenomenon emerging from interacting biological, psychological, and social factors operating across multiple system levels. The first theme describes vaping initiation as a socially embedded and often intermittent practice shaped by identity development, peer belonging, and low perceived harm. The second theme captures how vaping is enabled, sustained, and legitimised through interacting drivers operating across social, structural, commercial, and regulatory contexts. The third theme focuses on reinforcement processes, whereby nicotine dependence and emotional coping mechanisms contribute to more sustained use.

According to these expert accounts, adolescent vaping is sustained and legitimised through broader processes of normalisation operating across multiple societal levels. The expert perspectives identified in this study suggest that comparative risk framings of e-cigarettes as less harmful than cigarettes, and the social functions of vaping within adolescent peer cultures, may be important considerations in the development of risk communication targeting adolescents.

1 Introduction

The use of e-cigarettes has increasingly become a topic of concern in public health, particularly in relation to adolescent use [1] [2]. Concerns have been raised regarding nicotine dependence, smoking initiation, and the marketing of e-cigarettes to children and adolescents [1] [2] [3] [4]. The World Health Organization has called for "urgent and strong decisive action" to prevent non-smokers from initiating e-cigarette use and developing nicotine dependence [1, 2]. Whereas proponents of a precautionary approach emphasise uncertainties and potential harms, others view e-cigarettes as a potential harm reduction tool for people who smoke combustible cigarettes [5]. Consequently, perspectives on the risks and appropriate risk communication of vaping vary across public health and scientific communities.

The existing health and risk communication literature regarding vaping prevention remains heavily oriented toward audience-centred approaches, particularly on how adolescents interpret, perceive, and respond to vaping prevention messages [6] [7]. According to the mental models approach to risk communication, not only the perspectives of the target group are crucial for developing evidence-based communication strategies, but also those of the expert knowledge holders. Experts can pinpoint their construction of complexities, uncertainties, and causalities that are vital for a comprehensive understanding of vaping behaviour [8]. The current research on adolescent perceptions and message effects needs to be supplemented with research on expert knowledge and perspectives.

The mental models approach to risk communication outlined by Morgan et al. [8] provides a framework for mapping expert knowledge as causal systems, identifying key drivers, uncertainties, and relationships relevant for informed decision-making. This approach to systematically eliciting and representing expert knowledge is particularly valuable in a field where the evidence base is heterogeneous and rapidly evolving [9] as is currently the case for vaping research. According to Bruine de Bruin and Bostrom [10], expert mental models constitute an essential foundation for evidence-based communication because they provide a benchmark against which target audience understandings, knowledge gaps, and misconceptions can be assessed. The study seeks to answer the central research question: How do professionals and experts conceptualise and understand adolescent vaping behaviour, including its perceived causes and drivers?

2 Method

The present study applies a qualitative method designed as an individual interview study with Norwegian domain experts from medical research, drug science, public health prevention, and professionals working directly with adolescents.

The present study focuses on the first step of the mental models framework by eliciting expert knowledge to develop an expert mental model of adolescent vaping behaviour [8].

Similar expert-focused mental model studies have been conducted within other public health topics [8, [11] [12]. Following this tradition, the present study uses in-depth qualitative interviews to elicit experts' mental representations of vaping behaviour, including perceived causes, drivers, contributing factors, and their interrelationships.

2.1 Participants and sampling strategy

The experts held diverse professional roles, including 93 researchers, clinicians, public health practitioners, and frontline professionals working 94 directly with adolescents.

2.3 Context

This study was conducted in Norway, where e-cigarettes are subject to regulation [14] [15]. In short, the sale of nicotine-containing e-liquids through domestic retailers is prohibited and flavours other than tobacco, such as spices, fruit or candy, are not permitted in e-cigarettes and e-liquids sold in Norway. Further, the minimum age for purchasing e-cigarettes and related products is 18 years, and private individuals are not permitted to import e-cigarettes or e-liquids from other countries. Vaping is prohibited in the same places where smoking is already banned, applying to both nicotine and non-nicotine e-cigarettes [16]. Although adolescent vapers should not have access to e-cigarettes, particularly nicotine-containing products, such products-including e-liquids and related equipment-are known to be purchased illegally [17].

The implementation of strict tobacco control policies in Norway, have contributed to a marked decline in cigarette smoking and reduced everyday exposure to smoking/tobacco among younger generations. However, recent Norwegian data suggest that vaping has increased among adolescents. In 2025, 13% of women and 6% of men aged 16-24 years reported occasional e-cigarette use, whereas daily use remained at approximately 1%.

Among those who reported using e-cigarettes daily or occasionally in 2025, approximately 77% used nicotine-containing e-cigarettes, around 11% used nicotine-free e-cigarettes, and 12% did not know whether the e-cigarette they used contained nicotine [18].

2.4 Analysis

The expert interviews were analysed using inductive thematic analysis following Braun and Clarke's [19] six-phase framework. First, SHB transcribed and read all interviews to become familiar with the data; KBB also reviewed the transcripts, and initial impressions were discussed within the research team. SHB then generated initial inductive codes. These were organised into a four-level hierarchical structure (individual, peer, social media, and regulation) to identify patterns and relationships, and reviewed by all co-authors in analytic workshops and online meetings.

Themes were subsequently reviewed and refined to ensure coherence and distinction, and a structured coding document was developed to validate them against the dataset. The final themes captured experts mental models of key drivers of vaping behaviour, outlined as interconnected biological, psychological, and social factors across system levels. In the final phase, results were written, illustrative extracts selected, and interpretations discussed among co-authors. The themes represent the informants' understanding of vaping behaviour, and the influence diagram is an analytical construction and visualisation of the expert domain informants' causal understandings of adolescent vaping behaviour.

The research team's interdisciplinary background in public health, psychology, and health communication informed both the design and interpretation of the study. Reflexive discussions were conducted throughout the analytic process to critically assess how preconceptions may have shaped coding and interpretation.

3 Results

The analysis identified three interrelated themes capturing expert understandings of adolescent vaping. Across expert domains, adolescent vaping was understood as a multifactorial process emerging from interacting biological, psychological, and social factors operating across system levels. The first theme describes how the experts understood vaping initiation, and vaping as an intermittent phase-like and socially embedded practice within adolescent peer cultures, shaped by processes of identity formation, belonging, and low perceived harmfulness. The second theme captures how the experts understood adolescent vaping behaviour as being enabled, sustained, and legitimised through the interaction of multiple system-level drivers. The third theme focuses on processes of reinforcement, in which nicotine exposure and emotional coping mechanisms were understood by experts to contribute to more sustained use among a vulnerable subgroup of adolescents. 3.1 Vaping as a phase of adolescent in a peer driven social context

As illustrated in the influence diagram (Figure 1), peer context and individual factors represent central pathways through which broader system-level conditions are translated into vaping initiation. This theme shows that experts conceptualised adolescent vaping as an intermittent and socially embedded practice driven by peers, and shaped by processes of group belonging, identity formation, and low perceived harmfulness. Experts described the largest group of adolescent vapers as the occasional vaper, as adolescents are inherently risk-seekers driven by behaviour in peer-oriented social contexts. The function of vaping was understood as a marker of group identity and belonging, in which risk trivialisation legitimises use, while knowledge gaps regarding potential risks further reinforce this perception.

3.1.1 The occasional vaper

The experts conceptualised adolescence as a developmentally sensitive period characterised by heightened responsiveness to novelty, peer influence, and low-threshold risk-taking.

Vaping activates these age-specific dynamics by offering an accessible way to experiment with boundary-testing behaviour that is perceived as low in severity in terms of perceived health effects (ID: 3, 6, 9, 10, 11).

Adding to the general developmental vulnerability of adolescence, experts described current cohorts as particularly exposed to social conditions that increase susceptibility to riskoriented behaviour (ID: 6, 10,11). One social worker emphasises that lower secondary students are especially prone to substance use, framing this group as a generation characterised by increased norm-breaking (ID: 6). She linked this to post-pandemic changes, including greater visibility in public spaces and more frequent engagement in behaviours such as smoking. These developments were understood as rooted in disrupted socialisation, growing inequalities, and experiences of exclusion, which in turn drive young people to seek belonging in peer environments where norm-breaking functions as a marker of affiliation.

Across expert domains, vaping was conceptualised as an intermittent and largely transient phenomenon, particularly among eighth-grade students in the first year of lower secondary school (ID: 4, 5, 6, 9, 10, 11). The "everyday vaper," or clear behavioural indicators of nicotine dependence, was described as relatively uncommon in lower secondary school contexts (ID: 4, 5, 6, 9, 10, 11). As one drug science researcher noted:

"Most adolescent vaping seems to be transient and experimental.

There is very little regular, daily use among adolescents. Instead, it appears to be something that happens in phases-it becomes a hype, and then it fades and they stop." (ID: 4) Several experts emphasised that vaping is often seen as losing its appeal as adolescents grow older and transition into tenth grade and upper secondary school (ID: 5, 10, 11). As one drug science researcher noted, "This may indicate that attention is directed toward the playful or exploratory character of the behaviour" (ID: 5). At the time of the interviews, this developmental pattern reinforced experts' interpretation of vaping as primarily a phase-like phenomenon rather than a behaviour with high perceived addiction potential.

Although the typical adolescent vaper was conceptualised as an occasional social vaper across expert accounts, the phenomenon was also acknowledged as multifaceted, as described by a drug science researcher: "There is no single set of motivations, and the user group is not uniform.

People use it for different reasons, engage in different practices, and face very different levels of risk. This makes prevention work difficult and raises questions about which strategies or 'buttons' to push." (ID: 5).

Adolescents who develop stronger or more persistent dependence through vaping were understood to differ from the majority of experimental users. Across experts, these adolescents were characterised by additional risk factors, including limited boundaries at home, broader risk-taking behaviour, emotional regulation difficulties, and experiences of social exclusion (ID: 6, 9, 10). Experts emphasised that for this subgroup, vaping dependence often forms part of a wider pattern of vulnerability, rather than emerging from vaping alone.

3.1.2 A marker of group identity and belonging

Adolescent vaping was consistently conceptualised by experts as a socially embedded practice that serves important identity-forming and relational functions within peer groups. Rather than being driven primarily by individual preferences or substance effects, vaping was framed as a collective activity through which adolescents negotiated belonging, status, and social identities associated with being "cool". Several experts emphasised that vaping often unfolds within specific social rituals and contexts-such as school breaks, parties, or shared pauses, often in school toilets-where engaging in vaping legitimises participation in group activities and temporarily suspends other demands, giving the practice a ceremonial and contextual character (ID: 3, 4, 10, 11).

Experts further conceptualised vaping as a visible marker of identity. What product is used, how it is used, and with whom it is shared were understood to communicate signals about identity, taste, status and group affiliation, in ways comparable to clothing, style, or other symbolic markers. In this sense, vaping functions as a distinction marker, allowing adolescents to position themselves socially and to be read by others in intended ways (4), as described by the drug science researcher: "Nicotine use, in general, contains an identity-forming element. The type of product used, the manner of use, and the social context in which it is used can all signal aspects of one's identity to others" (ID: 4).

Vaping was described as something adolescents explore together. Experts emphasised that initial engagement often occurs through friendships, where trying vaping collectively becomes a shared project that strengthens social bonds. This shared exploration-selecting flavours, devices, or experimenting with effects-was understood as enjoyable in itself, independent of substance content, and as a way of building companionship and mutual experience (ID: 4, 9).

Group pressure emerged as a central mechanism shaping vaping behaviour, although experts stressed that such pressure is rarely explicit. School-based professionals described situations in which adolescents participate in vaping not because they want to, but because refusing risks exclusion from the group. In unstable social environments, where peer groups are described as fluid and insecure, joining peers in vaping is described as a strategy to avoid being left alone, marginalised, or pushed out of social spaces (ID: 3, 10, 11, 12). Participation also created implicit expectations of loyalty, where being part of shared rule-breaking reduces the likelihood of being reported or excluded, further binding individuals to the group and lowering barriers to continued involvement (ID: 10). Not wanting to be perceived as an outsider therefore functions as a central driver of vaping behaviour: actually think there is a lot of influence operating here-when one person does it, others follow.

It's almost as if, if you don't take part, you are seen as an outsider. I think that has a lot to do with it. If you feel unseen or unheard, that can trigger you to try it. And this doesn't necessarily require belonging to a close-knit group-it can operate at the level of the whole school. If others are doing it, you do it too, and you are pushed into it even if it wasn't something you had decided. It becomes a trigger, quite simply." (ID: 12).

Experts also linked vaping as a group marker to broader changes in adolescents' social environments, particularly the intensified role of digital platforms. Group chats, social media, and location-sharing technologies were described as amplifying feelings of comparison and exclusion by making social gatherings continuously visible. Within these contexts, adolescents were seen to navigate heightened pressure to be included, where not participating-or not being present-becomes immediately noticeable. Vaping was thus understood as one of several practices through which adolescents attempt to secure belonging and visibility in peer networks experienced as competitive and emotionally demanding (ID: 10, 11, 12).

3.1.3 Risk trivialisation and knowledge gaps legitimising the use

Experts described adolescent intermittent vaping as categorically different from adolescent smoking and therefore less readily associated with addiction or long-term health risks. This normalisation of the behaviour, together with adolescents' risk comparisons with high-risk and hazardous cigarettes, was further understood as reinforcing risk trivialisation. Risk trivialisation thus emerged as a driver both in adolescents' understandings of vaping and, more broadly, in how vaping is framed in public discourse, which in turn contributes to the normalisation of the behaviour (ID: 4, 5, 10, 11).

Moreover, excitement and curiosity were repeatedly identified by experts as central psychological drivers of adolescent vaping and were framed as part of normative developmental processes (ID: 4, 5, 9, 10, 11). As one community psychologist explained, "They vape out of curiosity. And they have a need to be engaged in something like that" (ID: 9). Experts described vaping as a form of experimentation aligned with normative adolescent boundary-testing. Within their mental models of adolescent vaping behaviour, experimental engagement with a product perceived as low harm, yet with a clear risk element, was understood as a key driver of occasional use, as illustrated by this statement from a schoolbased professional in lower secondary school:"It is a form of experimentation for adolescents.

It is exploratory. It is about excitement. It is somewhat illegal, but not entirely… It is not narcotics. It is not really crossing the line in a major way." (ID: 10)

In addition, experts highlighted substantial gaps in adolescents' knowledge about vaping products and associated health risks as a contributing factor to low perceived harm.

The toxicologist emphasised that vaping was not a uniform product; risk was described as depending on what is used, how it is used, and what substances are added to the device (ID: 2).

Experts further emphasised that adolescents may be unaware of whether and how much the products they use contain nicotine, and that devices may include substances such as THC or CBD (11). Moreover, experts emphasised that adolescents lack knowledge about what they are vaping. Even regulatory authorities are described as lacking a complete overview of permissible substances in e-cigarettes, as the product category is understood to remain largely unregulated (ID: 2, 4, 5, 7).

3.2 Vaping as a low-threshold and systemically legitimised adolescent behaviour

As illustrated in the influence diagram (Figure 1), experts conceptualised system-level conditions as contributing to adolescent vaping indirectly through broader processes of normalisation. This theme captures how product characteristics, social media dynamics, regulatory gaps, and frameworks of relativised risk collectively shape a context in which vaping is perceived as acceptable, accessible, and low-risk. Within experts' mental models, product appeal through social media lowers the threshold for initial use by framing vaping as an attractive, novel, and experimental part of peer practices. These dynamics are further supported by limited regulation and weak enforcement and competing risk logics, where vaping is downplayed by a large group of experts through comparison with smoking and deprioritised within prevention systems.

3.2.1 Product appeal through social media

Experts described product characteristics of e-cigarettes as central drivers of adolescents' experimentation with vaping. Product features and their appeal through social media frame vaping as playful and exploratory, contributing to low perceived harm and the normalisation of vaping among adolescents.

The frequent introduction of new devices was understood to generate immediate curiosity, particularly when products appear more technologically advanced or visually distinctive than earlier versions (ID: 4). As one drug science expert noted: "New products are constantly being introduced, and some of them may seem cooler than the old ones.

That creates curiosity about products with lots of extra features, like touch screens, Bluetooth connectivity, and all sorts of things (ID: 4).

He further perceived disposable e-cigarettes as especially appealing, as they are understood to be accessible, novel, and well suited to short-term experimentation rather than sustained use (ID: 4).

Flavours were similarly emphasised as a major source of appeal, lowering the threshold for experimentation and reinforcing vaping as an activity driven by excitement and sensory curiosity rather than by a deliberate pursuit of nicotine effects (ID: 2, 4).

Product appeals through social media were further perceived as powerful influences on adolescents' vaping behaviour, particularly when messages were repeated by influencers or adults. Combined with adolescents' limited source criticism, these appeals reinforce misconceptions about harm and contribute to the normalisation of risk (ID: 10).

3.2.2 Limited regulation and weak enforcement

Adolescent vaping was understood to occur in a context characterised by limited regulation and weak enforcement across multiple arenas. Experts described a lack of consequences from parents, schools, and authorities as contributing to a permissive environment in which vaping can continue largely unchallenged (ID: 8, 10, 11).

Adolescent vaping was frequently described as taking place within illegal and largely unregulated markets, where products are often obtained through social media platforms such as Snapchat, where adolescents connect with sellers (ID: 4, 5, 8, 10, 11). As one school-based professional noted:

It's Snapchat. If you don't know where to buy, if you haven't done it before and are going to start buying, you just search for 'Puff'. Then a lot of different sellers come up that you can buy from." (ID: 10)

Experts also emphasised the lack of effective regulation of illegal sales through social media platforms. This was framed as particularly problematic given that very young adolescents can arrange transactions with strangers and engage in illicit purchasing practices within potentially risky drug markets (ID: 4, 5, 8, 10, 11, 12). This regulatory gap is further facilitated by a lack of system-oriented prevention efforts, with insufficient coordination between prevention, regulation, and enforcement (ID: 8).

Moreover, school-based experts reported a lack of enforcement of rules by both parents and schools. Parents are frequently described as unaware of vaping products and practices, with limited knowledge of what devices look like or what to look for. This lack of parental awareness reduces the likelihood of detection and consequences at home, thereby weakening preventive efforts (ID: 10, 11). Vaping is also described as largely absent from formal drug prevention strategies at the school level, which further contributes to its normalisation (ID: 10, 11). Unlike smoking, vaping was described as difficult to detect and regulate in school settings due to the absence of smoke and persistent smell, often masked by perfume. This practical invisibility makes it challenging for staff to identify individual users or impose consequences, leading to uncertainty and hesitancy in enforcement.

Within experts' mental models, this absence of clear responses in adolescents' environments was understood to signal a form of tacit acceptance, thereby weakening the perceived seriousness of vaping as a risk behaviour (ID: 10, 11). The combination of easy access, novelty, and illegality, together with weak enforcement, reinforces the experimental and risk-seeking aspects of vaping behaviour (ID: 4, 5, 8, 10, 11).

3.2.3 Competing risk logics

Adolescent vaping behaviour was framed as a comparatively minor concern by some experts, and as a significant risk by others. This disagreement revealed a structural tension between harm-reduction and precautionary approaches to risk, which shapes the public discourse of vaping risk and thereby also its prevention efforts.

The drug science experts consistently assessed vaping in comparison to smoking, as well as in relation to other forms of substance use and broader public health challenges. They did not understand vaping as a major public health problem comparable to smoking, due to both lower assumed health risks and significantly lower prevalence (ID: 4, 5). Smoking was understood to occupy a fundamentally different risk category, often characterised as "in a league of its own" (ID: 5). In contrast, vaping was understood as a lower-impact phenomenon at the population level, particularly given predominantly occasional use among adolescents (5). As one drug science researcher explained: "If we look at whether vaping can become a public health problem in the same way as smoking, it never will.

That has to do with both lower risk and much lower prevalence" (ID: 4).

The drug science researcher explained how within prevention work, professional experience with severe substance use and tobacco-related disease shaped a relatively high threshold for concern, which in turn influenced how vaping was evaluated and prioritised (ID: 5). Within this comparative risk perspective, school-based professional described that adolescent vaping was often interpreted in school contexts as less urgent than other problems, thereby reducing its salience in prevention efforts (ID: 10, 11). Some of the experts argued for a pragmatic approach to risk, arguing that adolescent is inherently risk seeking, and having vaping as a low-risk alternative may be a safer option, than adolescents choosing other more harmful substances (ID: 4, 5, 10, 11).

At the same time, other medical experts were clear that lower relative risk should not be conflated with safety. These experts emphasised a precautionary stance towards adolescent health. They argued that uncertainty regarding long-term health effects, early nicotine exposure during developmental periods, and the ethical implications of normalising experimentation among young people necessitate a more restrictive and cautious approach, irrespective of relative risk comparisons (ID: 2, 3, 8). Others argued that the risks associated with e-cigarettes were substantial, particularly in light of concerns regarding nicotine addiction and adolescent brain development. They perceived the availability of e-cigarettes as a smoking cessation tool as coming at the expense of children and young people, whose health and neurodevelopment were being placed at risk (ID:3).

A central point of disagreement concerned how uncertainty about long-term health effects ought to be weighed in relation to adolescents. Some medical experts emphasised that the evidence base for vaping remains incomplete, particularly with regard to long-term outcomes such as cancer, cardiovascular disease, and respiratory harm (1, 2). This uncertainty is itself framed as a significant risk, especially when products are used during critical developmental periods. The toxicologist critically questioned the widespread use of comparative risk arguments:"It's become completely wrong to argue that it's 'not so dangerous' just because it's less harmful than smoking. These are products that carry a health risk, and we don't know the full consequences yet-especially when you start experimenting with 13-year-olds" (ID: 2). A researcher on cancer and smoking risk, argued that it was naïve to wait for definitive evidence that e-cigarettes cause cancer, given the existing knowledge that many of the carcinogenic substances found in e-cigarette aerosols are already known to cause cancer (ID: 3). Within this precautionary approach to risk, the focus for some experts shifted from individual biomedical harm to broader normative concerns. The key question is not only how harmful vaping is. Several experts raised concerns about vaping as a potential gateway to broader substance-use trajectories, particularly among adolescents already oriented towards risk-taking (ID: 6, 9, 12). Others highlighted that even if vaping does not lead to substantial disease burden, its normalisation may alter norms around substance use and rule-breaking during adolescence, with possible downstream effects (ID: 8).

3.3 Addiction reinforcing further vaping behaviour

As illustrated in the influence diagram (Figure 1), this theme captures the processes through which adolescent vaping, following initiation, may develop into reinforced and more sustained patterns of use over time. Experts described how nicotine exposure and emotional coping functions contribute to continued use, positioning addiction not as a primary driver of behaviour, but as a consequence of prior engagement that reinforces further vaping.

In experts' mental models, continued use was shaped through nicotine addiction and multiple nicotine product use, as well as through the externalisation of emotional coping, where vaping becomes a habitual strategy for managing everyday emotions.

3.3.1 Nicotine addiction and multiple nicotine product use

Nicotine addiction was positioned as a mechanism that can reinforce and stabilize vaping behaviour over time, rather than as a primary driver of initiation. Early vaping among adolescents without prior experience with cigarettes or snus is not understood to be nicotine-driven but motivated by other psychological and social factors. However, the drug science experts emphasized that once nicotine exposure becomes more sustained, the risk of dependence increase (ID: 4, 5).

Nicotine was described as a stimulant that triggers neurobiological processes involving dopamine, serotonin, and adrenaline, producing pleasurable yet activating effects such as increased alertness and a perceived "kick" or boost and temporary cognitive effects, including enhanced attention and memory (ID: 3, 4, 9). While these effects are not understood as central motivations for initial experimentation, experts described them as becoming more salient with more regular use, thereby reinforcing continued vaping behaviour (ID: 4, 9).

Drug science experts further stressed that vaping should not be understood as an isolated behaviour, but as part of a broader pattern of nicotine use that frequently overlaps with snus and cigarette smoking. Within this understanding, co-use of nicotine products was seen as substantially increasing the risk of cigarette smoking and nicotine addiction. Repeated nicotine exposure is therefore understood as increasing the likelihood of continued vaping and, for a subset of adolescents, the development of dependence (ID: 5).

3.3.2 The externalisation of emotional coping

Complementing biological perspectives, the some of the experts framed addiction as extending beyond nicotine's neurobiological effects, emphasizing its social and psychological function in regulating everyday emotions (ID: 9, 3). Within this account, vaping was understood by the community psychologist to serve as a form of emotional coping-helping manage boredom, unease and restlessness, sadness, or discomfort-regardless of nicotine content (ID: 9).

If you're not feeling well, it may be that you eat too much, become addicted to exercise, or start vaping. Vaping then becomes a kind of comfort. And then we find comfort in other things too, and it turns into an addiction-it becomes a go-to. Instead of dealing with life, it becomes a go-to. Something that sort of keeps you afloat (ID: 9).

In this perspective, addiction was described as emerging when individuals become unable to cope with ordinary emotions, with vaping serving an emotion regulation function as a short-term relief at the expense of interfering with normal reward processes and emotional regulation.

4 Discussion

The findings show that experts conceptualise adolescent vaping behaviour as a multi-causal and dynamic process operating across interacting system levels. Vaping is positioned as a socially meaningful group practice driven by affiliation, identity signalling, ritualisation, shared exploration, peer pressure, and performative status, which is an acceptable, normalized and tolerable risk by both adolescents and their immediate social environment. Within this understanding, vaping is less about individual risk-taking and more as a relational strategy for managing belonging and social vulnerability within contemporary adolescent peer cultures.

However, while these proximal drivers are primarily located within adolescent peer cultures, these behaviours are shaped and enabled by broader structural conditions. In particular, limited regulation of illegal products, the pervasive role of social media promotion, and a dominant risk discourse are highlighted as the reason why vaping can emerge, spread, and become normalized due to the framing of vaping as relatively low-risk compared to smoking.

These findings align with previous research showing that adolescents' perceptions of vaping are strongly shaped by social norms and exposure to marketing, access and e-cigarette use among peers [6, [20] [21] [22]. However, whereas previous research has primarily focused on individual-level determinants and social influences [6] [7], the present study suggests that the normalisation process is not confined to adolescent peer cultures alone, but also confined to the way e-cigarettes are marketed and regulated in the society, along with the public discourse of vaping risk.

The findings suggest that normalisation operates not only within adolescent peer cultures but also within professional and experts' understandings of vaping risk. While several experts identified risk trivialisation as a mechanism contributing to vaping uptake, some experts simultaneously framed vaping as a relatively minor concern when compared to smoking. This reflects competing risk logics within expert discourse itself, where harmreduction perspectives may unintentionally contribute to broader processes of normalisation.

This highlights the importance of considering how macro-level contexts, social environments, and individual processes interact over time to produce risk behaviour. Future research should therefore move beyond individual-reductionist models and examine how structural, social, and professional understandings of risk interact over time to shape this behaviour [23].

This tension between precautionary and harm-minimization logics in how experts understand adolescent vaping behaviour has been identified as a conflict in the e-cigarette debate [5]. In this study, experts adopting a precautionary perspective emphasise absolute risk and the uncertainty surrounding long-term health effects, which holds that a lack of scientific certainty should not delay preventive action. In contrast, experts drawing on harmminimization logic emphasise a relative risk perspective and the minimisation of overall harm, even in the presence of uncertainty [5]. Within this framework, risk behaviours are evaluated in relation to more harmful alternatives, and the aim is to reduce overall harm rather than eliminate risk entirely. Accordingly, these experts emphasise relative risk, often comparing vaping to more harmful behaviours such as cigarette smoking.

This internal tension within the expert panel establishes an analytically significant finding. Mental models do not necessarily display agreement across experts' accounts, which are in line with other expert mental models' studies [11]. How these accounts should inform a communication strategy is therefore also a normative judgement about how risk and uncertainty should be managed.

The evidence on risk communication is conflicting in how to approach expert disagreement, but studies indicate that if this disagreement is reproduced in the public health messaging, professional practice, and media coverage, it may increase perceived uncertainty and weaken the clarity of public health messaging and affect risk perception and trusted sources [24]. Moreover, when the public is exposed to conflicting messages and feels that authorities are "downplaying" information, or that they are not value-neutral in their presentation of risk, people often choose to trust information from other sources [25]. From a mental models perspective, the findings highlight comparative risk framings, scientific uncertainties, and the perceived social functions of vaping as potentially important topics for risk communication. These factors were identified by experts as influencing how vaping is understood, accepted, and normalised among adolescents.

Whereas recent consensus efforts have focused on synthesising evidence regarding the health effects of e-cigarettes and developing recommendations for practice [26], the present study contributes a complementary perspective by mapping how experts conceptualise the causal processes underlying adolescent vaping behaviour. Rather than identifying consensus alone, the study highlights both shared assumptions and competing risk logics within expert understandings and integrates these into a systems-oriented model of vaping initiation and continuation.

Furthermore, the present findings suggest that some drug science experts have a causal understanding of vaping behaviour as a temporally structured process, where initiation is socially embedded, while addiction emerges later as a reinforcing mechanism for continued use. This perspective contrasts with dominant biomedical framings, where nicotine dependence is typically treated as the central causal mechanism underlying use [4], in particular because dependence may emerge relatively early in the trajectory of use [27].

Although, this study only explores the perception of causal relationships, this conceptualization of vaping addiction may provide insight into why prevention messages focusing on addiction often show limited effectiveness among adolescents [7, 28]. If addiction is not a key driver, messages focusing on addiction and nicotine may fail, as they don't address the motivational drivers of vaping initiation among adolescents. Messages related to "vaping causes nicotine dependence", "this is dangerous for the brain" may not be relevant for the occasional and experimental vaper as they don't experience to be driven by addiction. It's the identity, the belonging social positioning and the hype, the messages need to address to reach this target group. This is supported by Gerson et al. [29] who argue that because adolescents may initiate or continue e-cigarette use as a way of coping with psychosocial stressors, prevention programmes should incorporate mental health education and adaptive coping skills to address these underlying risk factors for vaping initiation and addiction.

Systematic reviews support that effective elements of school health promotion across behavioural domains, including substance abuse address social influences and norms, alongside with cognitive-behavioral skills to enhance self-regulation and coping skills [30].

The systematic review by Wu et al. [6] supports the importance of vaping status in targeting messages, as non-vapers have different risk beliefs and policy support of e-cigarettes than vapers. This study underscores that the primary drivers of vaping may differ among nonvapers, occasional vapers, and nicotine-dependent vapers, as well as across age groups within the broad age range during which children and adolescents initiate vaping. Consequently, prevention and communication strategies will require tailored messages for different target groups.

4.1 Limitations

This study employed a qualitative approach to analyzing how experts within different fields of expertise view the underlying causal mechanisms of this highly complex phenomenon. As the study consists of experts' subjective perspectives on the topic, the presented findings aim to provide insights rather than generalisations. Furthermore, the assessments reflect the state of knowledge at the time of the interviews and should be regarded as a snapshot of expert perspectives in a field where the evidence base continues to develop.

The recruitment followed a purposive sampling strategy to ensure that participants would hold a wide range of expert backgrounds, including both research scientists and practitioners, on different system levels, thus providing a multi-systemic view. In similar research, a sample of 11-13 experts is often considered sufficient to allow for a rich understanding of the topic under study -here the potential mechanisms underlying adolescents vaping behaviour as viewed by experts in the field [11]. However -as our study has uncovered -vaping in adolescents as understood by experts is a highly complex phenomenon, and a larger sample or other fields of expertise might have revealed an even larger breadth of understanding of drivers and causes of vaping behaviour. Future studies with larger and more varied samples are needed to further explore the results from the present study.

5 Conclusion

Experts across domains conceptualised adolescent vaping as a dynamic and multifactorial phenomenon emerging from interacting biological, psychological, social, and structural influences operating across multiple system levels. The findings suggest that prevention and risk communication efforts may benefit from considering these multiple levels of influence rather than focusing solely on individual behaviour and risk knowledge. In particular, the expert accounts highlight the importance of social pathways of initiation and the social meanings and functions associated with vaping within adolescent peer cultures.

Furthermore, the prominence of comparative risk framings, particularly perceptions of vaping as less harmful than cigarette smoking, suggests that future risk communication may benefit from considering how absolute risks, scientific uncertainties, and the social acceptability of vaping are communicated. Together, these findings identify several factors that may be relevant to consider in the development of risk communication about adolescent vaping.
