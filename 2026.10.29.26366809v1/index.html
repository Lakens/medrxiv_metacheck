Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background: The emergence in England of ceftriaxone-resistant Neisseria gonorrhoeae, the bacteria which causes gonorrhoea, is a public health concern. Most cases of ceftriaxone-resistant gonorrhoea in England are travel-associated. We assessed the barriers and facilitators to sexually transmitted infection testing among international students living in England.

We conducted semi-structured interviews (March-August 2025) with international students attending a university in London. We recruited students through various university networks and snowball sampling. We used the Capability, Opportunity and Motivation (COM-B) model and conducted a thematic analysis.

Results: We conducted 15 interviews. Most participants were from China (n=8) and Nigeria (n=2), with other participants from countries in Europe, Asia, the Middle East and the Americas. Most participants were 25-34 years old and in a postgraduate programme. Two-thirds of our sample had previously been tested for STIs (7 in England, and 3 in their home country). STI language and knowledge were key psychological capability barriers to STI testing, awareness of STI testing options was a facilitator. Under physical capability, no STI symptoms influenced testing for some, but prompted testing for others. Belief in confidentiality, privacy, and anonymity of testing, alongside perceived social and individual benefits of STI testing, were identified as facilitators under reflective motivation. Perceived risk varied based on relationship status. Anticipated judgement and stigma were the main social opportunity barriers, while trust in health professionals was the main facilitator. Fragmented services and poor proximity were key physical opportunity barriers; perceived availability of services and access to free, straightforward testing were facilitators.

We integrated these findings into national STI public health messaging targeted at university students in the England. The findings can be used to create culturally appropriate resources to raise awareness of STI-related symptoms and, among those with a need, promote STI testing.

Introduction

Neisseria gonorrhoeae, the sexually transmitted bacteria which causes gonorrhoea, is designated a high-priority pathogen by the World Health Organization due to widespread antimicrobial resistance (AMR). In England, the emergence of ceftriaxoneresistant N. gonorrhoeae is a public health concern as ceftriaxone is the last remaining antibiotic currently available for use as empirical monotherapy. As of August 2026, most of the 88 cases of ceftriaxone-resistant gonorrhoea detected in England have been travel-associated; mainly from individuals, including international students, with links to the Asia-Pacific, the region with the highest prevalence of ceftriaxone-resistant N. gonorrhoeae globally (1) (2) (3).

England is a top destination for international students, with China and Nigeria as the main countries of origin. Students aged 21-24 consistently represented the largest age group among international students enrolled in UK higher education between 2020/21 and 2024/25 accounting for 42% of enrolments in 2024/25 (4, 5). In England, sexual health services are provided free at the point of delivery to everyone irrespective of nationality or visa status -and this includes sexually transmitted infection (STI) testing at sexual health services (SHS), or through online postal self-sampling kits for STIs.

While there have been some research studies with students examining barriers and facilitators to STI testing, they have predominantly been among domestic students (6) (7) (8); due to the unique set of cultural and structural challenges faced by international students, these findings may not be generalisable to international students. Though there is some existing evidence that indicates that knowledge, attitudes, social and structural factors, as well as inequalities in access to SHS can affect the uptake of STI testing among young people (6, (8) (9) (10) (11), including international students (12-16), this area is under-researched in England.

Rapid detection and contact tracing are essential for the control of ceftriaxone-resistant N. gonorrhoeae strains, so it is important that international students, who may be unfamiliar with sexual healthcare delivery in England, access STI testing when needed.

We assessed the barriers and facilitators to STI testing among international students living in England and developed recommendations to mitigate the barriers.

Methods

Sampling strategy

International students from a university in London with a high proportion of international students were recruited for this study. Participants were deemed eligible if they were current or prior students, aged between 18 and 34 years, had resided in England for at least 6 months up to a maximum of 5 years, and were fluent in English. Participants did not need to have had an STI test in England before, nor did they need to have had any sexual experiences with others before the interview. We aimed to recruit 20 participants, as this is a sample size that is used in similar research studies (8, 14, 17). Data were reviewed throughout data collection to assess whether saturation has been reached, defined as the point at which no new themes or substantive insights were identified (18). Data collection was halted after 15 interviews due to logistical reasons. However, all our participants shared a common background -being an international student in Englandwhich was critical to our research question, and we are confident that our sample is therefore specific enough to gain answers to our research question (19).

Design and theoretical framework

We performed one-on-one virtual semi-structured interviews, using a topic guide informed by the COM-B model (20). The COM-B model is widely used in public health research to explain behaviour and covers the domains of capability ("C"; physical and psychological), opportunity ("O"; social and physical), and motivation ("M"; automatic and reflective). It is suitable for identifying barriers and facilitators to a target behaviour.

The framework is linked to intervention frameworks, such as the Behaviour Change Wheel (20).

Data collection

We recruited participants through university contacts who digitally distributed the recruitment materials to their students via newsletters, mailing lists, and social media.

We also used a snowball sampling approach whereby students recruited for the study identified other participants through their social networks. Interested participants completed an intake survey to assess eligibility. Eligible participants were provided with a consent form and a participant information sheet.

CS and CH conducted the interviews guided by the topic guide (S1 Topic guide in S1 Text). Audio interviews were conducted via Microsoft Teams and ranged in length from 30-60 minutes. Interviews were audio recorded and transcribed verbatim. Interviews took place between 18 March and 8 August 2025. Participants were compensated with a £40 voucher for an online multi-retailer website. Participant contact information was kept separately from recordings, transcripts, and interview notes, and names were deleted from transcripts. Interview recordings were temporarily stored on a secure UKHSA drive and deleted immediately from any local drives after upload.

Data analysis

We conducted a thematic analysis using the COM-B model. We used NVivo 14 for data analysis, following a hybrid approach to coding (21) and a 6-step analysis: 1) CS familiarised herself with the interview data.

2) CS generated an initial codebook based on three transcripts. The codes were reviewed by CH. CS deductively coded the data to the COM-B domains as either barriers, facilitators, recommendations, other and/or general context. Data was assigned to two or more COM-B domains where relevant. In some instances, participants described factors that made the target behaviour more difficult or easier but did not discourage or facilitate it. These accounts were distinguished from those classified as barriers or facilitators to support a more precise mapping to the Behaviour Change Wheel. Mixed accounts, in which the same factor was described as facilitating and preventing STI testing by the same individual, were identified and accounted for during coding. Codes were inductively generated.

3) To ensure the reliability of the codes, the codebook was iteratively reviewed by CH and AS.

4) The final codebook was exported to Microsoft Excel, and codes were visually clustered into themes. Themes were cross-checked by CH, AS, PN, and HM, and were discussed among all authors. Some themes encompassed factors that both facilitated and discouraged STI testing and were therefore retained as mixed themes.

5) While all themes in qualitative analyses are considered meaningful irrespective of frequency, we applied pragmatic thresholds to identify higher-priority themes to inform intervention recommendations. Themes including accounts of eight or more participants were classified as high priority, and those of between five and eight participants as medium priority. The results section is comprised solely of these themes. The low-priority themes (under 5 participants) can be found in S1 Table.

6) A thematic map was generated to visualise the themes and condense them into larger narratives (or overarching themes).

Patient and public involvement and engagement

Participant-facing materials were reviewed by two former international students, one each from China and Nigeria -representing the two highest proportions of nationalities among our sample. Feedback was reviewed and integrated into the final products. The interview guide was piloted with two UK Health Security Agency (UKHSA) employees with who have lived experience of being international students in the UK.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the UKHSA Research Ethics and Governance Group (REGG) no. R&D 603. The research team also obtained permission from the ethics board of the target university in London (name omitted to protect participants' confidentiality) to recruit among their international students. Written consent from all participants was obtained prior to conducting the interviews.

Researcher characteristics and reflexivity

The study team comprised individuals with diverse disciplinary and methodological backgrounds including behavioural science, epidemiology, and qualitative research. The interdisciplinary team composition allowed for open discussion about the interpretation of the data through multidisciplinary lenses. None of the members were international students at the time of the study, therefore, the data was interpreted from an external perspective.

Despite not having lived experience as an international student in the UK, the lead author's (CS) own experience of adjusting to a new culture and navigating unfamiliar health systems created potential points of connection with participants. This could have resulted in greater attention being given to experiences that resonated with her own. Additionally, CS's position within a public health organisation could have influenced what participants felt comfortable sharing. Discussions with the wider team were used to challenge assumptions and consider alternative interpretations.

Cultural norms and STIs Social Opportunity Barriers Anticipated judgement and stigma surrounding STIs: Anticipated social stigma and negative impacts on relationships with friends and family were reasons for non-disclosure of testing. For other participants, it wasn't so much about the act or the outcome of the test, but the implications associated with getting tested: "[…] I feel like [my parents] are telling me 'you need to take good care of yourself' which means if I got [an] STI I'm the one the be blamed […] because I'm not taking good care of myself […] they would ask me […] 'why do you have unprotected sexual intercourse' [or] 'why are you not taking care of yourself' [...] I'm trying to avoid that judgement, so that's why I didn't tell them this time." -P1(18 to 24 years, female, born in China, time lived in England between 3 and 5 years) Most participants reported experiencing some degree of cultural taboo regarding sexual health, which impacted the way they internalised stigma and communicated about STIs: "[…] it might not be culturally accepted to go out in the open to discuss something like this […] society basically determines [how much people] are vocal about what they are going through […] I don't think the stigma ever completely leaves, especially when the information is out there in the open. Back home [it may] be a difficult thing [to do]." -P3 (25 to 34 years, male, born in Nigeria, time lived in England 6 months to 1 year) Three participants highlighted that while stigma still played a role, it did not prevent them from getting tested. Some were less concerned about stigma and instead described feeling uncomfortable or awkward; others were concerned about being recognised in clinics by peers. Mixed Cultural dynamics shaping STI outlook: Participants highlighted that England's sexual health culture is more open than in their home countries', with England-based social networks offering greater support for STI-related concerns. This openness was partly attributed to differences in sex education; Chinese participants specifically noted that sexual health lacks cultural acceptance in their home country, resulting in limited formal education on the topic, which reinforces discomfort around open discussion and leads individuals to seek STI knowledge privately: "[…] I think the stigma is very real […] I think this is rooted in wider culture of STIs, 'cause in China, sex and anything related to sex [is] rarely spoken about, especially in public. So, traditional parents they offer no sex education at home, and for me, my parents never did. So, I had to […] figure things out myself. I [also] think the sex education material covered in school is also very minimal" -P5 (25 to 34 years, male, born in China, time lived in England between 1 and 2 years) Participants' perceptions about STI testing had evolved since living in England -they described being more open to getting tested and seeking support from peers on how to perform or access STI testing: "[…] I must say […] England is a lot more supportive about these kinds of things. So, people might [be more] likely to talk openly about these problems […] For now, [I] definitely feel more open than like, let's say, six years ago… I can feel the gradual open phase within myself" -P2 (18 to 24 years, male, born in China, time lived in England between 3 and 5 years) Physical Opportunity Facilitators Environment facilitates knowledge transfer or gain: Participants mentioned learning about STIs and testing from a variety of sources while living in England. Some examples include bus stop advertisements, posters in sexual health clinics, NHS or official government websites, and social media (e.g., Instagram, Baidu, RedNote). Some participants observed that information is more widely available in England, than compared to their home country, particularly those from China and Nigeria: "I think there [is] more information available here, especially the information from NHS […] it's much more better and available here […] there's an official tool [to find sexual health clinics] […] in China, [if] I want to search for that, I can only find some things [are] not from government, it's mostly private companies or from some non-profits" -P14 (25 to 34 years, male, born in China, time lived in England between 3 and 5 years) Knowledge as a foundation for STI testing Psychological Capability Barriers Lack of knowledge about STI prevention and care: Some participants discussed having limited knowledge about STIs in general, while others expressed not being able to interpret symptoms in the context of STIs: "[…] their names [are] one thing […] because I'm not familiar with that name, I'm not familiar with their symptoms as well. I'm not familiar with like the treatment and then the overall awareness of those disease[s]" -P1 (18 to 24 years, female, born in China, time lived in England between 3 and 5 years) Participants also discussed not being aware of the different testing methods and other wider STI preventative interventions (e.g., vaccinations) available in England. Difficulty navigating STI-related language: Participants expressed difficulties remembering, understanding and using technical STI terminology. STIs were viewed as having complex names which made them hard to remember; except HIV. Some participants felt they could not confidently communicate at a technical level with healthcare professionals: "[...] I think language is also a barrier because there are a lot of medical terms regarding the STI test and if I'm not very familiar with these terms, I cannot communicate with the doctors or staff working in the NHS." -P5 (24 to 34 years, male, born in China, time lived in England between 1 and 2 years) Other participants reported having challenges with knowing the English equivalent of STIs, some of them used translation apps during conversations with health professionals. This barrier was mentioned by most of the Chinese participants in our sample. Facilitators Awareness of STI testing options available in England: Students presented a good general awareness of the testing options available in England. Participants distinguished between different testing methods (i.e., blood test, urine or swab samples), and testing locations (e.g., sexual health clinics, GPs, and community-based organisations). Most knew that testing was free, including testing through online selfsampling kits. For some participants, a product of living in England was obtaining greater knowledge about STI testing through open conversations with peers allowing them to learn more about testing through informal social interactions. However, participants did not always explicitly link their knowledge to STI testing uptake: "[…] three months ago I have learned that I can have a free STI test in some […] sex clinic, so I just registered and took some tests […] I just learned from one of my friends […] this is the only resource, the first time I heard about this" -P13 (25 to 34 years, male, born in China, time lived in England 3 to 5 years) Testing conditions influencing STI testing Physical Opportunity Barriers Proximity of STI testing options: The cost (i.e., time and distance) associated with travelling to clinics was used as a reason why participants might not get tested: "[...] the time cost, it takes a lot of hours to get there, get back and also waiting there. It's just time, it takes a lot [...] [travelling to get tested in the future] will be a barrier for me" -P14 (25 to 34 years, male, born in China, time lived in England between 3 and 5 years) Fragmented and inconvenient STI testing services: Participants who had previously used the English healthcare system found STI testing services complicated to navigate. Some were unable to secure appointments flexibly due to limited opening hours or because clinics were not accepting new patients. In other instances, participants delayed testing because they found the pre-appointment screening questions burdensome: "[when] I try to make an appointment, they ask detailed questions about my recent highrisk behaviour, but I didn't want to pretend I had some [...] so, I gave up" -P5 (25 to 34 years, male, born in China, time lived in England 1 to 2 years) Two participants described feeling overwhelmed when accessing information online about testing. Online information was described as fragmented and confusing to understand: "[...] it just felt very overwhelming because I was confused, so I just left it [...]it wasn't very clear [what the conditions were] [...]" -P7 (25 to 34 years, female, born in Nigeria, time lived in England between 6 months to a year) Facilitators Free STI testing: While most of the participants were aware that testing services were free in the UK, this did not often link to a decision to have a test, except for one participant: "[...] in the UK I think since this service is free, I think it sometimes encourages me to test" -P13 (25 to 34 years, male, born in China, time lived in England 3 to 5 years) STI testing is straightforward and easy: Participants (either through experience or perception) viewed in clinic and at home testing as convenient and easy. Similar views were expressed by those who had previously used self-sampling test kits: "I'm far more likely to test when the test method is rapid, accurate, and easy to use" -P5 (25 to 34 years, male, born in China, time lived in England 1 to 2 years) Reflective Motivation Facilitators Privacy and anonymity in STI testing experience: Privacy and anonymity of testing was valued among participants because it helped reduce stigma: "I think the more private and convenient the process is, the more likely our international students are to use it […] if the university provide[d] some convenient and truly anonymous services for STI test[ing], I think this will definitely reduce the barriers for STI tests […] I think I prefer to choose the university service instead of NHS service [if this was the case]" -P4 (25 to 34 years, male, born in China, time lived in England 3 to 5 years) For some participants this resolved concerns about being seen by other people. Discussion about the privacy of self-sampling tests was brought up predominantly by Chinese participants. Perceived importance of STI testing Reflective Motivation Facilitators STI testing is beneficial for individual and societal health outcomes: Some viewed testing as important in maintaining individual mental and physical well-being, especially in terms of early detection and treatment of STIs: "[...] you only have one body [...] you want to prevent getting serious illnesses [...] it's important to know whether you actually have an STI to get treatment the quickest you can to get rid of it [...] you're asking like is it important to breathe [...] the benefits are for yourself." -P8 (18 to 24 years, female, born in Europe, time lived in England 3 to 5 years) Another benefit to testing was that it protected current and future sexual partners and was framed as a shared responsibility between partners. There was also mention of engaging in open conversation with partners about testing behaviour to help keep all parties safe: "[...] protect the people around you because you're informed now of the situation. You're not acting ignorantly anymore" -P7 (25 to 34 years, female, born in Nigeria, time lived in England 6 months to 1 year) Reflective Motivation Mixed Perceived self-relevance of STIs: Participants viewed themselves as being at low risk of STIs if they were sexually abstinent, in a monogamous relationship or frequently used condoms when engaging in sexual activity: "It [perception of risk] wouldn't [change] cause I'm still going to make sure I'm protected. I'm aware of what is at stake, and I feel like I'm well informed when it comes to STIs and I am like consciously protecting myself so I wouldn't put myself now or later in any position that puts me at risk." -P7 (25 to 34 years, female, born in Nigeria, time lived in England between 6 months to 1 year) In other cases, participants relied on trust and fidelity as indicators of low risk and safety: "[...] I have a very stable sex partner [...] she's my only sex partner [...] [I] really believe in her, so, I don't think I have any risk of STI" -P5 (24 to 35 years, male, born in China, time lived in England between 1 to 2 years)

For instance, participants expressed an intention to test more frequently if their sexual activity with new partners, with or without condoms, increased.

Discussion

In this study, we applied the COM-B model (20) to identify the barriers and facilitators to STI testing among international students living in England. The confidentiality safeguards provided by SHSs, awareness of the availability of free STI testing at these services or online, and the perceived benefits of testing for individual and societal benefit were facilitators for STI testing. Conversely, technical STI-related language, limited knowledge of STIs, anticipated judgement and stigma linked to testing, fragmented service provision and poor proximity to services were barriers to STI testing in this group. The presence of STI symptoms, perceived self-relevance, different cultural dynamics and social support networks acted as both barriers and facilitators to STI testing. These findings highlight the need for clear, accessible language to describe STI-related symptoms and for clear public health messaging to reduce STI risk, especially among people for whom English is not their first language. These findings may apply to many young people, including British university students.

Participants expressed having limited knowledge about STIs, with some unable to interpret their own symptoms within an STI context. Others experienced challenges navigating England's healthcare system, which contributed to postponed or delayed testing. Lack of knowledge about STIs and STI testing services is commonly reported in the literature as a barrier to engaging with STI testing (16, (22) (23) (24). In the present study, English not being one's native language, constituted an additional barrier contributing to these knowledge gaps. Our participants noted that the names of STIs were hard to remember and found it hard to communicate at a technical level with medical professionals. This finding is consistent with evidence in the literature (14, 22, 25). This challenge might be explained by the fact that sexual health terms are not easily translated between languages, especially from Chinese to English (12, 25).

When compared to domestic students, several Australian studies have found international students to have lower levels of knowledge about sexual and reproductive health and STIs (12) (13) (14) 16), a disparity partly explained by differences in sex education.

Discussion of sex and sexual health are considered taboo in many cultures (13, 15, 17), shaping the extent and quality of sex education young people receive (23, 26). This aligns with our participants' perceptions, who felt domestic students were more open to discussing STIs, attributing this openness to a more sex-positive culture in England, supported by more comprehensive sex education and wider availability of sexual health information. In the absence of formal sex education, international students often turn to informal sources to fill these knowledge gaps such as social media, newspapers, and the internet (13, 23, (25) (26) (27). This is consistent with our findings: participants reported accessing general sexual health and STI testing information from social media platforms, and other trusted sources online (government, NHS, local councils, SHSs).

Participants also stressed the importance of accessing reliable and targeted information from trusted sources, a finding also reported in the literature (12, 14, 15).

In our study, cultural norms in participants' countries of origin did not appear to discourage participants from getting tested; rather, a cultural tension emerged more specifically around disclosing testing behaviours to others. Although some participants felt that living in England broadened their mindset through exposure to a more open, sex-positive environment with more supportive systems in place, this openness did not necessarily extend to how comfortable participants felt sharing their testing status with friends and family from their country of origin. This tension became apparent when disclosing a positive result: while participants generally had no issue disclosing a negative test, they were far more cautious about disclosing a positive one, given its association with unsafe sex and having multiple partners. Similar concerns regarding testing behaviour were observed in young people living in the UK, across Europe, and in the United States (6, 10, (28) (29) (30).

Participants managed this tension through selective disclosure and information-seeking with friends and family, choosing to share their testing status with selected friends and family members, and trusted health professionals, while withholding this information from those they feared might judge their behaviour. Social norms, and the associated stigma and judgement from others, appear to be a key driver of this behaviour, a pattern reported in the literature (6, 15, 17, 26, 28). In our findings testing attitudes were largely unaffected, which may reflect the predominance of students doing health-related degrees in our sample. This contrasts with previous research demonstrating that STIrelated shame and stigma are associated with more negative attitudes towards testing and can act as barriers to testing uptake in other populations (10, 30). This suggests that host-country norms can shift attitudes toward the act of testing while home-country stigma continues to shape testing disclosure.

Similar experiences of shame and stigma have been reported in the wider literature on young people in general. Barriers to testing reflected local social environment, institutions, shame, peer influence, and gendered expectations rather than home-culture carryover. A number of these barriers centred on fears of others' reactions: fear of parents' or partners' reactions and feeling judged by others more broadly (11), as well as anticipated judgement from peers specifically (8). Others reflect self-directed shame and negative self-evaluation, informed by part experiences and anticipated feelings about testing (6), including fears of receiving negative feedback about one's sexual behaviour or being viewed as "unclean" (8).

Practical implications

Based on the behavioural diagnosis and the Behaviour Change Wheel mapping (Table 2) we suggest three interventions to address the key barriers and mixed accounts described by participants. Culturally driven stigma is a deeper-rooted structural barrier that is complex to address; however, offering a peer support service at universities could, in part, help address the social opportunity and psychological capability barriers discussed previously. A peer-support service could be available for students to ask peers outside of their immediate social networks about accessing and navigating STI testing services and learn of others' experiences with STI testing. Offering an online peer support service, where students are also able to ask their questions anonymously, may help address privacy concerns and selective disclosure approaches used to maintain confidentiality. There are several existing community programmes offered by London-based organizations delivering services such as counselling and peer support (31), that could be used as models. Delivering education via peer facilitators that helps people understand where and how to get tested are considerations for optimisation outlined by the STI Prioritisation Framework for England (32).

Translating STI information into multiple languages can remove the burden of interpreting unfamiliar medical terminology, limit the risk of misunderstanding symptoms, or testing procedures. While international students attending university in England must meet a specific English Language Proficiency standard, this is unlikely to account for medical or health-specific jargon. Enabling international students to access information in their native language can help remove a language barrier while helping close pre-existing gaps in sexual health knowledge. This recommendation aligns with those found in the literature (13, 33, 34) and could be modelled after the multilingual toolkit produced by the New South Wales Multicultural HIV and Hepatitis Service (35).

Public-facing materials to promote vaccination programmes in England, including those targeted to university students, are provided in multiple languages (36).

Many sexual health services in England currently offer at-home STI self-sampling kits (37), which is a popular option (38, 39). However, participants continued to experience access barriers which could be addressed at universities by providing STI self-sampling kits to students in need of STI testing. The provision of these kits can address proximity and fragmentation barriers, as it would remove the need to locate, travel, or navigate multiple services. Furthermore, the information shared in these kits can improve understanding of both symptomatic and asymptomatic STIs, helping people recognise the potential relevance of STI testing to their own circumstances. Fresher's week, an orientation period for new university students featuring social events (40), could be an opportune time to raise awareness of sexual health provision and signpost to National

Health Service and university healthcare information and resources. Early insights from this study were used to produce STI public health messaging targeted at university students in the England by UKHSA.

Strengths and Limitations

A key strength of this study is the qualitative study design that allowed for collecting direct and in-depth accounts from international students living in England, a subgroup of younger people with distinct health needs, on the barriers and facilitators to STI testing.

Our study reached a diverse sample of international students, including those from countries with high gonococcal ceftriaxone resistance. Additionally, the application of the COM-B model enabled a theory-informed and systematic assessment of behavioural determinants and their mapping to intervention and policy approaches through the Behaviour Change Wheel. A potential limitation is that participants in our sample predominantly came from a health background. Given this, participants might have a greater awareness of sexual health topics and be more health conscious. They might have also felt inclined to express attitudes they perceived as professionally or socially expected, introducing the possibility of social desirability bias. The predominance of postgraduate students in our sample may limit the transferability of the findings to younger and undergraduate student populations, whose life circumstances, perceived susceptibility to STIs, and STI-testing behaviours may differ. Conversely, the study may not have adequately captured students who are less engaged with sexual health services, whose attitudes and experiences regarding STI testing may differ from those represented in the sample. Furthermore, this study largely captured views of participants who had previous testing experience, either in England or in their respective home countries. This may make them more comfortable discussing testing behaviour, more likely to express positive intentions to test, and less likely to report some anticipated barriers that first-time testers might feel more acutely. Lastly, we recruited only participants from a university in London, and the barriers and facilitators to STI testing may vary in other cities of England.

Conclusion

This study contributes to the limited evidence base on barriers and facilitators to STI testing in international students in England, a large and heterogeneous group of people of different cultural backgrounds, with many at the age of forming new or casual sexual partnerships. Insights from this research have been used by UKHSA to optimise STI public health messaging for all university students in England (41), and the accompanying social media assets used for STI health promotion. The evidence from our study can be used to create culturally appropriate resources to raise awareness of STI-related symptoms and, among those with a need, promote STI testing.
