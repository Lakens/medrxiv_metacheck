How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Abstract

Background: Sickle cell anaemia (SCA) is a major public health problem in Uganda. Monthly sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine (SP) remains the standard malaria chemoprevention regimen despite widespread antifolate resistance in East Africa. We assessed Pfdhfr and Pfdhps resistance mutations and the complexity of infection (COI) among children with SCA receiving malaria chemoprevention.

Methods: This prospective study of antifolate resistance was nested within the CHEMCHA randomised controlled trial comparing monthly SP with weekly dihydroartemisinin-piperaquine (DP) for malaria chemoprevention. Participants were children with SCA aged 6 months to 15 years and attending Jinja and Kitgum hospitals in Uganda. Dried blood spots were collected at enrolment, study closeout, and during febrile episodes. Parasite DNA from microscopyconfirmed clinical malaria samples was genotyped by amplicon sequencing for Pfdhfr (N51I, C59R, S108N, and I164L) and Pfdhps (A437G, K540E, A581G, A613S, and A613T). COI was estimated using MOIRE.

Results: Complete Pfdhfr/Pfdhps haplotypes were resolved for 7-28 isolates per site and time point, and COI was estimated for 67 samples. The quintuple mutant (Pfdhfr IRN/Pfdhps GE) accounted for 85.7-100% of isolates across sites and time points; A613S and A613T were not detected. Pfdhfr I164L was detected in 2/9 isolates in Jinja at baseline and 1/8 at endline. The Pfdhps A581G mutant allele was absent at baseline (0/44 isolates) but was found in 1/8 isolates in Jinja and 3/34 in Kitgum at endline, including quintuple-plus-581G (sextuple) haplotypes in 1/8 and 2/27 isolates, respectively. Most infections were monoclonal (51/67, 76.1%).

The high prevalence of quintuple antifolate-resistant haplotypes and the detection of higher-level resistance markers, including emerging sextuple mutants carrying Pfdhfr I164L and Pfdhps A581G, call into question the continued use of SP for chemoprevention in children with SCA in Uganda and support evaluation of alternative regimens.

Background

Sickle cell anaemia (SCA) is a major public health problem, with more than 300,000 affected births annually worldwide, approximately 75% of which occur in sub-Saharan Africa [1]. Global projections suggest that this burden will increase to 400,000 births per year by 2050 [2]. In Uganda, an estimated 15,000-20,000 infants are born with SCA each year [3], and 50%-90% of affected children die before the age of two years [4]. Beyond early mortality, children with SCA experience recurrent complications, including painful vaso-occlusive crises, haemolytic episodes, and severe anaemia, many of which are precipitated by febrile illnesses such as malaria [5].

Although sickle cell trait (HbAS) confers partial protection against Plasmodium falciparum malaria [6], children with SCA remain at substantially increased risk of death from malaria [5]. Malaria contributes to morbidity in this population by exacerbating chronic anaemia, a determinant of poor clinical outcomes, and precipitating severe SCA complications. Malaria chemoprevention is therefore a critical component of comprehensive SCA management and has the potential to reduce severe malaria-related morbidity, hospitalisation, blood transfusion requirements, and mortality.

In Uganda, malaria chemoprevention with monthly SP is the standard of care in children with SCA [1]. However, prolonged SP use across East Africa has been associated with the emergence and selection of drug-resistant P. falciparum [2]. Over 90% of P. falciparum isolates in the East African region carry mutations in the dihydrofolate reductase (Pfdhfr) and dihydropteroate synthetase (Pfdhps) genes associated with reduced SP efficacy [3] [4] [5] [6]. The quintuple mutant haplotype comprising Pfdhfr mutations N51I, C59R, and S108N together with Pfdhps mutations A437G and K540E is an established molecular marker of SP treatment failure [7, 8]. Some preventive benefit may remain in settings where the prevalence of the Pfdhps K540E mutation is below 50% [9]. Yet, the continued recommendation of SP for chemoprevention in Ugandan children with SCA, despite well-documented regional resistance, leaves an important gap between policy and the available evidence.

Despite widespread use, few data characterise molecular markers of antimalarial resistance in children with SCA. It remains unclear whether resistance profiles in this population are comparable to those observed in the general paediatric population, or whether chemoprevention in this group may selectively drive distinct resistance patterns. These evidence gaps have direct implications for malaria control policy in this high-risk group. Unlike the general paediatric population, in whom SP exposure is intermittent, children with SCA in Uganda receive monthly SP continuously for years. Sustained drug pressure may itself select for resistant parasites, and any loss of efficacy carries a disproportionate clinical cost in a group already at high risk of severe malaria. This study therefore determined the molecular markers of antimalarial resistance in children with SCA receiving malaria chemoprevention in Uganda, to inform malaria prevention strategies and policy in this population.

Methods

Study design

This was a nested prospective study of antifolate resistance in children with SCA in Uganda. The study was nested within a randomized, double-blind, parallel-group, placebo-controlled clinical trial of weekly DP versus monthly SP for malaria chemoprevention in children with SCA (CHEMCHA trial) [10].

Study setting and target population

The parent trial was conducted at two hospitals in Uganda (Jinja Regional Referral Hospital [RRH] and Kitgum General Hospital) and two hospitals in Malawi from April 2021 to May 2023 [10, 11]. This substudy was restricted to the two Ugandan sites, both of which lie in areas with high-grade P. falciparum antifolate resistance.

Sample size

The sample size for the parent trial was calculated to provide 90% power (two-sided α=0.05) to detect a 50% reduction in the incidence of clinical malaria, from an assumed 0.2 episodes per person-year in the SP arm to 0.1 episodes per person-year in the DP arm [11]. This required 824 person-years of follow-up in approximately 548 participants [10]. Because this was a secondary analysis of malaria parasites collected within the trial, no separate sample size calculation was performed; instead, all malaria-positive samples on microscopy were included.

Sampling procedure and selection of respondents

Participants in the main study [10] were 725 children aged 6 months to 15 years with a diagnosis of SCA on haemoglobin electrophoresis or isoelectric focusing. Of these, the 438 children enrolled at the two Ugandan sites were eligible for this sub-study. Children with other known chronic diseases, those receiving daily cotrimoxazole prophylaxis, or those participating in another trial were excluded.

Data collection process

The trial procedures have been described previously [10]. Briefly, at enrolment, between 17 April 2021 and 30 May 2022, sociodemographic information and medical history were recorded, and a physical examination was performed [11]. Participants were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to one of two malaria chemoprevention regimens using a computer-generated block randomisation sequence with randomly varying block sizes of 4, 6, or 8, stratified by site and weight category [10]. To maintain blinding, participants in each arm received one active regimen alongside a matched placebo for the alternative regimen: thus, those assigned to the DP arm received weekly active DP and monthly placebo SP, while those assigned to the SP arm received monthly active SP and weekly placebo DP [11]. Participants who were unwell at enrolment were initially resuscitated and enrolled once clinically stable [11].

Participants attended scheduled clinics with their caregivers every 2 months to receive their next doses of study medication, and unscheduled visits if they were unwell. At each study visit, participants receiving SP were provided with two additional doses to cover the subsequent two months, while participants receiving DP were provided with doses sufficient to cover 8 weeks; these additional doses were dispensed on the last day of the preceding scheduled visit. Participants also attended the clinic for all sick visits, including episodes of febrile illnesses. The study provided standard-of-care clinical services for all unscheduled visits. Parents or guardians were instructed on home management of children with SCA and on bringing children to the study clinic after identifying signs requiring medical attention [10, 11]. Participants who missed scheduled follow-up visits were contacted by phone and visited at home if required [10]. Participants were admitted to hospital if they developed severe complications of SCA.

Sample collection and processing

Samples confirmed for malaria by microscopic examination of blood smears were included in the study. For every positive microscopy finding, a sample was stored on filter paper (Whatman 3MM) for molecular analyses [11]. The blood-blotted filter paper was left to dry at room temperature before storage at 4 °C, awaiting molecular analysis. This procedure was repeated for all recruited participants. Dried blood Spots (DBS) were packaged according to the principles of triple packaging and transported to the National Malaria Reference and Research Laboratory (NMRRL) using the National Health Laboratory Diagnostics System (NHLDS) hub transportation system [12]. The primary container was the DBS cards on which blood was spotted. DBS was wrapped in zip-lock bags (secondary containers) alongside desiccants and sterile cotton gauze. Together with the filled Laboratory investigation forms and ice packs, the zip-lock bag was put inside a cool box (tertiary container). The entire package was handed over to the NHLDS hub transport system.

Genotyping drug resistance genes

Parasite genomic DNA from all microscopy-positive samples was extracted from blood spots using Tween-20/Chelex 100 as previously described [13]. Briefly, 6-mm diameter discs were punched from dried blood spots into 1.5-mL microcentrifuge tubes and incubated in 0.05% PBS-T [Tween 20 (Sigma Aldrich P9416) in 1× PBS (Corning 21-040-CV)] overnight at 4 °C. Samples then underwent centrifugation at 13,000 rpm for 1 min; the PBS-T was aspirated and replaced with 1 mL of 1× PBS, and the sample was incubated at 4 °C for 30 min. Following 5 min of centrifugation at 13,000 rpm and 4 °C, the supernatant was aspirated, and 150 µL of 7% Chelex 100 (Bio-Rad 1422822) in water was added. The sample was then incubated with shaking at 95 °C for 10 min. Following centrifugation at 13,000 rpm for 10 min, the supernatant containing extracted DNA was transferred to 1.5-mL sample storage tubes, avoiding transfer of any remaining Chelex beads. Extracted DNA was stored at -20 °C. Parasite density was estimated using quantitative PCR targeting the varATS gene, as previously described [13]. Samples positive by varATS were selected for genotyping. Library preparation was performed using the Plasmodium falciparum Public Health Sequencing Tool (PfPHAST) panel, the smaller panel within the Multiplexed Amplicons for Drugs, Diagnostics, Diversity, and Differentiation using High-Throughput Targeted Resequencing (MAD4HatTeR) panel [14], which comprises two primer pools, M1.1 (45 targets) and M2.1 (15 targets), including 15 drug resistance genes with loci in crt, mdr1, dhfr, dhps, and kelch13. Samples, including positive and negative controls, were pooled and sequenced on the Illumina MiSeq platform using MiSeq v2 flow cells. Samples with <50% of amplicons having >100 reads were re-prepped, and those with >50% but <75% of amplicons having >100 reads were repooled and resequenced to increase coverage, as previously described [14].

Data management and analysis

Data were collected and recorded at the point of contact using a combination of paper-based and electronic record forms [11], using the Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap™) system, locally hosted. Data quality was ensured through comprehensive training of the study team, standardized questionnaire administration and data collection procedures, and good clinical data management and laboratory practices. Built-in range, logic, and consistency checks were incorporated into the electronic system to minimize data entry errors.

Bioinformatic analyses were performed using the ampSeq pipeline, as previously described [18], with downstream analyses conducted in R version 4.2.2. Genotypes were called when an allele had ≥50 reads and a within-sample allele frequency (WSAF) ≥1%. Samples carrying both wildtype and mutant alleles were classified as mixed, and mutant alleles with a WSAF below [n]% were confirmed using a repeat library preparation. Complexity of infection (COI) was estimated using MOIRE, based on the median posterior COI metric, as previously described [20], and rounded to the nearest integer for presentation. Statistical analyses were performed using Stata version 18. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were summarised using means and standard deviations (SDs) for continuous variables and frequencies and percentages for categorical variables, with summaries stratified by study site. The primary outcome was the presence of polymorphisms in the Pfdhfr and Pfdhps genes. Samples collected at enrolment were classified as baseline samples, and those collected at study closeout as endline samples. Genotype prevalence was expressed as the percentage of successfully genotyped samples by site and time point.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

Written informed consent was obtained from a parent or caregiver, and assent from children aged 8 years or older. The parent trial was approved by the Makerere University School of Medicine Research and Ethics Committee (2020-103), Uganda National Council for Science and Technology (HS709ES), the Liverpool School of Tropical Medicine Research Ethics Committee (19.105), the Regional Committee for Medical and Health Research Ethics, Western Norway (30992), and the National Health Sciences Research Committee in Malawi (19/11/2442).

Discussion

This study characterised molecular markers of antifolate resistance and the COI among Plasmodium falciparum parasites infecting children with SCA receiving malaria chemoprevention in Jinja and Kitgum, Uganda, at enrolment and at the end of follow-up. The Pfdhfr N51I, C59R, S108N, and Pfdhps K540E and A437G mutations were present in nearly all successfully genotyped isolates. At the haplotype level, the Pfdhfr IRN/Pfdhps GE quintuple-mutant background predominated at both sites and time points. Additional markers associated with higherlevel antifolate resistance, Pfdhfr I164L and Pfdhps A581G, were also detected. These findings are particularly relevant in the context of the parent CHEMCHA trial, in which the incidence of clinical malaria was approximately five times higher among children receiving monthly SP than among those receiving weekly DP [10]. The appearance of A581G during follow-up, in parasites causing breakthrough malaria in children taking monthly SP, is consistent with selection of the most resistant parasites under continuous drug pressure. These data argue for revision of the Ugandan recommendation of monthly SP for children with sickle cell anaemia: weekly DP is the evidence-based alternative in children younger than 5 years receiving penicillin prophylaxis [10]; further evaluation is needed in older children, and continuing SP in this group risks selecting for parasites carrying still higher-level antifolate resistance.

At baseline, a higher proportion of the samples from Kitgum had parasite densities exceeding 1000 parasites/µL, consistent with the higher transmission intensity of that region [15]. At endline, parasite densities in Kitgum shifted toward lower strata, which may indicate partial suppression of high-density infections by chemoprevention. However, the persistence of infections in the 1-10 and 101-1000 parasites/µL strata also suggests ongoing low-grade transmission despite prophylaxis. In Jinja, parasite density distributions were broadly stable at the beginning of the study and endline, consistent with its peri-urban setting.

The prevalence and pattern of Pfdhfr and Pfdhps mutations observed in this study, including the near-universal quintuple mutant haplotype, are similar to those reported in general paediatric and antenatal populations elsewhere in East Africa [5]. This suggests that children with SCA are not exposed to a distinct resistance profile compared with the wider population, although their continuous use of SP chemoprevention may increase the clinical consequences of existing resistance.

The high prevalence of the Pfdhfr N51I, C59R, and S108N mutations, which together constitute the triple mutant (IRN) haplotype conferring high-level pyrimethamine resistance, at both study sites is also consistent with previous evidence showing that these variants are already highly prevalent across East Africa [2, 3, 5]. Their presence at or close to 100% at both time points and at both sites suggests that these mutations are now fixed, or nearly fixed, in the local parasite population. Similarly, the high prevalence of the Pfdhps A437G (88-100%) and K540E (88-95%) mutations indicates that the quintuple mutant haplotype is dominant in this population.

Haplotype-level analysis confirmed this pattern. The full quintuple mutant (Pfdhfr IRN/Pfdhps GE) accounted for 86-100% of isolates in Jinja and 86-96% of isolates in Kitgum at baseline and endline, with only a small proportion of isolates carrying partial mutant backgrounds. The presence of the quintuple mutant among children with SCA is particularly concerning because these children receive monthly SP chemoprevention continuously over several years, unlike the general paediatric population, in whom SP exposure is typically intermittent. Sustained drug pressure in this population may reduce the protective benefit of SP and favour the persistence or further selection of resistant parasite populations.

The presence of both I164L and A581G in this population deserves attention because these mutations have been uncommon in Uganda [6] and mediate high-level SP resistance [16]. In a 2024 survey of children with malaria in northern, north-eastern and eastern Uganda, I164L was found in a few samples, and A581G was reported as a mixed genotype [17]. By contrast, the Pfdhps A581G mutant allele, which was not detected at baseline, was found at endline at both sites. One possible explanation is selection by continued SP exposure, since SP given for intermittent preventive treatment in pregnancy in Uganda has been associated with selection of I164L and A581G [5, 18]. The presence of the quintuple and sextuple haplotype is particularly relevant in children with SCA, who receive monthly SP chemoprevention continuously over several years, in contrast to the more intermittent SP exposure typically experienced by the general paediatric population. Given that a Pfdhps K540E prevalence above 50% has been associated with reduced preventive efficacy of SP [9], the levels observed here (88-95%) suggest that SP chemoprevention may already provide suboptimal protection for children with SCA in Uganda, and there is a need to revise the malaria chemoprevention policies.

The predominance of monoclonal infections across both sites may reflect suppression of polyclonal parasite populations by continuous chemoprevention. Previous studies in Uganda have reported polyclonal infections in more than 50% of samples across sites of varying transmission intensity, in community-based cohorts of children (6 months-10 years) [19]. The lower COI observed in this chemoprevention cohort is therefore consistent with the capacity of antimalarial prophylaxis to reduce parasite diversity and select for persistence of single dominant clones [20].

Taken together, these findings suggest that most circulating parasites in this population carry a molecular profile associated with SP treatment failure. This raises important concerns about the continued use of SP as the standard chemoprevention regimen for children with SCA in Uganda and supports re-evaluation of the current malaria policy.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. It was a secondary analysis nested within a clinical trial, and the sample sizes at individual sites and time points were relatively small, which may have limited the precision of the haplotype-frequency estimates. The analysis was also restricted to the Ugandan trial sites; therefore, the findings may not be generalisable to settings with different transmission intensities, resistance profiles, or chemoprevention histories. In addition, the data are not linked to individual clinical outcomes; therefore, no inferences can be drawn about the association between a specific haplotype and the risk of clinical malaria.

Conclusions

Molecular markers of SP resistance are near universal among children with SCA receiving malaria chemoprevention in Uganda. The quintuple mutant haplotype predominated at both sites, Pfdhfr I164L was present from baseline, and Pfdhps A581G, undetected at baseline, appeared at endline in parasites causing breakthrough malaria, consistent with selection of sextuple mutants under continuous monthly SP. The profile is no different from that of the wider parasite population, but its consequences are, because these children take SP every month for years and are at high risk of severe malaria. Taken with the five-fold higher incidence of clinical malaria in the SP arm of the parent trial, these findings indicate that monthly SP is no longer adequate chemoprevention in this group. Weekly DP should replace it in children younger than 5 years receiving penicillin prophylaxis; its efficacy and safety should be established in older children, and molecular surveillance should continue under whichever regimen is adopted.
