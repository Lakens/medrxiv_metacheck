Power Analysis Check

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

The Effect Size module checks if effect sizes are correctly reported in t-tests and F-tests.

The Effect Size check searches for regular expressions that match typical ways in which effect sizes are reported. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

Article type: Methods article with a pre-specified analysis plan. No measured value of any measure is reported.

Background and Significance

Human review is the safeguard on which current guidance for LLM drafting in clinical care depends. NHS England guidance on ambient scribing products requires users to review and approve outputs before further action is taken. 1 The EU Artificial Intelligence Act requires human oversight of high-risk systems and asks that the people exercising it remain aware of automation bias. 2 Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency (MHRA) guidance of July 2026 states that an ambient voice product that only transcribes or summarizes an encounter for clinician review is not a medical device, and its device example is an "AVT agent" that "finalises and saves transcripts to the electronic patient record without requiring a clinician's review" and also autonomously determines follow-up tests and places "the relevant orders without clinician input or confirmation." 3 NHS England guidance, by contrast, states that generative summarization "likely would qualify as a medical device. "1 In each text the review step carries safety weight. None specifies how the quality of that review should be measured.

The evidence on deployed LLM tools mostly describes the tools. Randomized trials of ambient scribes measured documentation time and clinician well-being, 4, 5 and a multisite cohort measured time and visit volume. 6 Studies of drafted replies to patient portal messages measured utilization, read time and reply time. 7, 8 A newer body of work examines what clinicians do to drafts. In 23,760 ambient notes, 84.4% were edited before sign-off, variation was explained more by the individual clinician than by specialty, and notes signed later were edited less intensively. 9 A qualitative analysis found that edits concentrated on orders, symptoms, prescriptions and diagnoses rather than on style. 10 In 14,350 AI-assisted portal replies, every edit category was associated with longer response time. 11 A 1-year study used text similarity between draft and final reply as a measure of draft retention, 12 a cohort of 983,154 responses separated adoption, use and habituation, 13 an editing-load framework estimated how much a clinician would edit a given draft, 14 and clinicians' decisions to use drafted portal replies correlated with automated text metrics. 15 These are research analyses of logs, samples or test sets. To our knowledge, none was reported as a monitored control within the deploying organization's clinical safety process.

Unedited approvals carry particular safety weight, because an error in the draft then reaches the patient or the record unchanged. In a simulation study, 35% to 45% of erroneous drafts were submitted entirely unedited by primary care physicians, 16 and assessors judged that 7.1% of LLM drafts in a radiation oncology messaging simulation posed a risk of severe harm if left unedited. 17 Automation bias is established in clinical decision support and increases with workload, time pressure and verification complexity. [18] [19] [20] It persists after training. In a randomized trial, physicians who had completed 20 hours of AI-literacy training had diagnostic accuracy 14.0 percentage points lower than controls when LLM suggestions contained deliberate errors. 21 Pharmacists and trainees accepted 73.9% of incorrect and 80.2% of correct AI recommendations, 22 which indicates that acceptance can be almost insensitive to correctness. Exposure to AI support may also reduce unaided performance. 23 Regulators have begun to treat review quality as a quantity that changes over time. The MHRA AI Airlock phase 2 report concluded that human oversight may be inadequate to treat as a fixed safety control, observed that users applied less scrutiny as accurate output became familiar, and identified review time and edit depth as potential indirect indicators of engagement quality. 24 The UK National Commission into the Regulation of AI in Healthcare asked that oversight design consider automation bias and that devices log performance to detect drift. 25 Unedited acceptance has also been reported as a benefit. Trade press reported that 92% of AI-generated prior-authorization responses at one customer of an electronic health record vendor were accepted without edits. 26 Without a measure of review, such a figure is compatible both with accurate drafts and with approval without reading. Commentators have called for accountable authorship and auditability of drafted messages. 27 To our knowledge, no published deployment reports review fidelity as continuous telemetry computed at the point where a named person commits drafted content.

We considered that a usable measure of review fidelity should have five properties. It should be defined on a recorded approval event, be insensitive to formatting, be read jointly with measures that separate competing explanations, be protected from becoming a target or a tool for individual surveillance, and be analyzed under a plan fixed before the data exist. A sixth requirement follows from the first. A measure is usable only where the system actually records the event it is defined on, and that has to be checked in the code rather than assumed from the design.

Objective

We aimed to define the unedited acceptance rate (UAR) family formally, to establish for each approval route of a multi-agent clinical system in routine outpatient use which measures its instruments can compute and which instruments do not yet exist, to set out an interpretation framework and proposed governance uses, and to pre-specify the analysis of a prospective 12-week measurement window. 28, 29 This article reports the properties of the measures and the state of the instruments, and no measured values.

Materials and Methods

Setting and system

The system studied, wai, is a multi-agent clinical system in routine use at a private outpatient clinic in London, developed and operated by Wai Health Inc. 28, 29 Version 0.2 of its action inventory (September 8, 2026), compiled from the code, lists 153 live agent actions in 23 functional domains. 28, 30 The inventory states that 29 of these actions reach a patient or the clinical record with no person accepting them. It does not itemize the 29. Its own list of such actions names 33 live rows, of which 30 are classed as having no acceptance step, one as partial acceptance, one as not applicable and one as an action that has since been converted to a draft. Each no-acceptance row has an entry in the clinic's hazard log. 30 Some of these actions carry model-generated content. They include the voice agent speaking live to a patient, a live recap pushed to the patient's screen during a consultation, live translated captions, model-extracted details and model-written descriptions of patient media written to the record, and a command-chat tool that can change the body of a clinical document. Such content reaches patients or the record without approval and lies outside every measure defined here. Drafted text that does pass through approval does so on four routes, which are the measurement routes of this paper. The first is the help desk, on which staff approve drafted replies to patient messages on several channels, including email and in-app messages. The second is the WhatsApp agent, whose drafts are approved in the administration console or from an approval card posted to the team's Slack workspace. The third is clinical documentation, in which a clinician signs a drafted document. The fourth is approval cards for actions proposed by the operations agent in Slack, which offer approve or reject. Measurement surface

Review fidelity is measurable only where review is a recorded event. For an approval event i, the measures need five things: the drafted text Di, the committed text Ci, the identity of the approver, the time tavail,i at which the draft became available for review and the commit time tcommit,i (Figure 1). A sixth, the time the draft was first opened for review, would allow a tighter latency measure. Whether each route records each of these is reported in Results and Table 3.

Metric definitions

Let Ar,w be the set of approval events on route r whose commit time falls in week w and which pass the completeness rule of the analysis plan. Table 1 gives each measure with its numerator, denominator and missingness rule.

Normalization. Before comparison, both texts are normalized by a function N that collapses every run of whitespace, including line breaks, to a single space and trims leading and trailing whitespace. For clinical documentation, N first removes markup tags and non-breaking-space entities. N applies no Unicode normalization and removes no appended elements. Removal is not needed, because the committed text is taken at the approval step, before translation or channel formatting is applied. This is the normalization that the system's edit telemetry applies, and the analysis uses it unchanged so that stored and recomputed values agree. Unedited acceptance rate. UARr,w is the share of events in Ar,w with N(Ci) = N(Di).

Normalized edit distance. δi = lev(N(Di), N(Ci)) / max(|N(Di)|, |N(Ci)|), where lev is the Levenshtein distance 35 and |•| is length, both counted in UTF-16 code units, as implemented in the system. This differs from a count over Unicode code points only for characters outside the Basic Multilingual Plane, such as emoji. A word-level version computed over whitespace-delimited tokens is a pre-specified sensitivity analysis. The distribution of δi among edited approvals (δi > 0), described as edit depth, is reported separately from the UAR. Approval latency. Availability latency is Li = tcommit,i -tavail,i, summarized by the median and 90th percentile.

Dwell latency, measured from the first opening of the draft for review, is defined for completeness but cannot be computed in this deployment, because no route records that event.

Unedited fast-approval rate. For each event, a reading floor fi = 60•ni/ρ seconds is defined, where ni is the word count of N(Di) and ρ is a reading rate of 300 words per minute, the upper end of the typical adult silent reading range for English non-fiction. 36 A rate of 238 words per minute, the meta-analytic mean, 36 is a sensitivity analysis. Among events with a latency value, UFAr,w is the share committed unedited with Li < fi.

Because this joint share moves with the UAR, the conditional share of unedited approvals that were fast is reported beside it. The UFA is the only measure in the family that can indicate approval without reading.

Acceptance coverage. ACr,w is the share of commits on route r in week w, counted from a record written separately from the edit telemetry, that are matched to an approval event with complete capture. The commit record is the communications audit for the help desk, the draft status for the WhatsApp agent route, and the signed document, with its signature record and audit-chain event, for clinical documentation, which does not pass through the communications audit.

Attribution completeness. The share of approval events whose approver field identifies a named person, as opposed to being blank, holding the name of the approval surface, or holding a generic label. It is reported because the disclosure rules and any reading of a "named approver" depend on it.

Inventory coverage. At inventory level, coverage is the number of inventoried actions that take effect without human acceptance.

Disposition. Where discards are recorded, the share of drafts rejected or expired without commit.

Interpretation framework

The measures are read jointly, by route and over time, under five principles. First, no single measure is interpreted alone. A high UAR is consistent with an accurate model on routine content and with approval without reading, and a low UAR is consistent with attentive review and with a poor model. Second, an unedited approval is not evidence that review was absent, and an edit is not evidence that review was adequate. A clinician can verify a correct draft without changing it, and a superficial edit does not show that the content was checked. The measures bound behavior and do not observe cognition, which is why a calibration audit is part of the method.

Third, latency is informative in one direction only. Availability latency includes queue time and is therefore an upper bound on reading time, so a latency below the reading floor is evidence against complete reading, whereas a long latency is not evidence for it. This holds wherever a draft is read, because no draft can be read before it exists. A dwell latency would not have the same property here, because WhatsApp agent drafts can be read, and approved, from a Slack card outside the review screen. Fourth, case mix drives every measure, so readings are stratified by route, draft type and, where it is recorded, model version, and pooled figures describe composition only. Fifth, instrument failure is ruled out before behavior is interpreted. A reading that meets a pre-specified failure condition is reported as an instrument finding. Proposed governance uses

The plan proposes three uses, none of which is yet in operation. The first is threshold monitoring. On each route, a sustained rise in UFA, or a sustained rise in UAR together with falling latency, would trigger examination of that route's approval practice by the clinical safety officer. The trigger would start a human review and impose no automated sanction. The second is change evaluation. Every model or prompt change would be handled as a clinical change, with the UAR, edit depth, latency and UFA compared across it on each route. A version whose drafts are accepted with materially less review should be assessed with more caution, irrespective of its benchmark performance. The third is a calibration audit, in which a second clinician reviews a stratified sample of approved drafts (unedited and edited, within and below the reading floor) for content the approval should have corrected. The audit is the link between the telemetry and missed errors. Its sample size, its definition of a missed error and the blinding of the second clinician to stratum have not been fixed, and the audit design will be registered separately before its first sample is drawn. The hazard log already records the underlying gap. Its entry HZ-65, "We cannot tell a real acceptance from a reflexive one", was open and marked urgent in version 0.6 (committed November 9, 2026), which notes that the help-desk and WhatsApp surfaces are still not measured.

Any measure used to steer behavior is exposed to Goodhart effects, in which the measure ceases to track the property it was chosen to represent once it becomes a target. 37 The mitigations are structural. The measures are never reported as individual performance scores and never feed performance management, because the purpose is to detect systemic approval without reading and an individual target would reward cosmetic edits. Triggers lead to examination of a route's workflow, not of a person. The calibration audit assesses content directly and cannot be satisfied by editing. Staff who approve drafts will be told of the measures and their purpose before the window opens, and the date will be recorded in an amendment to the registration.

Pre-specified analysis plan

The plan is summarized here and given in full in Supplementary File 2.

Window. Twelve consecutive calendar weeks (Monday to Sunday, Europe/London), starting at 00:00 on the first Monday at least 14 days after the public time stamp of the OSF registration. The window is prospective, so no data it covers exist when the plan is registered.

Instruments and change points. Before the window opens, an amendment will record the configured models and the deployment date of any change made after commit 687f45e295 to the instruments in Table 3. A measure is computed on a route only from the deployment date of the instrument it depends on. A change to an instrument or a model during the window is a change point, and estimates are reported on either side of it.

Unit, eligibility and completeness. The unit of analysis is the approval event. The system has no field that marks test records, so events on records in a list of test records fixed before the window opens are excluded. The operations-agent route is excluded from all measures except inventory coverage. An event is complete if it has a drafted text, a committed text or a stored unedited flag, an approver field and a commit time. Incomplete events are excluded from the UAR and edit depth and reported as a completeness proportion by route and week. The UFA is computed only on events with a latency value. Estimators and precision. Proportions are reported as counts with Wilson score 95% confidence intervals, 38 edit depth as the median, 75th and 90th percentiles of δ among edited approvals, and latency as the median and 90th percentile, by route and week. Approvals cluster within approvers and weeks, so Wilson intervals are descriptive, and a cluster bootstrap over weeks (2,000 replicates) is a sensitivity analysis for window-level estimates, which are interpreted as approximate. The half-width of a 95% Wilson interval near 0.5 is about 0.20 at 20 events, 0.10 at 96 and 0.05 at 384, so weekly estimates on low-volume routes are shown as series and not compared week by week. No trend test is pre-specified, and any trend statement will be labeled exploratory. Strata are route, draft type, tertile of draft word count within route and, for clinical documentation only, model and prompt version.

Disclosure control. Cells with fewer than five events are suppressed, with secondary suppression so that no suppressed value can be derived by subtraction. Cells whose approvals come from fewer than three distinct named approvers are reported only after further aggregation. Events without a named approver are reported only at route level over the whole window. Counts by approver are never reported.

Instrument-failure conditions. A route-week is an instrument failure if (1) acceptance coverage is below 95%; (2) completeness among captured events is below 95%; (3) the UAR is exactly 0 or 1 with at least 20 events, unless both capture arms are shown to work on synthetic records in a non-production environment; or (4) for clinical documentation, more than 5% of events cannot be linked to their producing run. Failed route-weeks are counted, reported as instrument findings and excluded from behavioral interpretation. Condition 3 triggers verification rather than classifying behavior, because on a route whose true unedited share is 0.95, 20 approvals that are all unedited occur with probability 0.36. Version control. Extraction queries will compute distances, latencies and reading floors inside the clinical database and export only aggregates by route, stratum and week. Their identifiers will be recorded in an amendment before extraction, and every reported aggregate will cite the query version and extraction date. Deviations will be reported with reasons.

Results

Properties of the measures

Four properties follow directly from the definitions. They fix how the measures are interpreted and are not empirical findings. First, δi = 0 if and only if N(Ci) = N(Di), so the UAR equals the probability mass of the edit-distance distribution at zero. The UAR and edit depth therefore describe the two parts of one distribution and cannot contradict each other. Second, because the Levenshtein distance between two strings never exceeds the length of the longer string, 35 δi lies between 0 and 1. Third, availability latency is an upper bound on reading time, so the latency arm supports one-sided inference only, and the UFA is the only measure in the family that can indicate approval without reading. Fourth, the family is defined only over content that passes through an approval. The inventoried actions that reach a patient or the record with no person accepting lie outside the measurement surface by construction. A UAR, however favorable, says nothing about them, which is why coverage is reported with it.

Instrument status at the study commit

Table 3 gives, for each route, what the system records at commit 687f45e295 and which measures it supports. Clinical documentation is the one route on which the family, apart from dwell latency and disposition, is computable. The drafted and signed texts are both kept, the signer is recorded with a signature record, the commit time is the signature time, and availability can be taken from the finish time of the run that produced the draft, because documents carry no creation time. The model and prompt version are recorded on that run, which is linked to the document by encounter and agent. Documentation accept events were not written at all before a code change committed on November 16, 2026, because the writer referred to columns the table did not have. Until then the edit ratio was kept only on the document itself. Discards are not captured on this route. An unsigned draft is deleted when a newer draft of the same document is written (HZ-09).

On the help desk, edit telemetry and accept events are written when a draft is sent, but only if the review screen has stored the agent's version of the draft. A draft approved without that stored version produces no telemetry row. The UAR and edit depth are therefore computable only on a captured subset, and acceptance coverage states its size. Attribution is incomplete by construction. The approver field on help-desk drafts is optional, the help-desk approve path records no user identifier (HZ-34), accept events can carry the label "an unattributed operator", and the administration console accepts approvals without a named staff session unless a configuration setting requires one (HZ-33). Rejected and expired drafts are recorded.

On the WhatsApp agent route, the approve path records neither an edit signal nor an outcome, and, in the words of the hazard log, "a swept draft approved unchanged produces no telemetry row at all" (HZ-65). Whether an approval was edited cannot be established, so the UAR, edit depth and UFA are not computable on this route.

Availability latency can be derived from draft creation and approval times, rejected and superseded drafts are recorded, and attribution completeness can be measured. The approver is held as free text, which need not name a person, and drafts can be approved from a Slack card outside the review screen.

Other instruments are missing on every route or where they are needed. No route records when a draft is first opened for review, so dwell latency cannot be computed anywhere. No route has dedicated approval-latency capture, so availability latency is derived from creation and commit times that were written for other purposes. Draft records on the help-desk and WhatsApp agent routes carry no model or prompt identifier, so model stratification is possible only for clinical documentation. The operations-agent cards have no edit capture and no accept-event writer.

Coverage at inventory level

Inventory coverage is the headline figure of inventory version 0.2, 29 of 153 actions taking effect without acceptance, with the reconciliation caveat given in Methods. Because the inventory records reach only for unaccepted actions, the 124 remaining actions cannot be divided from it into those that cross an approval boundary and those with no external effect. The analysis plan therefore reports inventory coverage from an itemized list of unaccepted actions, fixed before extraction, rather than from the headline count.

Discussion

In this methods study at one private outpatient clinic, we specified a family of review-fidelity measures for LLM-drafted clinical content, defined at the event where a person commits a draft, and checked each against what the deployed system records. The measures are mutually consistent by construction, and the latency arm supports inference in one direction only. The check changed the paper. Only clinical documentation can support the family as specified. The help desk can support it on a subset whose size is itself a measure. The WhatsApp agent route cannot yet show whether an approval was edited, and no route records when a draft is opened.

The closest prior work analyzes edits after the fact. Guo and colleagues used a diff algorithm on 23,760 ambient notes and found that most were edited, 9 which corresponds to an unedited share of about 15.6% at note level, and the same group showed that edits concentrate on clinical content. 10 Jeanselme and colleagues modeled adoption, use and habituation separately and found that adoption and use were associated with turnaround time in opposite directions, with no further association after habituation over their study period. 13 The components of the UAR family are not new. Edit rates, draft-to-final similarity and response time have been measured before, 9, 11, 12 automated text metrics have been related to clinicians' use of drafts, 15 and the MHRA named review time and edit depth as candidate indicators. 24 The family differs in four respects. It is defined on the approval event, it is intended to be computed continuously by the deployment rather than retrospectively over a study period, it is designed to sit within the deployment's safety file, and it carries pre-specified rules for recognizing its own failure. Note-quality instruments such as the PDQI-9,39,40 the PDSQI-9 for LLM-generated summaries 41 and harm-potential ratings of unedited output 17, 42 answer a different question. They measure what a draft or note contains and need expert raters, so they cannot run on every approval. They are the natural reference standard for the calibration audit.

The joint reading matters because acceptance is a weak signal on its own. If acceptance of incorrect recommendations runs close to acceptance of correct ones, 22 and if trained physicians remain susceptible to erroneous suggestions, 21 then a falling edit rate after a model update is uninterpretable without latency and an independent check of content. The unedited fast-approval rate combines the two candidate indicators named by the MHRA 24 into one quantity computable on every approval where both texts and both times are kept. It cannot distinguish a slow but inattentive approval from a careful one, and only the calibration audit can estimate how many errors pass through each stratum.

The instrument check is itself a finding for other deployers. A system can be designed around human approval and still not record the approval in a form that supports measurement. In this deployment the gaps were of three kinds: the edited or unedited state of the draft was not kept, the person who approved was not named, and the time the draft was opened was never recorded. Each gap was invisible at the level of design documents and visible only in the code. Deployers who cite human review as a control could test their own systems against the five elements of the measurement surface before relying on any acceptance figure.

Three implications follow, each conditional on validation. For regulators and deployers, review-fidelity measures could serve as monitored controls within DCB0160 deployment safety cases and the post-deployment monitoring that NHS England guidance expects of deployers, 1, 32 and could support the automation-bias awareness that the EU AI Act requires of human oversight, whose obligations for AI in medical devices now apply from August 2, 2028.2,43 For vendors, a report of unedited acceptance 26 would be more informative if accompanied by acceptance coverage, attribution completeness, latency and a calibration result. For evaluators, the instrument-failure rules suggest that implausible review-fidelity values should be investigated as defects in capture before they are interpreted as behavior.

Limitations

This study has limitations. First, and most important, the measures have not been validated against missed errors. No evidence yet shows that a higher UFA predicts more errors reaching patients, and until the calibration audit reports, the family measures a necessary surface of review rather than review itself. Second, the setting is a single private outpatient clinic with a small team. The authors are describing their own system. Two authors hold equity in the developer and operator of the system, and one is the clinic's clinical safety officer, appointed on October 18, 2026. The hazard-log version that matches the inventory analyzed here (version 0.2) was not approved, and no clinical safety case report has been written. NHS England guidance expects deployer monitoring to be independent of the manufacturer, 1 and these measures would be computed by the operator. Their credibility depends on the integrity of the underlying records, which is examined in a companion manuscript. 44 Third, three of the four routes cannot yet support the full family. On the WhatsApp agent route no review-fidelity measure beyond latency, attribution and disposition is computable, on the help desk the measures cover only a captured subset, and model stratification is possible only for clinical documentation. Fourth, attribution is incomplete on the messaging routes. A qualified and an unqualified approver produce the same UAR, and seniority of the approver is recorded but not checked (HZ-35), so the measures say nothing about whether the right person approved. Fifth, character-level distance is blind to meaning. A one-word change, such as an inserted negation, may be clinically decisive yet produce a small δ, so edit depth should be paired with edit categories of the kind developed in prior work. 10, 11 Sixth, coverage is incomplete in a second sense. Model-generated content, including live voice-agent speech, reaches patients without approval, and the inventory's headline count of such actions does not reconcile exactly with its own list. Seventh, staff who know their approvals are measured may change behavior, and a small team makes individual inference a risk that the disclosure rules reduce but cannot remove. Finally, the reading floor rests on general reading-rate evidence 36 rather than on clinical review of drafts and will need empirical calibration, and for short drafts it may be shorter than the time needed to operate the review interface, which leaves the UFA insensitive on those drafts.

Whether these measures can be captured reliably once the missing instruments exist, and whether they detect review that fails to catch errors, are the questions that the first measurement window and the calibration audit are designed to answer. Multisite replication would be needed before they are used as general benchmarks.

Conclusion

The UAR, with acceptance coverage, attribution completeness, edit depth, availability latency and the unedited fast-approval rate, makes the fidelity of human review of LLM drafts specifiable as telemetry at the approval event, without exporting patient content. In the deployment studied, the instruments support the full family on clinical documentation only. Deployments that rely on human review as a safety control could check that they record the approval event before reporting acceptance, and could report these measures jointly and with their coverage stated. Validation against missed errors is required before they are used to judge review quality. reported for any individual member of staff, and no output of this work feeds performance management. The analysis of window data, which concerns staff review behavior in aggregate, is not reported here.
