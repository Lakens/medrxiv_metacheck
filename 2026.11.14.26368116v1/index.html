Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Abstract

Objective. Temporal and causal analyses of electronic health records (EHRs) treat the charted time of an observation as the time it was made. We quantified how often bedside pain and sedation assessments are charted to a time slot, how this affects estimates of analgesic onset, and when hourly charting is an adequate approximation. Methods. We used intensive care unit (ICU) data from one academic center (MIMIC-IV) and 78 hospitals (eICU-CRD). Slot labeling was identified from charted minutes and from the delay between charted and entry times. In MIMIC-IV, opioid administration times served as anchors, and faster onset of intravenous (IV) than oral opioids as a pharmacologic benchmark. We compared onset estimated from charted times with onset from exactly timed records, bounded true assessment times under alternative charting mechanisms, and used simulation to relate bias to the ratio of slot width to response time constant (∆/k).

Results. In MIMIC-IV, 68% of pain and 76% of sedation scores were charted on the hour and entered a median of 41-44 minutes later. Of the first reassessments after a dose, 32-34% of on-hour records carried a time before the dose, against 12-14% of off-hour records. Across eICU hospitals, the on-hour share ranged from 15% to 92% (10th-90th percentile). By charted time, oral opioids appeared nearly as fast as IV (pain change at 15-30 minutes, -1.76 vs -2.11 points); exactly timed records showed -0.80 vs -2.00. Slower oral onset held under every charting mechanism compatible with the data, but its magnitude was not identified (ratio of time constants 1.2-11.6). In simulation, analysis by charted time was adequate up to ∆/k ≈ 2 when rounding was to the nearest slot, but only up to ∆/k ≈ 0.1 when rounding had a direction. Conclusion. Charted times of bedside assessments are often slot labels, and charting practice is hospital-specific. For ICU analgesic onset, neither condition for an adequate approximation can be assumed, and EHR data alone cannot recover the size of the effect.

1 Introduction

Time series built from electronic health records (EHRs) are increasingly used for questions about order and timing: how fast a treatment acts, whether one event precedes another, and which variables drive which in time-series causal discovery, causal graph learning, and target-trial emulation [1] [2] [3] [4]. These methods share an assumption that is rarely stated: the charted time of an observation is the time the observation was made.

For machine-generated data-such as continuous bedside physiological monitors, electroencephalography, or automated operating-room motion and video capture [5] [6] [7]-the assumption is close to true. For bedside assessments that a nurse makes and then charts-pain scores, sedation scores, neurological checks-it need not be. The nurse chooses which time to chart. A score obtained at 07:40 can be charted at 08:00 because that is the row of the flowsheet the nurse fills in, and entered at 08:35 when the nurse sits down to document. The charted time is then a slot label: it identifies the charting slot, not the moment of assessment. Delayed documentation and rounded times have been described for other EHR data (Section 2). What is missing is (i) a large, multicenter quantification for bedside assessments, (ii) a demonstration of what slot labels do to an effect estimate whose true value is known, and (iii) a rule for when charting to a slot is an acceptable approximation.

Pain and sedation scores are central variables in ICU care and research [8], and opioid onset gives a natural test case, because pharmacology fixes the answer qualitatively: IV opioids act within minutes, immediate-release oral opioids in about 40 minutes (Section 2.4). Any analysis of bedside pain scores that does not find IV opioids acting faster than oral opioids is measuring something other than pharmacology.

This study is a measurement and data-quality study. We asked four questions.

1. How many bedside assessments are charted to a slot, how long is the delay to entry, and how much does this vary between and within hospitals (MIMIC-IV and 78 eICU-CRD hospitals)?

2. How does analysis by charted time distort the estimated onset of opioids, judged against the benchmark that IV is faster than oral?

3. How much can be recovered from records whose times are exact, and from bounding the true assessment time with exactly timed doses under all charting mechanisms compatible with the data?

4. How does the bias of slot charting depend on the ratio of slot width to the response time constant (∆/k) and on the direction of rounding (simulation)?

We do not claim to solve the problem. Exactly timed doses can bound the true time of an assessment, but where in that bound it lies depends on a charting mechanism that, as we show, the data do not identify. Corrections are therefore reported as sensitivity analyses under explicit assumptions.

2 Related Work

2.1 Timing of EHR documentation

EHR data reflect the processes of care that generate them [9, 10]. Delayed and batched documentation of vital signs [11, 12], timestamp errors and late documentation of perioperative events [13, 14], heaping of manually entered times on round minutes [15, 16], and gaps between documented and actual medication times [17, 18] have all been described. These studies show that recorded times can be wrong; they do not quantify slot labeling of bedside assessments across hospitals or show what it does to an effect estimate.

Statement of Significance Problem or Issue

Temporal and causal analyses of electronic health records assume that the charted time of an observation is the time it was made. For nurse-charted bedside assessments, this is often not true.

What is Already Known

Documentation is often delayed and follows care processes, and the timing of a measurement can itself be informative. How often bedside scores are charted to a time slot, and how this biases effect estimates, had not been quantified.

What this Paper Adds

In MIMIC-IV, 68% of pain and 76% of sedation scores were charted on the hour; across 78 eICU-CRD hospitals the share ranged from 15% to 92%. By charted time, oral opioids appeared nearly as fast as IV. A simulation-based rule (∆/k) shows when hourly charting is an adequate approximation.

Who would benefit

Informaticians and clinical researchers who use charted assessments in time-series, causal or target-trial analyses, and teams that design clinical documentation.

2.2 Informative observation timing and coarse data

When an observation is made can depend on the patient's state, so observation timing in EHRs is itself informative [19] [20] [21] [22]. A slot label is a coarsened time, and coarsening is ignorable only if it occurs at random [23, 24]. Related statistical tools include interval-censored data [25], measurement error [26], multiple imputation [27] and partial identification under weak assumptions [28]; we use multiple imputation to carry timing uncertainty into effect estimates, and partial identification to bound true assessment times.

2.3 Time resolution in temporal and causal inference

Time aggregation, discrete-time approximation of time-dependent dynamics, and sub-sampling are known to distort inferred causal links and system identification [29, 30]; lagged EHR associations can show diseases following their own effects because of workflow [31]; and misplaced time zero biases effect estimates [32], even when time zero is abstracted from records [33]. These results concern the resolution or anchoring of time in general, not slot labels whose direction of rounding is unknown.

2.4 Pain and sedation assessment and opioid onset

Pain and sedation scores are outcomes in ICU care and research, the basis of dosing decisions, and the content of reassessments after analgesic doses, which accreditation standards require to be documented [34]; systematic assessment has been associated with better outcomes [35, 36], although documented reassessment within an hour of an opioid dose can be uncommon [37]. Pharmacology fixes the expected order of opioid onset. IV hydromorphone relieved experimental pain in volunteers within 5 minutes [38], the effect-site equilibration half-life of fentanyl is about 6 minutes (EEG endpoint) [39], and after an IV morphine bolus in postoperative patients the effect-site concentration peaks at about 20 minutes [40]; immediate-release oral oxycodone, by contrast, had a median onset of 41 minutes after surgery [41].

3 Methods

3.1 Data

MIMIC-IV v3.1 contains de-identified records of patients admitted to the ICUs of Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center, Boston, from 2008 to 2022 [42, 43]. We included the first ICU stay of adults with a stay of at least 24 hours (51,838 stays). eICU-CRD v2.0 contains ICU stays at 208 US hospitals discharged in 2014-2015 [44, 45]. Both databases are available on PhysioNet [46] under a data use agreement. Use of the de-identified data is covered by the source institutions' review board determinations (waiver of informed consent for MIMIC-IV; exemption for eICU-CRD). All patient-level processing took place in the cloud database and on a secure institutional server; only aggregate results left that environment, and no reported cell contains fewer than 10 patients. We followed the RECORD reporting guideline where applicable [47].

3.2 Identifying slot labels

In MIMIC-IV every charted observation carries a charted time and an entry time (storage time). We analyzed numeric pain scores (0-10), the post-intervention pain reassessment item, the Critical-Care Pain Observation Tool (CPOT), the Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale (RASS), and, as a machine-generated contrast, heart rate validated from the monitor. A record was on the hour if its charted minute was 0. If times were not rounded, 1/60 (1.7%) of records would fall on the hour. We summarized the entry delay (entry time minus charted time) separately for on-hour and off-hour records, including the share entered before the charted time, which is only possible if the record was charted to a later slot than the one in which it was entered. We also examined whether scores sharing a nurse, patient and charted hour were entered together (batch entry), and how the on-hour share changed by unit and calendar period.

In eICU-CRD, times are minute offsets from ICU admission; the clock minute is the admission clock time plus the offset, modulo 60. The nurse charting table stores both an observation offset and an entry offset. We classified the charted minute as :00, :30, :15 or :45, another multiple of 5, or other. We analyzed hospitals with at least 1,000 pain scores and 50 ICU stays (78 hospitals). In some hospitals the entry offset equaled the observation offset for nearly every record, so the entry time carries no information; we called a hospital's entry time uninformative if at least 90% of its records had an entry delay of exactly 0, and analyzed entry delays only in the remaining hospitals.

3.3 Multicenter analysis

We decomposed the variance of the on-hour indicator into between-hospital, between-unit (within hospital), between-stay (within unit) and within-stay components using the law of total variance, weighting by record. We assessed stability by correlating hospital on-hour shares between random halves of ICU stays and between discharge years 2014 and 2015, and consistency across measures by correlating the on-hour shares of pain, sedation and heart rate within hospitals. We related the on-hour share to hospital characteristics (teaching status, bed count, region) and to the type of entry time by linear regression. Finally, we compared the mean pain score of on-hour and off-hour records within each hospital (Wilcoxon signed-rank test across hospitals).

3.4 Dose anchors and pharmacologic benchmark

In MIMIC-IV, oral opioid doses come from bar-code medication administration records, whose times are exact (2-8% on the hour, 78-96% entered within 5 minutes). IV opioid boluses come from the ICU input records (fentanyl, hydromorphone and morphine pushes). These times are themselves partly rounded (41% on a quarter hour, including 21% on the hour, in the analysis cohort); we therefore repeated the main analysis using only IV doses charted off the quarter hour and entered within 5 minutes (45% of IV doses).

The analysis cohort consisted of single opioid doses given when the last pain score in the preceding 2 hours was at least 4, with no other analgesic or sedative event between the dose and the outcome score (58,987 doses). Outcome scores were pain scores and reassessments entered after the dose and charted between 60 minutes before and 240 minutes after it; we included scores charted before the dose but entered after it, because these may be post-dose reassessments charted to an earlier slot. The pharmacologic benchmark was that IV opioids act faster than oral opioids [38] [39] [40] [41].

3.5 Onset estimates

We classified outcome records into three kinds. Exactly timed records were charted off the hour and entered within 5 minutes of the charted time; for these we took the charted time as the assessment time, and excluded records whose time fell before the dose. Slot records were charted on the hour. Other off-hour records, entered more than 5 minutes after the charted time, were counted separately in descriptive summaries and excluded from the exactly timed onset curves. For each record we computed the change from the pre-dose score. Onset curves averaged the change within patient and time bin (0-15, 15-30, 30-45, 45-60, 60-90, 90-120, 120-180, 180-240 minutes) and then across patients, so that standard errors are clustered by ICU stay. We also fitted an exponential onset model, change = -E (1 -e -t/k ) for t ∈ [0, 90] minutes, where k is the onset time constant (the time to about 63% of the maximum effect), and report the ratio k oral /k IV and the IV minus oral difference at 15-30 minutes. Intervals came from 1,000 cluster-bootstrap samples of ICU stays (500 for the exact-dose sensitivity analysis).

3.6 Bounding the true assessment time

For a slot record charted at c and entered at s after a dose at t d , the true assessment time T satisfies max(t d , c -60) ≤ T ≤ min(s, c + 60, next intervention), in minutes: it must follow the dose and precede entry and the next intervention, and a record charted to the hour slot is assumed to be no more than one slot away. The true time is thus interval-censored [25], or, in the terminology of coarse data, the charted time is a coarsening of it [23]. Where T lies within this interval depends on the charting mechanism. We modeled the mechanism as a mixture of rounding rules (to the previous, nearest or next hour) with a log-normal entry delay, fitted separately for IV and oral doses to the observed histogram of s -c among post-dose slot records. As a second moment, we used the share of first reassessments charted before the dose, after subtracting the share seen among exactly timed records (assessments that truly preceded the dose).

Because the mechanism was not identified (Results), we treated it as a sensitivity parameter. For each route we varied the shares of the three rounding rules over a simplex grid in steps of 0.25 (15 rules per route, 225 combinations), refitted the entry-delay distribution for each rule, and imputed true times by drawing from the posterior proportional to (prior × rounding rule × entry-delay density), analogous to posterior-based probabilistic data association and latent-state estimation under measurement uncertainty [48, 49], with the prior taken from the timing of exactly timed reassessments. We call a rule compatible if it fitted the s -c histogram about as well as the best rule (4 IV and 3 oral rules; 12 combinations). For the compatible combinations, we combined the cluster bootstrap with two imputations per bootstrap sample (250 samples) and estimated variances with the method of von Hippel and Bartlett [27]. This is an analysis of what the data allow under each assumption, not an estimate of the truth [28].

3.7 Representativeness of exactly timed records

Exactly timed records could differ from slot records in ways that matter for onset. We compared covariates of patients, doses and units (standardized mean differences), estimated how well patient and dose covariates predict whether a record is exactly timed (cross-validated area under the curve) compared with the identity of the charting nurse (leave-one-out), measured the split-half reliability of each nurse's share of exactly timed records, compared the late plateau (120-240 minutes, where timing errors matter little) between the two kinds of record, and reweighted exactly timed records to the covariate distribution of all records (weights capped at the 99th percentile).

3.8 Simulation

Consistency check.

To check the implementation of the imputation, we used exactly timed records as the truth, converted 72% of them into slot records with a known mechanism, and re-estimated onset (20 replicates). The same mechanism generated and corrected the data, so this tests only the code, not the assumptions (Supplementary Section A).

Time-scale simulation.

To ask when slot charting is an acceptable approximation, we expressed time in units of the onset time constant (k = 1). True assessment times were uniform on [0, 6k], the change was -2(1 -e -T /k ) plus normal noise (SD 2), and the dose fell at a uniform phase within the slot. Two processes with k = 1 and k = 2 (true ratio 2) each had 3,000 assessments; we varied ∆/k from 0.05 to 6 and the rounding rule (previous, nearest, next slot), with 200 replicates. We compared analysis by charted time, the slot midpoint, imputation with the correct mechanism, and imputation with a misspecified mechanism (for example, assuming nearest-slot rounding when rounding was to the previous slot). Adequacy was defined as a relative bias in k within ±10%.

4 Results

4.1 Slot labels in MIMIC-IV

Bedside assessments were mostly charted on the hour: 68% of pain scores, 71% of pain reassessments, 78% of CPOT scores and 76% of RASS scores, against 1.7% expected without rounding (Table 1). On-hour and off-hour records were charted differently (Fig. 1). Off-hour records were entered almost at once: median delay 0-1 minute, 71-80% within 5 minutes, and fewer than 1% entered before their charted time. On-hour records were entered a median of 41-44 minutes after their charted time, 38-41% more than an hour later, and 15-17% before their charted time-that is, they were charted to a later hour than the one in which they were entered. Heart rate, whose time is set by the monitor, was also mostly charted on the hour (hourly validation) but was entered before its charted time in only 0.9% of records. Pain and sedation scores were entered in batches: when a nurse charted both for the same patient at the same hour, 89% of pairs were entered within one minute of each other. The on-hour share was higher in the 2020-2022 anchor-year group than in 2008-2010 in every unit (for example, from 64% in 2008-2010 to 87% in 2020-2022 in the trauma surgical ICU, and from 63% to 82% in the medical ICU), and the median entry delay lengthened (medical ICU, 17 to 41 minutes).

The exact bar-code dose times show the consequence for ordering. Among all first reassessments entered after a dose, 32.2% of those after bar-code doses carried a charted time before the dose (26.8% after IV pushes). Some reassessments do truly precede a dose (for example, a reassessment of the previous dose entered late): among off-hour first reassessments in the analysis cohort, this share was 11.9% (oral) to 13.7% (IV). Among on-hour first reassessments it was 33.9% (oral) and 32.0% (IV), so roughly 20 percentage points can be attributed to slot charting.

4.2 Multicenter: 78 eICU hospitals

The 78 hospitals contributed 2.41 million pain scores. Pooled, 54.5% were charted at :00, with further heaping at :30, :15 and :45 and at other multiples of 5 minutes (Fig. 2a). The on-hour share varied widely between hospitals: 10th percentile 15%, median 37%, 90th percentile 92%. In 48 hospitals the entry offset equaled the observation offset for essentially every record (median 100%), so the entry time carries no information, possibly because those records were imported from the hospital's own EHR. In the other 30 hospitals the median on-hour share was 86% (25% in the 48), on-hour records were entered a median of 61 minutes late (median across hospitals; 90th percentile 216 minutes), and half were entered more than an hour late.

Slot labeling was a property of the hospital, not the unit. Between-hospital variance accounted for 28% of the variance of the on-hour indicator, between-unit variance within hospitals for 0.6%, between-stay variance for 12% and within-stay variance for 59% (sedation scores: 23%, 1.7%, 11% and 65%). Hospital on-hour shares were almost identical between random halves of stays (r = 0.999) and between 2014 and 2015 (r = 0.97), and correlated across measures within a hospital (pain vs sedation r = 0.78; pain vs heart rate r = 0.85), consistent with a hospital-wide flowsheet configuration. Teaching status, bed count and region together explained little (adjusted R² = 0.19); the type of entry time alone explained more (adjusted R² = 0.60). Among the 30 hospitals with informative entry times, a higher on-hour share went with shorter entry delays (Spearman ρ = -0.39); pooled over all 78 hospitals the correlation reversed (ρ = 0.72) because the two record systems differ in both (Fig. 2b). Within hospitals, the timing of a record carried information about the patient (Fig. 2c). Off-hour pain scores were on average 1.96 points higher than on-hour scores in the same hospital (pooled means 3.80 vs 1.27), and the share of scores ≥ 4 was 27 percentage points higher; 66 of 71 hospitals with enough scores showed the difference (p < 10 -11 ), in both kinds of record system. In MIMIC-IV the within-stay difference was smaller (0.51 points). MIMIC-IV has a 4-hourly scheduled assessment: at 00, 04, 08, 12, 16 and 20 h there were 5-10 times as many pain scores as at other hours, with means of 2.4-2.9 against 4.0-4.6. On-hour records thus mix routine scheduled assessments with event-triggered assessments charted to the hour.

4.3 Consequences for estimated onset

Analysis by charted time erased most of the difference between routes (Fig. 3a). At 15-30 minutes after the dose, pain had fallen by 2.11 points after IV and 1.76 points after oral opioids, and the fitted onset time constants were 7.2 minutes (IV) and 15.1 minutes (oral). An oral onset of 15 minutes is pharmacologically implausible [41]; it arises because charted-time bins mix in low-pain routine scheduled :00 assessments and post-dose reassessments rounded backward to the :00 slot just after the dose, producing a steep apparent early drop followed by an upward rebound after 60 minutes. In exactly timed records (5,538 ICU stays), pain at 15-30 minutes had fallen by 2.00 points after IV opioids but only 0.80 points after oral opioids, and oral opioids reached the IV effect only at 45-60 minutes (Fig. 3b; the slight positive change at 0-15 minutes after oral doses reflects pain worsening between the last pre-dose score in the preceding 2-hour window and actual administration, before oral absorption begins). The fitted time constant was 14.9 minutes for IV opioids [95% cluster-bootstrap interval 12.3, 18.1] and 80 minutes for oral opioids [55, 132], a ratio of 5.4 [3.5, 9.3]; the IV minus oral difference at 15-30 minutes was -1.19 [-1.57, -0.83] (Table 2).

The oral time constant is poorly determined because the oral curve had not reached its plateau by 90 minutes; the direction and the early difference are the robust quantities.

Restricting to exactly timed IV doses (8,577 IV and 3,189 oral records) made the IV onset faster: k IV = 11.7 minutes [9.3, 14.6], ratio 6.9 [4.5, 12.1], difference at 15-30 minutes -1.41 [-1.85, -1.01] (Fig. 3c). Rounded dose times had blurred the IV curve in the same way as rounded assessment times: error in the time variable attenuates the estimated response, as measurement error in a covariate does [26, 50].

Using the entry time instead of the charted time over-corrected (IV 33.8 minutes; the oral fit reached the upper bound), and the slot midpoint gave 35.3 and 91.2 minutes. Neither is a defensible estimate: the entry time is an upper bound on the assessment time, and the midpoint assumes symmetric rounding.

4.4 How much can be recovered

The charting mechanism is not identified. Fitted to the entry-delay histogram, post-dose slot records were best described by mostly nearest-hour rounding (IV 90% nearest and 10% to the previous hour, median entry delay 40 minutes; oral 90% nearest and 10% to the next hour, median 60 minutes). The histogram constrained the entry-delay median well but did not separate rounding rules: 4 IV and 3 oral rules fitted about equally well. The second moment, the share of first reassessments charted before the dose, should separate them, since rounding to the previous hour moves records before the dose. After subtracting the share of assessments that truly preceded the dose, the targets were 21% (IV) and 25% (oral). No rounding rule matched both moments: rules compatible with the histogram predicted at most 15%, and only rules dominated by rounding to the previous hour reached the target, at the cost of a poor histogram fit. Simple rounding of the true assessment time to a slot is therefore rejected by the data; other practices, such as charting the reassessment in the row of the dose or charting the time at which a reassessment was due, would be needed and are not identifiable from these records.

Direction robust, magnitude not identified.

Across all 225 combinations of IV and oral rules, the ratio k oral /k IV ranged from 0.45 to 32.6 (Supplementary Fig. S1). Ratios below 1 arose only when IV records were assumed to be rounded mostly to the previous hour and oral records mostly to the nearest or next hour, a pairing that contradicts the histogram for both routes. Under each of the 12 compatible combinations, oral onset was slower (Fig. 4): the ratio ranged from 1.2 to 11.6 and every 95% interval excluded 1 (union of intervals 1.05-15.3). The IV time constant ranged from 6 to 27 minutes and the oral one from 32 to 71 minutes, depending on the assumed mechanism.

Exactly timed records are informative but not a random sample. Exactly timed and slot records were similar in covariates (all standardized mean differences below 0.24). Whether a record was exactly timed was predicted poorly by patient and dose covariates (cross-validated AUC 0.61) and well by the charting nurse (AUC 0.84); nurses' shares of exactly timed records ranged from 0.3% to 35% (10th-90th percentile) and were stable (split-half r = 0.91, 386 nurses). Charting habit, rather than the patient's condition, thus largely decides which records are exactly timed. However, at the late plateau, where timing errors matter little, exactly timed records showed slightly larger reductions than slot records (IV -0.17 [-0.31, -0.04]; oral -0.52 [-0.70, -0.31] points). Reweighting exactly timed records to the covariate distribution of all records left the conclusions unchanged (Table 2).

4.5 When is slot charting an adequate approximation?

In the time-scale simulation, the direction of rounding mattered more than the width of the slot (Fig. 5). With rounding to the nearest slot, the error in assessment time is symmetric, and analysis by charted time estimated k within 10% up to ∆/k = 2. With rounding to the previous or next slot the error has a direction, and analysis by charted time was within 10% only up to ∆/k = 0.1; at ∆/k = 0.25 the bias was already -16% (previous) or +17% (next), and at ∆/k = 1 it was -49% or +70%. The ratio of two processes was distorted accordingly (at ∆/k = 1, 2.8 or 1.6 instead of 2). Imputation with the correct mechanism was unbiased up to ∆/k = 2 but failed beyond 3, where the slot is too wide to retain the information; imputation with a misspecified mechanism was no better than the charted time, and the slot midpoint was within 10% up to ∆/k = 1.5. With hourly slots, ICU IV opioids (k ≈ 12-25 minutes) have ∆/k ≈ 2.4-5, at or beyond the limit even with a known mechanism, and oral opioids (k ≈ 35-80 minutes) have ∆/k ≈ 0.75-1.7, where the result depends on the direction of rounding-which, in MIMIC-IV, is exactly what the data do not identify.

5 Discussion

5.1 Principal findings

Charted times of bedside pain and sedation assessments are mostly slot labels. In MIMIC-IV, two thirds to three quarters of scores sit on the hour and are entered about 40 minutes later, and some are entered before the hour they are charted to. Across 78 eICU hospitals, the on-hour share ranges from about 15% to over 90%, is a stable property of the hospital rather than the unit, and in most hospitals cannot even be checked against an entry time, because the stored entry time is a copy of the charted time. Slot labels scramble order: about one in five post-dose reassessments charted on the hour is placed before the dose it follows. Analysis by charted time makes oral opioids look almost as fast as IV opioids. Records whose time is exact restore the pharmacology, but the size of the difference cannot be recovered from the rest of the data, because the charting mechanism is not identified. Whether charting to a slot is harmless depends on ∆/k and on the direction of rounding.

5.2 Implications for temporal and causal analysis

Methods that infer effects or causal structure from the order of events-lagged regression, Grangertype tests, time-series causal discovery, target-trial emulation with time-varying treatments-take the charted order as the true order. Coarse time resolution and misplaced time zero are known to distort such analyses (Section 2). In our data, a reassessment of a dose can appear before the dose, and an assessment that prompted a dose can appear after it. An analysis that conditions on "the last score before the dose" or "the first score after the dose" can therefore pick up the wrong score. The same applies to any subjective, nurse-charted variable (sedation, delirium screening, neurological checks); it does not apply to monitor-timed signals, whose charted times were essentially never later than their entry.

5.3 The timing of an assessment is informative

On-hour and off-hour scores differ by about 2 points within eICU hospitals. This is not an artifact to be averaged away: on-hour records are dominated by scheduled routine assessments, and off-hour records by event-triggered ones (a patient's complaint, a reassessment after a dose). When an assessment is made, and therefore which time it is charted to, depends on the patient's state. As for other EHR observations (Section 2), the degree of coarsening (exact time or slot label) and the timing itself depend on the patient's state, so the coarsening is not at random: analyses that treat observation times as ignorable will be biased, and exactly timed subsets are not random samples of assessments. This also means that the measurement schedule is itself a clinical decision whose effect on treatment can be studied, which we address in separate work.

5.4 A checklist for analysts

1. Estimate ∆/k: the charting resolution relative to the time constant of the process studied. If ∆/k ≤ 0.1, slot charting is harmless whatever the rounding.

2. Check whether the entry time is informative (in eICU, it is not in most hospitals) before using it to bound or classify records. 5. If ∆/k > 2, use exactly timed subsets or better data, and report how representative those subsets are.

6. Check the anchors too: in our data, ICU bolus times were themselves partly rounded.

5.5 Implications for documentation

EHRs should distinguish the assessment time from the documentation time and should not pre-fill the assessment time with the flowsheet slot. Multicenter studies should report each site's charting practice, including whether the entry time is real. Automated time capture and real-time clinical sensor or workflow logging avoid the heaping of manual entry [15, 51, 52]. Events with machinerecorded times (bar-code administration, infusion pumps, monitor alarms, device data) can serve as anchors, but only if their own times are exact [18].

5.6 Limitations

Dose anchors were available in one center only; eICU-CRD lacks consistently timed bar-code administration and entry records across hospitals, so the consequences for onset could not be examined across sites. The charting mechanism was not identified: the family of simple rounding rules was rejected by the data, and we did not model extended mechanisms (charting a reassessment in the dose's row, or charting the time at which it was due). The semi-synthetic consistency check recovered k but did not recover the 15-30 minute difference (coverage of binned curves about 40%), and because it coarsened only true post-dose reassessments, it yielded a slower charted-time estimate rather than reproducing the artificially fast onset seen in the real data (where post-dose windows also include low-pain routine scheduled :00 assessments and reassessments charted when pain has improved). Exactly timed records are determined mainly by nurse habit but showed a slightly larger late effect. The oral time constant was poorly determined within 90 minutes. IV agents (fentanyl, hydromorphone, morphine) differ in onset by minutes, below the resolution of reassessment intervals, and could not be distinguished. The time-scale simulation is a simplified setting. Our data concern acute pain in ICUs; other settings have other slot widths (for example, daily headache diaries or visit-level ratings), and ∆/k must be estimated for each.

6 Conclusion

Charted times of bedside assessments are often slot labels, and charting practice differs between hospitals. Charting to a slot is an adequate approximation when ∆/k is small (about 0.1 or less) or when rounding is symmetric and ∆/k is at most about 2. For ICU analgesic onset neither condition can be assumed, and EHR data alone cannot recover the size of the effect. Recording the true assessment time is the remedy. Of the 225 combinations, 173 had intervals entirely above 1, 34 included 1 and 18 were below 1.
