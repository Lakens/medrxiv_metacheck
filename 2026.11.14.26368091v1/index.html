Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background & Aims: Most prognostic models for metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) target prespecified hepatic endpoints, potentially limiting attainable accuracy. We developed and externally validated a model that learns the future hepatic phenotype most reliably predictable from baseline clinical characteristics.

Methods: We analyzed 332 HALO-MASLD participants (456 follow-up observations). Sparse Canonical Outcome REgression (SCORE) learned a weighted phenotype from eight hepatic measures, coupled with broken adaptive ridge regression using 99 baseline predictor columns. Participant-level nested five-fold cross-validation compared prediction of the learned phenotype with future liver stiffness measurement (LSM), controlled attenuation parameter (CAP), and FIB-4 benchmarks. Models were externally validated without refitting in the independent NIDDK NAFLD Adult Database 2 cohort (520 participants, 732 follow-up observations). Biological validity and bootstrap stability were also assessed.

Results: The learned phenotype emphasized lower platelet count and higher bilirubin, alkaline phosphatase, and LSM. In HALO, predictive correlation was r = 0.860 (95% CI [0.824, 0.896]) and predictive R² = 0.671, versus r = 0.763 for LSM and r = 0.469 for CAP predicted from all baseline variables. All six prespecified comparisons remained significant after Holm correction (p Holm < 0.05). The phenotype correlated with MRE stiffness (ρ = 0.694) and biopsy fibrosis stage (ρ = 0.567), and was higher with imaging evidence of portal hypertension (p < 0.001). Bootstrap relearning yielded mean phenotype correlation 0.947. In NIDDK, predictive correlation was r = 0.836 (95% CI [0.805, 0.863]; predictive R² = 0.680), and associations with fibrosis and portal hypertension replicated.

Conclusions: Learning the prediction target produced a sparse, clinically interpretable hepatic phenotype more accurately predicted than conventional longitudinal MASLD endpoints in both cohorts. The resulting LIFT-MASLD model is available at https://liftmasld.github.io.

1 Impact and Implications

Conventional prognostic models for MASLD target prespecified endpoints, although no single marker fully captures future hepatic disease severity. We instead learned a multivariate hepatic phenotype optimized for longitudinal prediction and identified a sparse composite that was predicted more accurately than future liver stiffness or CAP in both development and external validation cohorts. The learned phenotype was also associated with independent measures of fibrosis and portal hypertension that were not targeted during outcome learning, demonstrating that improved predictability could be achieved while retaining clinical relevance. By forecasting this clinically meaningful hepatic state from a small set of routinely collected baseline measurements, LIFT-MASLD offers clinicians a new approach to longitudinal monitoring and provides researchers with a framework for developing more predictable, biologically meaningful prognostic outcomes.

2 Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is the most common chronic liver disease worldwide and spans a broad spectrum from steatosis to steatohepatitis, progressive fibrosis, cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma (European Association for the Study of the Liver et al., 2024). Approximately onethird of the global adult population has steatotic liver disease, and prevalence continues to increase (Riazi et al., 2022). However, the clinical course of MASLD is highly heterogeneous. Many individuals remain stable for years, whereas others progress to advanced fibrosis, portal hypertension, hepatic decompensation, or liver-related death (Sanyal et al., 2021a). Identifying which patients will develop clinically consequential disease therefore remains a central challenge.

Fibrosis has accordingly become a central focus of MASLD risk stratification because histologic fibrosis stage strongly predicts liver-related morbidity and mortality (Ekstedt et al., 2015; Angulo et al., 2015; Sanyal et al., 2021a). Clinical pathways therefore aim first to identify patients with advanced fibrosis, commonly beginning with the fibrosis-4 index (FIB-4), a blood-based score derived from age, aminotransferases, and platelet count, followed when indicated by imaging-based assessment of liver stiffness (European Association for the Study of the Liver et al., 2024; Shah et al., 2009). Prediction studies have then similarly focused on prespecified fibrosis-related targets, including future progression to fibrosis (Ghandian et al., 2022), liverstiffness or histologic fibrosis progression (Zhou et al., 2026), and classification of histologic fibrosis stage (Chang et al., 2023). Thus, although predictor models have become increasingly sophisticated, the clinical outcome to be predicted is generally specified in advance.

Fibrosis, however, does not capture the entire hepatic disease state. Clinicians routinely consider other measures such as platelet count, bilirubin, albumin, and evidence of portal hypertension, which reflect complementary aspects of hepatic function and disease severity. Fibrosis measurements are also imperfect: liver biopsy samples only a small fraction of the liver and exhibits substantial sampling variability (Ratziu et al., 2005), while transient elastography measures stiffness as a surrogate for fibrosis and can disagree with histologic staging (Myers et al., 2010). Consequently, a prespecified fibrosis-related endpoint may capture only part of the clinically relevant future disease state and may contain measurement error and biological variability that impose an intrinsic limit on predictive accuracy (Lundberg et al., 2024). Improving longitudinal prediction may therefore require optimizing not only the predictor model but also the clinical state being predicted.

We therefore sought to develop an accurate longitudinal prediction model of future hepatic status in MASLD by jointly optimizing the prediction model and the clinical outcome being predicted, while ensuring that the learned outcome retains a clear clinical interpretation. To this end, we employed Sparse Canonical Outcome REgression (SCORE), which learns an interpretable weighted combination of clinically meaningful outcomes that maximizes prospective predictability from baseline characteristics (Strobl, 2025). We applied SCORE to eight hepatic measures spanning steatosis, liver stiffness, biochemical function and injury, and platelet count, together with sparse predictor selection. We hypothesized that this approach would yield a clinically meaningful hepatic phenotype that could be predicted more accurately and consistently over time than conventional endpoints, while also being associated with independent measures of fibrosis and advanced liver disease. We finally evaluated the generalizability of the trained prediction model in an independent cohort.

3 Methods

3.1 HALO-MASLD Study

Data were obtained from the University of Pittsburgh Health Assessments and Longitudinal Outcomes in MASLD (HALO-MASLD) study, a prospective longitudinal cohort designed to characterize risk factors and biomarkers associated with MASLD progression. The parent cohort recruited adults aged 18-79 years with diagnosed MASLD through the University of Pittsburgh/UPMC liver program and affiliated research recruitment pathways. Participants underwent detailed annual assessments of hepatic and systemic health, including transient elastography, anthropometric and body-composition measurements, laboratory testing, medication and comorbidity assessment, patient-reported outcomes, lifestyle measures, and biospecimen collection.

For the present study, we included participants with MASLD who had baseline clinical characteristics available at the first study visit and hepatic outcome measurements available at the second and/or third visits. Measurements obtained at the first visit were used exclusively as predictors, whereas measurements obtained at subsequent visits were used to define and evaluate prospective hepatic outcomes.

3.2 Ethics Approval

The HALO-MASLD study was approved by the University of Pittsburgh Institutional Review Board (STUDY 21030003). The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and all participants provided written informed consent before participation. The present analysis was separately reviewed by the University of Pittsburgh IRB and determined not to constitute human subjects research (STUDY26100015).

3.3 Study Design and Outcome Definition

We developed a longitudinal clinical prediction model to predict hepatic status at subsequent study visits from clinical characteristics measured at baseline. We restricted the primary analysis to eight prespecified hepatic outcomes chosen to capture complementary dimensions of MASLD severity: controlled attenuation parameter (CAP) for steatosis, FibroScan liver stiffness measurement (LSM) for fibrosis-related stiffness, total bilirubin and albumin for hepatic function, alkaline phosphatase, aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) for biochemical injury, and platelet count as a marker associated with advanced fibrosis and portal hypertension. We excluded broader patient-reported measures, such as PROMIS domains, from the primary outcome set because they reflect general physical and psychological health rather than hepatic disease specifically (Cella et al., 2010).

We constructed the predictor matrix exclusively from measurements obtained at the first visit. The resulting matrix contained 99 predictor columns spanning demographic characteristics, anthropometric and body-composition measurements, baseline liver measurements, metabolic laboratory values, comorbidities, medications, lifestyle characteristics, family history, and indicators of missing measurements. We provide the comprehensive list of variables in Appendix Table 4. Sex was defined as sex assigned at birth and encoded as a binary indicator for male sex. We log-transformed selected right-skewed laboratory and elastography variables before analysis. Missing predictor values were median-imputed using medians estimated exclusively within each training sample.

Follow-up observations were included in the primary analysis only when all eight hepatic outcomes were observed; follow-up outcomes were not imputed. We oriented outcomes such that larger values represented greater hepatic disease burden and therefore reversed albumin and platelet count before scaling. Within each training sample, outcomes were min-max scaled and the predictor and outcome matrices were centered separately within each follow-up visit.

3.4 Clinical Prediction With SCORE

We used SCORE to determine the multivariate hepatic phenotype that could be most accurately predicted from baseline characteristics. Let Y t denote the matrix of hepatic outcomes measured at follow-up visit t and X t the corresponding baseline predictor matrix. To obtain a highly parsimonious clinical prediction model, we replaced the predictor-side ordinary least-squares step with broken adaptive ridge (BAR) regression (Dai et al., 2018). BAR uses iterative reweighted L 2 penalties to approximate L 0 regularization, which penalizes the number of nonzero coefficients rather than continuously shrinking their magnitudes. This typically yields sparser solutions than L 1 regularization while reducing shrinkage of retained predictors. Conditional on α, BAR repeatedly solved weighted ridge regressions of Y t α on X t . We optimized SCORE from multiple initial values to reduce sensitivity to local optima. During crossvalidation, we used three starting values for each candidate λ. After selecting λ, we used 10 starting values for the final fit and retained the solution with the largest SCORE objective.

3.5 Nested Cross-Validation

We considered 15 logarithmically spaced values of λ between 10 -4 and 10 0 . Predictive performance was estimated using five-fold nested cross-validation with folds defined at the participant level, such that all observations from the same participant remained in either the training or test sample.

Within each outer training fold, we performed five-fold inner cross-validation to select λ. The hepatic phenotype and corresponding predictor model were independently learned within each inner training sample, and λ was selected to maximize out-of-sample correlation between the observed phenotype and its prediction from baseline characteristics. We then refit SCORE-BAR on the complete outer training sample using the selected λ and evaluated the resulting clinical prediction model in the untouched outer test subjects.

After estimating predictive performance, we selected the final BAR penalty by maximizing mean innercross-validation performance across the outer training folds and refit SCORE-BAR using all eligible observations. This final fit was used to characterize the learned outcome weights, selected baseline predictors, and associations of the learned phenotype with independent measures of liver disease.

3.6 Clinical Prediction Benchmarks

We compared the predictive accuracy of the learned clinical prediction model with models targeting two conventional longitudinal MASLD endpoints: future FibroScan LSM and future CAP. For both endpoints, we first fitted BAR using the complete baseline predictor panel.

For future LSM, we additionally evaluated baseline LSM alone, baseline FIB-4 alone, baseline FIB-4 combined with baseline LSM, and a clinical model containing baseline LSM, age, sex, body mass index, type 2 diabetes, and ALT. The clinical model was fitted using both ordinary linear regression and BAR.

For future CAP, we evaluated baseline CAP alone and a clinical model containing baseline CAP, age, sex, body mass index, type 2 diabetes, and triglycerides, again using both ordinary linear regression and BAR. The same participant-level outer folds were used for all comparisons.

3.7 Independent External Validation in NIDDK

We externally validated the trained models using the independent National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases (NIDDK) NAFLD Adult Database 2 cohort (Sanyal et al., 2021b). We used enrollment measurements as baseline predictors and hepatic measurements obtained at approximately 48 and 96 weeks as follow-up outcomes, corresponding to visits 2 and 3 in HALO.

We applied the final SCORE-BAR model trained on the complete HALO cohort to NIDDK without refitting or recalibration. The learned phenotype was calculated using the original HALO outcome weights and min-max scaling parameters. Because albumin, AST, and ALT received zero weights in the final learned phenotype, we required complete follow-up measurements only for the five outcomes with nonzero weights: CAP, LSM, total bilirubin, alkaline phosphatase, and platelet count. The resulting external-validation sample contained 732 follow-up observations. Baseline predictors were harmonized to the HALO definitions, and missing values were imputed using the original HALO training medians.

For comparison, we similarly applied the conventional prediction benchmarks trained on HALO, including the final BAR models for future CAP and LSM using the complete HALO baseline predictor panel. These models retained their original selected predictors, regression coefficients, calibration parameters, and preprocessing transformations. Predictors unavailable in identical form were harmonized where possible; in particular, the HALO cirrhosis diagnosis indicator was approximated using baseline imaging and centrally reviewed histologic evidence of cirrhosis in NIDDK. No model parameters were re-estimated.

3.8 Bootstrap Confidence Intervals and Performance Comparisons

We evaluated predictive performance using Pearson correlation, normalized root mean squared error (NRMSE), and predictive R² . In HALO, Pearson correlations were calculated separately within each outer crossvalidation fold and follow-up visit, transformed using Fisher's z transformation, and combined using weights proportional to the corresponding sample size minus three. Prediction errors were normalized by the observed outcome standard deviation within each fold and visit, and NRMSE was calculated by taking the square root of the mean squared standardized errors pooled across all follow-up observations. Predictive R² was defined as i∈It (z it -z t ) 2 , where z it and ˆ︁ z it denote the observed and predicted values of the model-specific outcome for participant i at follow-up visit t, respectively, I t denotes the participants observed at visit t, and z t denotes the corresponding observed outcome mean.

Thus, predictive R² quantified the reduction in squared prediction error relative to visit-specific mean prediction. All three metrics combined observations across visits rather than taking an unweighted average of visit-specific performance. In NIDDK, the same aggregation principles were used, except that correlations and prediction-error normalization were calculated within follow-up visits without cross-validation fold stratification. Each model was evaluated against its designated outcome.

We quantified uncertainty in HALO predictive performance using 2,000 participant-level bootstrap resamples of the out-of-fold predictions generated by nested cross-validation. Participants were resampled so that repeated visits remained together. Predictive performance was recalculated without retraining the models in order to isolate sampling variability in performance evaluation from variability in model estimation (Tsamardinos et al., 2018). We formed 95% confidence intervals from the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the bootstrap distribution. For external validation, we similarly used 2,000 participant-level bootstrap resamples of the NIDDK predictions obtained from the frozen HALO models.

For pairwise comparisons, the same bootstrap sample was applied to both sets of out-of-fold predictions and differences in each performance metric were calculated within each replicate. We used 10,000 participantlevel bootstrap resamples for the prespecified comparisons of the learned clinical prediction model with BAR prediction of future LSM using the complete baseline predictor panel and with prediction of future LSM using baseline FIB-4 alone, across all three performance metrics. Two-sided bootstrap p-values were calculated by comparing the observed differences with the centered bootstrap distributions, using a finite-sample correction. We selected the former comparator because it used the full set of available baseline predictors while retaining future LSM as the prespecified outcome, and the latter because FIB-4 is widely used as a first-line clinical tool for fibrosis risk stratification in MASLD. The resulting six p-values were adjusted using the Holm procedure. The same paired participant-resampling approach was used for comparisons in NIDDK.

3.9 Temporal Stability of Predictive Performance

We evaluated persistence of predictive accuracy across follow-up intervals using the signed change in out-ofsample correlation, ∆ = r visit 3 -r visit 2 . Values near zero indicated stable predictive performance, whereas negative values indicated lower predictive accuracy at visit 3. We obtained 95% confidence intervals for visit-specific correlations and their changes using 2,000 participant-level bootstrap resamples in both HALO and NIDDK.

3.10 Validation Against Independent Measures of Liver Disease

We evaluated the clinical interpretation of the learned hepatic phenotype using complementary measures of liver disease severity available in subsets of participants. We prioritized MRE liver stiffness and histologic fibrosis stage as established measures of fibrotic disease, and imaging evidence of portal hypertension as a clinically consequential manifestation of advanced liver disease. Each measure was matched to the corresponding follow-up visit. Associations with continuous or ordinal measures were quantified using Spearman rank correlation to allow for monotonic but potentially nonlinear relationships. For binary imaging evidence of portal hypertension, we compared mean learned phenotype values between participants with and without portal hypertension. We obtained 95% confidence intervals and two-sided p-values using 10,000 participant-level bootstrap resamples, with p-values calculated from centered bootstrap distributions.

We repeated these analyses in NIDDK using centrally reviewed histologic fibrosis stage and imaging evidence of portal hypertension. Histologic fibrosis stages were harmonized to a common ordinal scale, and imaging evidence of portal hypertension was defined by documented splenomegaly, ascites, or other portal-hypertension features on abdominal ultrasound, CT, or MRI. These measures were matched to the corresponding follow-up visit. The same participant-level bootstrap procedures were applied. Quantitative MRE stiffness measurements were unavailable in NIDDK.

3.11 Bootstrap Stability of the Learned Phenotype

We assessed reproducibility of the model-defined phenotype using 200 participant-level bootstrap samples. For each replicate, we resampled participants with replacement and repeated five-fold cross-validation over the original 15-value λ grid using three SCORE starting values for each candidate λ. We then relearned the first hepatic phenotype using the selected λ and 10 starting values, thereby incorporating variability arising from participant sampling, BAR tuning, and SCORE optimization.

We quantified stability of the outcome weights using cosine similarity between the bootstrap estimate ˆ︁ α (b) and the original estimate ˆ︁ α. We separately quantified stability of the resulting phenotype using a common reference outcome matrix Y ref , containing the pooled follow-up observations after outcome orientation and min-max scaling, without visit centering. For each bootstrap replicate, we calculated the correlation between b) and Y ref ˆ︁ α. Both quantities were summarized using their means and 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles.

4 Results

4.1 Cohort Characteristics

The HALO cohort included 332 participants contributing 456 follow-up observations, comprising 297 observations at visit 2 and 159 at visit 3; 124 participants contributed observations at both follow-up visits.

Baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1.

The cohort was predominantly White and had a high burden of metabolic disease. Baseline liver disease was heterogeneous: CAP indicated substantial steatosis, whereas liver stiffness and FIB-4 suggested that most participants did not have advanced fibrosis. Liver synthetic function and platelet counts were generally preserved, although the distribution of liver stiffness indicated a subgroup with more advanced disease.

4.2 SCORE-BAR Identified a Sparse Hepatic Phenotype

SCORE-BAR identified the sparse hepatic phenotype plotted in Figure 1A. 4.3 SCORE-BAR Achieved the Highest Predictive Accuracy

SCORE-BAR achieved the highest predictive accuracy across the evaluated targets and models (Table 2). Its overall nested cross-validated correlation was r = 0.860 and predictive R² = 0.671, compared with r = 0.763 and R² = 0.523 for future LSM, and r = 0.469 and R² = 0.176 for future CAP, when each was predicted using the complete baseline predictor panel with BAR.

4.7 External Validation Replicated the Predictive Accuracy and Biological Relevance of SCORE-BAR

In the independent NIDDK NAFLD Adult Database 2 cohort, SCORE-BAR again achieved the highest predictive accuracy among the evaluated models (Table 3), with a Pearson correlation of r = 0.836, NRMSE of 0.563, and predictive R² = 0.680 across 732 follow-up observations. Predictive accuracy remained high across follow-up visits, although correlation decreased from r = 0.863 at visit 2 to r = 0.792 at visit 3 (∆ = -0.070, 95% CI [-0.119, -0.028]). Independent biological validation further demonstrated that the learned phenotype correlated with histologic fibrosis stage (ρ = 0.358, 95% CI [0.069, 0.600], p = 0.008) and was significantly higher among patients with imaging evidence of portal hypertension than among those without (mean difference = 0.962, 95% CI [0.496, 1.432], p < 0.001). MRE stiffness measurements were unavailable in NIDDK, precluding external replication of the corresponding HALO association. Together, these findings support the generalizability of SCORE-BAR's predictive performance and the biological relevance of its learned phenotype to fibrosis-related liver disease severity.

5 Discussion

We developed and both internally and externally validated a sparse clinical prediction model for future hepatic status in MASLD. Rather than prespecifying a single longitudinal marker as the prediction target, the model used SCORE to identify the multivariate hepatic phenotype that could be predicted most reliably from baseline clinical characteristics. The resulting phenotype emphasized lower platelet count, higher bilirubin, higher alkaline phosphatase, and greater liver stiffness and was predicted more accurately than conventional future LSM or CAP in both the HALO and NIDDK cohorts. Predictive accuracy remained high across follow-up visits, while independent biological validation in both cohorts supported the construct validity of the learned phenotype as a clinically meaningful measure of advanced liver disease.

The results suggest that the choice of prediction target can be as important as the choice of prediction algorithm. Most clinical prognostic models prespecify a marker such as liver stiffness or fibrosis stage and then seek to improve performance by adding predictors or increasing model flexibility. Prediction, however, remains constrained by the intrinsic predictability and measurement properties of that target. In the present study, allowing the clinical prediction model to learn a multivariate target produced a future hepatic state that could be predicted substantially more accurately from baseline information. This strategy is consistent with task-aligned outcome learning, in which the most useful outcome representation may depend on the inferential goal (Strobl, 2026). It does not replace conventional fibrosis endpoints when fibrosis itself is the clinical question, but offers a complementary strategy when the objective is accurate prospective prediction of overall hepatic disease severity.

The phenotype learned by the model was clinically coherent. Platelet count received the greatest weight, followed by bilirubin, alkaline phosphatase, and LSM, whereas CAP, AST, and ALT received little or no weight. Its associations with MRE liver stiffness, histologic fibrosis stage, and evidence of portal hypertension supported the construct validity of the learned composite outcome as a clinically meaningful representation of advanced liver disease, even though these measures were not explicitly targeted during outcome learning. This interpretation was further supported by independent replication in NIDDK, where the phenotype was associated with centrally reviewed histologic fibrosis stage and was significantly higher among patients with imaging evidence of portal hypertension. The predictor model was also highly sparse: only six of the 99 baseline predictor columns received nonzero coefficients, requiring just five routinely collected clinical measurements. Thus, the model provides interpretability on both sides of the prediction task: what clinical state is being predicted and which baseline measurements are used to predict it. We refer to the final prediction model as LIFT-MASLD (Liver Integrated Forecasting Tool for MASLD) and have implemented it as a web-based calculator at https://liftmasld.github.io.

These properties suggest potential applications in clinical monitoring and precision medicine. For example, a patient with modest baseline liver stiffness but low platelet count and elevated bilirubin might have a relatively high predicted LIFT-MASLD value, indicating greater anticipated hepatic disease burden than liver stiffness alone would suggest. Such predictions could complement conventional assessments when monitoring patients longitudinally. More broadly, LIFT-MASLD could be incorporated into a patient digital twin framework integrating computational predictions with experimental measurements from patient-derived liver microphysiological systems (Miedel et al., 2025). Comparing predicted hepatic status with disease characteristics and drug responses measured in patient-derived liver MPS could help connect clinical forecasts to underlying biological mechanisms and support the development of personalized therapeutic strategies.

Several limitations should be considered. First, the prediction model uses a linear predictor structure; nonlinear approaches could potentially capture additional interactions, although the present formulation offers interpretability and sparsity. Second, the analysis included only two subsequent follow-up visits, and longer follow-up will be needed to determine whether the model-defined phenotype correlates with other clinical outcomes such as hepatic decompensation, hepatocellular carcinoma, transplantation, or liver-related mortality. Third, although the model was externally validated in the independent NIDDK cohort, HALO was a single-center cohort that was predominantly White, and additional validation across diverse populations and clinical settings would further establish transportability. Finally, the predictors were primarily routinely available clinical, laboratory, anthropometric, and imaging variables; molecular measurements may provide additional predictive information.

In conclusion, we developed and externally validated a sparse clinical prediction model for longitudinal hepatic status in MASLD. By allowing the prediction target itself to be learned, the model identified a clinically meaningful hepatic phenotype with independent evidence of construct validity. This phenotype was more accurately predicted than conventional future LSM or CAP in both cohorts, using only a small set of routinely collected baseline clinical measurements.
