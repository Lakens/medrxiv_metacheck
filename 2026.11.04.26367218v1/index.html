How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Objective: We test whether data collected from daily passive smartphone sensing and self-reports can predict daily significant psychological distress with meaningful potential clinical utility, comparing across best-in-class machine learning algorithms.

In this prospective cohort study, 169 community-dwelling adults completed baseline surveys followed by 7 days of passive smartphone sensing (accelerometer, GPS, power state) and end-of-day Brief Symptom Inventory-18 (BSI-18) assessments. Cases were defined as days (total n = 1,062) where participants' psychological distress measured with the sex-specific BSI-18 Global Severity Index T-score was ≥63 (prevalence 11.2%). Four classical models (artificial neural network, elastic net, support vector machine [SVM], extreme gradient boosting [XGB]) and four transformers (sensor-only or multimodal; standard or fusion architecture) were tuned, Platt-calibrated and evaluated on held-out participants (210 records, 25 cases) with participant-level bootstrap 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Results: The SVM (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve [AUROC] 0.84; 95% CI 0.71-0.93), the multimodal fusion transformer (0.84; 0.69-0.95) and XGB (0.82; 0.63-0.94) discriminated best; sensor-only transformers performed at chance (0.49-0.50). At the default threshold of 0.50, elastic net logistic regression was the best classifier (sensitivity 0.56, positive predictive value 0.64). At 0.12 (near the outcome prevalence), the SVM detected all cases (negative predictive value 1.00), but positive predictive values were low for all models (0.13-0.26). Decision-curve analysis favored most models over treat-all and treat-none lines at thresholds between 0.05 and 0.25. Baseline survey items, mainly on functional impairment, had the largest feature importance.

Conclusions: Compact classical models performed as well as transformers, which were considerably more computationally intensive. Decision-curve analysis supports further clinical exploration of these tools for triaging daily psychological distress, though our results suggest the relative value of psychosocial assessments and digital data should be carefully analyzed. External validation is needed before clinical use.

Introduction

Psychological distress is a state of emotional suffering that combines symptoms of depression, anxiety, and somatic complaints. It is common and often precedes or accompanies mental disorders, which account for about 15% of years lived with disability worldwide. 1 Many affected people are not identified or treated. A median of 56% of people with depression receive no treatment, 2 and in primary care only 47% of patients with depression are correctly identified. 3 Brief self-report scales, such as the Kessler Psychological Distress Scale 4 and the Brief Symptom Inventory-18 (BSI-18), 5, 6 are reliable screening instruments but require active and repeated completion, limiting continuous monitoring outside clinical settings. There is growing interest in using machine learning and artificial intelligence to advance the assessment of psychological states from both active (self-report) and passive (sensor) data. 7 Digital phenotyping uses data from personal digital devices to measure behavior continuously in daily life. 8 Smartphones record passive signals such as mobility (global positioning system [GPS]), physical activity (accelerometer), and phone use (screen and power state) with minimal effort from the user, 9 complementing repeated self-reports with nearcontinuous behavioral data. Correlational studies have linked these signals to symptoms of depression. 10, 11 However, findings have been mixed: some signals showed contradictory associations across studies, 11 and correlations in a larger sample were weaker than in earlier, smaller studies. 12 Wearable sensor data also contributed little to predicting depression and suicidal ideation compared with daily self-report, 13, 14 and smartphone data alone predicted symptoms poorly until daily survey responses were added. 12 It is therefore unclear whether passive sensor data are informative on their own or mainly in combination with self-report, and whether they have screening utility.

Systematic reviews attribute these gaps partially to small samples, short follow-up periods, and inconsistent measures, 11, 15 with models from small, homogeneous samples often failing to generalize. 16, 17 Analytic choices may also contribute. Most studies use correlation, regression or classical machine learning, which require fixed inputs, so time series are usually reduced to daily or weekly averages; models using the raw time series remain uncommon. 15, 18 Such averages can obscure when and how behavior changed over time. 18 In addition, sensor data are often incomplete, and missingness itself correlates with participant engagement and symptom severity, 19, 20 so imputation may disguise behavioral changes. 18 Many studies also split observations rather than participants for validation, which can greatly overestimate accuracy. 21 Deep sequence models can learn directly from the sensor time series. Transformers 22 use attention mechanisms to model temporal dependencies and have been adapted for time-series data 23 and multimodal fusion. 24 However, transformers typically need large training datasets. 23 In clinical prediction, machine learning has shown no benefit over logistic regression at low risk of bias, 25 and tree-based ensembles often outperform deep networks on medium-sized tabular datasets. 26 Whether the extra complexity of transformers improves prediction at sample sizes typical in mental health research remains unclear. Many studies also report only discrimination, 25 although reporting guidance recommends also evaluating calibration and clinical utility. [27] [28] [29] Knowing which inputs drive a prediction can be as important as accuracy. 30 We used data from a prospective cohort of community-dwelling adults who completed baseline surveys, 7 days of passive smartphone sensing and end-of-day BSI-18 assessments. 31 Our objective was to determine whether new transformer models offer advantages over established classical machine-learning models for predicting daily clinically significant psychological distress from combined active (self-report) and passive (smartphone sensor) data, in terms of overall predictive performance and feature interpretability. To this end, we evaluated four classical models and four transformer configurations for discrimination, calibration, threshold-dependent classification and clinical utility, compared sensor-only with combined sensor and survey input to test whether passive sensor data were informative on their own, and examined which survey and sensor features drove the predictions.

Methods

Overview

This study compares classical machine learning models with transformer architectures for predicting daily psychological distress from combined passive smartphone sensor data and baseline self-report surveys. Transformers were evaluated because they can model temporal dependencies and natively fuse heterogeneous data modalities, capabilities that classical models applied to summary statistics do not offer. Because the binary distress outcome exhibited substantial within-person stability, all data partitioning and bootstrap resampling were performed at the participant level to prevent information leakage. The sections below describe the study sample and data sources, the predictive modeling pipeline, and the evaluation framework. All analyses were implemented in Python, mainly using scikit-learn, XGBoost, and PyTorch.

Study procedures and participants

The sample consisted of 400 Utah-based participants recruited using a stratified random sampling design with two crossed factors-difficulties in emotion regulation and perceived life stress-to balance participants at high and low risk of episodic mental health conditions. 31 Eligibility required age 18 years or older, Utah residency, smartphone ownership, English fluency, and health care through University of Utah Health or Intermountain Health; exclusions included suicidal ideation (past 3 months) or any suicide attempt, active mania or psychosis, or certain violence histories. 31 After completing baseline questionnaires on psychiatric, medical, and psychosocial history, participants entered a 7-day in situ phase, providing continuous passive smartphone sensor data and daily self-report measures. 31 Data were collected between June 2022 and June 2024. Of the 400, 253 provided sensor data; the analysis was restricted to 189 iPhone users by excluding 64 Android users for device consistency.

Study data

Passive smartphone sensor data were collected using the Beiwe Research Platform. 32 Accelerometer data recorded 3-axis acceleration at 10 Hz during 60-second on-cycles alternating with 60-second off-cycles. GPS data recorded latitude, longitude, and altitude, nominally sampled at 1 Hz on a 60-second on / 600-second off cycle. 19 Power state data were event-based, logging screen lock/unlock transitions, charging state, and battery level.

Baseline questionnaire data were collected 4-209 days before the sensor monitoring period. The measures are described in the study protocol 31 and in Supplementary Table 1. Features came from validated measures of emotion regulation (Difficulties in Emotion Regulation Scale [DERS]), 33 perceived stress (Perceived Stress Scale-4 [PSS-4]), 34 depression, anxiety, and stress symptoms (Depression Anxiety Stress Scales [DASS-21]), 35 substance use risk (Tobacco, Alcohol, Prescription Medication, and Other Substance Use Tool [TAPS]), 36 and psychosocial functioning (Life Functioning Questionnaire [LFQ]), 37 as well as questionnaires assessing demographics, clinical history, and medical conditions. 31 Measures were scored following published guidelines; all item-level responses and relevant subscale and total scores were included as features (260 in total).

The sample size was determined by participants meeting inclusion criteria rather than an a priori power calculation. After applying the outcome-based inclusion criterion described below, the final analytic sample comprised 169 participants.

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Utah (IRB #00149365). All participants provided written informed consent, obtained electronically, prior to enrollment. This study is reported in accordance with the STROBE statement 38 and the TRIPOD+AI guideline. 28 The protocol of the parent study has been published; 31 no separate protocol was prepared for this analysis, and it was not registered.

Outcome definition and inclusion criteria

Psychological distress was assessed daily using the BSI-18, 5 administered via an end-of-day selfreport survey. The BSI-18 comprises three six-item subscales-Somatization, Depression, and Anxiety-with each item rated on a 5-point Likert scale (0 = not at all to4 = extremely). When multiple surveys were completed on the same day, only the last was retained, yielding one BSI-18 administration per participant-day.

Following BSI-18 scoring convention, 6, 39 a subscale with at most one missing item was prorated (mean of the available items scaled to six items); otherwise it was invalid. The Global Severity Index (GSI), the sum of the three prorated subscale scores, required all three to be valid. Only records with a valid GSI were retained, yielding 1,062 participant-day records from 169 participants.

GSI scores were then converted to sex-specific, normalized T-scores (mean = 50, standard deviation = 10) using published general-population norms. 40 Psychological caseness was defined as a T-score ≥63, following the threshold recommended by Derogatis. 5 The overall prevalence across the 1,062 participant-day records was 11.2% (male: 12.9%; female: 10.9%).

Predictive task and feature extraction

The modeling objective was to predict daily psychological distress, operationalized as BSI-18 GSI caseness (T-score ≥63), for each participant-day record. Features were extracted from two sources: (1) baseline survey and demographic data collected prior to the monitoring period, and (2) passive sensor data with timestamps strictly preceding the end-of-day survey on each calendar day.

For each participant-day record, the considered window of sensor data was defined as the interval ending at the time of end-of-day survey completion and beginning at either (a) 24 hours prior to the survey, or (b) the time of the previous day's end-of-day survey, whichever was more recent. This prevented double-counting across consecutive records.

The considered window was partitioned into 96 equally spaced 15-minute bins. Within each bin, raw sensor observations were aggregated directly at four temporal resolutions-15minute, 1-hour, 6-hour, and 24-hour-and broadcast onto the shared 96-bin grid. This multiresolution strategy was adopted because the temporal scale relevant to daily psychological distress is not known a priori. Accelerometer features included step count, walking duration, cadence, and acceleration magnitude statistics. GPS features included displacement, path length, and speed.

Of the 169 participants, 156 (92.3%) reported their home address. For these participants, distance-from-home and at-home indicator features were derived from GPS coordinates relative to the reported address. For the remaining participants, these features were treated as missing. Power state features captured screen lock/unlock counts, screen-on and plugged fractions, and battery level. Rate-of-change features were computed as the difference from the nearest prior observed bin. Per-bin data availability indicators were included as features to account for differential sensor coverage across participants and time.

Further, ten daily summary features were derived from accelerometer data over the full considered window, capturing sleep metrics (e.g., sleep period count, longest sleep duration) and activity summaries (e.g., active minutes per observed hour, step rate, peak cadence). Clock time was encoded via sinusoidal transformation, and day of week was encoded as a binary weekend indicator. Baseline survey variables were included after one-hot encoding of nominal categorical variables. The daily summary, weekend, and survey features, henceforth collectively termed static features, were constant within a given participant-day record. All 564 features are defined in Supplementary Table 1.

Data preprocessing

The 1,062 participant-day records were first partitioned into non-overlapping sets using stratified group k-fold splitting, which preserved approximate class prevalence across sets while constraining all records from a given participant to fall exclusively into one set.

A 5-fold outer split was first applied. One fold was designated as the held-out test set (n = 210) and was excluded from all model development. The remaining 852 records underwent a separate 4-fold stratified group split for hyperparameter optimization, with three folds used for training and the held-out fold for validation in each iteration.

For final model retraining, the 852 non-test records were re-split into a training set (n = 730) and a validation set (n = 122) at a ratio of approximately 6:1. This training ratio maximized training data while retaining sufficient validation records for checkpoint selection and calibration.

The preprocessing pipeline comprised three steps, each fitted on the training partition and applied identically to the validation and test partitions to prevent data leakage. First, features that were constant or entirely missing in the training partition were removed from the initial set of 564 features. Second, continuous features were winsorized at ±3 standard deviations from the training mean to limit outlier influence. Third, all features were scaled to the unit interval [0, 1] via min-max normalization to ensure numerical stability.

The preprocessed data were then formatted differently for two model families. For classical machine learning (ML) algorithms, a two-dimensional representation was constructed by mean-aggregating each feature across 96 bins. Median imputation was applied to remaining missing values, with a binary missingness indicator appended for each imputed feature. For transformer models, the data were retained as a three-dimensional array (records × 96 bins × features) with missing values preserved for internal handling by the model's masking mechanism.

Modeling methods

Eight model configurations were evaluated. Four classical ML algorithms operated on the twodimensional (mean-aggregated) representation, and two transformer architectures-a singlestream and a two-stream fusion variant-were each evaluated under two feature sets: unimodal (sensor features only) and multimodal (sensor and survey features combined), yielding four transformer configurations that operated on the three-dimensional temporal representation. All models used weighted binary cross-entropy loss with a cost-sensitive class weight multiplier included as a tunable hyperparameter to address class imbalance.

Classical machine learning models. Elastic net logistic regression (EN), linear support vector machine (SVM), extreme gradient boosting (XGB), and artificial neural network (ANN) were applied to the multimodal two-dimensional representation.

Single-stream standard transformer. A transformer classifier was developed for multivariate time-series data with missing values, processing each participant-day record as a sequence of 96 regularly spaced 15-minute bins. At each bin, observed feature values were projected into a dense embedding via a masked linear layer that gates missing values to zero. Two missingness handling strategies were explored as a hyperparameter (Supplementary Table 2). Temporal position was encoded through Time2Vec. 41 The combined embedding was passed through transformer encoder layers 22 with multi-head self-attention, followed by learned attention pooling to produce a single fixed-length representation per record, which was mapped to a binary logit via a linear classification head. Training used the AdamW optimizer. 42

Two-stream fusion transformer.

The attention-pooling layer can dilute the contribution of time-invariant features. Thus, the fusion architecture routes static and temporal features through separate encoding streams. Temporal features follow the same pipeline as in the single-stream model. Static features are extracted at a single bin position and encoded via an additive maskembedding projection followed by a multilayer perceptron. The two representations are concatenated and passed to a linear classification head for intermediate fusion, trained end-toend. The static stream contained only daily activity summaries and the weekend indicator under the unimodal configuration, and additionally included baseline survey features under the multimodal configuration.

Hyperparameter optimization and model retraining

For each model configuration, hyperparameters were optimized via grid search over the 4-fold internal cross-validation described above. The configuration with the highest mean area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) across the four folds was selected, with ties broken by lower expected calibration error (ECE). The full hyperparameter grids are provided in Supplementary Table 2.

The selected configuration was retrained on the full training set (n = 730) with four random seeds (0, 1, 2, 42), with the validation set (n = 122) used to select the transformer checkpoint with the lowest validation loss over 300 epochs.

Predicted probabilities on the held-out test set were calibrated using Platt scaling, 43 which was chosen as the primary calibration method due to its stability on small validation sets. Isotonic regression and temperature scaling 44 were evaluated as secondary methods and reported in Supplementary Table 3.

Performance measurement

All metrics were computed on the held-out test set for a seed ensemble of each model: the Plattscaled probabilities of the four seed models were averaged for each record, and each metric was computed once. Confidence intervals (95% CIs) were obtained from 2,000 bootstrap samples of the 34 test participants, resampled with replacement together with all their records. Discrimination was assessed with the AUROC and the area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC). Calibration was assessed with the Brier score, 45 log loss, calibration intercept and slope, and ECE. 44 Classification was assessed with sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV; precision), negative predictive value (NPV), F1 score, balanced accuracy, Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC) and Youden index, at thresholds of 0.50 and 0.12, approximating the test-set outcome prevalence (11.9%). Clinical utility was assessed with decision-curve analysis, 27, 46 using net benefit standardized by the outcome prevalence. Complete metric definitions and additional results are reported in Supplementary Table 3.

Feature importance

Feature importance was assessed using SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations), 30 implemented via the GradientSHAP method in the Captum library. 47 Attributions were computed for the multimodal fusion transformer-the primary model of interest-independently for each of the four retrained seed models on the held-out test set and averaged elementwise across seeds to produce a stable importance estimate.

Intraclass correlation analysis

Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs), the proportion of total variance attributable to betweenparticipant differences, were computed for the binary outcome and passive sensor features. For the binary distress outcome, a logistic generalized linear mixed model with a random intercept for participant was fitted and the ICC derived on the latent scale. For continuous sensor features, linear random-intercept models were fitted using restricted maximum likelihood estimation.

Epoch-level features were aggregated to record level by averaging across epochs, preserving records with partial missingness.

Calibration and overall accuracy

After Platt scaling, XGB (0.09) and the multimodal fusion transformer (0.09) had the lowest Brier scores, against 0.10 for a prevalence-only model; the other models scored 0.10-0.11, close to this reference. Calibration slopes were 0.83-1.00 for the multimodal models except the multimodal standard transformer (0.68, overconfident), with wide CIs. The calibration intercepts (-0.16 to -0.93) of the multimodal models showed that they tended to overestimate risk; the CIs excluded the null value only for EN and SVM.

In sensitivity analyses (Supplementary Table 3), temperature scaling improved intercepts but gave underconfident slopes, and isotonic regression changed AUROC by -0.10 to +0.02, with the largest drop for the SVM (0.84 to 0.74).

Classification at two decision thresholds

The choice of threshold changed both the absolute performance and the ranking of the models (Table 2). At 0.50, the models flagged 0-15% of test records, and the unimodal transformers predicted no cases. EN was the best classifier, with the highest sensitivity and PPV of any model: it identified 14 of 25 cases (sensitivity 0.56, specificity 0.96, PPV 0.64). XGB had a similar PPV (0.63) but a lower sensitivity (0.40), and the multimodal standard transformer had the nexthighest sensitivity (0.48) but a lower PPV (0.41). The ANN and the multimodal fusion transformer detected only 2 and 1 cases, respectively. Only EN and XGB had a positive net benefit at this threshold (Figure 2).

At 0.12, the models flagged 32-52% of records. The multimodal models had a sensitivity of 0.72-1.00, a specificity of 0.50-0.70, an NPV of 0.93-1.00 and a PPV of 0.16-0.26, that is, three to five false-positive records per detected case. The SVM identified all 25 cases (NPV 1.00) while flagging 46% of records. EN, the best model at 0.50, was the weakest multimodal model at 0.12, with the lowest sensitivity, specificity, NPV and PPV.

Clinical utility

Figure 2 shows the decision curves. For threshold probabilities between 0.05 and 0.25, the SVM, XGB, ANN and both multimodal transformers had a higher standardized net benefit than both default strategies (treat-all; treat-none). At 0.12, the SVM had the highest standardized net benefit. Above about 0.40, most curves became unstable and often fell below treat-none, consistent with the few cases that received high predicted risks. The unimodal transformers gave no net benefit over treat-none above about 0.15.

Model complexity

EN, SVM and XGB had 1,014-1,576 parameters and trained in ≤1.1 s; the ANN had 782,337 parameters and took 7.0 s, and the transformers had 138,371-948,995 parameters and took 62-134 s (Table 2).

Feature importance

In the multimodal fusion transformer, survey features had the largest SHAP values (absolute values up to about 0.15; Figure 3). Four of the five highest-ranked survey features measured functional impairment summarized from the Life Functioning Questionnaire 37 (difficulty socializing with friends, socializing with family, fulfilling work or school duties, and completing household duties without conflict), and higher reported difficulty increased predicted risk. For the household-duties item, the SHAP values formed four clusters, one for each ordinal response level, and rose with each higher level of difficulty. Full-time student status decreased predicted risk. The most important daily and temporal sensor features had SHAP values mostly within ±0.04. More sustained-activity bouts and active minutes per observed hour were associated with higher predicted risk, and more steps per observed hour, phone use and a higher screen-on fraction with lower predicted risk. Two of the ten sensor features shown measured accelerometer data coverage (daily and 6-hour) rather than behavior.

Discussion

Principal findings

Three findings emerged. First, compact classical models (EN, SVM, XGB) performed as well as the best transformer: the SVM matched its AUROC, EN had the highest AUPRC and XGB the lowest Brier score (Table 2). Second, the transformers that received sensor data alone performed at chance; with survey data added, their AUROC reached that of the classical models. Third, baseline self-report, chiefly functional-impairment items, dominated the predictions, and sensor features contributed small effects of mixed direction (Figure 3). The predictive signal in these data therefore comes mainly from self-report; the added value of sensing was not tested directly.

Classical versus transformer models

The similar performance of compact and deep models agrees with a systematic review that found no benefit of machine learning over logistic regression in clinical prediction comparisons at low risk of bias 25 and with evidence that tree ensembles outperform deep networks on medium-sized tabular datasets. 26 With about 1,000 records and 10⁵-10⁶ parameters, the transformers were heavily overparameterized. The fusion architecture handled this better: it had about one-fifth as many parameters as the standard multimodal transformer and better point estimates for every threshold-independent metric, including calibration slope (1.00 vs 0.68). Even so, it performed no better than the SVM or XGB, with overlapping CIs and 300-1,700× more training time (Table 2). The classical models received each feature as a daily mean without within-day timing; because they still matched the transformers, within-day temporal patterns appear to add little in these data. Providing the classical models with each feature per 15-minute bin would have multiplied the feature count 96-fold relative to about 1,000 records. The transformers could offer more detailed explanations. SHAP can explain individual records for any model, but only the transformers can show when during the day a feature contributed: for the fusion transformer, SHAP values were computed for each 15-minute bin of each record (pooled across bins in Figure 3), whereas the classical models, which received daily means, can be explained only at the whole-day level. At the current data scale, the simpler models are preferable for deployment and transparency.

Contribution of passive sensing

The chance-level performance of the sensor-only transformers is an important negative result. It is consistent with reviews reporting small samples, heterogeneous features and limited reproducibility 11, 15 and with poor generalization across populations and datasets. 16, 17 Several explanations apply: (i) between-person differences in phone and activity habits can hide withinperson changes that indicate distress; (ii) the less-than-24-hour sensor window per record may be too short to capture slow behavioral changes; (iii) only 94 development cases are few for a transformer learning temporal patterns across 96 time bins; and (iv) all sensor data came from the phone, which is not always carried, so activity measures may partly reflect phone-carrying habits. The two data-coverage features among the top ten (Figure 3) may reflect engagement with the phone and the study protocol, 20 which itself relates to symptom scores, 19 rather than distress. For steps per observed hour, high values were associated with lower predicted risk, whereas medium to zero values were indeterminate: they could reflect either low physical activity or a phone that was not being carried. We therefore consider the sensor-level SHAP patterns hypothesis-generating only; SHAP values describe the behavior of the model, 30 not causal effects.

Interpretation of survey predictors

Four of the five most important survey predictors were baseline items on difficulty in social, occupational and household functioning (Figure 3). These items were collected a median of 28 days before the sensing week. The model therefore does not simply re-measure the outcome at the same time point. However, functional impairment is closely related to daily psychological distress, so a strong association between baseline impairment and later distress is expected.

Distress was strongly clustered within participants. The 119 case-days came from 49 of the 169 participants (29%), and in a random-intercept logistic model the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of caseness was 0.64: about two-thirds of the variation in the propensity for distress lay between participants and one-third within participants from day to day. Since most of the variance was driven by between-person differences, this observation supports splitting and bootstrapping the data at the participant level. Most sensor features varied more from day to day than between participants (median ICC of the daily feature values 0.42; range 0.02-1.00): GPS movement features, such as maximum speed, varied mostly within participants (ICC 0.02-0.12), whereas accelerometer sample counts, a measure of data coverage, were largely stable within participants (ICC 0.90-0.92).

Because the survey features are fixed for each participant, they can only separate participants with a high or low tendency to distress. They cannot tell which of a person's 6-7 days are distressed days. Only the sensor features vary from day to day, and those features had little predictive value on their own. Most of the discrimination in the multimodal models is therefore probably between-person: the models identify who is at risk, not when. This is useful for screening, because a few impairment items are much shorter than repeated BSI-18 assessments. However, it limits the claim that the models detect distress passively or in real time.

Choice of threshold and calibration

The two thresholds correspond to two clinical uses, and the best model depended on the use. At 0.50, the multimodal models worked as high-specificity classifiers but missed 44-96% of cases. This threshold treats a false positive as equally harmful as a missed case, which may suit an expensive or burdensome intervention but not screening; accordingly, net benefit at this threshold was small or negative.

At 0.12, the multimodal models worked as high-sensitivity classifiers with high NPV but low PPV. They are therefore best suited to ruling out distress or to triaging people for a low-cost confirmatory step, such as the full BSI-18 or a clinician check-in, at the cost of flagging more than a third of records. Decision-curve analysis supports this use: the better models exceeded both default strategies at thresholds of 0.05-0.25, where a false positive costs much less than a missed case. 27, 46 Because model rankings changed with the threshold, comparisons at a single threshold can mislead: EN's concentration of high scores in a subgroup of clear cases was an advantage at 0.50 but a disadvantage at 0.12. Threshold selection is a clinical decision; until the intended setting is known, threshold-independent metrics and decision curves are more informative than a single confusion matrix.

Calibration is necessary for risk communication and threshold selection. 29 Platt scaling tended to overestimate risk, possibly because it was fitted on only 122 validation records. Recalibration to the local prevalence before deployment is therefore necessary.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths include the multimodal design and the comparison of eight model configurations under a common pipeline, with discrimination, calibration, classification and decision-curve analysis reported together with participant-level bootstrap CIs, as recommended. 28, 45 This study has several limitations. First, the test set contained only 25 cases among 210 participant-day records from 34 participants. The 95% CIs for the AUROC of the multimodal models were therefore 0.22-0.38 wide. We did not test differences between models formally, so we cannot conclude that any multimodal model is better than another. Second, the evaluation used one internal split with no external or temporal validation. Third, each participant contributed several records. The participant-level split prevents identity-based leakage, 21 and the participant-level bootstrap accounts for the correlation between records of the same participant, but with so few test participants the CIs are themselves imprecise. Fourth, the sample was young, mostly female and White, and included many full-time students; it was recruited in one US state with risk-stratified sampling and excluded people with recent suicidal ideation, prior suicide attempts, mania or psychosis; and only iPhone users with sensor data were analyzed. These factors limit generalizability, particularly of PPV, NPV and calibration, because the case prevalence reflects the study design rather than the population. Fifth, caseness was based only on the GSI T-score criterion, a commonly used convention, 48 and not on the full caseness rule (Tscore ≥63 on the GSI or on at least two subscales), 5, 40 and the norms were derived from a 7-day, not a single-day, recall period. Sixth, with 564 candidate features and a small number of events, the sample size is below current recommendations for prediction-model development, 49 which increases the risk of overfitting.

Implications and future work

For clinical and public-health use, our results suggest that a simple, locally recalibrated classical model could serve as a first-line triage aid, and that a few functional-impairment items may carry most of its signal; passive sensing should be added only when it is shown to improve performance. Future work should quantify the incremental value of sensing over self-report, validate the models externally and prospectively in more diverse populations, and investigate within-person models and longer sensor windows.

Conclusions

Compact classical machine-learning models (elastic net, SVM and XGB) identified clinically significant daily psychological distress as well as transformer models with about 90-730 times more parameters. Most of the predictive signal came from baseline survey items, mainly on functional impairment, and passive sensing alone did not discriminate cases. At a prevalencebased threshold, the best models gave high sensitivity and NPV with low PPV, which makes them candidates for low-burden rule-out triage. External validation and a formal test of the added value of sensing are necessary before clinical use.
