Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

The Oldenburg Phrase Test (OLPHRA) is a novel speech-recognition test utilizing a fixed matrix structure of four-word everyday phrases delivered via a synthesized female voice.

This prospective, observational study evaluated the feasibility and clinical utility of the OLPHRA during early rehabilitation and compared it against established clinical standards, namely the Freiburg Monosyllabic Speech Test (FMST) and the Oldenburg Sentence Test (OLSA). Fifteen newly implanted cochlear implant (CI) users were tracked longitudinally at one, two, and four weeks post-activation, and compared to a cohort of fourteen experienced CI users. To index cognitive demand and listening effort during active perception, verbal response times (VRT) and task-evoked pupil dilations were recorded simultaneously. Speechrecognition scores for the newly implanted cohort improved significantly over the four-week period, and did not differ significantly from those of the experienced users at week four, with ceiling effects for both matrix-style tests. However, FMST scores were significantly poorer in both groups. The OLPHRA evoked significantly shorter log-transformed VRTs than the OLSA, indicating faster behavioural processing. In the exploratory pupillometric analysis, a growth curve model revealed a significantly steeper initial pupillary slope for the OLPHRA than for the OLSA, which may indicate faster recruitment of cognitive resources during phrase encoding. Taken together, these findings support the clinical feasibility of the OLPHRA for tracking auditory rehabilitation in the early post-activation period. VRTs and pupil dilation showed diverging patterns that warrant further study.

Introduction

A cochlear implant (CI) is a neural prosthesis that bypasses the damaged hair cells to electrically stimulate the remaining auditory nerve fibres [1] [2] [3], thereby improving speech recognition, social participation, and health-related quality of life [4]. In Germany, a structured multi-stage clinical guideline governs this interdisciplinary rehabilitation framework, combining speech processor programming, auditory training, and audiological evaluations [4, 5]. This timeline comprises initial fitting, follow-up therapy, and long-term aftercare [5].

Audiometric speech-recognition tests serve as the primary metrics for evaluating this clinical progress. In Germany, the Freiburg Monosyllabic Speech Test (FMST) is a clinical standard in the early post-activation period. It consists of open-set monosyllabic words, thus offering little linguistic context [6]. In later rehabilitation stages, matrix tests, such as the Oldenburg Sentence Test (OLSA), are used in adaptive procedures that determine the speech-recognition threshold (SRT) in noise. The OLSA uses a fixed syntactic structure with semantically unpredictable sentences [7]. Recently, [8, 9] developed the Oldenburg Phrase Test (OLPHRA) which retains the matrix layout of the OLSA but uses short everyday phrases embedded in a semantic context, spoken by a synthetic voice. The amount of context in speech material can be quantified by the j-factor [10]. It relates the probability of recognizing a whole speech item (sentence or phrase) to the probability of recognizing its individual word, and can be interpreted as the effective number of independent words per item. The stronger the contextual coupling between words, the lower the j-factor. The OLPHRA has a lower j-factor than the OLSA [9, 11]. Speech-recognition tasks assess more than simple peripheral sensitivity. They also place explicit demands on working memory and cognitive capacities [12]. The Framework for Understanding Effortful Listening (FUEL) defines this allocation of mental resources as listening effort [12]. According to the "Ease of Language Understanding (ELU) Model", optimal speech perception proceeds via rapid, automatic, implicit lexical matching with longterm memory representations [13]. However, when speech is degraded by noise or peripheral pathology, processing shifts to explicit, resource-demanding mechanisms that recruit working memory to reconstruct the message [13]. For CI users, this signal degradation is continuous.

The CI-induced Resource Capture (CIRC) Model suggests that decoding restricted spectro-temporal cues requires sustained central resource allocation [14]. Consequently, CI users can exhibit elevated listening effort even when standard clinical speech-recognition scores appear satisfactory [15].

To quantify these cognitive demands, research applies behavioural and physiological metrics alongside subjective self-reports [16]. Verbal Response Time (VRT), measured within a single-task paradigm, indexes processing speed and cognitive load by capturing the temporal latency between stimulus offset and participants' vocalisation onset alongside behavioural accuracy [17, 18]. VRT is sensitive to listening difficulty. Lower signal-to-noise ratios (SNRs), fluctuating maskers, advanced age, and hearing impairment systematically prolong response latencies [19] [20] [21]. However, questions remain regarding the specific cognitive subdimensions VRT isolates and its exact alignment with subjective self-reports of effort scales.

While [18] did not find a close link between VRT and subjective effort scales, [19] demonstrated significant correlations between the two measures.

Behavioural measures (such as VRT) and physiological indices offer complementary insights into listening effort. Pupillometry provides a time-resolved physiological index of autonomic nervous system arousal during speech processing [22] [23] [24]. Task-evoked pupil dilation reflects continuous resource allocation and is typically evaluated via baseline size, mean pupil dilation, peak amplitude, and peak pupil latency [25]. Task-evoked pupillary responses show a high test-retest reliability across variations in speech-recognition performance [24, 26].

While traditional research focused on peak metrics, other investigations utilized the initial pupillary slope following the stimulus onset as a marker of the immediate auditory processing demand during signal encoding with a steeper slope suggesting higher demands [21, 27, 28]. Furthermore, stimulus architecture directly shapes these physiological measures. Meaningful sentence structures evoke distinct pupil dilation compared to semantically unpredictable matrix tests in CI users, suggesting that test design alters central retrieval strategies [29].

This study aimed to investigate the feasibility and clinical utility of the OLPHRA in CI recipients during the early post-activation period, together with VRTs and pupillometry. We compared speech recognition in the OLPHRA with that in the OLSA and the FMST representing different rehabilitation stages and different amounts of linguistic context. Two cohorts of CI recipients were recruited: newly implanted users (CI-rehab) and experienced users (CI-experienced). The CI-rehab group was measured at three time points (one, two, and four weeks post-activation), whereas the CI-experienced group was measured once. To control for the contribution of the non-test ear across heterogeneous device configurations, a fixed contralateral masking noise was applied. Because accuracy alone does not fully describe speech processing, we simultaneously recorded VRTs and task-evoked pupil dilation. Pupillometric data were analysed with Growth Curve Analysis (GCA), similar to [21]. Owing to substantial data loss, this analysis was exploratory. We hypothesized that: a) The predictable semantic structure of the OLPHRA will yield higher speechrecognition scores, shorter processing latencies (VRTs), and decreased pupillary slope (less processing demand) compared to the unpredictable matrix structure of the OLSA. b) Speech-recognition performance across all test materials will exhibit systematic improvements over the first four weeks of device use for the CI-rehab group.

c) The cognitive load, indexed by VRT and pupillary slopes, will decrease as a function of both longitudinal rehabilitation time and the contextual availability of the speech materials.

Speech-Recognition Tests

To evaluate speech recognition across varying degrees of contextual and lexical complexity, this study used three different speech audiometry materials: FMST, OLSA, and OLPHRA.

The FMST consists of 20 lists of 20 open-set monosyllabic words spoken by a natural male voice to assess suprathreshold speech discrimination [6, 31, 32]. The OLSA utilizes a fixed, five-word syntactical matrix layout (name-verb-numeral-adjective-noun) with a closed-set vocabulary that remains semantically unpredictable ("Nina painted eleven white rings";

German: "Nina malt elf weiße Ringe"; [7, 11, 33]). Its sentences are spoken by a natural male speaker. The test also provides a corresponding speech-shaped masking noise (OLSAnoise) which is generated by a randomised temporal overlay of the speech material [7, 11, 33]. The OLPHRA consists of four-word everyday phrases with semantic context (article-adjectivenoun-infinitive) generated with a synthesized female voice ("to watch the little cat"; German: "die kleine Katze beobachten"). Like the OLSA, it includes a corresponding speech-shaped masking noise (OLPHRAnoise; [8, 9]). The j-factor of the OLSA is 4.29 at -5 dB SNR [7, 8], compared to a maximum of 5 for a five-word sentence. For the OLPHRA, the j-factor is 3.1 at -6 dB SNR, compared to a maximum of 4 for a four-word phrase [8].

Percent-correct hit rates were converted to rationalized arcsine units (RAU) [34] using MATLAB 2022b (MathWorks, Natick, Massachusetts, USA) to analyse speech-recognition performance on a linear scale. This yielded speech-recognition scores in RAU for each speech test, group, and time point.

Experimental Setup

Speech testing took place at the end of a routine clinical appointment. The CI-rehab group was tested following their standard 3-hour rehabilitation session (including audiometric testing, auditory training, and device fitting), whereas the CI-experienced group was tested after their annual 2-hour audiological assessment. The FMST was administered by qualified clinical personnel during the standard clinical routine using an Auritec AT1000 audiometer routed to an Auritec loudspeaker (Auritec, Hamburg, Germany). One test list was presented at 65 dB SPL in quiet in a sound-treated booth, with the loudspeaker at an azimuth of ±45° on the side of the CI and an ear-to-loudspeaker distance of 73 cm. The loudspeaker was calibrated for the participant's position.

The OLSA and OLPHRA were tested inside a separate sound-treated booth. As for the FMST, the loudspeaker was calibrated for the participant's position.

Procedure

The CI-rehab group was measured at three time points (one, two, and four weeks postactivation), whereas the CI-experienced group, serving as a reference cohort, performed the measurement protocol once. To minimize order effects, the order of OLSA and OLPHRA was randomized across participants. At each time point and for each test (OLPHRA and OLSA) participants completed two training lists: one in the best-aided condition (bimodal: with hearing aid; bilateral: with second CI, SSD: with normal-hearing ear) and one with the contralateral masking noise without contralateral device.

These were followed by one measurement list per test in the latter condition (contralateral masking, no contralateral device). Training and measurement lists were randomly selected from the available lists of the respective test. The contralateral masking noise was set to 75 dB SPL to ensure that participants with SSD, low and moderate hearing loss could not hear the sentences or phrases from the loudspeaker with their normal-hearing ear.

The sequence of events within a single trial is shown in Fig 1 (right panel). As in [21], no post-stimulus retention interval was included, allowing for the simultaneous capture of transient pupillary responses and VRTs. Participants were instructed to repeat the sentence or phrase immediately after stimulus offset. The next stimulus started 2-3 s after the participant's vocalisation, so as not to interfere with the normal course of a speech test procedure.

At the end of each measurement list, participants performed a two-alternative forced-choice (2AFC-) task on whether the speech material was synthetic or natural. They also rated their perceived listening effort for that test and time point on a scale from 1 ("no effort"; German: "gar nicht anstrengend") to 5 ("extreme effort"; German: "äußerst anstrengend").

Verbal Response Times (VRT)

To capture behavioural processing speed alongside speech-recognition accuracy, verbal responses were recorded using a Shure SM58 microphone (Shure, Niles, Illinois, USA) positioned directly in front of the participant inside the booth. The sampling rates (f s ) were set to the original rates of the speech material (OLSA: 32 kHz, OLPHRA: 44.1 kHz). VRTs were defined as the time elapsed between stimulus offset and onset of the participant's vocalisation. Vocalisation onset was detected automatically with MATLABs detectSpeech() function.

VRT data preprocessing was executed in MATLAB 2022b. Mean VRTs were calculated for each measurement list and timepoint. To ensure that the timing metrics reflected successful lexical access rather than listening errors or confusion, mean VRTs were calculated using only the trials where at least one word was correctly repeated. If no word was repeated correctly in the measurement list, no VRT could be computed for that list, and the value was treated as missing. Following the methodology of [19], VRT data were log-transformed using the natural logarithm ln, with VRT expressed in ms. No minimum VRT reference threshold was applied because no vocalisation onset occurred during active stimulus presentation. This procedure provided a measure of log-transformed mean VRTs per test and per time point.

Pupillometry

To track continuous cognitive workload during speech perception, pupillometric data were recorded inside the sound-treated booth under regulated ambient lighting conditions (mean illuminance: 30.54 lux; [25, 35]). Participants sat centrally facing a visual display at a viewing distance of approximately 115 cm (see Fig 1, left panel). Eye-tracker control from outside the booth was implemented in MATLAB 2017a using Psychtoolbox Raw pupil data were processed via the pupil_pipeline toolbox [37] in MATLAB 2022b following standard guidelines [25]. Blinks and saccades were identified and linearly interpolated from 50 ms before to 150 ms after the event. The interpolated traces were filtered using a 10 Hz low-pass filter and epoched into 6-s windows. We applied a subtractive baseline correction based on a 500 ms window preceding stimulus onset, and EyeLink recordings in arbitrary units were converted to pupil size in mm with an artificial pupil calibration. Individual trials were averaged per participant, time point and test.

Trials with more than 25% data loss due to artifact rejection were discarded, and participants with fewer than 50% valid trials remaining were excluded from further analysis [25].

Additionally, during the initial phase of data collection, a high-contrast visual cue caused systematic, stimulus-independent pupil elevation and was therefore removed from the experimental protocol. Consequently, the datasets affected by the systematic error and too few valid trials were excluded from the analysis. Of the 15 CI-rehab participants, 13 had valid pupil data at the four-week time point, and only 6 had valid data at all three time points (9/15, 60%, excluded from the longitudinal analysis). Of the 14 CI-experienced participants, 10 had valid data (4/14, 29%, excluded). Because the longitudinal sample (n = 6) was too small, the CI-rehab group was analysed at week four only. The final sample for the pupillometric analysis therefore comprised 23 participants (13 CI-rehab at four weeks, 10 CIexperienced). The analysis is therefore exploratory.

To isolate listening demand during active perception and separate it from motor responses (which can contaminate the signal and complicate interpretation), the pupillometric analysis was restricted to the stimulus presentation window. Consequently, the duration of the presented sentences was taken into account when defining the analysis window. The minimum, maximum, mean, and standard deviation of the sentence and phrase lengths are detailed in Table 2. We defined a conservative analysis window spanning 0 to 1.41 s, corresponding to the shortest overall stimulus duration recorded across speech materials. This physiological processing pipeline yielded the mean pupil dilation time series and its corresponding initial pupillary slope within the defined stimulus window.

Discussion

The primary objective of this study was to investigate the clinical utility and feasibility of the novel OLPHRA speech test in individuals with CI, tracking both newly implanted recipients during their initial rehabilitation phase and long-term, experienced CI-users. Beyond conventional behavioural speech recognition metrics, we utilized VRTs and, in an exploratory analysis, task-evoked pupillometry to capture continuous speech processing dynamics and index listening effort during active speech perception.

Clinical Utility of the OLPHRA in CI patients

Speech-recognition scores for the novel OLPHRA and the established OLSA did not differ significantly, neither in the longitudinal CI-rehab cohort (p = .47) nor in the comparison with the CI-experienced group (p = .69). As the study was not designed to test equivalence, this indicates similar scores but does not show equal performance. This outcome mirrors the findings of [9], who observed no significant differences in speech recognition thresholds when comparing the OLPHRA to synthesized versions of the OLSA and GÖSA in older hearing-impaired populations. While [9] evaluated speech recognition adaptively in noise, the performance in the current study was observed using fixed-level presentations in quiet with a contralateral masking noise. Despite these differing primary metrics, both datasets indicate that the lexical and structural variations between the phrase-based OLPHRA and the sentence-based OLSA do not result in a systematic divergence in raw speech-recognition accuracy.

These performance similarities occurred despite distinct structural and linguistic differences between the materials. The matrix-style OLSA utilizes a 5-word sentence structure (nameverb-numeral-adjective-noun) that is syntactically fixed but semantically unpredictable. In contrast, the OLPHRA consists of short, 4-word phrases with a fixed syntactic structure (article-adjective-noun-infinitive) providing everyday semantic context. This closer semantic coupling is reflected by the OLPHRA's lower j-factor (j = 3.1 at -6 dB SNR) relative to the OLSA sentences (j = 4.29 at -5 dB SNR; [9, 11]), although these values were determined in earlier studies at different SNRs and not in the present sample.

Despite the clearer semantic architecture of the OLPHRA, the lack of a distinct phrase-level performance benefit may reflect long-term familiarity with the OLSA. This explanation is speculative and was not tested here. The OLSA is integrated into German clinical routines; the CI-experienced cohort undergoes repeated OLSA testing during annual evaluations, and many early-stage candidates encounter it during pre-implantation diagnostics. Conversely, the OLPHRA represented entirely novel speech material to all participants, which may have reduced the expected semantic advantage.

The similar scores are unlikely to result from differences in procedural learning during the session. While the OLSA exhibits a known task-specific training effect of approximately 1 to 2 dB SNR [11] or up to 3 dB SNR [45] within the first two test lists, the OLPHRA demonstrates a negligible training effect of approximately 1 dB SNR across initial measurements, despite being completely unfamiliar to the listeners [9]. Because all participants completed two training lists prior to data collection for both tests, any immediate procedural training effects are unlikely to explain the similar scores. Long-term familiarity with the OLSA therefore remains a possible explanation [46], but this cannot be concluded from the present data.

In contrast to the matrix tests, scores for the open-set FMST were significantly poorer across both cohorts, reflecting several known factors. First, as a single-word test, the FMST lacks linguistic or semantic context to support top-down repairs [6]. Second, evaluations indicate that the underlying test lists do not meet contemporary standards for structural, perceptual, and phonemic equivalence [47]. Additionally, linguistic reviews show that approximately 45% of the original 1953 FMST test words have fallen out of contemporary German usage [48]. Combined with uncalibrated presentation levels across historical recording variants [31], these factors reduce the reliability of the FMST as a metric of modern speech perception. Moreover, the FMST also differed from the matrix tests in procedure (one list, no training lists, no contralateral masker, different booth and loudspeaker distance, natural male voice). The lower FMST scores therefore cannot be attributed to the test material alone and do not demonstrate better measurement properties of the OLPHRA. Despite these limitations, the FMST continues to be routinely administered in clinical settings. Therefore, comparing its results with those of the OLPHRA and OLSA is of considerable clinical relevance.

In both groups, the OLPHRA yielded plausible scores that were similar to those of the OLSA. In older adults with hearing loss, [9] reported a steep psychometric function for the OLPHRA, especially in comparison to the FMST, and the test uses speech tokens that resemble everyday language structures [8]. Furthermore, scores increased from week one to week two and from week two to week four, as they did for the OLSA. This shows that the OLPHRA reflects improvements during early rehabilitation, but not that it is more sensitive than the OLSA. The improvement cannot be separated from processor adjustments, growing familiarity with the test situation, and repeated testing, and the test-retest reliability of the OLPHRA was not assessed. Possible advantages of the OLPHRA over the OLSA are its shorter duration, its everyday material, and the shorter VRTs described below.

However, at the four-week time point, speech-recognition scores did not differ significantly from, but tended to be lower than, those of the CI-experienced cohort (p = .09). Ceiling effects were mainly visible in the CI-experienced group, whereas scores in the CI-rehab group were highly variable. The groups also differed in age and device configuration, so this comparison should be interpreted with caution. The maximum accuracy observed in the CIexperienced group suggests that testing in quiet, even with a continuous contralateral masking noise at 75 dB SPL, lacks the difficulty required to track later progress. A ceiling effect indicates limited sensitivity at high-performance levels and does not imply that abilities have converged. Studies with longer follow-up report continued improvement beyond the first weeks of CI use [49], so our conclusions are restricted to the first four weeks in this small sample. To preserve the clinical utility of the test as patient performance and improvement evolve, future protocols could address these ceiling effects by introducing an ipsilateral masking noise (such as test-specific OLnoise or OLPHRAnoise) as recommended in [5] or by transitioning to an adaptive threshold-seeking procedure beyond the initial weeks of device activation.

Behavioural dynamics of listening effort: VRT

While speech-recognition scores did not differ between the test materials, verbal response times revealed differences in processing speed. Overall, VRTs were shorter for the OLPHRA than for the OLSA (ratio 0.83), with the same direction at all time points. After correction for multiple comparisons, the difference was significant at week four only, and it was also present in the week-four comparison with the CI-experienced group. VRTs also decreased with device experience, but the OLPHRA advantage did not change significantly over time (no time × test interaction). This difference may be partially driven by structural variations. The OLPHRA consists of four words, whereas the OLSA contains five words, resulting in a mean phrase duration that is approximately 200 ms shorter for the OLPHRA (see Table 2). Fewer lexical units can reduce working memory demands during stimulus retention and recall, facilitating faster response execution upon stimulus offset [18, 20]. This interpretation aligns with [21], who recorded VRT and pupil dilation simultaneously during an Italian matrix sentence test and observed that more difficult listening conditions (lower SNRs) systematically prolonged VRTs. In this context, the shorter duration and the predictive semantic structure of the OLPHRA may have contributed to reduced processing difficulty, resulting in faster response execution compared to the semantically unpredictable matrix sentences of the OLSA. This is consistent with the lower j-factor of the OLPHRA [8]. However, word count, duration, speaker voice, and semantic context differ between the tests, so the VRT difference cannot be attributed to semantic context alone.

Acoustic properties may also influence these behavioural temporal patterns. The OLPHRA utilizes a synthesized female voice, whereas the OLSA features a natural male speaker.

Speaker gender categorization can be difficult for CI users due to altered fundamental frequency (f0) and vocal tract length cues [50]. Five participants spontaneously reported a preference for the female voice; this observation was not recorded systematically.

Perceptually, subjective ratings indicated that participants did not consistently distinguish the synthetic nature of the speech tokens in the OLPHRA. In the CI-rehab group, only about half of the participants classified each test correctly (8/15 for both), which is close to chance level.

In contrast, the CI-experienced group showed a clearer differentiation between the two, with OLPHRA more often perceived as synthetic and the OLSA more consistently identified as natural male voice. Although [51] reported poorer recognition for synthesized speech in CI users, we found no indication of a handicap for the synthetic OLPHRA in speech-recognition scores or VRTs. Voice and test material cannot be separated in this design.

Subjective listening effort was positively associated with VRT, but the association was mostly not significant. After correction for multiple comparisons, only the OLSA in the CIexperienced group showed a significant correlation (ρ = .61, adjusted p = .044). All other correlations were not significant, although all eight had a positive sign (ρ = .06-.64). This pattern agrees with [19], who observed that longer response latencies went along with higher self-reported effort, and only partly with [18], who found no close link between the two measures. Given the small samples (n = 10-14 per test), an adjusted p value close to .05, and a correction family of only two tests in the CI-experienced group, the single significant result is exploratory and needs replication. Differences in data processing may also contribute to the divergent findings in the literature. [19] z-transformed the log-transformed VRTs and normalised the effort ranks within each participant across several conditions. This removes individual differences in baseline response speed and in the use of the rating scale. Because each measurement interval of the present study comprised only two test conditions, such a within-participant normalisation was not feasible, and between-participant baseline differences remained in the data. These differences may have attenuated the correlations.

Whether VRT directly indexes listening effort or partly reflects other processes, such as lexical retrieval confidence and response hesitation, therefore remains open.

Pupillary Dynamics and Temporal Trajectories of Speech Processing

These pupillometric results are exploratory because of the small sample size (13 CI-rehab participants at week four and 10 CI-experienced participants). In addition to the VRT metric, pupillary dynamics provided a physiological index of listening effort. Utilizing a mixedeffects GCA including both groups with group as a factor, we show that the linear trajectory of pupil dilation was steeper for the OLPHRA than for the OLSA (difference 0.024 mm/s, 95% confidence interval [0.003, 0.045]). No effects involving group were significant, but the difference was visibly larger in the CI-experienced group (Fig 4, Tables 7 and 8), and the test × group interaction for mean pupil dilation was borderline (p = .050). We therefore interpret group differences with caution. Previous studies have suggested that the initial slope leading to maximum pupil expansion represents the speed and intensity of immediate auditory resource allocation during signal encoding [27, 28]. Furthermore, [21] demonstrated that alterations in cognitive demands during speech recognition systematically alter pupillary time courses. Applying this framework to our GCA findings, the steeper slope for the OLPHRA may reflect a faster recruitment of cognitive resources during active signal encoding.

However, mean pupil dilation and slope were strongly correlated (r = .90) and mean dilation was also higher for the OLPHRA, so a higher processing demand cannot be excluded. The result contradicts our hypothesis (a) of a flatter slope for the OLPHRA. Because the OLPHRA is shorter and more predictable in syntactic structure, it may facilitate more efficient allocation of cognitive resources during speech comprehension, but this remains speculative.

The pupil slope and the VRT results point in different directions relative to our hypothesis: the OLPHRA elicited shorter VRTs but a steeper pupil slope.

Whether the two measures capture different aspects of listening effort, as suggested by [21], cannot be concluded from our data, because their relation was not analysed. Finally, the present results show that continuous temporal profiles of the pupillary responses diverge between the two tests. For the OLPHRA, the pupil response peaks near the mean duration of the phrases, a timeline consistent with conventional pupillometric patterns in speech tasks [25, 29]. In contrast, the OLSA response appears to climb steadily to a peak at approximately 3-4 s post-stimulus onset, extending considerably longer into the response window. These peak latencies are visual observations from Fig 4 outside the analysed window and were not tested statistically.

While a later peak is expected for longer stimuli, the longest OLSA sentence ends at 2.77 s, leaving a substantial delay before the maximum pupillary response is reached. Peak timing is also confounded with speech production and response timing, which differ between tests, and the pupil did not return to baseline between trials.

This temporal shift may indicate different processing and recall strategies between the two tasks, extending the findings of [29]. They compared peak pupil dilations across different speech tests in CI users and demonstrated that structural variations between meaningful, everyday sentence material and semantically unpredictable matrix sentences alter physiological effort profiles. In the current study, this architectural difference may affect the temporal resolution of resource allocation. In the matrix-style OLSA, listeners must hold and match incoming acoustic tokens against a constrained, closed-set vocabulary repertoire, which could delay definitive integration until the full matrix is received. In the OLPHRA, listeners process an open-set, but semantically coherent and predictive phrase. Although both tests utilize structured layouts, decoding the highly predictable phrases of the OLPHRA may allow for rapid, immediate semantic reconstruction during stimulus presentation, explaining the highly accelerated initial GCA trajectory observed during active OLPHRA perception.

Methodological Considerations and Limitations

Several methodological factors and limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, testing occurred immediately following regular clinical appointments, which differed in duration and cognitive demand. The CI-rehab group completed a 3-hour rehabilitation session comprising audiometric testing, hearing therapy, and device adjustments, while the CI-experienced group underwent a standard 2-hour annual assessment, including audiometric testing and CI fitting. Consequently, baseline fatigue levels prior to the experiment were likely not comparable between cohorts. The groups also differed in age (the CI-rehab group was older) and in device configuration. Comparisons between groups were cross-sectional and based on small samples, so they do not allow conclusions about equivalence or about the development of individual users. No sample size calculation was performed, and the study was not powered for the group comparisons. Participants were recruited at a single centre, with heterogeneous device configurations; results may not generalise to other CI populations or languages.

For newly implanted participants, the testing environment also represented an unfamiliar auditory and technical situation. Many individuals in the CI-rehab group had experienced prolonged sensory deprivation in the implanted ear prior to surgery, making initial acclimatisation to electrical stimulation a cognitive challenge. Furthermore, during these initial weeks, programming adjustments to the speech processor map are larger and more frequent than the stable settings of long-term users. Managing these changing acoustic boundaries while the non-implanted ear is potentially impaired can introduce fatigue, which may have influenced the longitudinal behavioural data.

A second limitation relates to the heterogeneous device configurations across both cohorts, which included bilateral, bimodal, and SSD fittings (CI-rehab: 11 bimodal, 3 bilateral, 1 SSD; CI-experienced: 3, 8, and 3). Although continuous contralateral masking noise was applied at 75 dB SPL to control the non-test ear, the effective perception of this noise likely varied. We did not verify effective masking, and residual hearing and the device status of the non-test ear were not controlled beyond the protocol. Participants with SSD may have perceived the masking noise as louder or more disruptive than bilaterally or bimodally fitted individuals with residual hearing. Such differences in masking perception can influence cognitive load and speech-recognition performance, as higher noise levels are associated with lower speech-recognition scores, prolonged verbal response times [19, 20] and increased pupil dilation profiles [19, 20, 24, 29, 30]. The masker may thus also have affected the pupil responses directly. In addition, test material, speaker voice, word count, duration, and familiarity differed between OLPHRA and OLSA, and training in the best-aided condition differed from the measurement condition.

Third, the simultaneous recording of VRTs and pupillometric responses required a trial design without a post-stimulus retention interval [21]. This constraint meant that processing load could only be isolated during the active presentation phase, rather than during a distinct retention or recall window. Because the sentences and phrases varied in duration, with the OLSA sentences being longer than the OLPHRA phrases (see Table 2), defining the pupillometric analysis window presented a challenge. To ensure data consistency across all conditions, a window matching the shortest overall stimulus duration (1.41 s) was selected, restricting the formal GCA to the initial encoding slope. Because of data loss, only 23 of 29 participants could be analysed and only 6 CI-rehab participants had valid pupil data at all three time points, so no longitudinal pupil analysis was possible.

Consequently, peak pupil dilation could not be evaluated because the maximum pupillary response consistently shifted into the active listening and response window. Furthermore, differences in mean pupil dilation within this restricted window were small and were mainly present in the CI-experienced group. To approximate standard clinical routines, a fixed response interval was not enforced between trials; instead, the next stimulus was initiated 2 to 3 s after the participant's vocalisation ceased. In several instances, this interval was insufficient to allow the pupil trace to fully trend back to a resting state which may have contributed to elevated baseline pupil sizes. A baseline located on a falling flank of the preceding response, or carry-over between trials, may also have influenced the early slope.

These methodological challenges raise a practical question for future clinical protocols regarding whether both metrics are required simultaneously. The current data do not allow a conclusion on whether VRT and pupil slope reflect the same or different aspects of listening effort. However, implementing a pupillometric setup in standard clinical care demands specialised equipment, controlled lighting environments, and lengthy calibration procedures.

In contrast, tracking VRTs requires only a microphone setup and minimal timing software.

Given its logistical simplicity and sensitivity to test material differences, VRT is easier to implement and may merit further evaluation as a supplementary measure alongside standard speech-recognition scores. Its validity and reliability for clinical use remain to be established.

Conclusion

This study examined the feasibility of the novel OLPHRA in newly fitted and experienced CI users, using speech-recognition scores, verbal response times (VRTs) and, in an exploratory analysis, task-evoked pupil dilation. Consistent with hypothesis (b), speech-recognition scores increased over the first four weeks of device use for all three tests. OLPHRA scores did not differ significantly from OLSA scores, and at week four they did not differ significantly from those of the CI-experienced group, although the CI-rehab group tended to score lower. As the study was not designed to test equivalence, these results indicate similar scores but not equal performance. They suggest that the OLPHRA can be administered in early and long-term CI users and yields scores similar to the OLSA. They do not show greater sensitivity than the OLSA, and its reliability and clinical validity remain to be established. Scores of the CI-experienced group were close to ceiling, so for later follow-up the test should be combined with ipsilateral masking noise or an adaptive procedure. The improvement over four weeks cannot be separated from processor adjustments, growing familiarity with the setting and repeated testing, so conclusions are restricted to the first four weeks in this small sample.

Hypothesis (a) was only partly supported. OLPHRA scores were not higher than OLSA scores, but VRTs were shorter for the OLPHRA overall (ratio 0.83, 95% confidence interval [0.74, 0.93]), with the same direction at all time points and a significant difference at week four. VRTs also decreased with device experience, as expected in hypothesis (c), whereas the OLPHRA advantage did not change significantly over time. Because word count, duration, speaker voice and semantic context differ between the tests, the shorter VRTs cannot be attributed to semantic context alone. In the exploratory pupillometric analysis, the initial pupil slope was steeper for the OLPHRA than for the OLSA, contrary to hypothesis (a). This may reflect faster recruitment of cognitive resources, but a higher processing demand cannot be excluded. Given the small sample, the short analysis window and the incomplete return to baseline between trials, this finding is hypothesis-generating. Pupil slopes could not be analysed longitudinally, so this part of hypothesis (c) remains untested. The relation between VRT and pupil measures was not analysed, so no conclusion can be drawn on whether they capture different aspects of listening effort. VRT is easy to record and may merit further evaluation as a supplementary measure alongside speech-recognition scores. Future studies should use larger samples, adaptive or ipsilateral-noise conditions, comparable stimulus durations and a controlled inter-trial interval.
