Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background: Fentanyl's entrenchment in U.S. West Coast drug markets brought a shift from injecting to smoking illicit opioids. We characterize the next transition: the rise of 'dabbing,' vaporizing fentanyl with equipment sold for cannabis concentrates.

Methods: This mixed-methods study combined ethnographic interviewing and participant observation among people who use fentanyl in California and Oregon (2024-2026) with survey data from a Los Angeles drug checking program. After identifying dabbing ethnographically, we added an optional smoking-method item to the survey.

Results: Of 108 participants who stated a route of administration, 104 (96%) reported smoking; of 93 who named a smoking method, 81 (87%) reported dabbing. All 11 who first named another method and returned later then reported dabbing. Dabbing was reported at 250 of 405 (62%) encounters with a stated route. We observed participants adapting cannabis equipment. They praised dabbing's discretion, reduced stigma, and convenience, and its capacity for more large hits, more quickly; the same speed led some to call it wasteful, because it went through their fentanyl faster than other methods. Participants described rapidly rising tolerance and frequent dabbing-related burns, which we also observed. Some who had injected said dabbing approximated its "rush."

Conclusions: Dabbing has become the dominant way to consume fentanyl among people using drug checking services in Los Angeles, and was observed in every other city where we did fieldwork. Clinicians and programs should ask people how they smoke fentanyl, not only whether they smoke or inject; dabbing may reduce some risks while increasing others.

1. Introduction

The history of drug use in the United States has unfolded through shifts in dominant substances (Jalal et al., 2018), routes of administration (ROA), and subcultures, each carrying distinct harms (Golub & Johnson, 1999). Until recently, overdose deaths had climbed along a smooth exponential curve since 1979 even as the drugs driving them changed repeatedly (Friedman et al., 2026; Jalal et al., 2018). Generational cohorts often define themselves against the visible harms of preceding practices, as when young New Yorkers who watched crack and heroin injection ravage their elders turned instead to cannabis blunts (Golub & Johnson, 1999). ROA is as much a cultural inheritance as a pharmacological choice; drug trends take hold within generational cohorts and are usually recognized only once well underway (Agar & Reisinger, 2001). The displacement of heroin by illicitly manufactured fentanyl is a recent transition, that ushered in a marked shift from injection to smoking (Ciccarone et al., 2024; Karandinos et al., 2024). Most visible in the western United States but increasingly observed elsewhere, smoking opioids instead of injecting them has been associated with lower all-cause mortality among people in substance use treatment (Karandinos et al., 2025) and fewer skin and soft tissue infections (Megerian et al., 2024), and is expected to reduce other injection-associated harms, including bloodborne infections and endocarditis (Karandinos et al., 2026).

Notably, West Coast heroin markets persisted several years after fentanyl had entered East Coast heroin supplies. When fentanyl arrived in the western U.S. in earnest in 2019-2020 (Shover et al., 2020), it appeared largely as a separate product: counterfeit pills called 'Blues' or 'pressies' (Godvin & Gonzalez, 2021). As those pills displaced heroin, many people shifted from injecting heroin to smoking fentanyl on foil. The practice of smoking already had precedent in western opioid markets; black tar heroin can be smoked on foil and, being sticky, is not easily snorted (Karandinos et al., 2024; Mars et al., 2016), therefore smoking heroin had long been more common in the West than in the East (Karandinos et al., 2024).

As fentanyl continues to cement its status as the dominant street opioid, novel practices, specialized equipment, and practices of connoisseurship are emerging (Mars et al., 2024). Much of the transition to smoking has come through user-led innovation (Ciccarone et al., 2024; Mars et al., 2024), supplied by equipment for cannabis concentrates, now broadly available thanks to the legal cannabis market. "Dabbing" exemplifies this phenomenon. Borrowed from cannabis concentrate consumption (Loflin & Earleywine, 2014), dabbing transfers this technology to opioid use: a ceramic, glass, quartz, or metal tip, affixed to a tube-like device or inserted in a standard bong, is heated with a butane torch, often allowed to cool briefly, and touched to the substance, and the person inhales the resulting vapor (Figs. 1a and 2). Ciccarone and colleagues (2024) qualitatively characterized dabbing as an emerging ROA among people who use fentanyl in San Francisco, but its prevalence, geographic spread, and everyday practices have not been described.

Smoking fentanyl using aluminum foil is virtually identical to the decades-old practice of smoking heroin off foil ("chasing the dragon") (Hendriks et al., 2001). Dabbing, by contrast, has no direct precedent in earlier waves of illicit opioid use. Both practices are colloquially and clinically termed "smoking," though both more closely resemble vaporization than combustion (Ondocsin et al., 2025), as does cannabis dabbing (Chen et al., 2025). The person inhales a condensation aerosol, vapor that cools into fine particles off a heated surface (Rabinowitz et al., 2004). Nevertheless, for ease of interpretation, this paper refers to the ROA as "smoking" and the emissions "smoke."

During participant-observation fieldwork among people who use drugs in Southern California, the ethnographic team (principally the first author MG, with contextual information added by other authors) observed the rapid uptake and normalization of dabbing. We then quantified fentanyl route of administration among participants at a large community-based drug checking program run by the study authors, providing, to our knowledge, the first estimate of dabbing prevalence. Combining these data with clinical expertise in pulmonology and burn injury, we characterize the practice, material culture, social meanings, and potential health implications.

2.1. Ethnographic methods

This analysis builds on a related ethnographic program on shifting risk environments among people who use drugs in the US-Mexico border region, conducted in Tijuana and other northern Mexican cities beginning in 2018, under separate approvals (Friedman, Bourgois, et al., 2022; Friedman et al., 2023). All ethnographic data analyzed here were collected from 2024 through November 2026, in fieldwork led by MG, under UCLA IRB-21-0901. Observational fieldwork took place in street scenes, encampments, methadone clinics, vehicles, private residences, and, later, smoke shops. MG first observed fentanyl dabbing in Los Angeles in 2024, then continued to observe dabbing through 2026 in Tijuana (Friedman, Bourgois, et al., 2022), San Diego, Portland, and San Francisco. Smoke shops and vape shops across California and Oregon were toured and their inventory photographed.

MG conducted most of the ethnographic fieldwork, spending more than a year in sustained copresence with people who use fentanyl in California and Oregon, including hundreds of hours observing acquisition, preparation, and consumption. Long-term immersion of this kind reduces social desirability bias and permits comparison of self-reported beliefs and practices with directly observed behavior that cannot be ascertained from epidemiological records alone (Bourgois, 2003; Bourgois & Schonberg, 2009; Friedman, Montero, et al., 2022). All field notes, interviews, and recorded interviews analyzed here were produced by MG; CLS and LC additionally took part in fieldwork and contributed to interpretation as it unfolded. As a community health educator with Drug Checking Los Angeles, LC provided sustained access to community networks, facilitated recruitment for both arms of the study, and, with the drug checking team and community partners, collected the drug checking data. The remaining coauthors contributed clinical expertise, quantitative analysis, or manuscript review and did not generate the ethnographic data analyzed here.

We employed convenience sampling during chance encounters and then snowball sampling as participants introduced us to their wider social networks. We accompanied and informally interviewed participants during routine daily activities. With IRB approval and participant consent, MG audio-recorded 13 conversational interviews in 2025 and 2026 and photographed equipment and practices; dozens more interactions were recorded as field notes. Across fieldwork, MG observed approximately 40 people who use fentanyl and had repeated interactions with 13 key participants, all of whom were interviewed; 9 consented to audio recording (4 were interviewed twice, giving the 13 recorded interviews), and interviews with the other 4 were documented in field notes. Following the photo-ethnographic approach of Bourgois and Schonberg (2009), we treated these photographs as data alongside field notes have reproduced them as figures here. Canva was used to create a diagram to show the mechanics of dabbing.

Analysis was iterative, combining repeated fieldwork with review of field notes and recorded interviews. We developed and refined interpretations through discussions with coauthors, other ethnographers, and community health educators at Drug Checking Los Angeles. We triangulated the perspectives of people who dab, people who smoke on foil, people who inject, and people who sell drugs. Fieldwork continued until we reached saturation on the core practices described below. All names used here are pseudonyms, and identifying details have been altered. To protect confidentiality, accounts from a single individual may appear under more than one pseudonym, and quotes are attributed by name only when continuity requires it. Not everyone engaged in the ethnography participated in drug checking.

2.1.1. Positionality

MG has worked in overdose prevention since 2020, beginning at PrevenCasa in Tijuana, and has administered naloxone to people experiencing overdose since 2011. She has lost people to opioid overdose and is formerly incarcerated, which she discusses publicly, including on social media. MG is a white, working-class woman from the West Coast, and is queer. At six feet tall with a masculine presentation, she was able to relate to men and to feel safe in settings that many women would not enter alone. Her presentation did not fully remove gender dynamics: on a few occasions, the woman in a male-female couple accused her of trying to steal her partner, an explanation that made more sense to them than that MG was earnestly interested in such granular details of drug culture.

MG was occasionally accused of being an undercover police officer. In one instance, the evidence offered was her clean shoes: brand-new, bright white Air Force Ones, a recognized marker of undercover officers trying not to look like police. Not wanting to flaunt a visual marker of her economic privilege, she never wore clean shoes in the field again. In another, a couple suspected her because she could produce drug-checking results and tell them what was in their supply, a capability historically available only to law enforcement. Others, unable to believe that a researcher would take such detailed interest in drug practices, wondered aloud whether she was a police officer, then reasoned that no officer would behave this way, and found the real explanation, that this was academic research, harder to believe still. Because MG is publicly open about her incarceration, participants could verify her account, which usually dispelled the suspicion. That disclosure generated rapport and mutual vulnerability; we treat such enhanced rapport as an ethical issue to be managed rather than a methodological advantage (Harris, 2015).

2.2. Drug checking survey methods

Drug Checking Los Angeles operates anonymous, drop-in services at four syringe services programs across Los Angeles County, using portable Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy, immunoassay test strips, and laboratory-based confirmatory testing. Participants who brought samples for point-of-service analysis completed a brief intake survey. The survey asked what the sample was bought as, what the participant expected it to contain, and whether they had tried it, and recorded an anonymous code, built from partial personal details, that links visits. Staff entered a standard placeholder code when a participant declined to give one, the sample's origin was unknown, or it was brought on someone else's behalf; these encounters are excluded from the count of unique participants and cannot be linked across visits.

Participants who brought a sample acquired as fentanyl and had tried it before checking were asked, in an optional item, how they had used that sample; answers describe that sample only, not every route a person uses. A smoking-method question was added later, after changes were observed ethnographically. Participants who reported smoking a sample bought as or believed to be fentanyl could report foil, a bubble pipe/"pookie," a dabber/nectar collector, or another method. Multiple routes and methods could be endorsed. A recorded route response, including an explicit decline, defined the descriptive cohort; encounters without any route response were excluded. Decline-only responses were excluded from route-prevalence denominators. One response marking both "declined" and "other" was retained as a stated other route. Placeholder codes contributed to encounter-level estimates only.

Participant-level prevalence counts distinct people rather than encounters. if they reported the route or smoking method at any eligible encounter. Route-prevalence denominators include participants or encounters with an actual stated route. Dabbing estimates among smokers retain smoking reports without a named method; estimates specifically among smoking-method respondents include only those who named a method. We separately examined repeated dabbing reports on distinct calendar dates and subsequent dabbing reports among participants whose first answered smoking-method question named another method and who returned on a later date. First answered method does not necessarily mean first service visit or first use of that method. Percentages are rounded to whole numbers, and all denominators are stated explicitly. The quantitative and drug checking methodologies were approved by the University of California, Los Angeles Institutional Review Board (IRB-22-0760 and IRB-22-1273) and some aspects of the work were considered exempt from review as public health surveillance.

2.3. Terminology

In this paper, route of administration (ROA) refers to the broad categories recorded in the survey: smoking, injection, insufflation (snorting), and oral ingestion. The ways fentanyl is smoked (dabbing, foil, and bubble pipes) are referred to as smoking methods. This convention follows the structure of the survey (Table 1) and keeps the quantitative and ethnographic findings comparable. It is not a claim that dabbing is simply a variant of smoking; we consider dabbing a discrete, novel route of fentanyl administration (Section 4).

There is no accepted taxonomy of the ways fentanyl is smoked. This paper uses "dabbing" as the umbrella term for heating fentanyl via an intermediary device and inhaling the vapor. Smoke shops sell the devices under several names, including dabbers, dab straws, and nectar collectors; people who use fentanyl used these interchangeably. A "dabber" can refer to the device, alternatively called a "dab rig," yet a "dabber" can also mean "someone who dabs.". Tips refer to the interchangeable pieces meant to be heated that are inserted into a dab straw, though with onepiece glass or ceramic dabbers, it reverts to the plain English definition of the very end of the device, which is still the portion that is heated. Smoking through a bong or bubbler is classed here as dabbing because their use for fentanyl requires a dabbing-specific tip -a dab nail, "banger" (glass cup-shaped bong insert), or similar attachment meant to be heated directly first, to which the fentanyl is then applied at a separate, later step (Figs. 1b-c and 3).

Consequently, free-text "other method" answers naming a bong, vape, or crack pipe were coded as dabbing because an intermediary is heated, then applied to the fentanyl. When crack pipes are used to consume fentanyl, the only feasible way to do so is to use them as dab straws. Heating fentanyl with an open flame, as done for crack, would incinerate it before the vapor could be consumed.

We did not consider "oil burner" bubble pipes to be dabbing, because the fentanyl itself is heated with flame, albeit through the barrier of the glass bubble. Hammer pipes were not observed, but would represent a gray area, as they can be used in either way. In this text, when referring to pipes, we mean the ones sold under the euphemism "oil burner," bubble-style glass pipes known colloquially as "pookies."

We distinguish a hit, a single inhalation, from a dose, the amount of fentanyl a person means to consume in one session, which may take one hit or many; with injection, the two are one and the same. The dose is what is in the syringe. With smoking, they become distinct, though sometimes overlapping, concepts. How much of a dose actually reaches the person depends on both technique and equipment.

3.2. Ethnographic results

>Ethnographic results

3.2.1. From foil to dabs: the emergence of a practice

Foil was the dominant fentanyl smoking method in earlier fieldwork and remained commonplace when MG resumed fieldwork in Los Angeles in 2024 and first observed dabbing there; when and where people who use fentanyl first began dabbing is unknown.

Participants typically encountered dabbing through demonstration within their networks, often by sellers. Jack, a Southern California resident in his 30s, first saw it when he went to buy a gram from a dealer advertising openly on Craigslist: "He handed me this chunk that looked like plastic -it had this melted plastic yellow sheen on it. And I was like, 'Bro, what is this?' And he was like, 'Chill out, I just took a dab hit off it.' And I was like, 'You did a what?' … He torched up the nail and let me take a hit off of it. And I was like, holy shit." Adoption was not instantaneous. Jack described 'a psychological affinity to foil' built from repetition; his first solo attempt with a glass nectar collector and $4 torch lighter produced 'such little smoke' that he returned to foil. The same seller later handed him a full-sized torch: 'As soon as I started using that blue torch he gave me, I understood.' With sufficient heat, the practice transformed: 'I blew out this giant plume of white smoke… it was like five or ten hits from foil in one pass.' It was 'life-changing… I never went back.' Dabbing spread through self-teaching and increasingly through shared knowledge: "I didn't know very many other people that were doing it, so I just had a lot of trial and error, buying stuff at smoke shops -how do you deal with it when your nectar clogs? If you run out of dope, how do you scrape it? … I figured all that stuff out and just kept iterating. And as time went on, I saw more and more people dabbing… I'd start to recognize the signs. When I met other friends who also dabbed, we were swapping this knowledge back and forth."

Other trajectories ran through the exhaustion of alternatives. Alex, who had injected for years, switched to foil when his 'veins gave out' -but hated it, and kept attempting to inject anyway. Once someone showed him how to dab, he never returned to either. His partner Jane was blunter about foil: "Oh my god, no. I hated foil. Even at the time I hated foil." -Jane, asked whether she would ever go back to foil; field note, May 2026 Both Alex and Jane, who had moved from the East Coast, had injected daily, and both said they had not injected at all in the six months since they began dabbing. For Jack, dabbing had eliminated injection's rationale: "You got to find a vein, you got to have syringes, you got to poke yourself… when you could just take a fat dab rip. So what is the value add to injection?" Across the fieldwork period, nearly all individuals in the network who tried dabbing with a fullsized torch went on to adopt it as their preferred smoking method, although some survey participants reported more than one smoking method. Most of those still using foil had either not tried dabbing or had tried it only with a cheap torch lighter, as Jack first did. For some, dabbing's startup cost was off-putting, and people who did not dab tended to prefer bubble pipes, sold three for $5 across Los Angeles and given out by syringe services programs, though they rarely lasted more than a few days. Dabbers can last for months, but butane is a recurring expense.

Among people encountered during fieldwork, most preferred dabbing to pipes, and pipes to foil, while accessibility ran in the opposite direction: foil was cheapest and most readily available, followed by pipes, and dabbing required a larger initial investment in equipment. Taking up dabbing meant adding it to a repertoire, not abandoning other methods; smoking method was fluid and contextual. Because dabbing goes through fentanyl faster (Sections 3.2.3 and 3.2.5), even people who preferred it rotated methods as money allowed, dabbing when flush and switching to an oil burner to conserve. In the survey, two in five participants who reported dabbing (34 of 81, 42%) also reported another smoking method: foil (28, 35%), an oil burner (20, 25%), or both. Among participants who answered the smoking-method question on at least two distinct dates, 21 of 33 (64%) reported a different combination of methods on different dates, whereas only 4 of 37 (11%) who answered the route question on at least two dates reported a different route or combination of routes across their visits.

3.2.2. Artisanal assembly and the material culture of dab rigs

We observed elaborate customization among people who dab, including dab rigs made from bongs, bubblers, and custom-built apparatuses (Fig. 1b-d). Devices are most often assembled from a body and a tip, each in a range of materials, shapes, and sizes (Fig. 2). Tips are ceramic, glass, quartz, or metal and are heated directly; bodies are glass, silicone, or combinations thereof, which interrupt conduction and remain cool enough to handle. One-piece ceramic dab straws were also used (Fig. 1a, d), and crack pipes given out as safer smoking equipment were repurposed as dab straws.

One-piece glass dabbers, distributed by at least two overdose-prevention organizations in the region, were not considered ideal because fentanyl's sedation can cause people to drop things, and a dropped glass dabber shatters: "You take a fat rip, it kicks in, and you nod out and drop your dabber on the ground," one participant explained. "That's going to happen a million times a day." Glass also cools faster than other materials, so less drug can be consumed per hit. Silicone bodies were more commonly observed, including small silicone bongs. A glass dabberstylized as a black bear is displayed alongside silicone devices and firearm-shaped dab straws and torches illustrate this range of materials and novelty forms at retail (Fig. 4a-b) Glass cannot stretch to grip a tip the way silicone does, so glass tips are held in glass nectar collectors with plastic clips (Fig. 4c). Many participants preferred ceramic tips, describing them as staying hotter longer. Although many store-bought devices ship with metal tips, these were rarely observed in use: "The metal ones taste bad… It's cheap imported [metal]. I don't trust this metal. I don't want to superheat it and then inhale it." An Amazon listing for ceramic tips used both euphemistic sandblasting terminology and the insider slang of "nectar collector" in its product title, unable to sell cannabis dabbing equipment openly due to Amazon's terms of service, which didn't stop several participants from purchasing their equipment from the site. . Torches cost from a few dollars to approximately $50; tips, dab straws, nectar collectors, and assembled devices $5-35; and electronic battery-heated dab rigs $30-$300 or more. Prices were posted at the smoke shops toured during fieldwork and on Amazon and other online retailers, 2024-2026. Torch lighters at retail are shown in Fig. 5. Devices bought assembled are often customized further -the stock tip swapped out, or built from store-bought tips inserted into repurposed mouthpieces, such as the red rubber mouthpieces syringe services programs hand out to reduce disease transmission from pipe sharing. Equipment cost also shaped method choice: "If you just spent your last 20 bucks on a bag, you're not going to want to spend" on equipment, whereas foil "you can pretty much get for free, anywhere, anytime"; several participants framed even their occasional continued use of foil as something poverty imposed on them, not something they preferred. Butane became more widely available over the study period and is now a common sight at corner stores and even chain convenience stores, e.g. "7-11". Empty butane canisters have become part of the milieu of urban detritus, though not nearly as common as crunched-up burnt foils had once been.

Electronic dab rigs, vaporizers, and e-nails were seen more often in recent fieldwork, but, being expensive, only among people with more access to resources. Street fentanyl is typically cut with bulking agents and other additives, with a mean fentanyl purity of 12.5% in our previously published study; therefore, 87.5% by mass of product sold as fentanyl is actually bulking agents and other compounds (Shover, Koncsol, et al., 2025; Godvin et al., 2026). Those impurities clogged devices designed for cannabis concentrates; one participant would not risk returning a device to the store due to the incriminating residue, though it left him out $200 and broke within hours of purchase.

Fentanyl is usually held and dabbed from a small silicone container (a "slick," a term borrowed from cannabis dabbing), which people held in their fingers and brought up to the heated tip to shield their fingers from burns, like an inverted thimble; the slick also catches any fentanyl that melts or breaks off and, sold with a lid, doubles as a sealable container that does not look incriminating. Some people skipped the slick and held the chunk in their fingertips, which caused mild burns when the hot tip grazed an adjacent finger (Section 3.2.6). Others brought the fentanyl to the heated element instead, dumping it onto the hot surface or melting it onto a metal spoon that is then turned over and pressed down onto a dab nail sticking out of a bong (Figs. 3b and 6a).

Retail supply has expanded in step with demand, and participants experienced the change as a rapid cultural shift, even as foil persisted (Section 3.1): "Dabbers are infinitely customizable. All my friends are dabbing [now] … they'll go from not knowing how to dab to, the next time I go over there, they have these wicked custom-built rigs. People are modifying bongs, melting a gram or two into a tablespoon and then touching the tablespoon to the heated dab nail, giant threefooters, just DIY -and everybody's got their preferences now… It's generated this insane cultural shift." -Jack Alex owned at least eight customized rigs. His eyes lit with pride as he showed visitors his latest "Frankenstein" build. This artisanal diversity, sustained by continual peer exchange of parts and know-how, constitutes a visible, durable material culture among people who dab fentanyl.

3.2.3. Technique, hit size, and titration

Participants typically heated the tip with the open flame of a butane torch for 10-30 seconds and often let it cool, if only briefly, before touching it to the fentanyl. An ordinary cigarette lighter cannot heat ceramic sufficiently over a wide enough area. Flames range from under half an inch to nearly a foot; larger flames heat tips faster but burn more butane, cause more accidental burns, and are less portable. They also produce more noise, and discretion has two competing needs, speed and quiet. A quiet torch has a small flame that struggles to heat a 14 mm nail, and participants weighed these factors against one another. The decisive variable is reaching adequate temperature: as one participant put it, "You really had to get it super white hot." A water-pipe setup with a ceramic dab nail is shown in Fig. 1b.

Participants described longer inhalations and more concentrated smoke using dabbing than with foil. People who bought several days' worth of fentanyl supply at a time would often dab off one large chunk. The largest observed being dabbed off during fieldwork was a twenty five-gram rock. How much each draw consumed was hard to judge. With the tip against the fentanyl, nobody can see how much is vaporizing, and the only running measure is the shrinking chunk, sometimes smoked off for days or weeks at a stretch (Fig. 7). One participant called it "a blunt force instrument": "You can't exactly titrate your dose like you can on foil… But holy hell, can you get some fat rips off it."

The large inhaled hit was experienced as replicating the rush of injection (Jack: "it almost reminded me of shooting up… you took in such a large amount that you would immediately feel it going through your body"), echoing accounts from San Francisco (Ciccarone et al., 2024). Taylor estimated one large hit at 100-200 milligrams of total drug product ("a point, maybe two points"), while acknowledging the unknowns: "How much of that is getting trapped in the dabber and how much is actually converting to smoke that you inhale? I don't know." Because batches varied in how "harsh" they were, the feel of smoke in the lungs was an unreliable guide to how much had been taken.

Dabbing can also be done quickly, permitting large hits taken in quick succession: "you could smoke a half gram in a couple hits, just in a few minutes." Speed cut both ways: it returned hours of the day once lost to foil ("rip, rip, rip, go on about your life"), but with "not really a satiation point," participants described tolerance climbing fast, "up to doing many grams a day that way." As one participant put it, "Dabbing is a monster… it's a double-edged sword, because then your tolerance goes up so much, so fast." Participants had strategies for modulating the size of each hit: tips with less surface area, tips made of something other than ceramic, less fentanyl in the slick, and touching less of it with the heated tip; some made only the briefest taps with the very corner of the nail to conserve. Heat management was a trade-off: "You can't get it too hot, because then you're degrading the fentanyl. But if you don't get it too hot, then the device cools so quickly you can't take a big draw." Heating times varied with tip material and torch strength, so participants had no way to know a tip's temperature. Visible red heat was treated as the only reliable sign that it was hot enough, and many also understood it to be too hot: Alex and Jane both heated the ceramic to incandescence and then let it cool for several seconds before touching it to the fentanyl, because at full heat it "burns up the fentanyl." Styles ranged from precise to maximal. Alex favored small, precise hits at medium heat. Jane heated the ceramic nail of a plain silicone dab straw until it began to glow red, set the torch down, picked up the slick holding her chunk, put the other end of the straw in her mouth, and brought the fentanyl up to the tip, gripping the slick between thumb and index finger. The glow had mostly subsided in the few seconds it took to make contact. She inhaled until her lungs were full, tapping the nail rhythmically against the rock as wisps of smoke escaped from the sides.

Whereas people once portioned a small dose of fentanyl onto foil, people who dab often smoke off a large chunk, however it came from the seller. The form of the supply interacts with the method: loose powder is hard to dab, because it can be drawn through the straw before it vaporizes, leaving a mouthful of bitter powder. Swallowed fentanyl has substantial first-pass metabolism, with roughly 30% bioavailability and a delayed peak (Lötsch et al., 2013; Streisand et al., 1991); people counted powder in the mouth as wasted, although a swallowed dose could add to smoked doses after a delay. This has created a market preference for fentanyl "rocks" or "chunks" (Fig. 8a), which are also seen as less likely to have been adulterated. When loose powder circulated, participants pressed it back into chunks, bound it together with water, carefully melted it onto the slick (sometimes singeing the silicone), or heated it on a metal spoon from below, which reminded one participant of the days when every spoon in their home was bent and charred from preparing heroin injections.

3.2.4. Discretion, mobility, and stigma management

Participants generally valued dabbing's discretion and convenience outdoors. Torch flames stood up to wind, and the closed path kept smoke from blowing away before it could be inhaled, unlike foil, which could require shielding both lighter and smoke from the wind by conspicuously covering one's head with a towel or blanket. As one participant put it, "With dabbing there's none of that. I can sit outside in wind, torch up my nail -the torch is such high pressure it stays on even through windy conditions -and then you touch the nail directly to your stuff, so there's not a gap of air where wind could get in."

Dabbing is generally less conspicuous than smoking off foil and can be done in a wider range of settings. "You can just be sitting there all nonchalant, fire up the torch, and hit the dab nail sitting on your lap without your head bent over… really quick, put it up to your mouth, tap, tap, tap, and you're done," one participant explained. "That's protective of getting the cops called on you, or getting busted." Several described foil as the defining public image of the current era: slow, legible to police and passersby, and, because sessions stretch across hours, lending the appearance that drug use had "skyrocketed" even if it had not.

Buying dabbing equipment, ostensibly made for a legal substance, drew less stigma from store employees than buying syringes or glass pipes: "People knew that those little glass roses were actually crack pipes, and that an oil burner is actually a meth pipe. But with dabs, people actually think you're dabbing weed… You don't feel as dirty when you go to the store to buy it." Because smoke-shop staff assumed customers were smoking cannabis, Sam could discuss tools and techniques openly, which he contrasted with injection expertise, long "locked away, relegated to the shadows." If a family member or police officer discovered their equipment, participants could lean on the social acceptability of cannabis to explain it away without disclosing fentanyl use, which remains heavily criminalized.

Dabbing's discretion also sustained employment. Several participants in this network held regular jobs and used fentanyl several times a day. Fentanyl's short duration of action imposes a redosing rhythm that prior opioid eras did not: as one participant put it, "You could go a whole shift of work without doing a shot of heroin, but fetty is a different beast"; these participants smoked at least every few hours. Some used the bathroom; others dabbed in their cars at work, with strategies to further mitigate risk: "Just bring a [nicotine] vape to your mouth when you blow out the smoke." -Jane, on dabbing in the car; field note Chris taped his dabber to a hookah hose, making a seal, so he could dab while driving long distances: he could hold his dabber in one hand, the steering wheel in the other, and the hookah mouthpiece in his mouth, driving down the road without making movements suspicious to other drivers. Participants acknowledged the risk of dabbing in public and said they did it as little as possible: "but what's the alternative? I have to go to work, you know. I do what I gotta do."

3.2.5. Folk pharmacology and social meanings

Participants articulated a folk pharmacology in which dabbing recovers a sensation fentanyl otherwise withholds. Sam highlighted dabbing's ability to restore a perceptible "rush": "Fentanyl is kind of a shit drug. It's not euphoric, it's not warm and fuzzy. Half the time you can't even perceive that you're high. All the time you see people nodding out -you're like, 'Bro, you should sit down,' and they'll be like, 'What? I'm not even high,' and then nod out again. They're not lying… It's a lights-out drug -there is nothing between zero and nine, then ten you're asleep. But dabbing was able to recreate [the rush] because of the rapid onset of such a high dose. You could actually feel something." Sam was not alone. Participants in this network lamented the loss of heroin's long, warm euphoria, an hours-long reminder that you had consumed opioids. Opinions diverged on whether fentanyl was a lost cause entirely, or whether, in the right circumstances, it could still deliver the fleeting "rush" once thought possible only by injecting. No one said that dabbing fentanyl created a rush like heroin. Several said that a big enough dab hit absolutely produced a rush like injecting, and that belief kept some people innovating, perpetually chasing bigger hits.

Corey showed where the chase leads. In May 2024 we watched him sitting on his bed, casually heating a ceramic nail in a 3-foot bong with a full-sized blowtorch attached to a fuel canister (a comparable setup is shown in Fig. 8b). He tapped a several-gram chunk to the nail, inhaled, and slumped down the wall. We feared an overdose until he exhaled a thick cloud and let out a "now that's what I'm talking about," his voice a lower octave than it had been just moments before. A fat enough hit, he said, gave a rush almost like injecting. He had started on a regular dab straw and built bigger setups as tolerance grew, which took money, both to buy supplies and to buy enough fentanyl to have a tolerance that high in the first place. His cycle was common: people impressed by a rig's capacity soon found its biggest hit too small as tolerance caught up with each upgrade. Many concluded that a single hit has a ceiling and simply took more; we watched people take up to a dozen large hits and remain awake and alert. Smoke wisped from the bong; big hits meant waste.

Waste itself was contested. The word did double duty. It named material loss: fentanyl drifting off as smoke, degrading on an overheated nail, or left clinging inside the dab rig. Waste also named speed. One dab could feel like several hits from foil, but people seldom stopped at one, and with bigger hits, more of them, closer together (Section 3.2.3), a supply went much faster than on foil. Dabbing was not frugal, yet people without money to spare had to make a supply last; in November 2026, one participant described balancing the two: "I take small taps also very lightly to conserve and I actually rotate between dabbing and the oil burner to conserve since the stuff is so expensive and dabbing goes through it faster." Alex and Jane disagreed about which waste counted: Field note, April 2026: When Jane finishes, she sets her dabber down on a silicone mat, still emitting smoke. Alex is horrified -"You're wasting it!" and picks it up, inhaling the smoke still emanating from it. Jane is unmoved: "It's a lot of time that you waste trying to get every last drop. Not like it makes a difference anyway. It's never enough." Nearly everyone we observed valued the residue ("reclaim"), even those whose money made it unlikely they would ever need to smoke theirs. Residue built up inside ceramic tips, sometimes narrowing or blocking the opening, and it was often stored, a reserve against withdrawal that one participant likened to a fentanyl savings bank, which made empty dabbers targets of theft too. People had various ways of getting at the reclaim in dab rigs and dab-mounted bongs; one evaporated the water from his dab bong and smoked the gummy residue left behind (Fig. 6b). "I know I'm gutter as f***," he joked, remarking on the foul taste, "but I can't just throw drugs away. That's drug abuse!" Fine-grained knowledge of materials, temperatures, doses, and bodily sensation marks competence and belonging, consistent with the early signs of fentanyl connoisseurship described in San Francisco (Mars et al., 2024). Hierarchies of perceived risk attach to methods: people who dab are often seen within this community as less likely to overdose than those who smoke on foil, a view people who dab endorsed themselves ("I wouldn't want somebody who only smokes on foil to take a fat rip off my dabber"), and the distinction extends to sellers, who read method as a tolerance signal: "Some of those plugs won't even sell to you. If you're still on [foil], you're seen as an overdose risk -especially if you're trying to cold cop and buy from strangers, and they're worried that you're gonna die and bring them heat. But if you whip out your dabber and take a fat dab, they're like, 'Oh, shit, look at that.' It proves that your tolerance is pretty high."

This perception ran in one direction: people who smoked only on foil did not describe dabbing as riskier or their own hits as smaller, having no basis for comparison without dabbing themselves, and those who did compare rarely returned to identifying as "foil smokers." Participants described how dabbing both escalates tolerance and becomes a visual marker of it to others. Participants also recognized tolerance losses after breaks: "Even if you take a few days off, you can noticeably feel your tolerance go down." When people returned to use after incarceration or a period of abstinence, others advised them not to dab. Stylistic variation itself carried social meaning. For Alex, dabbing is a ritual he visibly savorsthe assembly, the heat management, the hardware -and his pride in his equipment is a form of expertise display. Jane "dabs to get high": a plain silicone dab straw with a ceramic nail, "no frills, nothing special… reliable, cheap." She rolls her eyes when he shows off a new build and hits her simple dab straw again. The contrast -technique and equipment as identity for some, bare delivery system for others -parallels the stratified connoisseurship described among people smoking fentanyl in San Francisco (Mars et al., 2024).

3.2.6. Burns, fires, and user-generated mitigation

Burns and fires were a recurring concern among participants, which has also been noted in recent clinical data from the West Coast (Englander et al., 2026); one participant observed that "everybody I know that dabs has burnt themselves." Even a brief loss of consciousness while holding a lit butane torch can ignite a fire. The most commonly reported burns came from the still-hot tip contacting skin or objects: participants described "perfect circular burns from the tip" on hands, arms, and legs (Fig. 9), and dabbers that had melted bedding and car seats. Torch burns were considered less dangerous than tip burns because the flame's heat is felt approaching the skin ("as soon as you get close, it wakes you up"), the illusion of control, while a hot ceramic nail was seen as striking silently, almost at random. Fentanyl residue within the tip also has a tendency to liquefy and drip out; participants described this liquid as "lava," and it caused severe burns when it dripped onto skin, instantly cooling and solidifying -"like napalm getting on you," lamented a participant who described having to pull off a stiff, candle wax-like dollop of residue, watching the first couple layers of their skin come up with it, and then having to decide whether or not the resulting debris should be smoked or thrown away. "This was late summer of 2024. I'm in shorts, and because the dope's so shitty, you're just firing through it -don't care about waste. I took a hit and went to put the dabber down, and this hot lava -melted fetty -dripped out the back of my dabber onto my bare thigh. And, like, instantly cooled and solidified. It burned the shit out of me… A half hour later, it happened again. A half hour later, it happened again. So I had these three quarter-sized wicked burns, and they just would not heal. And people were like, 'Oh, how'd you burn yourself?' And I'm like, 'Oh -hot oil, on the stove.'" -Chris

The torches themselves failed in hazardous ways, the smallest and cheapest most of all when overused. We observed balls of flame engulfing the lighter, contact points hot enough to burn the hand holding it, and torches that kept emitting gas after the trigger was released, so the valve had to be closed to stop the flame. The flint often went first: the torch would hiss butane but not catch. One participant called the fix "jump starting." With two hands, he pressed the trigger on the dead torch and brought a second, working torch to it, angling both flames so as not to burn himself, often a $4 gas-station torch relighting a far more powerful one. Cars, full of plastic and fabric, had nowhere to set a hot torch, and any makeshift surface shifts when the car moves. Startled by a passer-by, Jane set her still-lit torch on her car seat to pick up her nicotine vape and look like anyone else. It burned deep into the upholstery, now hidden under a seat cover that is itself riddled with burns.

Mitigation strategies were user-generated and borrowed, like the equipment itself, from cannabis dabbing. Chris no longer dabs in shorts and never passes the dabber over his legs, laying inexpensive, paper-thin silicone mats "protectively on everything" -"there's products made for this, or, I don't know, maybe they're made for us" -and others used silicone pouches to store hot equipment between hits. Participants also rejected specific equipment as too dangerous: "Some of the monster torches have flame locks, but that just seems like a recipe for disasteryou could nod out and drop it on the ground and it would still be flaming, just setting everything on fire." That participant was observed nodding out while torching their nail for another hit, bringing the still-flaming torch across the back of their hand. One participant also worried about the exposure inherent to heavy torch use: "I'm burning through so much butane -that can't be good for my lungs."

4. Discussion

In our Los Angeles sample, 87% of participants who reported a smoking method reported dabbing at least once, and dabbing exceeded foil as the most commonly reported method. Although we report dabbing as a smoking method to stay consistent with the survey, it is a discrete, novel route of fentanyl administration: the drug is vaporized off a separately heated intermediary rather than heated directly by flame, and it comes with its own equipment, technique, and patterns of dosing. We pair this with a sustained ethnographic inquiry that situates this finding within the broader transition from injection to smoking, with its own material culture, social meanings, and risks. Ethnographically, we observed most participants moving toward dabbing during our fieldwork. Similarly, in survey data, all 11 participants who initially reported another smoking method and returned on a later date subsequently reported dabbing (Table 2). This supports the ethnographic accounts while leaving the timing of adoption uncertain. Participants' descriptions of an injection-like "rush" raise a pharmacokinetic hypothesis, informed by a clinical study of a controlled fentanyl aerosol rather than street dabbing.

In an open-label crossover trial, inhaled fentanyl vapor reached the blood as completely and as fast as an injection (MacLeod et al., 2012): ten healthy volunteers received 25 µg of fentanyl as a thermally generated aerosol, flash-heated off metal foil and taken in one brisk, complete inhalation with the breath held, and on another day the same dose intravenously. Absolute bioavailability, relative to the dose the device emitted, was 96.8%; peak arterial concentration and total exposure were similar to injection; and the slightly earlier inhaled peak was attributed to circulation time from the arm vein to the arterial catheter. If illicit fentanyl behaved like the trial aerosol when dabbed, injecting would add neither speed nor size of effect. People who use fentanyl may have found a way to optimize the drug's inherent pharmacology, and dabbing may be unusually well suited to approximating the trial's conditions: the drug flashes to vapor on a pre-heated surface, travels a closed path to the mouth, and is taken in one hard, deep inhalation with the breath held.

The hypothesis is untested in people who use drugs, and the trial was small, funded by the device's maker, and run in healthy non-smokers. A dab rig diverges from it at every point that can be measured. Street fentanyl is a mixture of uncertain salt form; tip temperature is uncontrolled; particle size is unmeasured; and drug is visibly lost, in smoke wisping out of a bong and in the thick cloud exhaled after a big hit, where the trial lost 3%. In a separate study comparing inhaled and intravenous pharmaceutical heroin, inhalation bioavailability was estimated at 52% (Rook et al., 2006). This is a comparison within heroin, not of fentanyl dabbing with foil. Purpose-built devices that heat fentanyl to a controlled temperature can vaporize it with little degradation (Vazda et al., 2021), and a dab rig's heated intermediary keeps the drug away from a flame of variable temperature. Letting a glowing tip cool before use fits fentanyl's thermal chemistry: visible red heat begins at roughly 525 °C (Draper, 1847), whereas fentanyl has been reported stable to about 350 °C under flash pyrolysis, with degradation products by 500 °C (Manral et al., 2009). Cannabis e-nail users posting online cite a preferred temperature of about 378 °C (Meehan-Atrash et al., 2017). How fast a torch-heated tip cools has not been measured, and the hydrochloride salt, common in illicit fentanyl and far less volatile than the free base at ambient temperature (Ewing et al., 2022), has not been characterized at dabbing temperatures; for heroin, salt or base makes a large difference to how efficiently the drug volatilizes (Huizer, 1987). Whether dabbing delivers more fentanyl than foil is unmeasured. However, the perceived difference is large enough to organize individual preference and market behavior.

Most of what is known about dabbing comes from cannabis research, where concentrate use has been associated with higher THC exposure (Bidwell et al., 2020), stronger self-rated negative effects (Chan et al., 2017), and greater tolerance and withdrawal (Loflin & Earleywine, 2014) than smoked flower, and more frequent use has been associated with physical dependence (Meier, 2017). The analogy is imperfect: people who dab fentanyl use the same drug product as those on foil. Still, participants broadly reported single inhalations with a volume of vapor far exceeding that which is possible when smoking on foil, with implications for overdose risk and tolerance. What happens to adulterants and bulking agents such as xylazine, medetomidine, BTMPS, and lidocaine (Shover, Godvin, et al., 2025; Shover, Koncsol, et al., 2025) at dabbing temperatures is unknown. Among our samples, xylazine in samples sold as fentanyl was detected in 23% of samples (82 of 349) in 2024, and its median concentration was 0.1% among 106 quantified samples.

The high tolerance that dabbing likely indicates may itself be protective against opioid overdose. Two measured quantities suggest its scale: mean reported daily consumption of 1.07 g of drug product among 47 respondents in our previous Los Angeles study (Godvin et al., 2026) and mean fentanyl purity of 16.3% among 128 intact powders quantified between October 2025 and September 2026 (Drug Checking Los Angeles, unpublished data) together imply roughly 175 mg of fentanyl loaded per day, with an uncertain amount delivered. The people we observed from 2024 to 2026 were, after all, the survivors, more than a decade into an overdose crisis that has taken the lives of hundreds of thousands of Americans and multiple friends of every single person we talked to.

Dabbing is most likely to negatively impact the organs of the lungs and the skin. Acutely, the most salient harm is opioid-induced respiratory depression, and dabbing's large, quickly inhaled boluses of vapor could shorten the interval between onset and apnea; sedation also brings a risk of aspiration, and aspiration pneumonia was diagnosed in 11% of hospital admissions for accidental opioid overdose in British Columbia (Morrow et al., 2019). The pulmonary consequences of smoking fentanyl chronically have not been characterized. Heroin smoking has been associated with pneumonia among HIV-negative drug users (Mientjes et al., 1996), and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease has been reported among people who smoke heroin (Bitar et al., 2021; Nightingale et al., 2020). The hardware adds its own exposures: butane gas, open flame, carbon monoxide, super heating and then inhaling off of material not manufactured to withstand those temperatures. Of particular concern were the cheap metal tips that arrived preinstalled in Amazon dabbers of dubious supply origin, always sold for some other ostensible purpose and repeatedly reported to leave a strong metallic taste when heated, making multiple participants question how they could possibly be safe for their intended purpose.

Dabbing's capacity for rapid redosing of high-volume, large hits may raise overdose risk relative to foil. Going through fentanyl faster may also mean running out sooner and buying again more often, and because participants felt their tolerance decline after even a few days off (Section 3.2.5), a return to large hits after a gap may outpace the tolerance that remains. This is compatible with tolerance being protective: tolerance protects the person who has it, and the danger concentrates in the person whose tolerance has fallen, after jail, detox, or a hospital stay, while the equipment and the size of the hit stayed the same. At the same time, participants who had injected for years described dabbing as the thing that finally replaced it, and if that holds more widely, dabbing could help some people inject less often, and so suffer fewer invasive bacterial infections (Islam et al., 2019), skin and soft-tissue infections (Megerian et al., 2024), and other injection-associated harms (Karandinos et al., 2026). The evidence for substitution is ethnographic, including accounts from participants accustomed to injecting on the East Coast; the survey cannot establish whether dabbing replaced injection, and the two coexist: 5 of 8 participants who reported injection also reported dabbing. Dabbing may reduce injection, replace it temporarily when veins fail, or offer an alternative to returning to it. Opioid injection has declined among people who inject drugs in San Diego (Eger et al., 2024) and smoking has risen sharply in the South (Chen et al., 2024); what drives such shifts, and whether dabbing could contribute to them, warrants evaluation. Englander and colleagues (2026) found acute-care burn encounter rates 4.3 times higher among Oregon Medicaid members with a diagnosis code for smokable drugs. In qualitative interviews, butane torch use appeared be the primary vector of burn risk, although the Medicaid data did not specify route of use and dabbing was not examined separately. (Butane torches are sometimes also used to smoke methamphetamine out of pipes, their use is not exclusive to dabbing nor opioids.) Our participants' confidence that a torch cannot burn a sleeping person for more than a second or two, because the heat "wakes you up," is contradicted by that work: people interviewed in Oregon described burns from passing out with a torch locked on, and one recalled a friend who "burned right through his hand" without waking (Englander et al., 2026). Pairing opioid sedation with an open flame and a heated element almost certainly amplifies this risk.

Widespread dabbing complicates standard safer-smoking guidance. The usual kit of foil, tooters, or hammer pipes (Canadian AIDS Treatment Information Exchange [CATIE], 2025; Chung et al., 2025; Kielhold et al., 2025; Megerian et al., 2024) is of less use to someone who dabs. Programs encountering dabbing should warn that one inhalation can deliver a much larger dose than people used to foil expect; caution against flame locks and the most powerful torches; and share heat-resistant surfaces and tools already used by people who dab.

4.1. Limitations

The survey sample was people who chose to use drug checking services in Los Angeles County; it may not generalize to all people who use fentanyl, locally or elsewhere. We have no ROA prevalence data for the other West Coast cities where we did fieldwork. Tools and lexicon looked much the same in every city observed, though we cannot say whether dabbing was equally common in each, and the sameness may partly reflect snowball recruitment through interconnected networks. The ethnography also has little to say about pipes, which the survey shows were used nearly as often as foil. Many of the participants most prevalent in our fieldwork were housed, at least marginally; we also observed dabbing among unhoused people, but the present analysis did not assess differences by housing status. ROA was reported for the sample tested, not for all of a person's use. Geographic variation in fentanyl markets limits transferability, particularly outside the western United States where smoking is less common (Friedman & Shover, 2023; Karandinos et al., 2024; Shover et al., 2020). The smoking-method survey item followed from ethnographic observation, so the two data strands are not independent. No systematic health-outcome data were collected, and the health implications discussed here are hypotheses. Triangulating long-term observation with self-report and quantitative drug checking data narrows these constraints without removing them (Friedman, Montero, et al., 2022).

The smoking-method question was introduced after the route-of-administration question and was not consistently completed, particularly early in implementation, so more encounters have route responses than answers about smoking method. Missing smoking-method answers do not indicate absence of dabbing, and prevalence among respondents with a recorded method may differ from prevalence among all smokers. First-answer comparisons cannot establish when participants first adopted dabbing. Free-text answers naming a bong or a crack pipe were coded as dabbing (Section 2.3), which is why Table 1 records no other identifiable smoking method. Repeated encounters may reflect multiple samples submitted on the same date so we therefore restricted our analysis to encounters occurring on different days.

5. Conclusion

Dabbing predominated in this sample, yet remains largely unstudied. Participants recognized the gap: "Nobody's talking about it. Everyone I know is doing it. We've been doing it for years now… They don't even know it exists." Research should measure dose delivery relative to foil, bioavailability, pulmonary effects, adulterant behavior when heated in this fashion, and the frequency and severity of burns. Dabbing's potential to replace injection or avert a return to it warrants investigation alongside its risks. These questions require evidence-based, user-informed harm reduction responses that distinguish among smoking methods.
