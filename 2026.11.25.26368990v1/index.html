Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

• Four broadband MEG source networks described a three-stimulus auditory oddball • N1m and P3 network responses did not differ between MS patients and controls • An auditory network separated targets from non-targets less in MS patients • The network difference occurred without differences in task performance • Network measures showed no reliable relation to fatigue or cognition

1. Introduction

Cognitive impairment is common in multiple sclerosis (MS) and most often affects information processing speed and attention (Chiaravalloti and DeLuca, 2008). Because it can be present early and is not well captured by disability scales, there is interest in neurophysiological markers that index the underlying cognitive processes. The auditory oddball paradigm and its P300 response have been used most widely for this purpose. The auditory pathways are relatively spared in MS (Sanders et al., 1986), so that late cortical responses can be studied with limited confounding from afferent dysfunction.

Event-related potential studies have reported prolonged P300 latency and, less consistently, reduced P300 amplitude in patients with MS, with associations with neuropsychological performance, disability and lesion load (Magnano et al., 2006; Whelan et al., 2010; Sundgren et al., 2015). Changes have also been described in early relapsing-remitting MS and in clinically isolated syndrome (Kocer et al., 2008; Pokryszko-Dragan et al., 2016). Paolicelli et al. (2021) recorded simultaneous MEG and high-density EEG during a two-stimulus auditory oddball in MS patients and healthy controls and analysed the responses at the sensor level. P300 latency was slightly but not significantly longer in patients, P300 amplitude and scalp topography were similar in the two groups, and time-frequency analysis showed lower induced MEG power to standard tones in patients; P300 amplitude correlated inversely with fatigue in the pooled sample of patients and controls. Sensorlevel measures of evoked brain responses may therefore be relatively insensitive to alterations in MS, and the authors pointed to the cortical generators of the response as the next step. Source-level studies of the P3 in MS remain few. A high-density EEG study localized altered frontoparietal P3 generators during visual working memory in newly diagnosed patients (Arrabal et al., 2026), and an MEG study of an n-back task found a frontoparietal network active 300-600 ms after the stimulus, resembling the P300, alongside a reduced early prefrontal network in patients (Rossi et al., 2024). To our knowledge, the generators of auditory deviance responses, and how they separate task-relevant from task-irrelevant sounds, have not been examined in MS.

The classic two-stimulus oddball cannot separate the processing of a rare stimulus from that of a task-relevant one, because the rare stimulus is also the target. The three-stimulus oddball adds a rare non-target stimulus that must be ignored (Squires et al., 1975). Rare non-targets elicit a frontocentral P3a, linked to the orienting of attention, whereas targets elicit a parietal P3b, linked to taskrelevant stimulus evaluation (Polich, 2007). Comparing target and non-target responses therefore isolates task relevance at matched probability. P3a and P3b amplitudes have been related to PASAT performance in progressive MS (Kiiski et al., 2011), but how these processes are organized at the level of brain networks in MS is unknown.

Source reconstruction of MEG data can address this question, but analyses restricted to a priori regions or time windows can miss distributed effects and invite circular selection. BROAD-NESS (Bonetti et al., 2025a) estimates broadband brain networks directly from source-reconstructed evoked responses. Principal component analysis of the group-averaged source activity yields a small number of spatial patterns, and the time course of each pattern can be compared across conditions and participants.

A network-level description is particularly suited to MS. Damage in MS is diffuse, involving white matter tracts and grey matter throughout the brain, and cognitive impairment is increasingly understood as a disturbance of brain networks rather than of single regions (Schoonheim et al., 2015 (Schoonheim et al., , 2022)). MEG studies in source space, mostly of resting-state activity, have shown altered functional networks in patients. Projecting MEG activity onto atlas regions with a beamformer, Tewarie et al. (2014a) found lower global integration of alpha2-and beta-band networks in early MS, related to poorer cognitive performance, and in a larger sample with long-standing MS functional network changes were related to the topology of grey matter networks (Tewarie et al., 2014b). A reduced individual specificity of source-reconstructed functional connectomes predicted fatigue (Cipriano et al., 2023), and a systematic review of MEG studies in MS reported reduced power modulations and connectivity (Khan et al., 2021). Evoked responses such as the P300 also arise from distributed generators (Polich, 2007), so that alterations that are weak at any single location may become detectable when activity is combined across the sources of a network.

Here we applied BROAD-NESS to MEG recordings of a three-stimulus auditory oddball in MS patients and healthy controls and compared the network-level result with a conventional source-by-source analysis of the same contrast. We asked whether the networks supporting auditory deviance and target processing differ between groups, with particular interest in the differentiation of taskrelevant targets from task-irrelevant deviants. We also asked whether network measures relate to task performance, cognition and fatigue in patients.

2. Materials and methods

>Materials and methods

2.1 Participants and clinical assessment

Patients with multiple sclerosis (MS) and healthy controls (HC) were enrolled at the Centre for Diagnosis and Treatment of Demyelinating Diseases, University Hospital of Bari, Italy. The cohort overlaps with that of Paolicelli et al. (2021), who analysed sensor-level MEG and high-density EEG responses to a two-stimulus auditory oddball (240 standard and 60 target tones). The present study analyses a different task recorded in the same sessions, a three-stimulus auditory oddball. According to the acquisition dates stored in the recording files, the included participants were recorded between February 2018 and February 2020 (patients: March 2018 to July 2019), a period extending beyond the enrolment period reported by Paolicelli et al. (2021) (March 2018 to April 2019).

Twenty-two HC and 25 MS patients were enrolled, and three-stimulus oddball recordings were available for analysis for 21 HC and 23 MS patients. Participants were excluded only for problems of the recording file or of its preprocessing, never on the basis of the neural signal: stimulus trigger codes were absent from the recording (three HC, one MS patient); the recording file could not be read (two HC); the file stored for one HC was a copy of another participant's recording and the original could not be retrieved; and Maxwell filtering returned no usable data for two MS patients (all data segments were rejected from the start of the recording in one case and after 88 s in the other; Section 2.3). No participant was excluded by the data-quality criteria described in Section 2.3. The final sample comprised 15 HC and 20 MS patients (participant flow in Supplementary Fig. S1).

The study protocol (Paolicelli et al., 2021) required age between 18 and 55 years and, for patients, a recent diagnosis of clinically isolated syndrome or relapsing-remitting MS according to the 2017 McDonald criteria (Thompson et al., 2018), a non-active phenotype (Lublin et al., 2014), minimal disability and no previous disease-modifying treatment. Exclusion criteria were disease duration above 5 years, Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS; Kurtzke, 1983) above 6, relapse or steroid treatment in the previous 30 days, psychiatric or other neurological disease, antidepressant or antipsychotic treatment, substance abuse, major head injury, moderate-to-severe hearing loss, and vascular or metabolic conditions. The clinical records of the included patients show deviations from some of these criteria (age above 55 years in two patients, disease duration above 5 years in three, previous disease-modifying treatment in two, antidepressant treatment in one and congenital hearing loss in one). All patients were included in the analyses, and the characteristics of the sample are reported in Table 1.

All participants underwent a 45-min neuropsychological evaluation with Rao's Brief Repeatable Battery of Neuropsychological tests (BRB-N), covering verbal memory, visuospatial memory, information processing speed and attention (Symbol Digit Modalities Test, SDMT; Paced Auditory Serial Addition Test, PASAT-3 and PASAT-5) and verbal fluency, together with the Trail Making Test. A test was considered altered when its z-score fell more than 2 standard deviations below the Italian normative mean (Amato et al., 2006), and cognitive impairment was defined as two or more altered tests (Paolicelli et al., 2021). Fatigue was assessed with the Fatigue Severity Scale (FSS; Krupp et al., 1989; cut-off 4.5) and depressive symptoms with the Beck Depression Inventory-II (BDI; Beck et al., 1996; cut-off 9). The total lesion count was the sum of juxtacortical/cortical, periventricular, infratentorial, spinal cord and optic nerve lesions on the baseline clinical MRI (median 3 months before the visit, range 1 day to 20 months). Clinical data were available for 16 of the 20 patients included. In HC, demographic data were available for all 15 participants, neuropsychological and BDI scores for 13 and FSS scores for 6. Demographic and clinical variables were compared between groups with Mann-Whitney U tests and, for sex and cognitive impairment, with Fisher's exact test.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Azienda Ospedaliero-Universitaria Consorziale Policlinico di Bari (protocol code 5692, 20 June 2019). All participants gave written informed consent.

2.2 Task and MEG recording

Participants performed a three-stimulus auditory oddball task. Each session comprised 380 tones of 100 ms: 304 standard tones (250 Hz; 80%), 38 target tones (1000 Hz; 10%) and 38 non-target tones (625 Hz; 10%). Tones were presented binaurally through headphones at 75 dB, in pseudorandom order, with a stimulus onset asynchrony of 2-3 s; a session lasted about 16 min. Participants sat with eyes open and were instructed to press a button with the right hand as quickly as possible after each target tone and to ignore the other tones. Stimulus onsets were marked on the trigger channel. A second trigger, present for every stimulus, started 12-13 ms after the stimulus code. Latencies are reported relative to the stimulus code.

MEG was recorded with a 306-channel Elekta Neuromag TRIUX system (Elekta Neuromag Oy, Helsinki, Finland; 102 magnetometers and 204 planar gradiometers) in a dimly lit magnetically shielded room (ETS-Lindgren Euroshield, Eura, Finland), with active shielding (MaxShield) on. Data were sampled at 1000 Hz, the rate stored in the recording files (Paolicelli et al., 2021 , report 1024 Hz), with an online band-pass filter of 0.1-330 Hz. Head position was tracked continuously with five head-position indicator coils, three on the forehead near the hairline and one over each mastoid. Coil positions, the three anatomical fiducials, the EEG electrode positions and 96-152 additional head-shape points (median 107) were digitized before the recording. Vertical electro-oculogram (EOG) was recorded in all participants except one HC, and the electrocardiogram (ECG) in the other HC but not in patients. A 64-channel EEG was recorded simultaneously but is not analysed here.

2.3 Preprocessing, source reconstruction and epoch rejection

Raw data were processed with MaxFilter 2.2.15 (Elekta Neuromag) using temporal signal space separation (tSSS; Taulu and Simola, 2006) with a 4-s buffer and a subspace correlation limit of 0.98, fine-calibration and cross-talk correction, an SSS expansion origin at (0, 0, 40) mm in head coordinates, internal and external expansion orders of 8 and 3 (80 and 15 components), and movement compensation based on continuous head-position estimates from the five coils. Automatic bad-channel detection was off. Data were downsampled by a factor of 4 to 250 Hz, with an anti-aliasing low-pass at 83.3 Hz applied by MaxFilter.

Data were then converted to SPM format and processed in MATLAB (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA) with custom code (LBPD, https://github.com/leonardob92/LBPD-1.0) and the OHBA Software Library (OSL; Woolrich et al., 2011), which integrates functions from FieldTrip (Oostenveld et al., 2011), FSL (Woolrich et al., 2009) and SPM (Penny et al., 2011). Independent component analysis (ICA; FastICA, Hyvärinen, 1999) was applied to the continuous data, and components reflecting eye blinks, eye movements and heartbeat were identified and removed. Sixty-two components were estimated per participant; components correlating with the EOG or ECG channel (|r| > 0.3) were flagged automatically, and all components were then inspected for topography and time course before removal (in patients, without ECG, cardiac components were identified from topography and time course only). The number of removed components, obtained from the reduction in data rank, was 2.6 ± 0.9 (range 2-5) in HC and 3.4 ± 1.5 (range 2-7) in MS patients (Welch t-test, p = 0.08). For the HC without EOG and ECG recordings, artefactual components were identified from topography and time course alone; this participant was included in the sensitivity analysis (Section 2.5).

Trigger values were read as unsigned 16-bit integers, and stimulus codes were taken from the loworder bits, retaining one event per stimulus. This step was needed because the response-pad bit was stored as a negative value in some recordings and because the second trigger could overlap with the stimulus code. Epochs from -100 to 3400 ms around stimulus onset were extracted and baselinecorrected (-100 to 0 ms). The number of extracted epochs was 380 in 20 participants, 378 or 379 in 11 and between 239 and 370 in four (median 380).

Individual structural MRIs were not available, so the same MNI152 template (2-mm T1) served as head model for all participants. The template scalp surface was extracted with FSL (FLIRT, BET and BETSURF; Jenkinson et al., 2012), and each participant's digitized fiducials and head-shape points were coregistered to it with the RHINO procedure of OSL: initial alignment on the three fiducials, then refinement by iterative closest point on the head-shape points with 10 random starts. A singleshell forward model (Nolte, 2003) was computed for 3559 sources on an 8-mm grid within the MNI152 brain mask.

Source time series were estimated with a linearly constrained minimum variance (LCMV) beamformer (Van Veen et al., 1997) using the 102 magnetometers. Each sensor's time series was zscored within each epoch, and the data covariance matrix was computed over all epochs of the three conditions and over the full epoch (-100 to 3400 ms). The covariance was inverted with a pseudo-inverse restricted to its first 50 principal components, to account for the reduced rank of tSSS-processed data. For each source, the lead field was reduced to the orientation of maximum output power (singular value decomposition), and the weights were normalized by their norm (W/√(WWᵀ)) to reduce the bias towards the centre of the head (Huang et al., 2004). A single set of weights per participant was applied to the condition-averaged evoked fields, and source time series were baseline-corrected (-100 to -4 ms).

Epochs contaminated by artefacts not removed by ICA, such as movement related to the button press, were excluded from the evoked averages. Detection used the ICA-cleaned magnetometer data in the analysis window (-100 to 900 ms), low-pass filtered at 40 Hz and linearly detrended for this purpose only. An epoch was rejected if the peak-to-peak amplitude exceeded 4000 fT on any magnetometer, or if all magnetometers were flat (< 1 fT). Condition averages were recomputed from the retained epochs and projected through the same beamformer weights. Recomputing the averages from all epochs reproduced the stored source time series exactly in every participant (r = 1.0), confirming that this step used the original beamformer.

Two exclusion criteria were set before the group data were examined: fewer than 20 retained target or non-target epochs, and baseline noise (root mean square across magnetometers, -100 to 0 ms, median over retained epochs) above the sample median + 3 scaled median absolute deviations. Criteria were based on data quality only. The signal-to-noise ratio of evoked responses was not used for exclusion, because it depends on response amplitude, which may itself be altered by the disease.

2.4 Broadband network estimation

All analyses used the interval from -100 to 900 ms (251 samples), which excludes the response to the following stimulus. Because the sign of beamformer time series is arbitrary for each source and participant, signs were aligned across participants before averaging. For each source and participant, the sign was inverted if the covariance between that participant's time course (0-600 ms, three conditions concatenated, mean removed) and the average of all other participants was negative. The procedure was iterated until fewer than 0.5% of source-by-participant pairs changed sign. It did not use group labels, and the same sign was applied to all conditions, so that condition contrasts were unaffected.

Brain networks were estimated with the BROAD-NESS (Bonetti et al., 2025a (Bonetti et al., , 2025b;; Andersen et al., 2026; Christensen et al., 2026; Fernández-Rubio et al., 2026) analytical pipeline developed in the larger NESS project (Rosso et al., 2025; Shinozuka et al., 2025 Shinozuka et al., , 2026;; Kudriavtsev et al., 2026; Malvaso et al., 2026). Source time series were averaged over all participants and then over the three conditions, and principal component analysis (PCA) was applied to the resulting sources × time matrix, with time points as observations. Each component's eigenvector defines the spatial activation pattern of a network (sign set so that the largest absolute weight is positive). Network time series for each participant and condition were obtained by projecting the participant's source time series onto each eigenvector. Networks were retained according to the elbow of the explainedvariance curve; effective dimensionality was computed as the participation ratio of the eigenvalue spectrum. For anatomical description, the 5% of sources with the largest absolute weight in each network were labelled with the Automated Anatomical Labeling atlas (Tzourio-Mazoyer et al., 2002).

2.5 Statistical analysis

Analysis windows were defined for each network with a collapsed localizer (Luck and Gaspelin, 2017), computed on the average of the two group means so that both groups contributed equally. Two localizer signals were used: the mean across the three conditions, searched between 30 and 300 ms, and the deviant-minus-standard difference ((target + non-target)/2 -standard), searched between 150 and 700 ms. After smoothing with a 20-ms moving average, local extrema exceeding 25% of the maximum absolute amplitude and at least 40 ms apart were selected. Windows were centred on each extremum (±20 ms for peaks before 150 ms, ±50 ms thereafter), and overlapping windows were merged. Windows are named by network and latency. The localizer is independent of the group contrast and also of the target-versus-non-target contrast, whose condition weights are orthogonal to those of the deviant-minus-standard signal.

For each participant, network and window, mean amplitudes were computed for each condition and for three planned contrasts: target -standard, non-target -standard and target -non-target. The last contrast compares two equally rare deviants that differ in task relevance and addresses the main question of the study (Section 1); the other two characterize the response to deviance. Groups were compared with Welch's t-test; effect sizes are reported as Hedges' g with 95% confidence intervals (Hedges and Olkin, 1985), and evidence for or against a group difference as a Jeffreys-Zellner-Siow Bayes factor (BF10; Cauchy prior scale √2/2; Rouder et al., 2009). Condition effects were tested across all participants with one-sample t-tests on the contrasts. Window-based tests are reported without correction for multiple comparisons and interpreted together with effect sizes and Bayes factors; inference on group differences rests on the cluster-based tests below.

For each network and planned contrast, groups were compared at each time point between 0 and 900 ms with Welch's t-statistic. Adjacent time points with |t| above the two-tailed critical value (t(33) = 2.03) and the same sign formed clusters, whose mass was the sum of their t-values (Maris and Oostenveld, 2007). This cluster-forming threshold corresponds to an uncorrected two-tailed p < 0.05 at each time point; because the degrees of freedom of Welch's test vary with the data, a single threshold based on the pooled degrees of freedom was used for all time points and permutations. The threshold affects the sensitivity of the test to effects of different extent but not its control of the family-wise error rate (Maris and Oostenveld, 2007). The null distribution was obtained from 5000 random permutations of group labels (fixed random seed), taking in each permutation the maximum absolute cluster mass across the four networks. This controls the family-wise error rate across networks within each contrast (p = (k + 1)/5001, where k is the number of permutations with a mass at least as large as the observed one). Because three contrasts were tested, a more conservative correction across all contrasts and networks, using the maximum over the twelve tests in each permutation, is also reported.

Group differences in latency were tested with the jackknife procedure (Miller et al., 1998; Ulrich and Miller, 2001; Kiesel et al., 2008). Within each window, latency was the time at which 50% of the area under the response, rectified with the polarity of the localizer peak, was reached (linear interpolation between samples). Jackknife standard errors were combined across groups into a tstatistic with 33 degrees of freedom. For windows defined only on the deviant-minus-standard signal, latencies were computed for target and non-target responses only.

The main result was examined in five ways: (i) leave-one-participant-out estimation of the effect; (ii) re-estimation of the networks from the average of the two group means, so that groups were weighted equally; (iii) comparison between groups of baseline noise in each network and condition, of the signal-to-noise ratio of the N1m, of the number of retained epochs and of the number of removed ICA components, which was also entered as a covariate of the group effect; (iv) a sensitivity analysis excluding four participants from all statistics (networks were still estimated on the full sample). They were chosen because each could directly affect the measured auditory responses or their cleaning: a patient taking central nervous system-active medication (an antidepressant and an antiepileptic), a patient with congenital hearing loss, a patient with exactly 20 retained target epochs, and the HC without EOG and ECG recordings. Other deviations from the protocol criteria (age, disease duration, previous treatment) were not grounds for exclusion. (v) A second sensitivity analysis, added after inspection of the behavioural data, excluded the three participants who pressed the button after most non-target tones (Section 3.1), for whom non-targets may also have been task-relevant.

To compare the network-level result with a conventional source-level analysis and to describe where in source space the main effect arose, the target -non-target amplitude in the localizer window of network 2 (378-626 ms) was computed for every source after sign alignment and compared between groups with Welch's t-test. Each source was oriented by the direction of the mean response of the two groups, so that positive values indicate a smaller difference in patients. Clusters of adjacent sources (26-neighbourhood on the 8-mm grid) with |t| > 2.03 were tested with 5000 permutations of group labels, controlling the family-wise error rate across all sources.

2.6 Behaviour and relationships with clinical measures

Button presses were read from the trigger channel of the Maxwell-filtered recordings (1000 Hz). A press was assigned to a stimulus if it occurred 150-1500 ms after stimulus onset. Hits, misses, false alarms to standards and false alarms to non-targets were counted, and median reaction times were computed for hits. Discrimination between targets and non-targets was quantified as d with a log-′ linear correction (Hautus, 1995). Button presses were not recorded for one HC and three MS patients. Groups were compared with Mann-Whitney U tests.

Within MS patients, two relationships were specified before the analysis, using Spearman's correlations and localizer windows rather than cluster windows to avoid circular selection: (H1) target -non-target amplitude in network 2 (378-626 ms) with d and with the false-alarm rate to ′ non-targets; (H2) the P3 response of network 1 (target -standard, 310-742 ms) with FSS, as a source-level test of the association reported by Paolicelli et al. (2021). Correlations of the same two measures with hit rate, reaction time, EDSS, disease duration, BDI, SDMT, PASAT-3, number of altered cognitive tests and lesion count were exploratory and corrected with the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (Benjamini and Hochberg, 1995). FSS and PASAT-3 values of zero were treated as missing.

Analyses were run with MaxFilter 2.2.15 and MATLAB R2022a, using LBPD, OSL (Woolrich et al., 2011), FieldTrip (Oostenveld et al., 2011), SPM (Penny et al., 2011), the BROAD-NESS toolbox (Bonetti et al., 2025a) and custom scripts (see Data and code availability).

3.1 Sample, data quality and behaviour

The final sample comprised 15 HC and 20 MS patients (Table 1). The groups did not differ in age, sex, education, fatigue, SDMT score or proportion of participants with cognitive impairment (all p ≥ 0.25), whereas MS patients had higher BDI scores (p = 0.008) and lower PASAT-3 scores (p = 0.022) (Table 1; Supplementary Table S8). After epoch rejection, HC retained on average 293.1 standard (range 190-304), 36.8 target (23-38) and 37.1 non-target epochs (26-38), and MS patients 298.4 (260-304), 36.2 (20-38) and 36.7 (29-38). The number of retained target and non-target epochs did not differ between groups (p = 0.67 and p = 0.72). No participant met the exclusion criteria for data quality. ICA removed 2.6 ± 0.9 components in HC and 3.4 ± 1.5 in MS patients (p = 0.08). Sign alignment inverted 33.1 ± 4.2% of sources in HC and 31.9 ± 4.5% in MS patients (Welch t = -0.82, p = 0.42). Baseline noise did not differ between groups in any network or condition (all p ≥ 0.19), nor did the signal-to-noise ratio of the N1m (median 31.0 in HC and 25.6 in MS patients; p = 0.97) (Supplementary Table S5). Task performance was recorded in 14 HC and 17 MS patients and did not differ between groups (Table 2). Hit rates were high in both groups (median 0.97), and median reaction times were 565 ms in HC and 558 ms in MS patients (p = 0.38). Three participants (one HC and two MS patients) detected 37 of 38 targets but also pressed the button after 33 to 38 of the 38 non-target tones, which lowered their d ; excluding them did not change the main result (Section 3.4).

3.2 Broadband networks

The first four components explained 48.8%, 31.3%, 10.6% and 3.3% of the variance (94.0% in total), after which the explained-variance curve flattened; effective dimensionality was 2.86 (Fig. 2). Anatomical labels refer to the 5% of sources with the largest absolute weight (Supplementary Table S1); with an 8-mm grid and a template head model they indicate approximate locations. Network 2 loaded on bilateral perisylvian cortex (postcentral gyrus, Rolandic operculum, insula, superior temporal gyrus and left supramarginal gyrus), with opposite signs in the two hemispheres. Network 4 covered a similar bilateral perisylvian and sensorimotor territory with the same sign in both hemispheres (postcentral and left precentral gyrus, Rolandic operculum, insula), together with the left thalamus. Network 1 was right-lateralized, with its largest weights in the right hippocampus and parahippocampal gyrus, mid-cingulate cortex, supplementary motor area, precentral and superior temporal gyri. Network 3 loaded mainly on right deep and temporal structures (caudate, putamen, thalamus, parahippocampal and inferior temporal gyri), and 40 of its strongest sources fell outside the AAL labels. Labels of deep and medial temporal sources should be read with caution, given the limited sensitivity of MEG to deep activity. Network 2 showed the auditory evoked sequence, with a large N1m peaking at 100-106 ms for all three tones and larger for the two deviants, followed by a sustained response to the deviants between about 160 and 630 ms (Fig. 3). Network 1 showed early responses at 80-91 ms and a large sustained deflection that began at about 250 ms, peaked at about 350-400 ms and lasted until about 800 ms; it was present for target and non-target tones, larger for targets, and small for standards, with the time course of the P3 complex. Network 3 responded at about 45 and 90 ms and showed a deviant-specific deflection between about 190 and 300 ms, larger for targets. Network 4 responded to all tones at about 75 and 130 ms; between about 170 and 260 ms the response to standards had the opposite polarity to that to deviants, and after about 300 ms target and non-target responses had opposite polarities.

3.3 Condition effects and window-based group comparisons

Eleven analysis windows were identified across the four networks (Supplementary Table S2). Deviant tones differed from standards in all four networks: the target -standard and non-target -standard contrasts were significant across participants in every window after 110 ms (all p ≤ 0.03), except target -standard in network 4 between 294 and 394 ms (p = 0.62), and target and non-target responses differed in network 1 (310-742 ms), network 2 (162-370 and 378-626 ms), network 3 (186-298 ms) and network 4 (294-394 ms) (all p ≤ 0.002) (Supplementary Table S3).

Of the 66 group comparisons computed in the analysis windows, four had uncorrected p < 0.05. Two concerned the target -non-target contrast in network 2: between 162 and 370 ms (Hedges' g = -0.78 [-1.47, -0.08], p = 0.034, BF10 = 2.5) and between 378 and 626 ms (g = -1.11 [-1.82, -0.39], p = 0.003, BF10 = 16.1). In both windows HC responded differently to target and non-target tones (mean difference 258 and 297 a.u.), whereas MS patients did not (94 and 41 a.u.) (Fig. 4E). The other two concerned the non-target response of network 3 between 28 and 68 ms (non-target: g = 0.68, p = 0.046; non-target -standard: g = 0.76, p = 0.027, BF10 = 2.3), a window preceding the N1m in which condition effects were absent (p ≥ 0.46). N1m and P3 amplitudes did not differ between groups in any condition (all p ≥ 0.13; BF01 between 1.1 and 2.9), and no group difference in latency was found in any window or condition (all jackknife p ≥ 0.13; Supplementary Table S4).

3.4 Cluster-based group comparisons

The cluster-based tests identified one group difference: in network 2, the target -non-target contrast differed between 372 and 496 ms (cluster mass -96.5; p = 0.010 for network 2, p = 0.020 corrected across the four networks) (Fig. 4D). In this interval the mean difference between target and non-target responses was 313 a.u. in HC and 24 a.u. in MS patients. No other cluster reached significance for any contrast or network (all corrected p ≥ 0.24; Table 3). When the correction extended to all three contrasts and four networks, the p-value of this cluster was 0.108.

The effect was robust (Supplementary Table S6). Removing one participant at a time left the effect size between g = -1.23 and -1.02 (p between 0.002 and 0.006). Networks estimated with equal weight for the two groups were nearly identical to the original ones (|r| ≥ 0.99 for all four networks) and gave the same result (g = -1.10, p = 0.003; cluster 372-496 ms, p = 0.006 for network 2). Excluding the four participants of the sensitivity analysis (14 HC, 17 MS patients) left the cluster at 376-464 ms (p = 0.041 corrected across networks) and the effect in the localizer window unchanged (g = -1.16 [-1.92, -0.40], p = 0.003, BF10 = 14.4) (Supplementary Fig. S5). Excluding the three participants who also responded to non-target tones (14 HC, 18 MS patients) did not change the result (cluster 368-500 ms, p = 0.016 corrected across networks; localizer window g = -1.22 [-1.97, -0.46], p = 0.002, BF10 = 22.5) (Supplementary Table S6). The number of removed ICA components was unrelated to the effect (Spearman ρ = -0.15, p = 0.39), and the group difference remained when it was entered as a covariate (-233 a.u., t = -2.89, p = 0.007).

At the level of single sources, no spatial cluster survived correction (largest cluster: p = 0.35). The largest uncorrected clusters both indicated a smaller difference in patients and lay in left postcentral, precentral, inferior parietal and middle temporal regions and in right postcentral, mid-cingulate and inferior parietal regions, overlapping the sensorimotor and perisylvian part of network 2 (Supplementary Fig. S7, Table S9). The group difference was therefore expressed at the level of the network rather than in a circumscribed region.

3.5 Relationships with behaviour and clinical measures

In MS patients, the target -non-target amplitude of network 2 was not related to discrimination between targets and non-targets (d : ρ = -0.04, p = 0.88; false-alarm rate to non-targets: ρ = -0.09, p ′ = 0.75; n = 17), and the P3 amplitude of network 1 was not related to fatigue (FSS: ρ = -0.20, p = 0.49; n = 14). The largest were between the network 2 target -non-target amplitude and the SDMT (ρ = -0.54, uncorrected p = 0.030) and the number of altered cognitive tests (ρ = 0.54, p = 0.032; n = 16) (Supplementary Table S7).

4. Discussion

We applied BROAD-NESS to MEG recordings of a three-stimulus auditory oddball in MS patients and healthy controls. Four broadband networks captured the main spatiotemporal dynamics of the response in both groups and were strongly modulated by stimulus type. Early auditory responses and the P3 complex did not differ between groups. The one group difference concerned network 2, which involved bilateral perisylvian and sensorimotor regions: between 370 and 500 ms, controls responded differently to target and non-target tones, whereas patients did not. This difference was observed although task performance was comparable in the two groups.

4.1 Reduced differentiation of task-relevant and task-irrelevant deviants

In controls, the late sustained response of network 2 was larger for non-targets than for targets, so that two equally rare deviants were processed differently according to their task relevance. In MS patients the two responses were similar, and neither condition alone differed significantly between groups: the difference lay in their differentiation. The effect was large (g = -1.11 in the localizer window), was supported by a Bayes factor of 16 and survived correction across networks in the cluster test. It was stable when single participants were removed, when the networks were estimated with equal weight for the two groups and when the number of removed ICA components was taken into account, and it remained when the participants with particular clinical or recording characteristics, or those who also responded to non-target tones, were excluded.

The effect emerged after the N1m, during the P3 interval, while early auditory responses were preserved. This timing suggests that the difference arises at stages of attentional selection and stimulus evaluation rather than of initial sensory encoding, and it fits a stage at which attention is redirected after a distractor (Schröger and Wolff, 1998). A reduced differentiation could therefore reflect weaker modulation of auditory processing by task relevance, in line with the deficits of attention and processing speed that characterize cognitive impairment in MS (Chiaravalloti and DeLuca, 2008). Target and non-target tones, however, also differed in frequency and in their acoustic distance from the standard (1000 vs 625 Hz, standard 250 Hz), so that a reduced sensitivity to the magnitude of deviance is an alternative account. The present design cannot separate the two, although the absence of group differences in the earlier, acoustically driven responses makes a purely sensory explanation less likely. Designs that counterbalance the assignment of tones to the target and non-target roles would resolve this question.

4.2 Preserved sensory and P3 responses

N1m and P3 responses did not differ between groups in amplitude or latency, with Bayes factors mildly favouring the absence of a difference. This agrees with the similar P300 amplitude and topography found in an overlapping cohort with a two-stimulus task (Paolicelli et al., 2021) and extends it to the source level. The slight, non-significant latency prolongation reported there was not observed here; the tasks, the measures (scalp peaks vs network time series) and the latency methods differ, and Bayes factors close to 3 indicate that the data were only weakly informative about the absence of differences. Network 1, whose late response had the time course of the P3 complex, showed a non-significant tendency towards a smaller non-target response in patients (310-742 ms: g = 0.54, uncorrected p = 0.13), whereas its differentiation between target and non-target tones did not differ between groups (p = 0.68). Given the sample size, smaller alterations in this network cannot be excluded, and this observation is reported as descriptive only. Together, these results suggest that alterations of auditory processing in MS may not be expressed uniformly across networks but may preferentially affect specific aspects of late stimulus processing.

4.3 Neural differences in the absence of behavioural deficits

Patients detected targets as accurately and as fast as controls, and within patients the network 2 differentiation was not related to d or to false alarms to non-targets. The network difference was ′ therefore not accompanied by a behavioural deficit, and the similar reaction times make it unlikely that it reflects differences in the timing of the motor response, which overlaps the late time window. Neural differences may thus be detectable while performance is preserved. Cross-sectional data cannot establish whether such differences precede cognitive decline or reflect compensatory processing, and the simple task, with high accuracy in both groups, may have been insensitive to subtle behavioural differences.

The network 1 P3 response was not related to fatigue. The inverse correlation between P300 amplitude and fatigue reported by Paolicelli et al. (2021), computed across patients and controls with a different task, was therefore not reproduced at the source level. The strongest exploratory correlations, with SDMT and with the number of altered tests, did not survive correction and pointed in an unexpected direction; they are not interpreted. Longitudinal studies and more demanding attentional tasks are needed to establish whether network measures of this kind have predictive or clinical value.

4.4 Contribution of network-level analysis

The main effect was identified at the network level, whereas a conventional mass-univariate analysis of the same contrast over the 3559 sources found no spatial cluster that survived correction (largest cluster: p = 0.35); the largest uncorrected clusters overlapped the sensorimotor and perisylvian sources of network 2. By combining sources that share a common time course, the network representation concentrates a distributed effect into a single time series and reduces the number of comparisons (Bonetti et al., 2025a). This observation does not show that network-level analysis is more sensitive than other source-analysis approaches in general, but it supports its use for subtle, distributed changes in clinical populations, in line with the view of MS as a disorder of brain networks (Schoonheim et al., 2022).

4.5 Limitations and future directions

The sample is small (15 HC and 20 MS patients), which limits power for smaller effects in other networks and for relationships with clinical and cognitive measures; effect sizes selected by statistical significance also tend to be overestimated. The main result survived correction across networks within the target -non-target contrast, but not the more conservative correction across all three contrasts (p = 0.108), and should be considered preliminary until it is replicated. Source reconstruction used a template head model, because individual MRIs were not available, which limits anatomical precision, particularly for deep sources; the beamformer used magnetometers only, and lesions were not modelled. Networks were estimated at the group level, so individual differences in their spatial layout are not captured. Some patients did not meet all the inclusion criteria of the original protocol (age, disease duration, previous treatment, antidepressant use or hearing loss; Section 2.1). They were retained, so the sample is more heterogeneous than the protocol implies; excluding the patients whose medication or hearing could most directly affect auditory responses did not change the main result. Behavioural data were missing for four participants and clinical data for four patients. Finally, the acoustic differences between target and non-target tones and the button press required for targets are potential confounds that future designs should control.

4.6 Conclusions

In this sample of MS patients, the source-level networks engaged by an auditory three-stimulus oddball were largely preserved, including the sensory N1m and the P3 complex. A distributed auditory network, however, differentiated less between task-relevant and task-irrelevant deviants in the late interval, in the absence of differences in task performance. This selective alteration is a candidate marker of changes in attention-dependent auditory processing in MS, to be tested in larger and longitudinal samples.
