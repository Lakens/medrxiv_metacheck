Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Introduction

Treatment-resistant depression (TRD) is defined as major depressive disorder (MDD) that fails to respond to adequate trials of at least two antidepressant medications (Thase and Rush, 1997; Conway et al., 2017; Tor et al., 2022). 30% of patients with MDD are estimated to be treatment-resistant (Rush et al., 2006; McIntyre et al., 2023). TRD represents a significant public health burden due to its profound impact on quality of life and high healthcare utilization (Ho et al., 2013; Johnston et al., 2019; Chodavadia et al., 2023). Conventional pharmacological and psychotherapeutic approaches often prove insufficient for TRD, necessitating neurostimulation techniques, such as transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS; George et al., 2013) or electroconvulsive therapy (ECT; Sackeim et al., 2001). TMS operates by inducing localized electric currents in brain tissues, modulating cortical excitability (Huerta and Volpe, 2009; Fox et al., 2012). Accelerated intermittent theta burst stimulation (iTBS), a more efficient variant of repetitive TMS, condenses sessions into shorter treatment windows, accelerating treatment timelines (Huang and Rothwell, 2004; Blumberger et al., 2018; Cole et al., personalized targets with a 5-day accelerated iTBS protocol, yielding exceptionally high response rates in TRD (Cole et al., 2020 (Cole et al., , 2022)). Subsequent trials have suggested that personalized TMS targets may outperform scalp-based targets in TRD (Li et al., 2026; Taylor et al., 2026a). However, previous TRD trials have generally used restrictive eligibility criteria that excluded several psychiatric comorbidities commonly encountered in clinical practice (Cole et al., 2022; Li et al., 2024 Li et al., , 2026;; Taylor et al., 2026a). Consequently, the feasibility and clinical outcomes of accelerated, connectivity-guided TMS remain underexplored in naturalistic TRD populations.

To address these gaps, we applied our previously developed personalized targeting procedure, TAO-TMS (Tree-based Algorithm for Optimized Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation; Kong et al., 2026), in an open-label trial of naturalistic treatment-resistant depression (TRD) patients in Singapore (NCT06341803). In our previous study, TAO-TMS targets compared 4 favorably with other personalized targets (Fox et al., 2013; Cash et al., 2021) in terms of testretest reliability, scalp proximity and anticorrelations with the subgenual anterior cingulate cortex (Kong et al., 2026). Because TAO-TMS identifies near-scalp targets, the present protocol was designed to use lower stimulation intensities (Stokes et al., 2005), with the aim of reducing scalp discomfort. The patient population examined here is clinically complex, with a higher psychiatric comorbidity burden than populations enrolled in previous accelerated connectivity-guided iTBS trials, as well as substantial polypharmacy, and variable episode durations. Notably, a retrospective study from the same hospital reported a 22% response rate immediately after nonaccelerated BeamF3 TMS (Ye et al., 2024), suggesting that the present sample represents a population with lower historical TMS responsiveness than those commonly described in published studies.

As in the SNT protocol, each participant received 50 iTBS sessions (1,800 pulses; 10 sessions/day over 5 days) delivered using a neuronavigated TMS system. As in SNT, stimulation intensity was linearly adjusted for the cortical depth of the target relative to the motor hotspot (Stokes et al., 2005). We additionally used post hoc electric field modeling to characterize the network composition and baseline sACC connectivity of TAO-TMS hotspots, using BeamF3 as a conventional anatomical reference. We also assessed pre-treatment to post-treatment functional connectivity (FC) changes, and performed an exploratory health economic comparison with ECT. This proof-of-concept study evaluated the feasibility, tolerability as measured by treatment completion, preliminary clinical outcomes, and targeting characteristics of TAO-TMS in a naturalistic TRD population with complex psychiatric comorbidities. Given the pilot design, the institutional review board (IRB) application included an approximate G*Power calculation indicating a minimum of 20 participants with paired pre-post observations, based on a large within-participant effect (Cohen's d = 0.8; paired-sample t-test; α = 0.05). Under these assumptions, this sample size provides approximately 92.4% power to detect the assumed effect using a paired two-tailed t-test. Twenty-one participants were enrolled; one withdrew during treatment, leaving 20 participants with immediate post-treatment data.

Methods and Materials

Participants

Because the calculation was based on continuous pre-post change rather than the binary primary response endpoint, it did not constitute a formal power calculation for the primary endpoint. The study was not powered for the subgroup, neuroimaging, or health-economic analyses.

TMS treatment protocol

Stimulation was delivered with a MagPro X100 stimulator (MagVenture, Farum, Denmark). To ensure precise and reproducible coil placement, we utilized the Axilum Robotics TMS-Cobot (Axilum Robotics, Strasbourg, France) with the Localite neuronavigation system (Localite GmbH, Sankt Augustin, Germany). Real-time neuronavigation was maintained to keep the coil within 2 mm of the personalized target.

We employed the Stanford Accelerated Intelligent Neuromodulation Therapy (SNT) stimulation protocol using our TAO-TMS targets. Each session delivered 1800 pulses consisting of bursts of 3 pulses at 50 Hz, repeated at 5 Hz, with a train duration of 2 seconds and an intertrain interval of 8 seconds. The coil (MagVenture Cool-B65) was positioned tangential to the scalp with the handle oriented at 45 degrees to the mid-sagittal line. Participants received 10 6 sessions per day over 5 consecutive days (50 sessions total). Sessions were separated by a 50minute inter-session interval.

Stimulation was delivered at 90% resting motor threshold (RMT), linearly adjusted for the cortical depth of the target relative to the motor hotspot (Stokes et al., 2005; Cole et al., 2020). To mitigate scalp discomfort, intensity was titrated from 70% to 90% of the adjusted RMT over the initial three sessions. Participants were asked at the end of each treatment session whether they had experienced any side effects or discomfort. Although a standardized adverseevent instrument was not administered, reported events were documented by the clinical team.

Treatment-delivery accuracy was quantified in each participant's native T1 volumetric space. The Localite neuronavigation system recorded the coil position for each delivered pulse relative to the participant's native MRI. These records were used to derive the actual treatment location for each pulse, and the Euclidean distance between this location and the intended TAO-TMS target was calculated in the individual's native T1 space. Distances were averaged across all delivered pulses within a treatment session to obtain the mean spatial deviation ("COBOT accuracy") for each participant.

Clinical assessments and outcomes

Participants were assessed at baseline, post-treatment, 1-month and 3-month follow-ups using the MADRS score (Montgomery and Åsberg, 1979) 1993). QIDS-SR16 and GAD-7 scores were additionally assessed after each treatment day to capture short-term symptom changes. Clinician-rated assessments were administered by trained raters, and self-report measures were completed by participants.

The IRB-approved study application specified the primary endpoint as response immediately after treatment, defined as a ≥50% reduction from baseline in MADRS total score.

QIDS-SR16, MoCA, EQ-5D, and Q-LES-Q-SF were prespecified secondary outcome measures.

The remaining clinical outcomes were exploratory: MADRS response at the 1-and 3-month follow-ups and at any assessment within 4 weeks after treatment; remission (MADRS <11) immediately after treatment, at the 1-and 3-month follow-ups, and at any assessment within 4 weeks; and changes over time in GAD-7 and SDS scores. All MRI scans were acquired at the Centre for Translational Magnetic Resonance Research (TMR), National University of Singapore, on a 3T Siemens Prisma scanner. The scalp-based BeamF3 (Beam et al., 2009) stimulation location was also identified and marked on the scalp using a vitamin E capsule. Participants completed a second MRI session on average 5.0 ± 1.9 days after treatment, consisting of one T1-weighted structural scan and two restingstate fMRI runs. Data was preprocessed using the Computational Brain Imaging Group (CBIG) fMRI preprocessing pipeline (Li et al., 2019; Ooi et al., 2025; Kong et al., 2026). More details can be found in Supplementary Methods.

Personalized TAO-TMS target

A personalized TAO-TMS target in the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) was generated for each participant using baseline rs-fMRI. To preserve individual anatomical fidelity, localization was performed in the participant's native volumetric space. We employed the Multi-Session Hierarchical Bayesian Model (MS-HBM) to estimate reliable individual-specific cortical networks (Kong et al., 2019), which were then mapped into the participant's native volumetric space. A tree-based algorithm was then used to estimate a personalized target within attention networks in left DLPFC that exhibited the strongest negative FC (based on Pearson's correlation) with the subgenual anterior cingulate cortex (sACC), while remaining close to the scalp to minimize TMS intensity requirements (Kong et al., 2026). The attention networks were chosen because dorsal attention and salience/ventral attention networks are known to be negatively correlated with the default network (Fox et al., 2006) and thus the sACC time course (Siddiqi et al., 2023). We also note that the salience network has been shown to be enlarged in depression patients (Lynch et al., 2024). 8 To quantify the spatial variability of personalized TAO-TMS targets across participants, each participant's target was transformed into every other participant's native T1 space using Advanced Normalization Tools (ANTs; Avants et al., 2011). For each pair of participants, we computed the Euclidean distance between participant A's transformed target and participant B's target in participant B's native space, and vice versa in participant A's native space. These two distances were averaged to obtain a symmetric pairwise estimate. The mean pairwise distance was then calculated across all participant pairs. To compare personalized TAO-TMS targets with conventional BeamF3 targets, both targets were identified in each participant's native T1 space, and their Euclidean distance was calculated directly within that space.

Exploratory analyses of coil orientation and target constraints on clinical improvement

To explore whether coil orientation and targeting constraints were associated with clinical improvement, we conducted two sets of exploratory analyses. First, the coil orientation in our trial was fixed at 45 degrees, following the SNT stimulation protocol. For each participant, we modeled alternative coil orientations to minimize hotspot sACC FC (i.e., maximize anticorrelations). The difference between the minimum hotspot sACC FC and the actual hotspot sACC FC (based on the 45-degree coil angle) was then correlated with percentage MADRS improvement across participants. A positive correlation would suggest that optimizing coil orientation to further minimize hotspot sACC FC might potentially improve clinical outcomes.

We repeated the same analysis by replacing hotspot sACC FC with attentional-network 10 engagement. Here, a negative correlation would suggest that optimizing coil orientation to further maximize attention-network engagement might potentially improve clinical outcomes.

Second, recall that TAO-TMS restricted the target to be near the scalp and to be within the attention networks. To explore the impact of these targeting constraints, personalized TMS targets were regenerated without the near-scalp constraint, and the Euclidean distance between these unconstrained targets and the original targets was correlated with percentage MADRS improvement. A negative correlation would suggest that the near-scalp constraint was potentially worsening clinical outcomes. The same analysis was repeated by removing both the near-scalp and attentional network constraints.

Exploratory health economic analysis

We conducted an exploratory cost-effectiveness analysis comparing TAO-TMS with ECT using a standard hybrid decision tree-Markov model over a one-year time horizon (Figure S1; Sonnenberg and Beck, 1993). Analyses were performed from healthcare system and societal perspectives.

The model comprised two stages with the same structure for TAO-TMS and ECT. First, the decision tree classified patients immediately after treatment into one of three acute-outcome categories: responders (≥50% reduction in MADRS), partial responders (≥25% but <50% reduction in MADRS), or nonresponders (<25% reduction in MADRS). Second, at 1 month after treatment, the same patients entered the Markov model and were assigned to one of three health states based on their MADRS score at that assessment: remission (MADRS <11), partial relapse (MADRS 11-20), or relapse (MADRS >20). Thus, the acute-outcome categories described the initial percentage improvement, whereas the Markov health states described symptom severity at 1 month. During each subsequent monthly cycle through month 12, patients could remain in their current health state or transition to another state.

Treatment-specific probabilities were assigned to this common model structure. For TAO-TMS, data from the present trial were used to determine both the probabilities of the acuteoutcome categories and the probabilities of moving from each acute-outcome category immediately after treatment to each health state at 1 month after treatment (when the Markov model began). Subsequent transition probabilities among the health states were estimated from changes observed between the 1-month and 3-month assessments. Because these assessments 11 were separated by two months, the observed transition probabilities were converted into monthly probabilities assuming constant transition hazards. These monthly probabilities were then applied through month 12.

For ECT, the corresponding acute-outcome and follow-up probabilities were obtained from published economic evaluations of nonpsychotic TRD populations (Zhao et al., 2018; Fitzgibbon et al., 2020). Because TAO-TMS outcomes were unavailable beyond 3 months and the published ECT inputs did not include recovery from relapse, relapse was treated as an absorbing state in both arms: once patients entered relapse, they remained in that state for the remainder of the modeled period.

Health-state utility values were derived from EQ-5D data collected in the present trial.

Quality-adjusted life-years (QALYs) were calculated by weighting the time spent in each health state by its corresponding utility value (Drummond et al., 2015). Costs included TMS, ECT, imaging, medication, adverse events, and productivity loss. Cost inputs were drawn from published Singapore Ministry of Health rates, Singapore Agency for Care Effectiveness resource sheets, institutional cost data, and data from the present trial. All model parameters and their sources are reported in Table S6.

Incremental cost-effectiveness ratios (ICERs) were calculated (Weinstein et al., 1996), and uncertainty was assessed using one-way sensitivity analyses and probabilistic sensitivity analyses with 10,000 Monte Carlo simulations (Briggs, 1999; Briggs et al., 2006). From these simulations, cost-effectiveness acceptability curves (CEACs) were generated to show the probability that TAO-TMS is more cost-effective than ECT across a range of willingness-to-pay thresholds. Scenario analyses examined shorter time horizons (3-month and 6-month) and two health states (instead of three). Study population and safety

Figure 1 shows the CONSORT flow diagram. Of 27 participants screened, 21 participants were recruited (mean age 35.1 ± 12.3 years; 57% female; 81% Chinese, 19% South Asian). The sample exhibited substantial clinical complexity (Table 1), with 86% meeting criteria for at least one additional psychiatric diagnosis; the average number of comorbid psychiatric diagnoses was 1.67. The enrolled participants had 4.7 ± 2.3 failed antidepressant trials, and 19% had previously received electroconvulsive therapy (ECT). Mean baseline MADRS was 28.3 ± 5.1. Detailed demographic and clinical characteristics are summarized in Table S1.

Of the 21 enrolled participants, 20 completed the full 50-session treatment protocol (95.2%). Ten participants (47.6%) reported at least one adverse event, whereas 11 (52.4%) reported none (Table S2). The most commonly reported events were treatment-site discomfort or headache during stimulation (5/21; 23.8%), insomnia (4/21; 19.0%), and anxiety (3/21; 14.3%).

One participant (4.8%) withdrew after three treatment sessions, reporting that treatment had triggered PTSD symptoms. No serious adverse events were reported.

The final treated cohort had a mean age of 34.9 ± 12.5 years with mean baseline MADRS of 28.4 ± 5.3 and 4.7 ± 2.3 failed antidepressant trials. The average number of comorbid psychiatric diagnoses was 1.6, and only three participants had no comorbid psychiatric diagnosis.

Clinical outcomes of TAO-TMS

To provide descriptive local context, Figure 2E presents the mean MADRS reduction in the TAO-TMS sample (57% ± 19%; N = 20) alongside that observed in a retrospective sample receiving non-accelerated BeamF3 TMS at the same hospital (29% ± 34%; N = 23; Figure 2E; Ye et al., 2024). An exploratory propensity-matched analysis of the historical sample achieved a MADRS reduction of 19% ± 45% (Figure S2). Because the samples differed in study design, treatment schedule, and clinical characteristics, the historical findings are presented only as descriptive context and cannot establish comparative efficacy.

Within the TAO-TMS sample, the primary endpoint -the MADRS response rate

immediately after treatment -was 65% (95% CI, 40.8%-84.6%; Table S3). In exploratory analyses, response rates were 50% (95% CI, 26.0%-74.0%) at 1 month and 31.3% (95% CI, 11.0%-58.7%) at 3 months. The corresponding remission rates were 30% (95% CI, 11.9%-54.3%), 27.8% (95% CI, 9.7%-53.5%), and 6.3% (95% CI, 0.2%-30.2%), respectively (Table S3). At any assessment within 4 weeks after treatment, 70% of participants achieved response (95% CI, 45.7%-88.1%) and 45% achieved remission (95% CI, 23.1%-68.5%).

E-field characteristics of TAO-TMS and BeamF3 targets

TAO-TMS selects a personalized target within attention networks in left DLPFC that exhibits the strongest negative FC with sACC (Figure 4A). E-field modeling indicated that salience/ventral attention network B showed the greatest mean engagement within TAO-TMS hotspots, followed by control and default networks, while the other attention networks contributed relatively little (Figure 4B). Although the relative contributions varied substantially across individuals, engagement was generally concentrated within this limited set of networks (Figure 4C). Despite BeamF3 not being designed to target attention networks, the overall ordering of network engagement was broadly similar between TAO-TMS and BeamF3 (Figure 4B). As expected from the targeting design, TAO-TMS hotspots showed 21.4% stronger engagement of attention networks (p = 0.012; Figure S4A) and 87.6% stronger (more negative) sACC FC (p = 0.012; Figure S4B) than BeamF3 targets at baseline. These results confirm that TAO-TMS achieved its intended targeting characteristics but do not establish clinical superiority.

Treatment-related functional connectivity changes

Of the 20 participants who completed treatment, one missed the post-treatment scan and one was excluded due to excessive motion, leaving 18 participants for the following analyses.

We investigated whether clinical improvement was related to FC changes from pre-treatment to post-treatment in 18 participants. Recall that the E-field hotspot (top row in Figure 4A) corresponded to the actual stimulation hotspot. There was no significant correlation between percentage MADRS reduction and change in hotspot sACC FC (r = -0.18, p = 0.476; Figure S4C).

At the whole-brain level, there were statistically significant changes in whole-brain FC in every participant (FDR q < 0.05), but the spatial distributions of these significant edges were highly variable across participants (Figure 5; Figures S5 to S7). Qualitatively, responders might exhibit greater FC changes than non-responders, but the between-group difference was not statistically significant. We note that among the non-responders, one participant displayed substantial FC changes post-treatment and subsequently showed clinical response at the 3-month follow-up (non-responder 4 in Figures S6 to S7). Impact of coil orientation & target constraints on observed clinical improvements

Following the SNT stimulation protocol, coil orientation was fixed at 45 degrees. In post hoc exploratory analyses, modeled changes in attention-network engagement and hotspot sACC FC under alternative coil orientations were not significantly associated with percentage MADRS improvement in this small sample (attention-network engagement: r = -0.35, p = 0.16; hotspot sACC FC: r = 0.14, p = 0.59; Figure S8). TAO-TMS constrained targets to lie near the scalp and within the attention networks. In post hoc exploratory analyses, target displacement resulting from removal of these constraints was not significantly associated with percentage MADRS improvement in this small sample (removal of the near-scalp constraint alone: r = -0.18, p = 0.46, Figure 6A; removal of both the near-scalp and attention-network constraints: r = -0.08, p = 0.73, Figure 6B).

Exploratory health economic outcomes

In an exploratory cost-effectiveness analysis, the base-case model estimated lower costs and slightly higher quality-adjusted life-years (QALYs) for TAO-TMS than for ECT. From the healthcare system perspective, the estimated costs were S$45,172 (US$35,017) for TAO-TMS and S$55,767 (US$43,230) for ECT, corresponding to a difference of S$10,596 (US$8,214), while QALYs were similar: 0.67 (TAO-TMS) vs 0.65 (ECT). From the societal perspective, which included productivity losses, the model estimated a cost difference of S$19,213 (US$14,894) in favor of TAO-TMS (Table S7).

Cost-effectiveness acceptability curves (Figure 7) indicated a high model-estimated probability that TAO-TMS was cost-effective across the evaluated willingness-to-pay thresholds.

In one-way sensitivity analyses, the conclusion from the healthcare system perspective was most sensitive to the modeled cost of ECT: TAO-TMS lost dominance when ECT treatment cost fell below S$695 (US$539). The societal perspective remained unchanged across parameter variations (Figure S9). Across shorter time horizons (3 and 6 months; Table S8) and the conservative two-state Markov model (Table S9; Figure S10), TAO-TMS remained less costly than ECT, while estimated QALYs were similar between treatments. Further details can be found in Supplementary Results.

Discussion

This study supports the feasibility of personalized connectivity-guided accelerated iTBS in a naturalistic TRD population with complex psychiatric comorbidities often excluded from previous trials. TAO-TMS demonstrated a 65% response rate immediately after treatment, compared with the historical response rate of 22% with non-accelerated BeamF3 TMS from the same hospital (Ye et al., 2024). Post hoc E-field analysis confirmed that TAO-TMS achieved its intended targeting characteristics while revealing a broadly similar ordering of network engagement between TAO-TMS and BeamF3. Statistically significant post-treatment FC changes were found within each individual, but the study was not sufficiently powered to find any relationship between FC changes and treatment response. Finally, exploratory economic modeling estimated lower costs and similar QALYs for TAO-TMS relative to ECT. increased from 65% to 75% immediately after treatment and from 50% to 60% at 4 weeks among participants with available data, while remission was essentially unchanged. Effects of psychiatric comorbidities and target personalization on clinical outcomes

Our prior work showed that TAO-TMS achieved greater reliability, closer scalp proximity and stronger sACC anticorrelation than several alternative individualized targeting approaches (Kong et al., 2026). Because the SNT targeting algorithm is proprietary, it was not included in that comparison. Reliability primarily reduces localization uncertainty, while scalp proximity may allow lower stimulation intensity and improve tolerability. sACC anticorrelation is an associative targeting marker whose causal and predictive validity remains under investigation. Although desirable, these target characteristics should not be interpreted as validated surrogates for clinical efficacy. 29 We cannot rule out that SNT targets are more clinically effective than TAO-TMS targets.

Limitations

Our small sample size offered limited statistical power for subgroup and neuroimaging analyses and increased uncertainty in the health economic estimates. Another key limitation is the lack of a placebo arm or anatomical/scalp-based target as an active control. We are addressing this question in an ongoing double-blind trial directly comparing TAO-TMS with BeamF3 targeting in a naturalistic TRD population with complex psychiatric comorbidities (NCT06385223).

The durability of clinical benefit also warrants consideration. Although MADRS scores remained significantly below baseline at both 1-and 3-month follow-up, the magnitude of improvement was greatest immediately after treatment and attenuated over time. Future studies should evaluate the longer-term durability of benefit and the potential role of maintenance treatment strategies.

Exploratory health economic analysis

An exploratory model-based cost-effectiveness analysis compared TAO-TMS with electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) over a one-year time horizon. We used a hybrid decision tree-Markov model with the same structure for both treatment arms (Figure S1). The decision tree represented acute treatment outcomes at the fixed assessment immediately after treatment. Patients were classified as responders (≥ 50% reduction in MADRS), partial responders (≥ 25% but <50% reduction), or nonresponders (<25% reduction). All three branches, including nonresponse, were retained in the model. At 1 month after treatment, patients from each acute-outcome category entered the Markov model and were assigned to one of three health states based on their MADRS score at that assessment: remission (MADRS <11), partial relapse (MADRS 11-20), or relapse (MADRS >20). Thus, the decision tree characterized percentage improvement immediately after treatment, whereas the Markov states characterized absolute symptom severity during follow-up. The Markov model used monthly cycles through month 12. During each cycle, patients could remain in their current health state or transition to another state.

Treatment-specific probabilities were assigned to this common model structure. For TAO-TMS, data from the present trial determined the probabilities of the acute-outcome categories and the probabilities of moving from each acute-outcome category immediately after treatment to each Markov health state at 1 month after treatment. Subsequent transition probabilities among the health states were estimated using changes observed between the 1-month and 3-month assessments. Analyses used patients with the observations required to estimate each probability; missing outcomes were not imputed. Because the 1-month and 3month assessments were separated by two months, the observed two-month transition probabilities were converted into monthly probabilities. The resulting monthly probabilities were applied through month 12. This extrapolation assumes that transition probabilities observed between months 1 and 3 remained constant during subsequent follow-up.

For ECT, acute-outcome and follow-up probabilities were obtained from published economic evaluations of nonpsychotic treatment-resistant depression populations (Fitzgibbon et al., 2020; Zhao et al., 2018). Because TAO-TMS outcomes were unavailable beyond 3 months and the published ECT inputs did not include recovery from relapse, relapse was treated as an absorbing state in both arms: once patients entered relapse, they remained in that state for the remainder of the modeled period. All clinical probabilities and their sources are reported in Table S6.

Health-state utility values were derived from EQ-5D data collected in the present trial.

These utility values were used to estimate quality-adjusted life-years (QALYs). Analyses were conducted from healthcare system and societal perspectives. The healthcare system perspective included treatment, imaging, outpatient visits, medication, laboratory testing, and adverse-event costs. The societal perspective additionally included transportation and productivity losses. Cost inputs were obtained from published Singapore Ministry of Health rates, Singapore Agency for Care Effectiveness resource sheets, institutional cost data, and data from the present trial. All model inputs and their sources are summarized in Table S6.

All costs were expressed in 2025 Singapore dollars. Results were also converted to US dollars using a fixed exchange rate of US$1 = S$1.29. Costs and QALYs were not discounted because the model horizon did not exceed one year. The primary outcomes from the cost-effectiveness analysis (CEA) model were costs, QALYs gained, and the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER). A willingness-to-pay threshold of S$100,000 (US$77,519) was used.

One-way and probabilistic sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the robustness of the base-case results to parameter uncertainty across different willingness-topay thresholds. The probabilistic sensitivity analysis used 10,000 Monte Carlo simulations. Exploratory health economic outcomes

An exploratory hybrid decision tree and Markov cohort model (Figure S1) was used to assess the costs and effectiveness of TAO-TMS and ECT, utilizing data collected from the study and relevant literature (Table S6). From the perspective of the healthcare system, the base-case model estimated costs that were US$8,214 lower for TAO-TMS than for ECT, with costs amounting to US$35,017 for TAO-TMS versus US$43,230 for ECT. Additionally, TAO-TMS resulted in a higher QALY of 0.67, compared to 0.65 for ECT. When viewed from a societal perspective, estimated costs were US$14,894 lower for TAO-TMS than for ECT.

Under the base-case assumptions, TAO-TMS was dominant from both perspectives, with lower estimated costs and similar QALYs (Table S7).

Tornado diagrams were employed to illustrate the outcomes of the one-way sensitivity analyses (Figure S9). From the healthcare system perspective, the modeled cost of ECT was the most influential input parameter. Specifically, if the cost of ECT treatment were to decrease to below US$539 (S$695), the ICER would exceed the WTP threshold, thereby altering the conclusion regarding the cost-effectiveness of TAO-TMS. From the societal perspective, the conclusion favoring TAO-TMS remained unchanged across the one-way sensitivity ranges.

In the probabilistic sensitivity analyses, we conducted 10,000 Monte Carlo simulations, varying the input parameters simultaneously. The results were presented in costeffectiveness acceptability curves (CEACs), which illustrate the probabilities of each intervention arm being cost-effective across a range of WTP thresholds. The CEACs indicated a high model-estimated probability that TAO-TMS was cost-effective across the evaluated willingness-to-pay thresholds from both perspectives (Figure 7).

In scenarios involving shorter time horizons, the cost savings and QALY gains associated with TAO-TMS were diminished across all scenarios (Table S8), but TAO-TMS remained the preferred option under the modeled assumptions. In a conservative scenario that considered only two treatment responses and two Markov health states (Figure S10), TAO-TMS still demonstrated cost savings relative to ECT (Table S9). However, the QALY gains were found to be comparable between the two treatments, with ECT yielding 0.01 QALY gain compared to TAO-TMS. In this conservative scenario, the ICER for ECT relative to TAO-TMS was US$1,221,700 per QALY from the healthcare system perspective and US$506,450 per QALY from the societal perspective. Both ICERs substantially exceeded the willingness-to-pay threshold, indicating that the additional QALYs associated with ECT were not cost-effective under the assumptions of this scenario.
