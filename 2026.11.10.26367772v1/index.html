Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

What we found

We found 5 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Diabetic retinopathy is a leading cause of preventable blindness. Because it is asymptomatic until substantial damage has occurred, periodic retinal examination is essential for early detection.

Evidence on uptake where eye services are co-located with diabetes care in Ethiopia is lacking.

This study assessed the prevalence of, and factors associated with, adequate diabetic retinopathy screening practice among adults with diabetes attending Menelik II Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, Addis Ababa.

We conducted an institution-based cross-sectional study from December 2023 to February 2024 among adults with type 1 or type 2 diabetes on follow-up for at least three months, selected by systematic random sampling and interviewed with a structured questionnaire. Knowledge and attitude were classified using a modified Bloom's cut-off of 80%, and screening practice as adequate when at least two of three recommended practices were met. Factors associated with adequate practice were identified using multivariable logistic regression.

Of 404 patients approached, 371 were interviewed and included in the analysis (response rate 91.8%). Adequate screening practice was found in 139 participants (37.5%, 95% confidence interval [CI] 32.7-42.5); 62.0% had adequate knowledge and 72.8% a favorable attitude. Adequate practice was associated with adequate knowledge (adjusted odds ratio [AOR] 5.10, 95% CI 2.81-9.25), a known eye disorder (AOR 3.96, 95% CI 2.11-7.43), higher education (AOR 3.82, 95% CI 1.33-10.95), age 41-65 years (AOR 3.15, 95% CI 1.10-9.03) and being married (AOR 1.73, 95% CI 1.01-2.97). Cost, lack of time and travel distance were the most commonly reported barriers.

Fewer than four in ten adults with diabetes underwent retinal examination at the recommended frequency despite on-site specialist eye care. The strong association with existing eye disorders 36 suggests that examination is often prompted by ocular problems rather than undertaken for 37 prevention. Strengthening patient education during diabetes follow-up and a direct referral 38 pathway to on-site eye services may improve uptake.

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus is a group of chronic metabolic disorders in which persistent hyperglycemia, arising from deficient insulin secretion, impaired insulin action, or both, damages the microvasculature and macrovasculature of multiple organ systems [1]. In the eye, this damage manifests as diabetic retinopathy, a progressive condition that remains asymptomatic until the retina is already substantially damaged and that is among the leading causes of preventable blindness in working-age adults [2]. Globally, an estimated 103 million people were living with diabetic retinopathy in 2020, a figure projected to reach 161 million by 2045, with the largest increases expected in low-and middle-income countries [3].

Ethiopia is experiencing this transition. A national systematic review and meta-analysis estimated the pooled prevalence of diabetic retinopathy among people with diabetes at 19.5% [4], while facility-based studies have reported estimates ranging from 13% in southern Ethiopia [5] to 34% in the north-west of the country [6]. Because early retinopathy produces no symptoms, it can only be identified through periodic examination of the retina. International and regional guidance therefore recommends that every person with type 1 or type 2 diabetes undergo a dilated retinal examination at least annually, with more frequent review once retinopathy is present [7, 8]. Timely detection allows laser photocoagulation or intra-vitreal therapy to be delivered while vision is still preserved, which makes screening uptake, rather than retinopathy prevalence alone, the operationally decisive indicator [9]. Uptake varies widely across sub-Saharan Africa, with estimates ranging from 21.7% in Ghana to 49.0% in South Africa, suggesting context-specific determinants [10, 11]. Ethiopian estimates fall within this range: 31.5% of adults attending two referral hospitals in the north-east had used eye care services in the previous year [12], and 39.6% of patients at a district hospital in the north-west reported a regular eye check-up [13]. These figures contrast sharply with 71.9% reported from Saudi Arabia [14] and 70.5% from Switzerland [15], where organized screening programs and near-universal insurance coverage are in place.

Patient knowledge and attitude are the determinants most frequently implicated in this gap [13, 16, 17], consistent with the knowledge-attitude-practice-outcome framework that links what patients understand about a condition to the preventive behaviors they adopt [18]. Much of the available Ethiopian evidence comes from regional and district facilities in the north of the country.

In Addis Ababa, which holds the largest urban concentration of people with diabetes in Ethiopia, a recent study across five public hospitals found that 55.1% of adults with diabetes had undergone retinal screening in the previous year [19], but no study has examined uptake where specialist eye care is co-located with diabetes follow-up. This gap matters for a specific reason. Menelik II Comprehensive Specialized Hospital houses both a high-volume diabetes follow-up clinic and a national referral center for specialist eye care, with retinal screening delivered on site by trained optometrists five days a week. Uptake at this facility therefore approximates a best-case scenario for physical access, and a low figure would implicate demand-side and care-process barriers rather than the absence of services. In Ethiopia, as elsewhere in sub-Saharan Africa, diabetic retinopathy screening is not organized as a program: there is no standardized national screening pathway, and patients with diabetes are typically linked to eye care only after complications or visual symptoms develop [6, 20]. Weak referral links between diabetes clinics and eye services, together with outof-pocket costs and the lack of recall systems, mean that co-located services do not by themselves guarantee uptake [20]. We conducted this study to determine the prevalence of adequate diabetic retinopathy screening practice, and to identify the factors associated with it, among adults with diabetes on follow-up at this hospital.

Materials and methods

Study design, setting and period

An institution-based cross-sectional study was conducted from December 2023 to February 2024 at Menelik II Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, Addis Ababa, Ethiopia. Established in 1903 and administered by the Addis Ababa Health Bureau, the hospital is a national referral center for specialist eye care, staffed by 22 specialists and six subspecialists. Adults with diabetes are followed at the hospital's Medical Referral Clinic, which operates Monday to Friday with appointment intervals of one week to three months. Diabetes care is delivered by general practitioners, internists and nurses; diabetes health education is provided principally by nurses; and trained optometrists perform diabetic retinopathy screening at the outpatient triage area on all working days. Health Management Information System records showed 4,300 diabetes follow-up visits between January and June 2023, a mean of 717 patients per month. The study is reported in accordance with the STROBE statement for cross-sectional studies (S1 Checklist).

Participants and eligibility

The source population comprised all adults with diabetes mellitus on follow-up at the Medical Referral Clinic. Patients aged 18 years and above with either type 1 or type 2 diabetes who had been on follow-up for three months or longer were eligible. Patients who were acutely unwell or presenting as emergencies, and women with gestational diabetes, were excluded. Participant flow is summarized in Fig 1.

Sample size

The sample size for the primary descriptive objective was calculated using the single population proportion formula, n = Z²₁₋α/₂ p(1 -p)/d², with a 95% confidence level (Z = 1.96), an absolute precision (d) of 5%, and p = 0.396, the proportion of patients reporting a regular eye check-up in the most closely comparable Ethiopian study, conducted at Debark Hospital [13]. This yielded 367 participants; allowing 10% for non-response gave a required sample of 404. Sample sizes were also computed for the analytical objective using the double population proportion formula in Epi Info StatCalc version 7.2.5.0 (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, Atlanta, GA, USA), at 95% confidence and 80% power, taking the exposure proportions and odds ratios reported at Debark Hospital for attitude (23.0% exposed among controls, odds ratio 7.46), previous eye disease (20.9%, odds ratio 7.07) and knowledge of diabetic retinopathy (24.5%, odds ratio 6.79) [13]. These gave requirements of 48, 51 and 51 participants respectively, all substantially smaller than the descriptive estimate. The largest figure, 404, was therefore adopted as the final sample size, which conferred more than 80% power for each of the hypothesized associations.

Sampling procedure

Participants were selected by systematic random sampling in the order of their clinic appointments.

Using the mean monthly clinic volume of 717 patients as the denominator and the required sample of 404, the sampling interval was calculated as K = 717/404 ≈ 2. Every second eligible patient exiting the outpatient department was therefore approached, and recruitment continued on consecutive working days until the required sample size was reached.

Variables and measurement

The outcome variable was diabetic retinopathy screening practice. It was measured with three items adapted from a validated knowledge, attitude and practice instrument [16]: whether the respondent had a regular (at least annual) eye check-up; how often they attended for a dilated eye examination; and how long after the diagnosis of diabetes their first dilated eye examination took place. Each item was scored as correct (1) or incorrect (0) against the recommended standard, giving a total score of 0-3. Participants meeting at least two of the three items were classified as having adequate screening practice, and the remainder as having inadequate practice. The three practice items showed high internal consistency (Cronbach's α = 0.91).

Knowledge was assessed with 10 items adapted from the Debark Hospital study [13], covering the ocular effects of diabetes, the nature of diabetic retinopathy, its risk factors, and the recommended timing of screening. Each knowledge item was coded as correct (1) or incorrect/don't know (0), giving a total score of 0-10. Attitude was assessed with six five-point Likert items (1 = strongly disagree to5 = strongly agree) adapted from the instrument of Srinivasan et al. [16], giving a total score of 6-30. Both scales were dichotomized at the same 80% cut-off: adequate versus inadequate knowledge (≥ 8 of 10) and favorable versus unfavorable attitude (total score ≥ 24 of 30). Internal consistency in this sample was good for the knowledge scale (Cronbach's α = 0.85) and very high for the attitude scale (α = 0.98), the latter reflecting limited differentiation between attitude items. Independent variables were selected a priori on the basis of the knowledge-attitude-practiceoutcome framework [18] and prior Ethiopian studies [12, 13]. They comprised socio-demographic characteristics (age, sex, marital status, educational level, residence, employment, health insurance coverage and usual mode of transport to the hospital) and clinical and behavioral characteristics (type of diabetes, duration since diagnosis, family history of diabetes, comorbidities, known eye disorders, exposure to diabetes education and its source), together with the knowledge and attitude scores.

Data collection and quality control

The questionnaire was developed in English, translated into Amharic and back-translated to check consistency of meaning. It was pre-tested one week before data collection on 10% of the calculated sample size at Zewditu Memorial Hospital, and ambiguous items were revised before fieldwork began. Three nurses holding a Bachelor of Science degree and with at least one year of clinical experience at the Medical Referral Clinic collected the data. They received a one-day training session covering the study objectives, the questionnaire and interview technique. Interviews were conducted face to face as patients exited the outpatient department, and responses were entered directly into the KoBo Toolbox platform, which applied range and skip-pattern checks at the point of entry. The principal investigator supervised data collection daily and reviewed completed questionnaires for consistency.

Statistical analysis

Data were exported to IBM SPSS Statistics version 26 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) for cleaning and analysis. Categorical variables are summarized as frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables as medians with interquartile ranges, since their distributions were skewed.

The prevalence of adequate screening practice is presented with a 95% Wilson score confidence interval.

Binary logistic regression was used to examine the association between each independent variable and adequate screening practice. Variables associated with the outcome at p < 0.25 in bivariable analysis were entered simultaneously into a multivariable model; attitude was retained a priori because of its centrality to the conceptual framework, despite not meeting this screening threshold.

Multicollinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor, with all values below 5 and therefore acceptable. Model fit was assessed with the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test; a p-value above 0.05 was taken to indicate adequate fit. Discrimination was assessed with the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve. Crude and adjusted odds ratios are reported with 95% confidence intervals, and statistical significance was declared at a two-sided p-value below 0.05.

Ethics statement

Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of the Addis Continental December 22, 2023). Written informed consent was obtained from every participant after the purpose, procedures, risks and benefits of the study had been explained. Participation was voluntary, and participants were informed of their right to decline, to skip any question and to withdraw at any point without any effect on their care. Medical record numbers were used to link questionnaires during data collection, and no personal identifiers were retained in the analytical dataset. Access to the data was restricted to the investigators, and the study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Discussion

In this study, just over one-third of participants had undergone retinal examination at the recommended frequency. This shortfall is striking given that diabetes follow-up and a national referral center for specialist eye care operate within the same compound, with retinal screening available five days a week. Adequate knowledge of diabetic eye disease was the strongest correlate of screening uptake, followed by the presence of a known eye disorder, higher education, middle age, and being currently married.

The prevalence we observed is similar to the 39.6% reported at Debark Hospital in north-west Ethiopia [13] and to findings from tertiary hospitals in India [16] and Bangladesh [17]. It is somewhat higher than the 31.5% found in two referral hospitals in north-east Ethiopia [12], yet substantially lower than the 71.9% and 70.5% reported from Saudi Arabia [14] and Switzerland [15]. The latter settings differ in ways that plausibly explain the gap: both operate organized recall systems with defined screening intervals, and both have near-universal insurance coverage that removes the point-of-service cost. The more informative comparison, however, is local. Uptake at this hospital, where specialist eye care is available on site, was no higher than at Debark Hospital and was lower than the 55.1% reported across five other public hospitals in Addis Ababa [19], although that study measured a single dilated examination in the previous year rather than a composite of recommended practices. This suggests that the availability of screening capacity is not, by itself, the binding constraint. Attention should instead fall on how patients are identified, counseled and referred within the diabetes clinic.

Participants aged 41-65 years had more than three times the odds of adequate screening practice compared with those aged 18-40 years, consistent with findings from north-east Ethiopia [12], Hawassa [21] and South Africa [11]. Age-related ocular morbidity accumulates during this period, leading to more frequent contact with eye services [22]. In contrast, younger adults may have less flexibility to attend appointments because of work and study commitments [23]. The lack of a significant association in the oldest age group is notable and may reflect a countervailing effect: reduced mobility and greater dependence on others for transport can offset the higher clinical need of older patients.

This interpretation aligns with the prominence of transport-related barriers in our data.

Currently married participants had higher odds of adequate screening practice than those who were not, consistent with evidence that spousal support improves uptake of preventive services in general [24]. Educational attainment showed a clear graded relationship: participants with higher education had almost four times the odds of adequate practice compared with those who had no formal schooling. This pattern has also been observed at Felege Hiwot Specialized Hospital in Ethiopia [25] and in the Swiss CoDiab-VD cohort [15]. Higher health literacy is likely to improve both the comprehension of screening advice and the capacity to navigate referral pathways.

The strong association with a known eye disorder carries the clearest public-health message.

Participants who already had an ocular diagnosis had almost four times the odds of adequate screening practice. This suggests that eye examination in this population is often prompted by existing ocular problems rather than undertaken as routine prevention, although the cross-sectional design cannot exclude the reverse explanation that eye disorders were detected because participants attended. Similar patterns have been described in north-east Ethiopia [12], at Debark Hospital [13] and in southern India [26]. This is precisely the pattern that screening programs are designed to reverse, because the window in which treatment can preserve vision closes before symptoms appear.

Adequate knowledge was associated with a fivefold increase in the odds of adequate practice, consistent with studies from Kenya [27], Turkey [28], Bangladesh [17], Indonesia [29] and the United Kingdom [30]. Because knowledge is modifiable and because almost all participants (96.2%) had already received information on diabetes and its complications, most often from clinic staff, this represents the most tractable point of intervention: the content of existing counseling, rather than its reach, appears to be the limiting factor.

The inverse association between a favorable attitude and adequate screening practice is counterintuitive and warrants cautious interpretation. The relationship was absent in the unadjusted analysis and only emerged after multivariable adjustment, a pattern that typically signals residual confounding or collinearity, most plausibly with knowledge, given the strong inter-correlation of these constructs in knowledge-attitude-practice scales. Compounding this, the attitude measure suffered from a pronounced ceiling effect, with nearly three-quarters of participants classified as favorable, thereby reducing its ability to differentiate behavior. Beyond these methodological limitations, a substantive explanation remains plausible: even participants who value screening may be unable to act on that preference when confronted with the practical barriers of cost, time, and distance that participants most often reported. Similar gaps between positive attitudes and actual attendance have been reported in Saudi Arabia [31] and in northern India [32]. We therefore view this finding as hypothesis-generating, underscoring the need for future work that disentangles attitudinal measures from the structural constraints that ultimately shape screening behavior.

These results point to three practical priorities. First, diabetic retinopathy screening should be offered opportunistically at every diabetes follow-up visit, using a documented annual due-date in the clinic record. Second, given that the optometry service operates within the same hospital, a direct internal referral pathway from the medical referral clinic to the triage screening point would eliminate the need for a separate patient journey. Third, counseling should be restructured to address the specific knowledge gaps identified here, particularly the asymptomatic nature of treatable retinopathy. Cost was the barrier most often reported, yet uninsured participants were more likely than insured participants to have adequate screening practice (47.2% vs 33.5%), a difference that was not statistically significant after adjustment. This may reflect limited coverage of eye examinations under current insurance schemes or differences in the characteristics of uninsured patients attending this hospital, and the role of insurance benefit design in screening uptake warrants further study.

Strengths and limitations

The strengths of this study include a sample size adequate for the planned analyses, a high response rate, systematic random selection of participants, use of an instrument adapted from previously published and locally pre-tested tools, and the fact that it provides the first estimate of screening practice from an Ethiopian facility in which specialist eye care is co-located with diabetes followup.

Several limitations should be considered. The cross-sectional design precludes any inference about the direction of the observed associations, particularly for knowledge and attitude, which may equally be consequences of prior screening attendance. Screening practice was self-reported, and interviews were conducted by facility nurses, so recall and social desirability bias are likely. The study was conducted at a single tertiary facility with co-located specialist eye services and a predominantly urban, relatively well-educated population, so the findings are unlikely to generalize to rural or primary care settings. Clinical measures known to influence both retinopathy risk and care-seeking, notably glycemic control, were not available, so residual confounding cannot be excluded. Age and diabetes type were closely related (70 of the 82 participants with type 1 diabetes were aged 18-40 years), so the adjusted estimates for these two variables should be interpreted with caution. Responses to the attitude items were highly uniform, with most participants giving the same response to all six items; this limited the scale's ability to distinguish between participants and may partly explain the unexpected direction of the attitude association.

Conclusions

Fewer than four in ten adults with diabetes attending a tertiary hospital in Addis Ababa underwent diabetic retinopathy screening at the recommended frequency, despite the availability of on-site specialist eye care. Screening was strongly associated with having a known eye disorder, suggesting that eye examination is often prompted by existing ocular problems rather than undertaken for prevention. Adequate knowledge of diabetic retinopathy was the strongest modifiable factor associated with screening uptake. Integrating diabetic retinopathy screening into routine diabetes follow-up, establishing a streamlined referral pathway to on-site eye services, and strengthening patient counseling to emphasize the asymptomatic nature of early disease may improve screening uptake more effectively than general awareness campaigns alone. Future studies should verify screening attendance using clinical records and include primary care settings and health-care provider perspectives to better inform strategies for improving screening coverage.
