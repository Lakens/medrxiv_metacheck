How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

It failed to detect 27 statements. Thus, among all statements flagged by the module, 63% were genuine cases (positive predictive value). However, the module missed 42% of all true statements (false negative rate). This validation was made with an earlier, looser pattern; the pattern now requires “significant” or “significance” to follow the trigger word closely (#695).

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Full text

Abstract

Clinical interventions for cancer treatment toxicities can reduce morbidity, mortality, and healthcare cost, if high risk patients can be identified. We developed SPARC, a transformer-based foundation model, using real-world, longitudinal, routinely collected health measurements, to predict adverse event risk. Pre-training and fine-tuning SPARC on 101,920,379 measurements from 224,047 patients with cancer led to state-of-the-art performance at predicting cancer-related adverse events on (i) held-out data from the development cohort, (ii) a prospective validation cohort from the same center (Npatients=23,126), (iii) multiple publicly available external datasets (Npatients=26,012), and (iv) external, multi-hospital cohorts (Npatients=562,620, 206,840). SPARC generalized to multiple external centers and identified patients most likely to benefit from an intervention (G-CSF). Pooled multi-institution, more than single-institution, fine-tuning improved generalizability. We release SPARC as a community resource. Our results highlight the utility of AI time series models in oncologic management.

Introduction

Cancer treatment toxicities are a major source of morbidity, healthcare cost, and mortality. Common cytotoxic chemotherapies cause febrile neutropenia 1 and acute kidney injury (AKI) in up to a third of patients. 2, 3 Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI), now used for a wide range of cancers, can cause serious immune-related adverse events in up to 15% of patients. 4 Frailer patients at imminent risk of death may benefit from palliative care rather than cancer-directed therapy. 5 Identifying patients at the highest risk of these adverse events would allow for morbidityreducing treatment strategies including dose reduction or administration of prophylactic supportive medications.

Quantitative data from longitudinal electronic health records (EHRs), including laboratory values and vital signs, remain underutilized for cancer outcome prediction. Models leveraging continuous clinical time series have shown promise at predicting outcomes in the intensive care unit (ICU)https://paperpile.com/c/r0viBk/4k47O+wbfia 6, 7 a setting fundamentally different from most cancer care in sampling frequency and treatment setting. EHR data have also been used in forecasting cancer before its formal diagnosis. [8] [9] [10] However, cancer mortality and adverse event prediction has generally relied on static nomograms built on a small number of variables and limited to specific disease and treatment contexts. [11] [12] [13] [14] The full potential of these routinely collected data for cancer side effect prediction remains underexplored.

Given previous findings that deviations from personalized baselines in laboratory values and vital signs are more informative than standard ranges for disease diagnosishttps://paperpile.com/c/r0viBk/ovClq 15 modeling such patient-specific baseline physiology can inform patient outcome prediction. More generally, the value of leveraging temporal AI models capable of fully capturing longitudinal trends remains unclear in patients undergoing cancer treatment. Here, we used patient-specific clinical time series trajectories for routinely collected quantitative data to train a foundation model to predict cancer treatment-related adverse events and mortality, and evaluated the capacity of the model on key clinical end points.

We hypothesized that transformer-based foundation models trained on routinely collected, longitudinal EHR measurements can better predict cancer treatment-related adverse events and mortality than methods based on static data or single time points. To test this hypothesis, we: 1.

Assembled a model development dataset of 224,047 patients with cancer (101,920,379 measurements) from Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center (MSK, New York, NY USA). 2. Developed SPARC (Surveillance of Patient Adverse events using Routine Clinical data), a time-series, transformer-based foundation model pretrained on this institutional dataset. 3. Tested model input and training objectives to optimize SPARC performance for downstream predictive tasks. 4. Tested SPARC and other longitudinal and single-timepoint models to predict clinically actionable treatment-related adverse events and mortality. 5. Assessed model consistency across patient subgroups, identified key predictive features, and demonstrated SPARC's potential to identify patients most likely to benefit from prophylactic therapy, e.g. granulocyte colony stimulating factor (G-CSF), a prophylactic medication for chemotherapy-induced neutropenia. 6. Further validated these models' utility in a prospective validation dataset and four external validation datasets spanning patient data from eleven distinct hospitals. We also tested the utility of additional site-specific fine-tuning.

Results

Routine clinical time series reflect the personalized nature of cancer progression

We first analyzed a development cohort of 224,047 patients from MSK seen between 2015 and 2021 with at least one measurement for each lab test in the complete blood counts and comprehensive metabolic panels (for demographics see Table 1). To characterize the overall structure of these measurements, we computed pairwise Pearson correlations between features across patients, using each patient's first value per feature. As expected, blood cell counts were correlated according to lineage, with red blood cell and immune related labs clustered respectively. Similarly, measurements for liver enzymes, kidney function markers (BUN and creatinine), and blood pressure were also correlated within each respective module. These modular trends were consistent across cohorts (Supplemental Figure 1). As in cohorts of patients without cancer, women had lower red blood cell and creatinine levels, and older patients were more likely to have anemia or reduced renal function (i.e. elevated creatinine and BUN).

In order to assess the patient specificity of different time series features, we then computed the ratio between within-patient, temporal variability and inter-patient variability of each feature. Like others 15 https://paperpile.com/c/r0viBk/ovClq we observed (Supplemental Figure Figure 2) that intra-patient variability in laboratory values was often greater than inter-patient variability; i.e. individual patients often have unique baselines. As previously identified in healthy patients, hematological lab tests had individualized baselines in patients with cancer. We similarly found individualized baselines in liver function tests, consistent across cohorts. This motivates personalized, time series modeling to better identify individual patient trajectories rather than models based on cohort averages.

Pretraining encodes clinical states

To create an AI model that can identify meaningful changes in physiology while accounting for individual patient baselines, we pretrained a time series transformer model, SPARC, based on a missingness-aware autoregressive objective. Specifically, SPARC was pre trained to predict lab values at the last time point of a randomly selected patient window. After pretraining on 157K windows containing an average of 10.2 time points and 211.9 measurements per window, SPARC predicted held-out future lab values with a mean squared error (MSE) of 0.423, compared with 0.872 for the feature mean, 0.590 for carrying forward the most recent value, 0.519 for per-feature linear regression on the most recent value, 0.487 for a ridge regression on all most-recent values and their missingness, and 0.451 for a ridge regression on historical lab values. SPARC had lower MSE than each baseline (Wilcoxon signed-rank p < 10⁻⁶ for each comparison; Supplementary Tables 2 3). A UMAP of the embedding space confirmed that SPARC's latent representations, derived from autoregressive pretraining, encode patient future laboratory and vital sign values (Supplementary Figure 3a). Embeddings for patients of select cancer types whose abnormalities are captured in routine lab tests also form clusters without labelled supervision (Supplementary Figure 3b).

SPARC prediction tasks and design optimizations

To test whether SPARC and related time series models outperform static time-point models, we trained or fine-tuned multiple models to predict a range of cancer-related adverse events. Because of well-documented concerns about the accuracy of billing codes as ground truth for medical events, we focused on adverse events that were unambiguously encoded in the EHR either through mortality status or quantitative measurements: A. mortality within 180 days following hospital discharge, which has implications for hospice discharge decisions B. mortality within 30 days following ICU admission, which may help with triaging of critical care resources C. anemia, within 30 days of an infusion of a cytotoxic chemotherapy associated with these side effects D. neutropenia within 30 days of an infusion of a cytotoxic chemotherapy associated with these side effects E. febrile neutropenia within 30 days of an infusion of a cytotoxic chemotherapy associated with these side effects; F. AKI within 30 days of either chemotherapy or bisphosphonate infusion; G. hepatotoxicity within 90 days of infusion of ICI [16] [17] [18] [19] [20] [21] Details including lab value cutoffs and agents included are provided in Methods. For events C-G, each infusion cycle of the relevant medication was considered a separate prediction window in which an event could occur. Only infusions before and up to the first such adverse event per category were included (e.g. if a patient had a neutropenic event during multiple chemotherapy infusion events, only the first infusion with a neutropenia event and any preceding infusions were included as prediction windows).

We optimized SPARC through a series of design studies, varying one component at a time, its feature set, architecture, and loss function. Because these experiments required multiple candidate modalitieshttps://paperpile.com/c/r0viBk/sp9BG we used a harmonized dataset (N=57,510) including tumor genomic profiling with MSK-IMPACT and annotated data 22, 23 including genetic ancestry, cancer type, organ sites of involvement, socioeconomic status (Yost index), 24 functional status (ECOG score) 25 , smoking status, and less commonly ordered labs. The final architecture was then retrained on the full MSK cohort and validated on held-out, prospective and external datasets.

We evaluated whether adding these less routinely captured modalities improved predictive performance relative to models based on standard laboratory values and vital signs finding that the rarer data modalities did not consistently improve performance (Supplementary Figure 4a). As such we restricted subsequent training to laboratory and vital sign measurements. Interestingly, adding medication types as features and training subcohort-specific models also did not improve performance at predicting medication side effects (Supplementary Figure 4b-c).

Motivated by the modular structure seen in the correlation analyses (Supplemental Figure 1), we found that multi-layer perceptron embeddings grouped by input type (i.e. red blood cell-based, liver-based, etc.) outperformed a single mixed embedding fed all raw inputs in most tasks (Supplementary Figure 4d). As a result, models with modular embeddings were used for all downstream analyses.

Most cancer treatment spans multiple cycles. We therefore tested whether combining binary cross-entropy with a time-to-event loss function 26https://paperpile.com/c/r0viBk/PmNrD+AMeZv 27 , where we substitute time to event with number of infusion cycles to side effect onset, would yield performance gains over a purely binarized outcome (see Methods, Supplemental Figure 4e, Supplemental Table 4). Because of improvements in performance using time-to-event loss, such loss functions were used for medication side effect prediction tasks.

We also investigated whether a more effective representation of time further improves model performance. SPARC maps each measurement's relative timestamp through periodic functions with learned rates and offsets, then linearly projects the result into an embedding added to the visit's modular embeddings. Compared with a raw relative timestamp or no time input, this continuous-time encoding matched or improved performance on all seven tasks (Supplemental Figure 4f). We thus incorporated this continuous-time encoding into the SPARC architecture.

The SPARC architecture may be trained from scratch to perform prediction tasks without pretraining, though the amount of training data required for this is unclear. In our analysis, pretraining was especially important in a regime with less training data. In all tasks, we see a negative correlation between increasing training data size and the benefit pretraining provides prior to supervised training (p<0.05 in hospital mortality, ICU mortality, anemia, Supplementary Figure 5). This initial design phase informed our final SPARC model (Figure 1). The final model uses a modular design built on parsimonious, routinely collected clinical time series features: complete blood count tests (e.g., red blood cell count, hemoglobin concentration), comprehensive metabolic panel tests spanning kidney function (e.g., creatinine), liver function (e.g., ALT), and electrolytes (e.g., sodium), along with routine vital signs (e.g., heart rate). The model undergoes autoregressive pretraining followed by task-specific finetuning, using Cox losses for the adverse event tasks.

Benchmarking models for cancer adverse event prediction

We tested the performance of SPARC as well as several other AI architectures for either time series or static time point data for predicting cancer adverse events (Figure 2, Supplemental Table 5). In a held-out test set, SPARC outperformed or matched all other models at predicting both mortality tasks as well as AKI, anemia, liver toxicity, and neutropenia. TabPFNhttps://paperpile.com/c/r0viBk/73yEr a foundation model pretrained on large-volume tabular data 28 , performed best at predicting the rare febrile neutropenia side effect. Other time series models, including both neural network and tree-based models, did not outperform SPARC or TabPFN at any tasks. In addition, single time point models generally did not outperform time series models. Together, these results highlight the relative utility of pretrained transformer architectures for cancer adverse event prediction tasks using clinical time series data.

We further compared SPARC performance in AKI, anemia, febrile neutropenia, and anemia with nomograms from literature [12] [13] [14] . As we lacked standard inputs for the nomogram or used different endpoint definitions, we retrained nomograms on our cohort using overlapping features for fairness of comparison. SPARC significantly outperformed both the original and retrained nomograms in anemia and febrile neutropenia prediction and matched the performance of nomograms for neutropenia prediction (Figure 3a). As the number of AKI events was small, it was challenging to compare SPARC with nomogram in AKI predictions, although SPARC performed significantly better than random chance, whereas the nomogram did not.

Model interpretability and subset analyses

We quantified the relative importance of input features to SPARC's predictions using LIME (Figure 3b, Supplemental Figures 6), 29 averaging attributions across patients. For the highlevel summary in Figure 3b, we averaged the magnitude of these attributions over the patient's history, weighting each time point by its overall contribution. As expected, red cell indices (hematocrit, hemoglobin, and red blood cell count) contributed most to anemia prediction, white blood cell and platelet counts contributed most to neutropenia prediction, and liver function tests (alkaline phosphatase, ALT and AST) contributed most to liver toxicity prediction. Febrile neutropenia prediction was driven chiefly by temperature, platelet count and age. Interestingly, the red cell distribution width (RDW) was the single most important laboratory predictor of AKI. Time relative to the last included measurement was the most important feature for AKI and among the most important for liver toxicity, but contributed less to anemia, neutropenia and 30D ICU mortality prediction. Blood urea nitrogen (BUN) was among the top predictors of both 180D hospital mortality and 30D ICU mortality, whereas ICU mortality prediction relied more heavily on vital signs (temperature, oxygen saturation, respiratory rate and blood pressure). We also observed that feature missingness in CBC differential tests which are less frequently prescribed contributed more to model prediction than the other lab tests. Temporally, the cumulative importance of a patient's history outweighed that of the single most recent measurement for six of seven tasks, most markedly for 180D hospital mortality, 30D ICU mortality and febrile neutropenia, with anemia being the exception (Supplemental Figures 6b). This demonstrates that SPARC integrates longitudinal clinical information rather than relying on a single most recent snapshot and does so in a task-dependent manner. SPARC thus leverages both diverse clinical and temporal features to accurately predict adverse events. Each of these prediction tasks span heterogeneous groups of patients. To better understand the predictive power of these models, we performed subset analyses of treatmentrelated adverse events stratified by treatment regimen. In these analyses, SPARC retained high accuracy in a majority of cases, although performance was heterogeneous in some tasks (Supplementary Figure 7, Supplemental Table 6). AKI was most accurately predicted in patients receiving biphosphonates (AUROC 0.87); performance was weaker for patients receiving cisplatin (AUROC 0.64). We performed further stratified analyses based on cancer type. In these analyses, SPARC achieve robust performances in cancer types with large amounts of training data (breast, prostate cancers) as well as sarcoma and lymphoma.

SPARC supports G-CSF prophylaxis decisions

We sought to assess SPARC's ability to identify patients who may derive significant benefit from timely prescription of prophylaxis mitigating treatment side effects. We performed Inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) matched analysis on the held-out test cohort to test whether SPARC's predicted risk strata were associated with the number needed to treat (NNT) with granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF) to avoid neutropenia events. Patients who received G-CSF prophylaxis had lower observed neutropenia event rates in the medium and high risk, but not low risk strata. Furthermore, NNT decreased across increasing predicted risk (Figure 3c, Supplemental Figure 8). These results demonstrate that SPARC can identify high-risk patients in whom G-CSF prophylaxis is most useful, supporting its use to target prophylactic interventions.

SPARC generalizes in prospective and external validations

We sought to test the reliability and generalizability of SPARC through validation on orthogonal cohorts, including 1. A prospective validation cohort including 23,126 patients from Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center after data lock for SPARC training, 2. Patients with cancer from the publicly available MIMIC-IV dataset 30 (Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center ICU patients, Boston MA, N=25,466) , 3. Patients with cancer from the publicly available EHRSHOT dataset 31 (Stanford University Medical Center, Stanford CA, N=546), 4. The CAIA consortium, which includes four NCI-designated cancer centers (total N=562,620), including Johns Hopkins Hospital (Baltimore, MD), Dana-Farber Cancer Institute (Boston, MA), and Fred Hutchinson Cancer Center (Seattle, WA), along with MSK (excluded from this analysis), 5. The INSIGHT dataset 32 https://paperpile.com/c/r0viBk/yqKgo a multihospital consortium including six comprehensive hospitals across New York City, NY and Houston, TX (total N=206,840).

The demographics of these cohorts is presented in Supplementary Table 1. More women received care at CAIA tertiary cancer centers compared to comprehensive hospitals at the INSIGHT dataset due a high incidence of breast cancer in the former. Tertiary cancer centers in CAIA included more non-Hispanic White and Asian patients, whereas more Hispanic and Black patients were treated at INSIGHT hospitals.

In the prospective validation cohort, the only task where the performance consistently degraded by more than 5% in AUROC was febrile neutropenia, possibly reflecting the small number of events and changes in patient case mix over time (Figure 4a). These results confirm SPARC's validity across time despite changes in cancer treatment paradigms and temporally associated environmental factors.

We first assessed SPARC zero-shot at the three external cancer centers in the CAIA consortium. Febrile neutropenia was the only task that consistently degraded relative to the MSK held-out set (maximum ΔAUROC ~10%), again likely reflecting limited training data, lower precision in evaluation metrics for rare events, and institutional differences. For all other tasks, results remained close to, and occasionally exceeded, those at MSK, suggesting the model generalizes to other cancer centers.

We next validated the model at six hospitals in the INSIGHT consortium (anonymized as A-F). Hospital E lacked enough eligible patients to evaluate liver toxicity, febrile neutropenia, and neutropenia. Zero-shot results held up for higher-prevalence tasks, including 180-day mortality, anemia, AKI, and liver toxicity, with AKI and liver toxicity slightly outperforming MSK at hospitals A and E, respectively. Consistent with the CAIA findings, febrile neutropenia and neutropenia proved harder to predict, in line with the greater sensitivity of low-prevalence endpoints to crossinstitutional shift.

Finally, we tested SPARC on cancer patients from two public datasets spanning comparable time scales, MIMIC-IV-hosp and EHRSHOT. Results in EHRSHOT were comparable to MSK, whereas MIMIC-IV-hosp showed weaker performance, possibly because its inclusion criteria (ICU or emergency department admission) create patient populations that differ notably from the training population.

Fine-tuning on external data

Motivated by the demographic shift and performance loss observed in the INSIGHT data, we investigated whether locally fine-tuning MSK-trained SPARC models further improved performance (Figure 4b). We fine-tuned MSK-trained SPARC models in two ways: (i) using only training data from each individual INSIGHT hospital and validating using a held out dataset in that hospital, and (ii) pooled fine-tuning using data from all INSIGHT hospitals and evaluating on heldout data from a single hospital site. Interestingly, pooled fine tuning improved on the zero-shot baseline in more task-site pairs than site-specific fine-tuning (24/30 vs 15/30; discordance 10 vs 1; McNemar exact p = 0.0117). We further found that, by titrating the amount of training data from the pooled training set for model training, performance on most tasks plateaued with one-eighth of the pooled data, whereas liver injury continued to improve with more data, with smaller gains for anemia and mortality (Supplemental Figure 9). These findings underscore the value of aggregating data across sites rather than fine-tuning on any single site in isolation, with the amount of pooled data needed varying by outcome.

Discussion

We have shown that routinely collected EHR time series measurements can predict cancer outcomes and treatment side effects. Pretraining was helpful in many tasks, but not all, and primarily in low data regimes. Integrative time series modeling performed nearly uniformly better than single time points, or nomograms. Without further optimizations, SPARC generalized to other cancer hospital settings, and for many prediction tasks, to non-cancer center hospitals. Adding more training data from other hospitals only improved SPARC's performance for more challenging tasks, like liver toxicity; for many tasks, SPARC has already achieved maximum performance in contrast to scaling law observations in other domains. 33 Our results have direct implications for precision supportive care. Patients at highest SPARC-predicted risk of neutropenia were most likely to benefit from G-CSF administration, directly motivating the use of SPARC in interventional, randomized studies of G-CSF administration.

Our study has limitations. The variable performance of SPARC with cancer types and treatment agents may be due to either the inherent difficulty of these tasks or inadequate data. Selection bias can guide which patients receive which medications, with ramifications for prediction; for example, AKI was hard to predict in patients following cisplatin chemotherapy possibly because clinicians are less inclined to prescribe cisplatin to patients with underlying renal dysfunction. We only used SPARC for outcomes clearly defined by laboratory results, further work should consider outcomes derived from clinical notes. For the outcomes studied here, we incorporated additional modalities and found no substantial added predictive values. However, modalities including comorbidities and imaging have proven important for predicting other outcomes, such as treatment response and cancer recurrence [34] [35] [36] [37] [38] . Future efforts can expand these modalities to broaden the range of predictable outcomes. Although we validated SPARC models using large, geographically and demographically diverse cohorts, all these patients were treated at academic medical centers in large US cities. Laboratory testing patterns may differ in community and non-US hospitals due to resource constraints, insurance coverage, and demographic differences.

We present SPARC as a community resource, and we hope that our results support future work in translating AI modeling into further personalization of care.

Genomic features

Static genomic features were derived from the MSK-IMPACT targeted tumor sequencing cohort, as described previously 39 for the harmonized ablation cohort. Each sequenced sample was annotated for the presence of nonsynonymous, truncating and OncoKB-annotated oncogenic mutations in each panel gene; gene-and allele-level cancer hotspot mutations; focal amplifications and deep deletions; arm-level copy-number gains and losses; gene fusions; pathway-level alterations; mutational signatures inferred from trinucleotide substitution contexts; and continuous measures of mutational burden, including tumor mutational burden, fraction of genome altered and microsatellite instability score. To allow samples sequenced on the 341-, 410-, 468-and 505-gene panel versions to be represented consistently, features were restricted to genes on the IMPACT341 panel, which is common to all versions, yielding 5,044 genomic features per sample. Sample metadata and demographic covariates were excluded from this feature block, as they are represented by separate model modules, and any remaining missing values were set to zero. Patients with more than one sequenced sample were represented by their most recently accessioned sample.

Additional features

Sex was one-hot encoded with male as the reference level (1 feature). Age at first sequencing was binned into 5-year intervals and passed as a single ordinal value (1 feature). These two features are included in the parsimonious feature set.

Each patient in the harmonized ablation cohort carries a set of additional static features as available from MSKCC cBioPortal repository and the MSK-CHORD dataset 40 https://paperpile.com/c/r0viBk/1AUdJ including the patient-specific Yost index, an area-level socioeconomic measure passed as a continuous value imputed to the cohort median, ranging 1-100; ECOG performance status as a single ordinal value ranging 0-4, with unrecorded status set to 0; smoking status, one-hot encoded over three classes (former/current, never, unknown) with unknown as the reference level (2 features); and primary cancer type, one-hot encoded over 34 categories with "others" as the reference level (33 features), including non-small cell lung, colorectal, breast, prostate, pancreatic, endometrial, ovarian, bladder, esophagogastric, hepatobiliary, head and neck, thyroid, cervical, appendiceal, salivary gland, bone, and small cell lung cancers, renal cell carcinoma, cancer of unknown primary, glioma, melanoma, non-melanoma skin cancer, mesothelioma, soft tissue and uterine sarcoma, gastrointestinal stromal tumor, germ cell tumor, leukemia, mature B-cell and mature T-and NKcell neoplasms, myeloproliferative neoplasms, myelodysplastic syndromes, and myelodysplastic workup; one-hot encoded genetic ancestry (6 categories: EUR, ADM, EAS, AFR, SAS, NAM); and one-hot encoded site of metastatic disease (10 categories), including adrenal glands, bone, CNS/brain, intra-abdominal, liver, lung, lymph nodes, pleura, reproductive organs, and other.

Description of prediction tasks

The fine tuning tasks for SPARC are defined as follows: • 180D hospital mortality: binary classification following the latest available hospital discharge for each given patient, as motivated by the 6-month mortality risk estimate as required by Medicare to reimburse hospice care 41 .

Patients whose last contact was prior to 180 days post discharge and remained alive at that date were excluded.

• 30D ICU mortality: binary classification following the latest available ICU admission for each given patient, to assess the benefit of ICU admission to the patient given only information prior to admission. Patients whose last contact was prior to 30 days post ICU admission and remained alive were excluded.

• Grade 3 anemia: binary classification of whether a patient's hemoglobin falls below 8 g/dL per CTCAE definition 42 https://paperpile.com/c/r0viBk/PtD5w within 30 days following an infusion (or before the next infusion, whichever comes first) of a treatment regimen including one or more of: docetaxel, sacituzumab govitecan, cisplatin, topotecan, irinotecan, irinotecan liposomal, cyclophosphamide + doxorubicin + vincristine (in combination), bendamustine, carboplatin, etoposide, cabazitaxel. The qualifying medications are selected based on risk of hematological adverse events per NCCN guidelines 43.

• Grade 3 neutropenia: binary classification of whether a patient's absolute neutrophil count falls below 1https://paperpile.com/c/r0viBk/PtD5w000 cells/µL per CTCAE definition 42 , within 30 days following an infusion (or before the next infusion, whichever comes first) of a regimen including one or more of the same medications for grade 3 anemia.

• Febrile neutropenia: binary classification of whether a patient's absolute neutrophil count falls below 1,000 cells/µL together with a temperature ≥ 38.0 °C measured within 2 days of each other, within 30 days following an infusion (or before the next infusion, whichever comes first) of a regimen including one or more of the same medications for grade 3 anemia.

• Acute kidney injury: binary classification of whether a patient meets KDIGO serum-creatinine criteria for AKI (stage ≥ 1)https://paperpile.com/c/r0viBk/BQ7jg a rise of ≥ 0.3 mg/dL within 48 hours, or ≥ 1.5X a rolling 7-day baseline (most recent prior value, floored at 0.5 mg/dL 44 , within 30 days following an infusion (or before the next infusion) of a regimen including one or more of: cisplatin, ifosfamide, pemetrexed, methotrexate, pamidronate, zoledronic acid. The qualifying medications are selected per Onconephrology Core Curriculum 2023 45.

• Grade 3 liver toxicity: binary classification of whether a patient experiences either bilirubin > 5 mg/dLhttps://paperpile.com/c/r0viBk/PtD5w or AST / ALT / ALP exceeding 5× baseline per CTCAE definition 42 , within 90 days following an infusion (or before the next infusion) of a regimen including one or more of: ipilimumab, atezolizumab, nivolumab, pembrolizumab, avelumab, cemiplimab, dostarlimab. The qualifying medications are selected as they increase risk of hematological adverse events per ASCO irAE guidelines 46.

A patient's infusions after they have a positive event were excluded from further model training or evaluation.

Cohort-level feature correlation and coefficient of variation analyses

Within each cohort, we characterized the inter-feature and time-dependent properties of laboratory, vital sign, and demographic features.

To assess inter-feature relationships, we computed pairwise Spearman rank correlation coefficients using each patient's first available value for each feature and visualized the resulting correlation matrices.

To assess time-dependent variability, we computed a CV (coefficient of variation) ratio for each feature using an approach consistent with Foy et al. 15 The CV is defined as the ratio of the standard deviation to the mean of a given distribution. The inter-patient CV was the CV of all measurements of a feature pooled across patients. To make intra-patient variability independent of follow-up length, the intra-patient CV was computed within 60-day windows and sliding in 30day steps. For each patient, we took the median CV across windows containing at least three measurements of the feature, and then the median across patients with at least one such window.

The CV ratio is then intra-patient CV divided by the inter-patient CV, where a lower value indicates that a feature's variability is driven more by within-patient fluctuation over time than by differences across patients.

SPARC Pretraining

SPARC has a predefined maximum context window of k timepoints (default k = 20) and is pretrained using a missing-and time-aware autoregressive objective. All longitudinal features are first z-score normalized per feature across all patients. For each patient with m timepoints (dates) with at least one measurement (m ≥ 2), we sample a consecutive window of length a, where a is drawn from [2, min(m, 1 + k)]. SPARC learns to use the first (k -1) timepoints in the window to predict the last timepoint's normalized feature values, with mean squared error loss computed over non-missing features at the last timepoint. Timepoints within the context window that are unfilled (k -a + 1 remaining slots) are handled via padding, and missing features within sampled timepoints are assigned a sentinel value of -10 to distinguish them from z-score normalized nonmissing values. Multiple non-overlapping windows may be sampled sequentially from the same patient's record; to prevent overrepresentation of patients with long clinical histories, sampling is capped at three windows per patient.

SPARC setup and finetuning

The default, optimized SPARC has the following designs: • A parsimonious feature set: gender, 5-year binned age, and the parsimonious lab and vital signs features • Module-specific feed-forward networks prior to transformer encoder: RBC related (HGB, HCT, RBC, MCV, MCH, MCHC, RDW), immune-related (WBC, Neut, Mono, Lymph, Baso, Eos, Platelets), renal and metabolic (Creatinine, BUN, Sodium, Potassium, Chloride, CO2, Calcium, Glucose), hepatic (ALT, AST, ALP, Bilirubin Total, Albumin, Protein Total), routine vitals (BMI, Systolic BP, Diastolic BP, O2, Pulse, Resp Rate, Temperature), age (5-year binned age), gender.

• Missingness for each time series feature is an explicit input, where each module's feature value projection receives the concatenation of its feature values and their missingness indicators. In addition, each module token is multiplied by the fraction of that module's constituent features actually observed at that visit.

• Continuous-time encoding: elapsed time is encoded with learned Fourier features of the relative number dates with respect to the last lab/vital date to accommodate irregular intervisit spacing and variable numbers of visits. 256 frequencies and 256 phases are initialized on a log scale (as in sinusoidal positional encoding) and learned during training; the resulting sine and cosine features are projected through a single linear layer to 512 dimensions and added to the embedding prior to transformer. 10-fold cross validation with one-fold held-out testing was used: data was split randomly into 11 equally sized folds, with one held out for testing; in each of 10 rounds of cross validation, one out of ten non-heldout folds was used for validation and the rest for training. The SPARC model is first pretrained, and then, for each of the 7 tasks, finetuned using the pretrained model serving as weight initialization, both using the 10-fold cross validation set up. In held-out test set evaluation or evaluation using external data, SPARC predictions were obtained by averaging the predicted probabilities of the 10 resulting finetuned models.

Titration experiments

We sought to further investigate the benefits of pretraining, especially where a learned prior from a larger population may help the model converge given limited data. To this end, we randomly titrated the training data in the MSK dataset to 1/4, 1/16, 1/64, 1/128, 1/256, when available, and compared the performance of directly trained versus pretrained-then-finetuned SPARC models.

Benchmarked model architectures

We compared SPARC to the following model architectures, adapted to predict the equivalent finetuning task outcomes with task-specific hyperparameter tuning using first-fold validation loss. These included those trained with only the last timepoint feature valueshttps://paperpile.com/c/r0viBk/Sn8vu including MLP model 47 https://paperpile.com/c/r0viBk/YL87B implemented using the pytorch package 48 https://paperpile.com/c/r0viBk/eEc5a XGBoost 49 as implemented in the scikit-learn 50 packagehttps://paperpile.com/c/r0viBk/YwCzC as well as T-XGBoost, with input features equivalent to SPARC fed into XGBoost as features, TabPFN v3 51 , an in-context learning time series foundation model, as well as DuETT 52 , where our features were averaged and binned to cater to its inputs, and Raindrop https://paperpile.com/c/r0viBk/BqL8H a graphical neural network based EHR model. Significance was assessed using a paired bootstrap of the model predictions on the held-out test set (1,000 resamples), as implemented in the sci-kit learn 50 package. Unless otherwise stated, the principal metric reported is weighted AUROC for sideeffect tasks and overall AUROC for mortality tasks for performance analyses. Weighted AUROC is the average of within-cycle AUROCs, weighted by the number of patients per cycle. This captures discrimination between patients at the same treatment stage rather than differences in baseline side-effect rates across cycles, and the weighting limits the influence of smaller later cycles. AUROC-based metrics were chosen because their standardized range allows comparison across tasks. Additional metrics, such as AUPRC, are reported in the supplementary materials.

Downstream task clinical benchmarks

We compared SPARC to the following clinical nomograms and settings in the held-out test set of the ablation cohort where there is sufficient data required for the nomograms. We compared SPARC against each nomogram's published coefficients, and we also refit the same features on our training and validation data, for fairness of comparison, using DeLong's test to assess statistical significance.

• AKI: Motwani et al. 13 built a 4-variable additive score from age, first-course cisplatin dose, history of hypertension and serum albumin, converted to probability with the published calibration (4% baseline incidence, OR 1.49 per point). We evaluated it on each patient's first cisplatin-containing cycle; baseline eGFR was left out, as in the original model.

• Febrile neutropenia: Aagaard et al. 12 built FENCE score to predict first-cycle febrile neutropenia from sex, age, cancer type, disease setting, albumin, bilirubin, eGFR, infection, number of drugs and chemotherapy classes, and we used its published regression coefficients to compute cycle-1 FN probability. As in the original study, we restricted evaluation to first cycles and excluded patients on weekly cisplatin; we also report a logistic regression refit on the same features.

• Neutropenia: Lyman et al. 11 built a logistic model for first-cycle severe or febrile neutropenia from age, prior chemotherapy, laboratory values (AST, alkaline phosphatase, bilirubin, GFR, WBC), cancer type, chemotherapy class and G-CSF prophylaxis. We evaluated both the published coefficients and a version refit on our training data; planned relative dose intensity was left out because it cannot be reconstructed from our records.

• Severe anemia: Dranitsaris et al. 14 developed a logistic model for severe chemotherapyinduced anemia (Hb <8 g/dL) in breast cancer, using age, TNM stage, baseline hemoglobin, steroid use, MCV and GFR. Due to the limited number of well labelled breast cancer samples in the test set, we applied the published odds ratios to our pan-cancer cohort, which makes it an off-population external validation with a closely matched grade-3 anemia endpoint.

Feature importance analysis

LIME 29 values were used to evaluate the relative importance of each feature at each time point. For each task, we repeated the analysis with each of the ten cross-validation models on the same test set. For each fold, signed importances were averaged across test windows, and the fold-level maps were then averaged. Missingness indicators and padding were excluded from reporting. To understand the relative importance of each feature and the contribution of time series, we calculated each feature's mean absolute importance across time points weighted by the importance of each time point.

External validation and fine tuning

We fine tuned SPARC models at INSIGHT institutions in two ways, using the equivalent model designs and hyperparameters at MSK: site-specific and pooled. Because of limited data, each site's patients were randomly split into three equally sized sets for training, validation and testing.

In the site-specific setting, models were fine-tuned and validated on a single site's training and validation sets. In the pooled setting, the training and validation sets of all sites were combined for fine-tuning. In both settings, performance was evaluated separately on each site's held-out test set. The ICU mortality task was not included in external validation because ICU admission and discharge dates could not be mapped reliably. Certain tasks at certain INSIGHT institutions with low count of <20 patients were excluded in evaluation.

G-CSF statistical analysis

SPARC predictions for the neutropenia task were generated on the MSK test set. A sample was considered G-CSF-exposed if G-CSF was administered within 2 days after the prediction cutoff date (and before its onset). Samples were stratified into tertiles of SPARC-predicted risk. The propensity of receiving G-CSF was estimated by L2-regularized logistic regression on the standardized model inputs (feature values, missingness indicators, visit times and infusion number), trained on the training set. On the test set, these scores were recalibrated against G-CSF receipt alone, without using outcomes, and then used for weighting. As the number of control samples matched with G-CSF treated samples is a hyperparameter that may change results, for the main analysis, we used inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) with overlap weights and unexposed infusions overall and within each tertile (Supplementary Figure 8b).

As a sensitivity analysis, we used propensity score matching (Supplementary Figure 8a). Each G-CSF-exposed infusion was matched to 1, 2 or 10 unexposed infusions via nearest propensity score (nearest-neighbor matching with replacement). within the same risk tertile and infusion cycle, by nearest neighbour on the logit propensity score with a caliper of 0.2 standard deviations, with replacement. NNTs, 95% confidence intervals and p-values were computed with the same patient-level bootstrap.
