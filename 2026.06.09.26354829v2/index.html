Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. 1 Introduction

Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by the degeneration of both upper and lower motor neurons, ultimately leading to muscle weakness, atrophy, and death [1]. Although its clinical manifestations are well described, the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms remain only partially understood.

One of the most consistent neurophysiological findings in ALS is cortical hyperexcitability [2] [3] [4], with potential utility for prognosis and stratification [5]. Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) can be used to assess short-interval intracortical inhibition (SICI), which indexes cortical excitability, by probing GABA-A-mediated inhibitory interneuronal circuitry [6] [7] [8]. Reduced SICI reflects diminished inhibitory control and the consequent increase in neuronal recruitment. SICI is diminished in ALS, both idiopathic and familial [9] [10] [11], before lower motor neuron impairment [2]. Increased cortical hyperexcitability has been linked to greater functional impairment, as measured by ALSFRS-R scores [12], while reduced average SICI independently predicts shorter survival in early-stage patients [13] [14] [15].

Although cortical hyperexcitability has been extensively investigated in motor circuits, ALS-related alterations also involve regions beyond the motor system [16] [17] [18] [19]. Accordingly, the reorganization of brain dynamics extends beyond motor areas, encompassing the entire brain [20], and is strongly associated with clinical impairment [20] and with disease staging [21]. These observations motivate investigation of how local circuit alterations interact with distributed network organization to shape whole-brain dysfunction. This points to widespread changes in meso-scale circuitry that reverberate across the whole brain. Together, these observations support the hypothesis that whole brain dysfunction in ALS is best understood as the result of the interaction between local cortical hyperexcitability and the active, potentially compensatory or maladaptive, functional reorganization of distributed brain networks.

However, the link between local cortical hyperexcitability and distributed brain dysfunction remains difficult to establish from empirical measurements alone. TMS-derived metrics provide a powerful and clinically relevant index of inhibitory dysfunction within motor cortical circuits, but they do not directly reveal how altered excitation-inhibition reshapes whole-brain dynamics. Conversely, whole-brain electrophysiological or imaging markers can capture distributed reorganization, but they remain largely descriptive unless embedded within a framework that explicitly models the underlying biophysical mechanisms.

These limitations motivate the adoption of model-based approaches [22]. In fact, mechanistic wholebrain models allow us to move beyond purely correlational analyses by simulating how different, nondirectly observable neurodegenerative processes may give rise to measurable large-scale brain dynamics [23] [24] [25]. By comparing these simulated dynamics, generated by "virtual brain twins" with data recorded from individual patients, one can estimate latent pathophysiological variables, thereby characterizing the pathophysiological processes underlying the observed brain activity.

We hypothesized that model inversion would identify a recurrent-excitation parameter associated with ALS-related alterations in resting-state brain dynamics. We further hypothesize that the correlation between motor-frontal hyperexcitability and clinically relevant features would be more prominent than that with non-frontal regions. In this study, we analyzed a cohort of 39 patients diagnosed with ALS and 39 controls, for whom clinical parameters, source-reconstructed magnetoencephalography, and magnetic resonance data were acquired, as in [20]. First, we assessed whether features extracted from resting-state brain activity could distinguish ALS patients from controls and whether these alterations were associated with clinical outcomes. The characterization of clinical features was performed under the hypothesis that aperiodic bursts of activity (i.e., neuronal avalanches) would index pathophysiological processess [20, 26] . This empirical characterization provided the basis for subsequent computational modeling and model-inversion analyses. To test whether the observed changes in local excitability could explain the observed shifts in large-scale brain dynamics, we used the reduced Wong-Wang model [27, 28], which describes the mean synaptic activity of interacting excitatory populations. Whole-brain neural activity was simulated by coupling neural masses via structural connectomes derived from diffusionweighted tractography [29, 30]. Because of its low-dimensional formulation, this model is particularly well-suited to whole-brain simulations aimed at linking regional excitability-related parameters to emergent macroscale neural dynamics. Along these lines, rather than explicitly introducing a separate parameter for inhibition, we abstracted the local excitation-inhibition balance within each cortical region through the recurrent excitation parameter. Two complementary model configurations were examined.

In the first, we assumed spatially uniform recurrent excitation, and jointly inferred the global recurrentexcitation parameter w and the global coupling parameter G. This configuration allowed us to test whether ALS-related alterations in whole-brain dynamics were compatible with an overall increase [31] in recurrent excitation while accounting for the contribution of interregional coupling. In the second model configuration, we divided the brain into two anatomically defined partitions: a motor-frontal partition, including frontal and motor cortical areas, and a complementary partition comprising the remaining regions. The second model configuration was motivated by the heterogeneous anatomical distribution of ALS-related pathology. Although ALS primarily affects the motor system, including the primary motor cortex and corticospinal pathways, premotor and frontal association regions are also fre-quently involved, with the latter particularly implicated in patients exhibiting cognitive and behavioral impairment [?, [32] [33] [34] [35]. Conversely, although alterations may extend throughout the brain, the clinical manifestations of ALS are generally dominated by motor and, to a variable extent, frontal-executive dysfunction [36] [37] [38]. Based on these observations, we introduced a parsimonious anatomical subdivision into a motor-frontal partition comprising 34 regions, including primary motor, premotor, and frontal association cortices, and a complementary non-frontal partition comprising the remaining 46 regions. Importantly, this subdivision does not imply that regions outside the motor-frontal partition are unaffected, nor that all regions within it are equally involved. Rather, it provides a simplified, biologically motivated framework to test whether ALS-related alterations in local recurrent excitation are more pronounced in anterior regions than in the rest of the cortex. In particular, we hypothesized the motorfrontal regions to be more strongly hyperexcitable and proportional to individual clinical features. To test this hypothesis, separate recurrent-excitation parameters, w F and w N F , were assigned to the two partitions and jointly inferred with the global coupling parameter G. This allowed us to investigate the spatial heterogeneity of inferred recurrent excitation while accounting for interregional coupling. Both configurations were inferred independently using summary features extracted from whole-brain avalanche transition matrices. To this end, we adopted a Bayesian framework based on simulation-based inference (SBI) [39], which enables efficient inversion of complex generative models [40, 41]. This approach was applied at the individual level, allowing us to investigate both group-level alterations and interindividual variability in inferred parameters. Finally, we examined whether the estimated parameters were associated with clinical measures of functional impairment and disease staging. An overview of the computational framework is provided in Fig. 1.

2 Results

2.1 Characterization of large-scale dynamics of empirical data

In this study, we set out to identify informative features from MEG data acquired in ALS patients and controls (CTRL), and to produce a generative model to explain empirical data features as a function of local hyperexcitability. We identified neuronal avalanches as bursts of activities spreading across the whole brain, using a threshold-based approach [42], and turned to avalanche-based metrics (see also Methods). To characterize the spatial propagation of neuronal avalanches, we computed the Avalanche Transition Matrices (ATM) [43]. Each entry ATM ij encodes the probability that a suprathreshold activation in region i is followed by one in region j , within the same neuronal avalanche and then averaged across all avalanches. These matrices therefore describe how transient regional activations propagate through the brain. The ATM is intrinsically directed, as the probability that activation in region i is followed by activation in region j need not equal the probability of the reverse transition. Fig. 2 displays the ATM averaged across ALS patients (Fig. 2a) and healthy controls (Fig. 2b). Warmer colors correspond to higher transition probabilities between brain regions. Fig. 2 (c-h) report the distributions of the selected metrics of interest (MOIs). We computed the mean and median separately for the strictly upper and lower triangles, excluding the diagonal, and complemented these summaries with their Frobenius norm and entropy of the full matrix. Under the regional ordering held constant across participants and simulations, the two triangles summarize opposite directions for the same unordered pairs of regions. They provide complementary descriptions of the directed transition structure, rather than representing distinct anatomical subnetworks. Here, directionality refers to the temporal ordering of activations and does not establish causal influence between regions. The mean ATMs averages the transition probabilities across the regional pairs included in the analysis and therefore summarizes the overall propensity for activity propagation during avalanches. Higher mean ATM values indicate that suprathreshold activation in one region is, on average, more likely to be followed by suprathreshold activation in another region. ALS patients exhibited higher mean and median transition probabilities in the upper triangle than controls (Fig. 2c, mean: Cohen's d = 0.83, 95% CI [0.41, 1.29], p = 0.000523; panel e, median: d = 0.84, 95% CI [0.41, 1.32], p = 0.000480). In the lower triangle, the mean was also higher in ALS (Fig. 2d) (d = 0.47, 95% CI [0.03, 0.95], p = 0.0419), whereas the median (Fig. 2f) showed a smaller, nonsignificant difference in the same direction (d = 0.39, 95% CI [-0.05, 0.87], p = 0.0932).

Together, these findings indicate higher average avalanche transition probabilities in ALS, consistent with an enhanced propensity for large-scale activity propagation. ATM entropy (Fig. 2g) showed a negative but nonsignificant group difference (d = -0.27, 95% CI [-0.73, 0.18], p = 0.238). This measure quantifies the distribution of normalized singular values, with lower entropy indicating a greater concentration of spectral weight in fewer dominant components. Higher entropy reflects a more even contribution of multiple spectral modes. Stronger propagation does not necessarily imply greater spectral diversity, as transition probabilities may increase without substantially changing their relative spectral organization.

Although the negative effect estimate was consistent with a greater concentration of spectral weight in dominant modes, the group difference was not statistically significant. Together, these findings indicate that group differences were more pronounced in the strength of avalanche propagation than in the spectral organization of the underlying transition matrix. The Frobenius norm of the ATM (Fig. 2h) was higher in ALS patients (d = 0.55, 95% CI [0.12, 1.03], p = 0.0182) , indicating an increased overall magnitude of avalanche transition probabilities. The convergent increases in mean, median, and norm support an amplification of avalanche-mediated activity spreading at the whole-brain level, compatible with the hypothesis of cortical hyperexcitability in ALS. We additionally investigated whether the six empirical ATM features were associated with functional impairment and disease staging in ALS patients. Spearman rank-order correlations were computed between each feature and ALSFRS-R, King's stage, and MiToS stage (Supplementary Fig. SM1). Although some associations were observed, only the median of the upper-triangular entries was associated with King's stage at the nominal significance level (Spearman's ρ = 0.345, unadjusted p = 0.0425, q = 0.1102). None of the associations survived Benjamini-Hochberg correction across the three clinical scales within each metric. Thus, the empirical ATM features revealed alterations in large-scale brain dynamics at the group level but did not exhibit detectable corrected 5 . associations with the examined clinical outcomes when considered individually.

2.2 Computational model and simulation-based inference to investigate a mechanistic substrate of altered ALS brain dynamics

To mechanistically interpret the empirical alterations described above, we investigated whether the largescale dynamical signatures extracted from resting-state MEG recordings could emerge from changes in recurrent excitation and interregional coupling within a tractography-informed whole-brain model. We employed a reduced Wong-Wang neural mass model in which regional excitatory populations interact through empirical structural connectivity (Fig. 3a; see Methods).

The parameter w controls recurrent excitation uniformly across regions, whereas the global coupling parameter G scales interactions between regions. Representative simulated synaptic activity and empirical MEG signals are illustrated in Fig. 3b. To examine the contributions of these two mechanisms, we varied w and G jointly over the ranges [2, 4] and [1.5, 2.5], respectively. For each simulation, we computed the same six whole-brain ATM features used to characterize the empirical recordings: the mean and median of the upper and lower triangles, the Frobenius norm, and SVD-based entropy. Each feature was independently normalized across the simulation ensemble. The resulting parameter-space maps show how these observables depend on the combination of local recurrent excitation and global coupling (Fig. 3c-h). Mean and median transition probabilities generally increased with w in both triangles of the ATM, accompanied by an increase in the Frobenius norm. These changes indicate that stronger recurrent excitation enhances the propensity for avalanche-related activity to propagate across the network. Global coupling also modulated these features: at a given excitation level, differences in G shifted the resulting transition statistics. Thus, the magnitude of avalanche propagation reflected contributions from both local recurrent excitation and interactions mediated by structural connectivity. ATM entropy showed an opposing overall pattern, with lower values at higher recurrent excitation. Because this measure summarizes the distribution of normalized singular values, its reduction indicates that the transition matrix becomes more strongly dominated by a smaller number of components. Stronger propagation can therefore coexist with a more concentrated organization of avalanche-related interactions, consistent with the reinforcement of dominant configurations.

The direction of the simulated increases in the ATM mean, median, and norm, along with the positive ALS -CTRL effect sizes observed empirically, are consistent with hyperexcitability in ALS patents. The simulated decrease in entropy matched the direction of the (nonsignificant) empirical effect in the same direction. The dependence of the features on both w and G means that the empirical alterations cannot be attributed to recurrent excitation from these qualitative comparisons alone. We therefore used the simulated feature vectors to train a joint inference model for p(w, G | y), allowing us to estimate the recurrent excitation and the global coupling while also assessing the corresponding uncertainty.

Using simulations sampled from uniform priors over w ∈ [2, 4] and G ∈ [1.5, 2.5], we trained a neural density estimator to approximate the joint posterior p(w, G | y) (Fig. 4a). This analysis tested whether the two parameters could be distinguished despite their overlapping effects on large-scale activity propagation. Validation in 800 held-out synthetic simulations revealed different levels of recovery precision for the two parameters. The marginal posterior distributions, shown for six illustrative cases, successfully recovered the variation in ground-truth values (Fig. 4b,e). Across the entire test set, the posterior means of w closely followed the identity line, while the estimates of G exhibited greater dispersion (Fig. 4d,g).

These results indicate that the selected ATM features supported the recovery of recurrent excitation, although the uncertainty is larger for G as compared to w. To assess the confidence of the inference, we additionally evaluated the posterior shrinkage and the posterior z-score metrics. According to established simulation-based inference diagnostics, the ideal inference regime is characterized by posterior shrinkage values approaching 1 and posterior z-scores close to 0, reflecting strong parameter identifiability and accurate posterior calibration [44, 45]. In our analysis, shrinkage was generally higher for w than for G, indicating a stronger reduction in uncertainty relative to the respective priors (Fig. 4c,f) and demonstrating that the observed features strongly constrained the latent parameter space. Absolute posterior z-scores were low for most test cases, although larger standardized errors occurred for both parameters. Additional parameter-dependent recovery analyses revealed that estimation accuracy and credible-interval coverage varied across the investigated prior ranges, with larger errors and reduced coverage near some parameter's boundaries (Supplementary Figs. SM3 and SM4). These results indicate that aggregate recovery statistics may conceal local estimation biases and nonuniform posterior calibration, which should be considered when interpreting individual parameter estimates. Together, these findings support the feasibility of jointly estimating recurrent excitation and global coupling from wholebrain ATM features, with stronger relative posterior concentration and more accurate point recovery for w. The synthetic validation provides a basis for applying the joint model to empirical recordings and examining whether the ALS vs CTRL difference in recurrent excitation persists when global coupling is estimated rather than fixed.

2.3 Bayesian estimation of cortical excitability reveals hyperexcitability in ALS

We next applied the validated inference framework to empirical whole-brain ATM features, jointly estimating the global recurrent-excitation parameter w and the global coupling G for each participant. The parameter w modulates the effective recurrent excitation of cortical populations and therefore controls the operating regime of large-scale brain dynamics. This approach allowed us to assess whether the ALS-related increase in recurrent excitation persisted when interregional coupling was estimated rather than fixed. Figure 5a,b shows the joint posterior distributions for illustrative (i.e., selected randomly) ALS and control participants. The elongated contours indicate posterior dependencies between w and G, highlighting the importance of retaining uncertainty in both parameters during model inversion.

These individual examples illustrate the joint estimates; group comparisons were based on subject-level posterior means. The inferred global coupling did not differ significantly between groups (p = 0.558, d = 0.14; Fig. 5c). In contrast, inferred recurrent excitation was significantly higher in ALS patients than in controls (two-sided Welch's t-test, p = 0.00432, Cohen's d = 0.68; Fig. 5e). Although the distributions overlapped, ALS patients showed a group-level shift toward higher w values. Thus, the increase in model-derived recurrent excitation remained detectable when global coupling was allowed to vary across participants. Within the tested model and prior ranges, this finding supports an interpretation of the empirical ALS dynamics in terms of enhanced recurrent excitation, rather than an effect contingent on fixing G to a single value. The absence of a statistically significant group difference in G suggests that the observed alterations are more strongly associated with recurrent excitation within the present model.

All in all, these results indicate that the empirical dynamical alterations observed in ALS are consistent with a shift toward a hyperexcitable cortical state.

To assess the reliability and informativeness of the inversion procedure, we quantified posterior shrinkage, which measures how much the empirical data constrain the posterior distribution relative to the prior distribution. Posterior shrinkage indicated substantial reductions in uncertainty relative to the priors for both parameters (Fig. 5d,f). Shrinkage for w was consistently close to one, whereas G exhibited greater variability across participants, consistent with the differential precision observed in synthetic validation.

These results show that the fitted posterior distributions were more concentrated than the priors, particularly for recurrent excitation.

Together, these results indicate that ALS-related changes in large-scale avalanche dynamics are compatible with increased model-derived recurrent excitation, even when uncertainty in global coupling is explicitly incorporated. This finding supports recurrent excitation as a candidate mechanism contributing to the altered propagation dynamics observed in ALS, while remaining distinct from an independent physiological measurement of cortical excitability.

2.4 Effects of regional recurrent excitation on whole-brain dynamics

Building on the spatially uniform model, we next investigated regional differences in recurrent excitation.

We distinguished excitation in the 34 motor-frontal regions (w F ) from that in the remaining 46 regions (w NF ). In this configuration, both regional excitation parameters and global coupling G were inferred jointly from the whole-brain ATM features. To illustrate the effects of regional excitation, Figure 6 shows an exploratory parameter-space analysis at a fixed illustrative coupling value of G = 1.89. This value corresponds to the mean of the subject-specific posterior estimates of G obtained for healthy controls in the preceding spatially uniform model inversion. For each pair (w F , w NF ), we generated regional time series and computed whole-brain activity summaries (that is, the average synaptic activity and its variance) and the six ATM features extracted from the empirical recordings. The fixed coupling value was used only for this exploratory analysis; global coupling remained a free parameter in the subsequent subject-specific inference. Fig. 6a shows higher mean synaptic activity toward parameter combinations with high excitation in both partitions, along with decreased variance. Stronger recurrent excitation was therefore associated with higher sustained activity and smaller fluctuations. Representative frontal time series from the highlighted parameter combinations illustrate these contrasting regimes (Fig. 6b).

Within the range w F , w NF ∈ [2, 4], the six whole-brain ATM features exhibited distinct dependencies on the two parameters (Fig. 6c-h). Each point represents one simulation, with color indicating the corresponding feature value normalized across the displayed simulation ensemble. Mean and median transition probabilities generally increased with recurrent excitation (Fig. 6c,d,e,f). Both parameters nevertheless contributed to the resulting feature landscapes. The Frobenius norm increased toward combinations with high excitation in both partitions (Fig. 6g), consistent with an increased overall magnitude of avalanche-related transition probabilities. ATM entropy showed the opposite overall pattern, with the lowest values occurring when excitation was high in both partitions (Fig. 6h). Together with the reduction in synaptic-activity variance, this pattern is compatible with a regime in which stronger activity propagation coexists with more constrained dynamics. Increased excitation may therefore reinforce dominant configurations of coordinated activity rather than expand the diversity of configurations explored by the network. The differing sensitivities of the whole-brain features motivated their combined use for joint inference of (G, w F , w NF ). This formulation allowed us to investigate regional differences in recurrent excitation while accounting for uncertainty in global coupling and retaining posterior dependencies among all three parameters.

2.5 Joint regional inference identifies elevated motor-frontal recurrent excitability associated with functional impairment

We next applied the regional model to empirical whole-brain ATM features, jointly estimating motorfrontal recurrent excitation w F , other regions recurrent excitation w NF , and global coupling G for each participant. This analysis investigated whether allowing recurrent excitation to vary between anatomical partitions revealed alterations and clinical associations not captured by the spatially uniform model.

To infer the three parameters from empirical observations, we employed the same simulation-based inference framework used for the spatially uniform model. Specifically, a neural density estimator was trained on simulated whole-brain dynamics generated from known combinations of (G, w F , w NF ), learning an approximation of the joint posterior distribution conditioned on the six whole-brain ATM features. This approach enabled simultaneous estimation of the two regional recurrent-excitation parameters and global coupling while retaining potential dependencies among them, similarly to previous applications of Bayesian whole-brain model inversion [40].

3 Discussion

In this study, we combined source-reconstructed MEG recordings, whole-brain dynamical modeling, and simulation-based inference to investigate whether alterations in large-scale brain dynamics in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) could be explained by changes in recurrent excitation. Building on previous analyses of this cohort [20] and established whole-brain modeling approaches [27, 28], we first characterized the empirical dynamics using directed avalanche transition matrices (ATMs). We then examined how recurrent excitation and interregional coupling shaped the corresponding observables in a reduced Wong-Wang model. Finally, we inferred subject-specific model parameters using two complementary configurations: a spatially uniform model and a regional model distinguishing motor-frontal from the remaining brain regions. The motor-frontal partition allowed us to examine whether whole-brain avalanche dynamics were compatible with preferential recurrent excitation in motor and frontal systems, motivated by their involvement in ALS. This partition is a hypothesis-driven simplification of the disease's heterogeneous anatomical distribution. In particular, it does not explicitly separate frontotemporal systems associated with cognitive and behavioral manifestations, which may occur independently of memory impairment.

Alternative partitions distinguishing extended motor, frontotemporal, and remaining regions could provide a more differentiated account, but would require additional validation of parameter identifiability and more refined clinical stratification, especially in terms of assessments of extra-motor symptoms.

The results indicate that ALS-related changes in avalanche propagation are consistent with increased model-derived recurrent excitation, while the regional parameterization reveals an association between motor-frontal excitation and functional impairment.

The empirical analysis revealed higher avalanche transition probabilities in ALS patients than in healthy controls, particularly in the mean and median of the upper ATM triangle, together with an increased Frobenius norm (Fig. 2). These findings indicate an increased propensity for sequential regional recruitment during neuronal avalanches. Importantly, the ATM describes the temporal ordering of suprathreshold activations within avalanche events, rather than direct anatomical or causal interactions. The different effect magnitudes observed in the upper and lower triangular summaries reflect properties of the directed transition structure under the chosen regional ordering and should not be interpreted as evidence for alterations in distinct anatomical pathways. In contrast to the measures of transition magnitude, SVD-based ATM entropy showed a negative but nonsignificant group difference. Although stronger propagation could coexist with a greater concentration of activity in dominant dynamical configurations, the present results do not establish a reduction in the diversity or complexity of brain dynamics in ALS.

ATM-derived spectral entropy should be distinguished from sequence-based measures of neural complexity, such as Lempel-Ziv complexity. While the latter quantifies the diversity and compressibility of temporal or spatiotemporal activity sequences, ATM spectral entropy characterizes the distribution of singular-value weight in a matrix summarizing interregional avalanche propagation. Accordingly, these measures capture different properties of neural dynamics and need not exhibit the same relationship with cortical excitability. In fact, some models suggest that excitability would relate to higher complexity [46], while others point in the opposite direction. In our simulations, increased recurrent excitation was associated with stronger avalanche propagation and lower ATM spectral entropy, suggesting a greater concentration of transition structure into dominant matrix components. However, the empirical reduction in ATM entropy observed in ALS patients did not reach statistical significance. Therefore, our findings do not establish a reduction in temporal neural complexity in ALS. Overall, the most consistent empirical finding was an increase in avalanche-related transition strength. The modeling results provide a mechanistic bridge between the empirical alterations observed in ALS and recurrent excitation, which is not directly observable from the MEG features, and is understood here as a hidden variable.

Hence, the model's epistemic scope accounts to providing a possible mechanistic interpretation of these observations. Increasing local recurrent excitation produced higher ATM mean and median values and a larger Frobenius norm, reproducing the direction of the principal empirical differences between ALS patients and controls (Fig. 3). The simulated decrease in ATM entropy was also directionally consistent with the empirical effect, although the latter was not statistically significant. These relationships suggest that stronger local recurrent feedback can promote sequential activity propagation across structurally connected regions. However, global coupling also influenced the simulated features, indicating that recurrent excitation and interregional interactions jointly determine the resulting whole-brain dynamics. This motivated the joint inference of excitation and coupling parameters, rather than attributing the observed alterations to recurrent excitation based on parameter-space explorations alone.

To infer latent model parameters from empirical data, we used simulation-based inference for Bayesian model inversion. This approach is well suited to whole-brain neural mass models, where the relationship between parameters and observed dynamics is nonlinear, stochastic, and analytically intractable. Simulations across the prior parameter ranges were used to train neural density estimators of subject-specific posterior distributions. Validation on held-out synthetic simulations supported parameter recovery, although precision varied across parameters. Posterior shrinkage further indicated substantial reductions in uncertainty relative to the priors, particularly for excitation parameters. The two inference configurations addressed complementary questions. In the first, recurrent excitation was assumed spatially uniform, and (w, G) were inferred jointly. In this case, ALS patients exhibited higher inferred recurrent excitation than controls, whereas global coupling did not differ significantly between groups (Fig. 5). The persistence of the excitation effect when coupling was estimated simultaneously supports the interpretation that the observed dynamical alterations are compatible with increased recurrent excitation within the investigated model and parameter ranges. Nevertheless, the absence of a significant group difference in coupling does not demonstrate equivalence between groups, and posterior dependencies indicate that compensatory relationships between parameters remain possible. Accordingly, the inferred excitation parameter should be understood as an effective model-derived quantity describing local recurrent feedback under the assumed generative model, rather than a direct measurement of physiological cortical excitability. As such, the inferred parameters will require empirical validation in future studies. This analysis tested whether ALS-related dynamics were compatible with an overall increase in excitation while accounting for global coupling. Although inferred w distinguished ALS patients from controls, it did not show statistically significant associations with the clinical measures examined.

The regional configuration, jointly inferring (G, w F , w NF ), examined whether the spatial distribution of excitation carried clinical information not captured by a single uniform parameter. Both motor-frontal (w F ) and remaining-region w N F recurrent-excitation parameters were higher in ALS patients, with a numerically larger standardized group effect for (w F ) (Fig. 8). The combined elevation of the two parameters was supported by the significant group difference in their sum. However, the regional contrast (w F -w N F ) did not differ significantly between groups. Thus, although the observed effect was more pronounced for motor-frontal excitation, the results do not establish a statistically significant diseasespecific imbalance between the two anatomical partitions. The findings support a distributed increase in model-derived recurrent excitation, with a potentially greater motor-frontal contribution that requires further investigation. This distinction is particularly important because the motor-frontal partition includes both motor-related and frontal association regions and does not represent an exclusively motor network. Moreover, both regional parameters were inferred from whole-brain ATM summaries, making their anatomical interpretation dependent on the predefined partition and the assumptions of the model.

The regional parameterization revealed an association with clinical impairment. Higher motor-frontal recurrent excitation was associated with lower ALSFRS-R scores, and this association survived correction for multiple comparisons (Fig. 8j). In contrast, the spatially uniform excitation parameter did not show a corrected significant clinical association (Supplementary Fig. SM2), nor did the individual empirical ATM features (Supplementary Fig. SM1). This suggests that, although the overall elevation of recurrent excitation may characterize ALS-related alterations in large-scale dynamics, the motor-frontal component may provide additional information about interindividual differences in functional impairment. Such an interpretation remains exploratory and does not establish that motor-frontal excitation is more strongly associated with clinical impairment than the other inferred parameters. For these reasons, the model inversion may integrate information distributed across several dynamical observables into a compact parameter associated with functional impairment.

The classification analyses further clarify the contribution of the inferred parameters. Motor-frontal excitation achieved a mean cross-validated AUC of (0.741 ± 0.016), numerically close to the (0.748 ± 0.029) obtained using the six original empirical ATM features, whereas the three jointly inferred regional parameters achieved an AUC of (0.700±0.025) (Fig. 8k; Supplementary Fig. SM3). These findings indicate that a single inferred parameter retained much of the disease-discriminative information represented by the original feature set, without demonstrating superior classification performance. Because the inferred parameters are estimated from the empirical observables, they cannot be interpreted as providing independent information absent from those inputs. Their principal contribution lies instead in translating descriptive properties of large-scale activity into parameters with a defined role in a generative dynamical model. Unlike the original ATM summaries, these parameters can be systematically varied to investigate the consequences of altered recurrent excitation and coupling, including dynamical regimes not directly sampled in the empirical recordings. Such simulations provide a basis for formulating mechanistic hypotheses and potentially testable predictions, rather than establishing diagnostic or clinical predictive utility, which will require perspective validation.

We also evaluated the generative model against empirical observations by producing subject-specific simulations using the inferred posterior mean parameters. For each participant, ten stochastic realizations were generated and the resulting ATM features were compared with those extracted from MEG recordings (Fig. 9). These analyses assessed whether the inferred parameter configurations could generate dynamical summaries compatible with the observations and characterized variability across noise realizations. The distribution of the empirical and simulated features were overlapping. However, one should keep in mind that the inference had been performed utilizing normalized data and, as such, the simulations also underent the respective anti-transformation. Hence, the similarity of the distributions should not be interpreted in terms of raw , but rather relative, magnitudes. These analyses therefore constitute a model-fit assessment rather than complete posterior predictive validation.

A complementary test of the simulated dynamics was obtained by examining neuronal avalanche-size and duration distributions (Supplementary Figs. SM6-SM9). Both empirical recordings and subject-specific simulations exhibited broad, decreasing distributions, indicating qualitative similarities in avalanche-like activity. The estimated avalanche-size exponents showed no statistically detectable difference between empirical and simulated data in controls after multiple-comparison correction, whereas simulated exponents were significantly higher in ALS patients. For avalanche durations, simulated exponents were systematically lower than their empirical counterparts in both groups. These discrepancies are informative because the inference procedure was constrained by ATM-derived features describing sequential regional recruitment within avalanches, whereas the distributions of avalanche size and duration were not directly fitted. Although avalanche duration may indirectly influence the number of transitions contributing to an ATM, neither the total amplitude-weighted avalanche size nor the full distribution of event durations are explicitly encoded by the selected summary statistics. The differences mentioned above identify aspects of the empirical dynamics that the present model does not fully capture and may provide useful additional constraints for future model development. Furthermore, approximate scaling over a restricted range should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of criticality or of a unique power-law generating mechanism. Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, the reduced Wong-Wang model represents recurrent excitation without explicitly resolving inhibitory populations.

The inferred parameters may therefore reflect effective changes in the excitation-inhibition balance, but the model cannot distinguish altered excitatory feedback from changes in inhibitory regulation. Second, all participants were simulated using a common structural connectome [43] derived from healthy subjects, rather than individual connectomes. Because recurrent excitation, global coupling, and network topology jointly influence whole-brain dynamics, the inferred parameters may partially accommodate effects of unmodeled individual or disease-related structural differences. Estimating global coupling as a free parameter does not eliminate this limitation, since a scalar parameter cannot reproduce changes in connection topology. Finally, independent physiological measures of cortical excitability, such as TMSderived indices, were unavailable for this cohort. Future studies should assess sensitivity to alternative connectomes and incorporate patient-specific structural connectivity where feasible.

Overall, the present study links alterations in large-scale avalanche propagation in ALS to changes in the recurrent-excitation parameters of a whole-brain generative model. The results support a distributed increase in model-derived recurrent excitation, with a larger observed motor-frontal group effect and an association between motor-frontal excitation and functional impairment. Beyond the discrimination of patients from controls, the main value of the framework is its capacity to provide a mechanistically interpretable representation of empirical dynamics and to generate model-dependent predictions that can be tested in future physiological and longitudinal studies.

Numerical simulations.

The stochastic differential equations were integrated using the Heun scheme with a time step of ∆t = 1 ms. Independent Gaussian noise increments were generated for each brain region and integration step as σ √ ∆t ξ i , where ξ i ∼ N (0, 1) and σ = 0.05, with time expressed in milliseconds. Each simulation lasted 300 s, with the first 60 s discarded as a transient. The same numerical protocol was used throughout each simulation ensemble.

4.3 Tractography data

The structural connectivity matrix used in this study was obtained from the healthy cohort previously investigated by Sorrentino et al. [43]. Specifically, we employed a group-averaged structural connectome derived from diffusion MRI tractography data acquired from 47 healthy participants. The reconstruction procedure, including diffusion MRI preprocessing, deterministic tractography, and estimation of interregional connectivity weights [43]. Diffusion MRI data for subject-specific connectome reconstruction were collected using echo-planar imaging (TR/TE = 12,000/95.5 ms; voxel size = 0.94 × 0.94 × 2.5 mm 3 ), with 32 diffusion-weighted directions and 5 non-diffusion-weighted (B0) volumes.

All datasets were corrected for head motion and eddy current-induced distortions using the eddy correct routine, with appropriate rotation of diffusion gradient directions. Brain masks were generated from B0 images using the Brain Extraction Tool. A diffusion tensor model was fitted voxel-wise, and whole-brain streamline reconstruction was carried out via deterministic tractography using Diffusion Toolkit, implementing the FACT propagation algorithm (angle threshold = 45 • , spline interpolation, and masking based on fractional anisotropy maps thresholded at 0.2). For connectivity analysis, regions of interest (ROIs) were defined according to the Automated Anatomical Labeling (AAL) atlas in MNI space. The atlas was constrained by gray matter tissue probability maps derived from Statistical Parametric Mapping (SPM; threshold = 0.2). Individual FA maps were spatially normalized to MNI space using the FSL FA template and the normalization routine implemented in SPM12. The resulting transformation matrices were inverted and applied to warp ROIs into each subject's native space. Normalization quality was verified through visual inspection. Structural connectivity was represented by a matrix C ij , whose entries quantify the strength of anatomical connections between brain regions. The connectome was aligned with the 80 regions considered in the present whole-brain model. The same group-averaged structural connectivity matrix was used for all simulations and was held fixed across ALS patients and healthy controls. This common anatomical scaffold was employed in both the spatially uniform and regional model configurations, ensuring that differences in simulated dynamics arose from variations in the model parameters rather than from differences in the underlying structural network. Consequently, individual variability and ALS-related alterations in structural connectivity were not explicitly represented in the model.

4.4 Feature Extraction

We characterized whole-brain dynamics using six summary features derived from directed Avalanche Transition Matrices (ATMs): the mean and median of the strictly upper and lower triangles, the Frobenius norm, and singular-value-based entropy. The same feature definitions and regional ordering were used for empirical recordings and simulated activity.

Neuronal avalanche detection and Avalanche Transition Matrices

Starting from source-reconstructed MEG signals, each regional time series is first standardized using a z-score transformation. To determine the activation threshold, we examined the distribution of standardized activity across participants and identified the point at which it deviated from a Gaussian distribution [42]. This deviation occurred at approximately 2.5 standard deviations, which was therefore adopted as the threshold for identifying suprathreshold activity. Time points exceeding this threshold are considered active, resulting in a binarized representation of the data. Based on this representation, neuronal avalanches are defined as contiguous periods of activity bounded by time bins in which no regions are active. The size of an avalanche is defined as the cumulative suprathreshold amplitude excess of the z-scored regional signals, summed over all regions and time samples belonging to each avalanche. Specifically, for an activation threshold of (|σ| = 2.5), avalanche size was calculated as ∑︁ r,t∈A max(|z r (t)|-2.5, 0). Avalanche duration corresponds to the number of consecutive time bins during which the avalanche persists. To validate the presence of neuronal avalanches, we examine the distributions of avalanche sizes and durations, which are reported in the Supplementary Material. These distributions exhibit power-law behavior, consistent with the characteristic signatures of avalanche dynamics in neural systems [54, 55].

Within each detected avalanche, we analyze the spatiotemporal propagation of activity by constructing avalanche transition matrices (ATMs) [20]. Each ATM encodes the probability that activation in one region is followed by activation in another region in the subsequent time step. Avalanches shorter than two samples were excluded. Avalanche-specific transition estimates were aggregated into a subject-level Avalanche Transition Matrix (ATM). The matrices were retained in their directed, non-symmetrized form, preserving differences between opposite transition directions. Accordingly, ATM ij and ATM ji were not constrained to be equal. Directionality denotes temporal ordering of suprathreshold activations and does not establish causal influence.

Subsequently, data features are extracted from the avalanche transition matrices (ATMs), including the mean value, median for the upper and lower triangles to take into account the directionality of the transitions, Frobenius norm and entropy. For each participant, the mean ATM was calculated as the arithmetic mean of the entries included in the analyses. It summarizes the average transition probability across regional pairs and therefore characterizes the average propensity for avalanche-related activity propagation. The Frobenius norm was calculated as the square root of the sum of squared matrix entries and quantifies the overall magnitude of the transition weights. Unlike the mean, it gives greater weight to larger entries. To characterize the organization of the directed matrix, we computed Shannon entropy from its normalized singular values. Empirical statistical comparisons.

For each metric shared between the ALS and CTRL groups, potential outliers were identified separately within each group using the interquartile range (IQR) criterion, defined as values lying outside the interval [Q1 -1.5 • IQR, Q3 + 1.5 • IQR]. Outlier observations were excluded from both statistical analyses and clinical correlations. Group differences were then assessed on the resulting datasets using Welch's two-sample t-test, which does not assume equal variances between groups.

For the model inversion procedure, all selected metrics were subsequently normalized to the [0,1] range using min-max scaling. This was done to place heterogeneous features on a common scale and to ensure that the inference procedure focused on reproducing the relative pattern of alterations observed across empirical metrics, rather than the absolute values of the individual metrics. Accordingly, the objective of the inversion was not to match the exact magnitude of each individual metric, but to capture the overall trend and relationships characterizing the empirical data.

Clinical associations.

Associations between subject-level parameter estimates and ALSFRS-R, King's stage, and MiToS stage were assessed using two-sided Spearman rank-order correlations. Each participant contributed one posterior mean per parameter; posterior samples were not treated as independent observations. Analyses used participants with available values for the parameter and clinical outcome.

4.5 Bayesian model inversion via simulation-based inference and pipeline validation

The Bayesian framework provides a principled approach for parameter estimation, prediction, and uncertainty quantification by updating prior knowledge with evidence from empirical observations [56]. In this setting, uncertainty is explicitly represented through probability distributions: prior beliefs about model parameters are updated using observed data to obtain posterior distributions. However, performing Bayesian inference on large-scale brain data is challenging due to the high dimensionality of the observations, the nonlinearity of brain dynamics, and the complex interactions imposed by the underlying brain network structure. In particular, the likelihood function is typically computationally intractable for such models, making conventional approaches such as Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) computationally expensive [39].

To overcome these limitations, we adopt a likelihood-free approach based on Simulation-Based Inference (SBI) [57, 58], in which the computational model is treated as a stochastic simulator capable of generating synthetic data that resemble empirical observations. This framework enables parameter inference without explicitly evaluating the likelihood function. Specifically, we use neural density estimation techniques based on Normalizing Flows (NFs), which learn flexible transformations from simple base distributions (e.g., the prior) to complex posterior distributions. By construction, these transformations are invertible and differentiable, enabling efficient sampling and exact likelihood or density evaluation.

Our objective is to infer the posterior distribution of the model excitability parameters that best explain the observed empirical features. To this end, we considered two complementary model configurations. In the first spatially uniform configuration, a single global excitability parameter (w) controlled the excitability of all brain regions, while G scaled interregional coupling. Joint inference of (w, G) addressed whether the empirical dynamics were compatible with increased recurrent excitation when global coupling was also allowed to vary. This configuration therefore provided a parsimonious assessment of a spatially uniform alteration in excitation without fixing the coupling regime. In the partition-specific configuration, the uniform excitation parameter was replaced by w F for the 34 motor-frontal regions and w NF for the remaining 46 regions. Global coupling G remained an inferred parameter. Joint inference of (w F , w NF , G) allowed us to examine whether the empirical dynamics were compatible with different excitation levels across the two anatomical partitions. The uniform model is recovered when w F = w NF . The two configurations thus address complementary questions concerning the overall elevation and regional distribution of recurrent excitation. They were fitted separately; estimates from the uniform configuration were not used to fix G in the partition-specific inference. Independent uniform priors were assigned to the parameters. Recurrent-excitation parameters were sampled over [2, 4], and global coupling over [1.5, 2.5]: p regional (w F , w NF , G) = U(w F ; 2, 4) U(w NF ; 2, 4) U(G; 1.5, 2.5).

Note that the independence of the priors does not impose independence on the inferred posterior distributions. We employ Sequential Neural Posterior Estimation (SNPE) [58], a simulation-based inference (SBI) framework that learns the relationship between model parameters and observed summary statistics directly from simulations. This approach is particularly well-suited for mechanistic whole-brain models, for which the likelihood functions are generally intractable. The posterior density is approximated using a Masked Autoregressive Flow (MAF) [59], a class of normalizing flows that enables flexible and computationally efficient posterior density estimation through a sequence of invertible nonlinear transformations. SNPE requires three key components: (i) a prior distribution over parameters, (ii) a generative model capable of simulating brain activity for a given parameter configuration, and (iii) a set of lowdimensional features summarizing the empirical data. In our case, the generative model is the reduced Wong-Wang whole-brain neural mass model, and the features are computed from source-reconstructed signals. Specifically, we extract a set of summary statistics, as described in the previous paragraph.

For participant s, the trained estimators were conditioned on the empirical feature vector y s,expt to approximate for the uniform model configuration, and for the partition-specific model configuration.

Both configurations used the same six whole-brain ATM features computed on the whole brain. Regional excitation parameters were therefore inferred from their combined effects on whole-brain dynamics, rather than from features computed separately within each anatomical partition. Joint posterior sampling retained dependencies among excitation and coupling parameters, including potential compensatory relationships. We generated 4,000 simulations for the spatially uniform configuration, jointly inferring (w, G), and 20,000 simulations for the partition-specific configuration, jointly inferring (w F , w NF , G). The parameter vectors were sampled from the corresponding prior distributions. Held-out simulations used for synthetic validation were excluded from training.

Once trained, each conditional density estimator was applied to participants' empirical feature vectors to evaluate and sample from the approximate posterior without additional simulations. This amortized inference procedure does not require the generative model to be differentiable.

Before applying empirical observations, we evaluated parameter recovery using held-out synthetic simulations with known ground-truth parameters. Test simulations were excluded from network training and generated using the same structural-connectivity matrix and simulation protocol as the training data.

The uniform-model ensemble comprised 4,000 simulations, of which 3,200 were assigned to training and 800 to held-out testing. The regional-model ensemble comprised 20,000 simulations, partitioned into 16,000 training and 4,000 test simulations. For each test simulation, the trained estimator was conditioned on the corresponding feature vector, and joint posterior samples were drawn. Parameter recovery was evaluated by comparing posterior means with ground-truth values. Marginal posterior uncertainty was summarized using posterior standard deviations and equal-tailed 95% credible intervals.

The quality of the inferred posteriors is assessed using diagnostic measures including posterior shrinkage and z-scores, defined as z = ( θ -θ true )/σ posterior , where θ is the posterior mean, θ true is the ground-truth parameter, and σ posterior is the posterior standard deviation.

We additionally evaluated marginal empirical coverage as the proportion of test simulations for which the 95% credible interval contained the ground-truth parameter. Coverage was compared with the nominal 95% level, and recovery plots were examined across the parameter range to identify regions with larger errors. High shrinkage was interpreted as posterior concentration rather than evidence of accurate recovery or calibration. The illustrated posterior examples complement the evaluation across the full held-out set. The resulting estimates exhibit low z-scores and high shrinkage, indicating accurate and informative Bayesian inference across the explored parameter range. Discrimination between ALS patients and controls.

We evaluated whether the inferred parameters discriminated ALS patients from healthy controls using separate classification models for each parameter. Each participant contributed one observation, represented by the corresponding posterior mean; posterior samples were not treated as independent observations. ALS was designated as the positive class. Classification was performed using L2-regularized logistic regression and nested stratified five-fold cross-validation, repeated five times with different random partitions. Identical outer splits were used across parameter-based classifiers. Within each outer training set, the inverse regularization strength was selected from C ∈ {0.01, 0.1, 1, 10, 100} using an inner stratified three-fold cross-validation, maximizing the mean validation ROC AUC. Predictors were standardized using statistics estimated exclusively from the corresponding training folds. For each repetition, predicted probabilities for the held-out participants were pooled across the five outer folds, yielding one out-of-fold prediction per par-ticipant. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were constructed by varying the classification threshold and plotting sensitivity against the false-positive rate. Discrimination was quantified by the area under the ROC curve (AUC). We report the mean and standard deviation of the five repetitionspecific AUCs. For visualization, ROC curves were interpolated onto a common false-positive-rate grid and averaged across repetitions. This analysis assessed discrimination conditional on the previously estimated model parameters and the upstream preprocessing and subject-selection procedures. The inference pipeline was not re-estimated within each classification fold; the evaluation therefore concerns the downstream classifier rather than an independently validated end-to-end diagnostic pipeline.
