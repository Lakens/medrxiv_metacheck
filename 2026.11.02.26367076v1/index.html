Power Analysis Check

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background: Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have transformed cancer treatment, yet evidence on their utilization, treatment trajectories, and effectiveness in routine clinical practice remains limited. We characterized real-world ICI use and outcomes across cancer types using the multicenter FAIR-ICI cohort and explored differences between real-world and pivotal trial outcomes.

We conducted a retrospective cohort study of adults receiving ICIs across four Belgian hospitals. Structured and unstructured electronic health record data were harmonized using the Observational Medical Outcomes Partnership Common Data Model (OMOP-CDM) and natural language processing. We evaluated overall survival (OS), time on treatment (ToT), time to next treatment (TTNT), ICI discontinuation (>12-week treatment gap without re-initiation), and post-discontinuation survival. OS was descriptively compared with pivotal trials, and prognostic factors were assessed using multivariable Cox regression. An interactive public dashboard was developed to enable exploration of cohort characteristics, treatment patterns, and outcomes.

Results: Among 3,231 ICI-treated patients, 2,514 (77.8%) had metastatic disease. Among metastatic patients, 568 (22.6%) discontinued ICI without subsequent systemic therapy. Median post-discontinuation OS was 21.4 months (95% CI 16.5-37.2), with 49.1% alive at 24 months. ToT and TTNT were generally shorter than OS, particularly in melanoma (median OS 78 months; ToT 7 months; TTNT 26 months). Real-world survival was frequently less favorable than in pivotal trials. ECOG ≥2 was associated with poorer OS than ECOG 0-1 (adjusted HR 2.18, 95% CI 1.82-2.60). In NSCLC patients receiving pembrolizumab plus chemotherapy, survival with ECOG 0-1 more closely approximated trial outcomes, whereas survival with ECOG ≥2 was substantially poorer.

Conclusions: Real-world ICI treatment trajectories extend beyond active treatment, with some patients experiencing prolonged survival after discontinuation. Differences between real-world and trial outcomes highlight the importance of patient characteristics, particularly performance status, when interpreting trial evidence in routine care. FAIR-ICI provides a scalable framework for evaluating real-world immunotherapy effectiveness, treatment trajectories, and future investigations of treatment-related toxicity.

Introduction

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have transformed cancer treatment, improving survival and becoming standard-of-care across multiple malignancies. Since the European Medicines Agency (EMA) approved ipilimumab for metastatic melanoma in 2011 1 , ICI indications have expanded rapidly across tumor types, disease stages, and treatment combinations 2, 3 . Recent estimates indicate that approximately 57% of patients with advanced or metastatic cancer may be eligible for ICI treatment 4 , highlighting the growing role of ICIs in contemporary oncology care.

Belgium, with around 77,000 new cancer diagnoses annually 5 , provides an informative setting for evaluating real-world ICI use. Since 2017, an accelerated reimbursement pathway has enabled access to ICIs immediately following EMA approval, rather than through the conventional Belgian reimbursement procedure 6 , with ICIs currently reimbursed across 23 cancer types 7 . A recent European Comparator Report ranked Belgium among countries with the highest ICI uptake (roughly six standard weekly doses per incident cancer case) 8 . At current Belgian list prices, this corresponds to an estimated drug cost of approximately €10,800 per incident cancer case. As ICI use continues to expand globally, evidence from this setting can inform understanding of real-world treatment patterns, effectiveness, and resource implications across healthcare systems.

Despite widespread use, evidence supporting ICIs remains largely derived from randomized controlled trials (RCTs), whose populations and treatment pathways may not reflect routine clinical practice 9 . Previous studies have estimated that approximately 70% of NSCLC patients were not eligible for pivotal phase III ICI trials 10 . Such differences may contribute to the efficacy-effectiveness gap between clinical trials and real-world populations. Furthermore, RCTs provide limited insight into treatment trajectories, including treatment duration, discontinuation, and transitions to subsequent therapy 11, 12 , which are important for understanding clinical decision-making, patient outcomes, and healthcare resource use.

Real-world evidence (RWE) complements clinical trials by evaluating treatment use and outcomes in routine practice 13 . However, comprehensive multicenter studies spanning multiple tumor types remain limited, often constrained by fragmented data sources and insufficient clinical detail 14 . Most studies focus primarily on overall survival (OS), whereas treatment-trajectory endpoints such as time on treatment (ToT) and time to next treatment (TTNT) are less frequently reported. Similarly, little is known about treatment discontinuation patterns, including the proportion of patients discontinuing ICIs without subsequent systemic treatment and their survival.

To address these challenges, the FAIR-ICI cohort was established as a multicenter Belgian real-world dataset integrating structured and unstructured electronic health record (EHR) data within a standardized Observational Medical Outcomes Partnership Common Data Model (OMOP-CDM) framework 15, 16 . Natural language processing (NLP) enriches structured data with clinically relevant information extracted from routine records. By combining multicenter patient-level data, standardized data harmonization, and NLP-enriched clinical information, FAIR-ICI enables high-resolution evaluation of ICI utilization, treatment trajectories, and outcomes across routine oncology practice.

In this study, we aimed to (1) assess patient demographics, disease characteristics, and treatment patterns among patients receiving ICIs; (2) estimate real-world outcomes, including OS, TTNT, and ToT; (3) characterize treatment discontinuation patterns and survival following discontinuation; and (4) compare real-world survival with pivotal clinical trials. To facilitate transparent exploration, we additionally developed an interactive dashboard that enables dynamic subgroup analyses and visualization of outcomes. Together, these analyses provide a comprehensive overview of ICI use and effectiveness in routine oncology care and establish FAIR-ICI as a platform for future research on immunotherapy-related toxicity.

Methods

Study design and setting

This multicenter, retrospective observational cohort study used routinely collected EHR data from four Belgian hospitals: two academic centers (University Hospital Antwerp and University Hospital Brussels) and two regional hospitals (Maria Middelares Hospital Ghent and AZ Groeninge Hospital Kortrijk). All institutions maintain OMOP-CDM version 5 databases generated through standardized extract-transform-load (ETL) procedures. The study period extended from September 2015 to November 2024 and covered all available data, with start and end dates varying across data partners and data sources (start date ranging from September 2015 to January 2018, and end date from November 2023 to November 2024).

Data sources and processing

The FAIR-ICI database integrates structured data (hospital cancer registries, medication administration records, laboratory measurements, administrative datasets, and minimal hospital data-mandatory Belgian reporting datasets containing harmonized administrative and diagnostic information) and unstructured clinical text (tumor board reports, consultation notes, pathology reports, radiology reports, and discharge summaries).

Unstructured clinical text was processed using a previously developed NLP pipeline 17 . NLP-derived clinical concepts were mapped to Unified Medical Language System (UMLS) Concept Unique Identifiers (CUIs) and subsequently converted to standardized OMOP concept identifiers using OMOP Athena vocabularies.

Structured and NLP-derived variables were transformed into the OMOP-CDM. The data extraction and processing workflow is illustrated in Supplementary-Figure S1. Supplementary-Tables S1-S3 provide the datapoint list (including source systems, OMOP tables, and UMLS CUIs), data dictionary (variable definitions, derivation logic, timing, and data sources), and NLP performance metrics, respectively. Data were pseudonymized before ETL processing.

Study population

The study population included adult patients who initiated ICI treatment during the study period. The index event was defined as the first ICI administration. Eligible ICIs, available in Belgium during the study period, included PD-1 inhibitors (nivolumab, pembrolizumab, cemiplimab, and dostarlimab), PD-L1 inhibitors (atezolizumab, durvalumab, and avelumab), and CTLA-4 inhibitors (ipilimumab and tremelimumab), either as monotherapy or in approved combination regimens. To make sure we only included new ICI users, patients were required to have at least 183 days of prior observation, except when cancer diagnosis occurred within 21 days before the index.

Patients were followed from the index date until cohort exit, which happened at death, diagnosis of a second primary malignancy, initiation of a new ICI regimen, ICI re-initiation after a gap exceeding 12 weeks, or end of available data.

Cancer type was assigned using structured cancer records and NLP-derived diagnoses, considering reimbursed ICI indications (Supplementary-Methods S1; Figure S2). Tumor stage at diagnosis and ICI initiation was determined using TNM staging and metastasis-related information (Supplementary-Methods S2).

Variables and outcomes

Baseline variables included demographic characteristics (age, sex, body mass index [BMI]), lifestyle factors (smoking status and alcohol abuse), Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status, tumor type, and stage at ICI initiation.

Treatment characteristics included ICI initiation year, ICI agent, mechanism of action, and regimen classification. Regimen type was determined according to all systemic anticancer therapies administered on the index date and categorized as ICI monotherapy, dual ICI therapy, ICI plus chemotherapy, ICI plus tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI), or other ICI-containing combinations.

Patients were considered to be on treatment until 12 weeks after last ICI administration or initiation of a new systemic antineoplastic regimen, whichever occurred first. A gap exceeding 12 weeks was considered permanent discontinuation of the index treatment episode. Treatment status at data cut off was classified as ongoing treatment, death during treatment, switch to a new systemic anticancer regimen, or permanent discontinuation.

The primary time-to-event outcomes were OS, ToT, and TTNT. OS was defined as the time from the index date to death from any cause, based on linkage to hospital and governmental death records. Patients without a registered death date were censored at the last recorded observation. ToT was defined as the time from the index date to treatment-ending events, including death, initiation of a new systemic anticancer regimen, or permanent discontinuation of the index ICI regimen. TTNT was defined as the time from the index date to initiation of a new systemic treatment regimen not part of the initial combination regimen. The event date was defined as the first administration of the new regimen. Switching to another ICI or addition of chemotherapy, targeted therapy, or hormonal therapy was considered a next treatment. Death was treated as a censoring event.

Validation

Data quality was assessed through a multilayered validation framework: data intake validation, NLP validation, clinical validation of individual datapoints, and clinical validation of aggregated outputs.

During data intake validation, participating hospitals verified completeness and coverage of source datasets through checks for missing values, date inconsistencies, duplicate records, and compliance with inclusion criteria.

LynxCare Clinical Informatics evaluated NLP-derived variables against human-annotated reference datasets, calculating precision, recall, and F1 scores for each datapoint. Models were iteratively refined until predefined performance targets were achieved (Supplementary-Table S3).

Physicians at participating hospitals validated individual datapoints by reviewing concept frequencies, terminology mappings, source attribution, and concordance between structured and unstructured sources. Investigators then reviewed aggregated cohort outputs at site and consortium levels, iteratively assessing summary statistics, phenotype definitions, and variable distributions to identify inconsistencies, refine extraction rules, and update the FAIR-ICI data dictionary. These validation procedures, together with predefined phenotype algorithms, aimed to minimize data extraction errors and misclassification.

The multidisciplinary FAIR-ICI team brought together clinical oncologists, epidemiologists, and clinical informatics specialists from participating hospitals and LynxCare to support data harmonization, validation, analysis, and clinical interpretation.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) for continuous variables and frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. Missing values were retained as a separate category.

OS, ToT, and TTNT were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method, reporting median times with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and survival probabilities at selected time points. Differences between Kaplan-Meier curves were assessed using the log-rank test. To explore factors associated with OS, a multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression model was fitted among patients with metastatic disease. The model was stratified by cancer type to allow for cancer type-specific baseline hazards. Adjusted hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% CIs were reported.

To contextualize real-world outcomes, pivotal phase III randomized controlled trials were identified by cancer type and treatment regimen, focusing on combinations known to represent first-line treatment in routine clinical practice. Published Kaplan-Meier survival estimates were digitized using WebPlotDigitizer and plotted alongside FAIR-ICI estimates for descriptive comparison.

The primary descriptive analyses and dashboard-based subgroup exploration were planned, while comparisons with pivotal clinical trials and post-discontinuation survival analyses were added during the study.

Interactive dashboard

To facilitate transparent exploration of the FAIR-ICI cohort, we developed an interactive dashboard that provides descriptive summaries of patient characteristics, treatment patterns, and clinical outcomes, with dynamic filtering by tumor type, treatment regimen, demographic characteristics, and disease features. Users can define granular subgroups of interest (e.g., women aged >70 years with metastatic breast cancer treated with anti-PD-1) and explore corresponding treatment trajectories and outcome estimates. Results

Patient cohort

Of 3,780 patients with recorded ICI exposure, 3,231 met the eligibility criteria and were included in the final FAIR-ICI cohort (details in Figure 1). The cohort included patients receiving anti-PD-1, anti-PD-L1, and anti-CTLA-4 therapies, either alone or in combination.

Baseline characteristics

Overall, the cohort was predominantly older (59.7% aged ≥65 years) and male (62.5%) (Table 1). ICI uptake increased over the years, from 185 patients during 2015-2017 to 598 in 2022. Most patients had metastatic disease at treatment initiation (77.8%), but the number of patients receiving ICI in the non-metastatic setting increased markedly over time, from 5 patients (1.5% of ICI initiations) in 2018 to 71 (12.3%) in 2023. Treatment patterns varied across cancer types. ICI monotherapy was the most common regimen (55.0%), particularly in melanoma and urothelial carcinoma, whereas ICI combined with chemotherapy was more frequent in NSCLC.

Treatment discontinuation patterns and post-discontinuation survival

Among metastatic patients (Figure 2A), the majority (62%, n=1,548) either died during treatment (23.9%, n=601) or initiated a new regimen within 12 weeks of the last ICI administration (37.7%, n=947), the latter potentially reflecting progression or toxicity. In contrast, 22.6% (n=568) discontinued ICI without subsequent treatment within 12 weeks, potentially reflecting deep remission or toxicity without the need or possibility for further treatment in the short term. This was most frequent in melanoma (104/297, 35.0%) and RCC (39/190, 20.5%), consistent with known ICI efficacy, and least frequent in head and neck cancer (33/215, 15.3%).

Among patients permanently discontinuing ICI without subsequent treatment (Figure 2B), median postdiscontinuation OS was 21.4 months (95% CI 16.5-37.2). Post-discontinuation survival was most favorable in melanoma (median not reached; 24-month OS 76.2%), followed by RCC (median 28.9 months, 95% CI 9.8-NR; 24-month OS 53.0%). Corresponding 24-month OS estimates were 49.8% for NSCLC, 35.0% for urothelial carcinoma, and 26.6% for head and neck cancer.

Real-world survival and treatment outcomes Among metastatic patients, median OS varied substantially across the five major cancer types, ranging from 11 months in head and neck cancer and urothelial carcinoma to 78 months in melanoma (Table 2). Twelvemonth OS ranged from 44.1% to 75.4%, while 24-month OS ranged from 24.9% to 67.2%. Among these five cancer types, OS event proportions ranged from 32.7% in melanoma to 66.0% in head and neck cancer, while median follow-up ranged from 7.6 months in head and neck cancer to 20.8 months in melanoma. Detailed maturity metrics, including follow-up among surviving patients and the number of ICI administrations, are presented in Supplementary-Table S6.

Median ToT was consistently shorter than median OS, ranging from 4 to 7 months, corresponding to a median of 5-6 ICI administrations (Supplementary-Table S6). TTNT showed greater variation, ranging from 8 months in head and neck cancer to 26 months in melanoma. This discrepancy between treatment duration and survival was particularly pronounced in melanoma, where median OS reached 78 months despite a median ToT of 7 months and median TTNT of 26 months.

Comparison with pivotal clinical trials

We compared real-world outcomes in unselected patient populations with pivotal clinical trials for all cancer type-regimen subgroups known to represent first-line treatment settings (Figure 3). Real-world survival curves generally followed patterns similar to the corresponding pivotal clinical trials but were frequently less favorable, particularly during early follow-up. The most pronounced visual discrepancies were observed in NSCLC treated with pembrolizumab plus chemotherapy, HCC with atezolizumab plus bevacizumab, and RCC with pembrolizumab plus TKI. In contrast, closer alignment was observed for RCC and mesothelioma treated with ipilimumab plus nivolumab, and head and neck cancer treated with pembrolizumab plus chemotherapy.

Survival according to ECOG performance status

Patients with ECOG 0-1 consistently showed longer OS than those with ECOG ≥2 (Figure 4), with significant differences in NSCLC, melanoma, RCC, and urothelial carcinoma, but not head and neck cancer. Among patients with NSCLC receiving first-line pembrolizumab plus chemotherapy, survival in patients with ECOG 0-1 more closely approximated that reported in the pivotal KEYNOTE-189 trial, whereas patients with ECOG ≥2 had substantially poorer survival.

Factors associated with overall survival

Additional Kaplan-Meier analyses by age, sex, BMI, smoking status, and alcohol abuse are presented in Supplementary-Figures S3-S7. Modest differences in survival by age and sex were observed in selected cancers, particularly melanoma and RCC, although these patterns were not consistently significant across tumor types. In contrast, associations with BMI, smoking status, and alcohol abuse were less consistent.

In multivariable Cox regression among metastatic patients (Table 3), older age, male sex, and ECOG ≥2 were associated with poorer OS. ECOG ≥2 showed the strongest association with mortality (adjusted HR 2.18, 95% CI 1.82-2.60), while mortality increased progressively with age. No clear associations were observed for BMI, smoking status, or alcohol abuse.

Discussion

In this multicenter real-world cohort of 3,231 patients receiving ICIs across four Belgian hospitals, we described treatment utilization, trajectories, and clinical outcomes across multiple cancer types using an NLPenriched OMOP-CDM framework. Several key findings warrant particular attention. First, nearly one-quarter of metastatic patients permanently discontinued ICIs without initiating another systemic therapy within 12 weeks and nevertheless experienced prolonged survival (2-year OS 49% after stop). Second, treatment-based endpoints (ToT and TTNT) were substantially shorter than OS across tumor types, suggesting that many patients remained alive long after treatment discontinuation and often before requiring additional therapy. Third, real-world outcomes generally followed pivotal trial pattern but were frequently less favorable, emphasizing the need for complementary RWE alongside RCTs. Finally, ECOG performance status emerged as one of the strongest prognostic factors across analyses, highlighting the importance of systematic assessment in routine oncology practice.

Treatment discontinuation and durable benefit

One of the most notable findings was that permanent ICI discontinuation did not necessarily indicate treatment failure or poor prognosis (Figure 2). Although discontinuation reasons require further validation, preliminary analyses suggest a heterogeneous mixture of treatment-related toxicity, disease progression, patient preference, and sustained disease control without immediate need for subsequent systemic therapy. Favorable post-discontinuation outcomes, particularly in melanoma and RCC, suggest durable benefit beyond active treatment in a subset of patients.

These observations are consistent with growing evidence that durable disease control can persist after treatment cessation. ICIs may promote durable antitumor immune memory, sustaining T-cell-mediated tumor control beyond active treatment. Optimal duration of ICI treatment remains uncertain, with pivotal trials generally continuing treatment until progression, unacceptable toxicity, or a maximum of two years. In melanoma, long-term trial evidence has demonstrated durable disease control and survival following ICI therapy 18, 19 . RWE further showed low relapse after elective anti-PD-1 discontinuation among patients achieving complete response after more than six months of treatment 20 , while a recent emulated target trial suggested that around one year may be sufficient in patients with disease control 21 . Similarly, in metastatic RCC, elective discontinuation after approximately two years did not appear to compromise long-term outcomes among responding patients 22 . Collectively, these findings suggest that cessation does not necessarily indicate loss of clinical benefit and highlight the need for further investigation of optimal ICI duration in routine practice 23.

The discrepancy observed between OS and treatment-trajectory endpoints further supports this interpretation. Across tumor types, median ToT and TTNT were consistently shorter than OS (Table 2), indicating that many patients remained alive for prolonged periods after treatment discontinuation and often before requiring subsequent systemic therapy. These findings suggest that treatment duration, switching, and survival capture distinct aspects of real-world treatment trajectories. Consequently, ToT and TTNT should be considered complementary treatment-trajectory endpoints that provide information distinct from OS, such as health care consumption, when evaluating real-world immunotherapy outcomes 12, 24.

Real-world versus trial overall survival

Compared with pivotal clinical trials, real-world survival was frequently less favorable, particularly during early follow-up. The most pronounced visual discrepancies were observed in NSCLC treated with pembrolizumab plus chemotherapy, HCC with atezolizumab plus bevacizumab, and RCC with pembrolizumab plus TKI (Figure 3). These findings are consistent with the well-recognized differences between trial populations and routine clinical practice. Pivotal trials often employ restrictive eligibility criteria that exclude patients with poor performance status, substantial comorbidities, organ dysfunction, autoimmune disease, or advanced age, thereby limiting the representativeness of enrolled populations 9, 10, 25 . Recent analyses also identified prognostic heterogeneity as an important explanation, with high-risk real-world patients experiencing substantially poorer survival than would be predicted from pivotal trials 26 . Our ECOG-stratified analysis of NSCLC patients receiving pembrolizumab plus chemotherapy further illustrates this (Figure 4). Survival with ECOG 0-1 more closely approximated KEYNOTE-189, whereas patients with ECOG ≥2 had substantially poorer survival, suggesting that performance status contributes to trial-real-world differences.

The observed RCC treatment patterns further highlight the complexity of interpreting real-world comparative effectiveness. While outcomes with nivolumab plus ipilimumab were broadly comparable to CheckMate214, early survival with pembrolizumab plus TKI appeared less favorable than in KEYNOTE-426. Although the number of patients receiving pembrolizumab plus TKI was small (n=24) and no comparative conclusions can be drawn, one possible explanation is confounding by indication. In routine practice, ICI-TKI combinations may be preferentially selected for patients with higher disease burden, more symptomatic disease, or a need for rapid disease control, given the high response rate (59.3%) and early tumor responses in metastatic RCC 27 . Consequently, patients receiving apparently similar first-line therapies in real-world settings may differ substantially in underlying prognosis, introducing biases that are difficult to fully capture using routinely collected data 26 . This underscores the importance of considering treatment-selection processes and baseline prognosis when interpreting real-world comparative effectiveness studies.

Prognostic factors and the importance of ECOG performance status Among the prognostic factors evaluated, ECOG performance status emerged as the strongest predictor of survival in both subgroup and multivariable analyses (Figure 4; Table 3). Patients with ECOG ≥2 had more than twice the hazard of death compared with ECOG 0-1, consistent with extensive literature identifying functional status as a major determinant of outcomes in patients receiving immunotherapy [28] [29] [30] [31] . This is particularly relevant because pivotal trials predominantly enrolled patients with ECOG 0-1, whereas routine clinical practice includes a broader spectrum of patients, including those with impaired functional status. Consequently, performance status may contribute substantially to the differences observed between trial and real-world outcomes 9.

Notably, ECOG performance status was unavailable for a substantial proportion of patients in our cohort (59%), reflecting a common limitation of routinely collected data. Nevertheless, the strong and consistent association observed among patients with available data suggests that performance status may explain an important component of outcome heterogeneity in routine practice. Given its strong prognostic value, more systematic capture of ECOG in EHRs should be prioritized to improve risk adjustment and interpretability of future real-world studies. The ability of FAIR-ICI to extract ECOG information through NLP-enhanced extraction from unstructured clinical documentation highlights the value of integrating structured and unstructured data sources for oncology RWE.

Beyond performance status, older age and male sex were associated with poorer OS in the multivariable analysis (Table 3), although differences were generally modest in cancer-specific Kaplan-Meier analyses (Supplementary-Figures S3-S4). Associations with BMI, smoking status, and alcohol abuse were less evident, with no clear associations observed after adjustment.

Strengths and limitations

To our knowledge, FAIR-ICI represents the first multicenter immunotherapy-focused RWE platform integrating structured and unstructured EHR data within a standardized OMOP-CDM framework. By combining data from four hospitals and incorporating NLP-derived clinical variables, FAIR-ICI enabled evaluation of treatment trajectories and outcomes across multiple tumor types with greater clinical granularity than typically available in administrative or registry-based datasets. The reuse of routinely collected clinical data also enables largescale analyses without additional burden to patients or resource-intensive prospective data collection. Furthermore, the publicly available interactive dashboard supports transparent exploration of results and facilitates subgroup analyses by clinicians, researchers, and policymakers. Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective observational design is inherently susceptible to residual confounding and treatment-selection bias. In particular, treatment allocation in routine clinical practice may be influenced by factors that were not fully captured in the available data, including disease burden, symptom severity, physician preference, and other clinical considerations, potentially resulting in confounding by indication. Second, several variables contained substantial missingness, particularly ECOG performance status and some lifestyle characteristics. Missing ECOG data may also introduce bias if their availability is associated with patient prognosis. Third, treatment line could not always be determined directly, and selected trial comparisons therefore relied on treatment-regimen subgroups considered likely to represent first-line settings. Fourth, reasons for treatment discontinuation were not directly assessed, limiting interpretation of the clinical circumstances underlying ICI cessation. Further characterization of discontinuation reasons and treatment-related toxicity represents an important direction for future FAIR-ICI analyses. Finally, despite including four hospitals, several cancer type-treatment subgroups remained small, limiting the precision of subgroup analyses and restricting interpretation. Belgium's broad ICI access and the inclusion of academic and regional hospitals support the relevance of these findings to routine oncology practice. However, differences in reimbursement policies, patient characteristics, and data availability may limit generalizability to other healthcare systems.

Conclusion

This multicenter real-world study provides a comprehensive overview of immunotherapy utilization, treatment trajectories, and outcomes across multiple cancer types. A notable proportion of patients remained alive for prolonged periods after treatment discontinuation without subsequent systemic therapy, highlighting the potential for durable clinical benefit beyond active treatment exposure. Differences between OS, ToT, and TTNT demonstrate that these endpoints capture distinct, complementary dimensions of immunotherapy benefit. While real-world outcomes broadly mirrored those observed in pivotal clinical trials, they were frequently less favorable, underscoring the importance of understanding differences between trial populations and routine practice. ECOG performance status was strongly associated with survival, emphasizing the importance of functional status in immunotherapy-treated populations and the need for its systematic capture in real-world datasets. The interactive dashboard further enables exploration of FAIR-ICI findings across clinically relevant patient subgroups. Together, these findings establish FAIR-ICI as a valuable RWE platform for future investigations, particularly for extending the dataset into treatment toxicity and reasons for treatment discontinuation, as well as treatment sequencing, and biomarker-driven precision immuno-oncology.
