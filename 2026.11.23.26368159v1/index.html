No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

How Replication Check works

This module checks references and warns for citations of original studies for which replication or reproduction studies exist in the FLoRA database.

The Replication Check module compares the reference list against studies in the FLoRA (FORRT Library of Replication Attempts) database based on the DOI. If a study in the database is found, a reminder is provided that a replication or reproduction of the original study exists, and should be cited. Abstract

Background: School prevention strategies are key to reducing the impact of youth mental health difficulties, such as depression and anxiety. Visual art programmes may be beneficial for prevention but evidence is limited. To better understand the potential for visual arts-based prevention programmes in schools, we explored staff experiences of using visual art for mental health and views on what is needed to facilitate successful programmes.

We used a convergent mixed-methods approach, combining online surveys (n=77) and in-depth interviews (n=27) from art teachers and staff supporting student mental health in UK secondary schools. Complementary survey and interview questions covered current use of visual art for student mental health, what a visual art prevention programme might look like, and potential concerns. We conducted descriptive statistics for quantitative survey data and reflexive thematic analysis for qualitative survey and interview data.

Results: Visual arts were seen as important for student mental health and commonly used: 65.8% of staff used visual arts to support student mental health in the last year, and 31.6% did so weekly, despite most participants reporting no relevant training. Theme 1, 'Art is a sanctuary in the school day', considered art as intrinsically linked to mental health, with art freedom conferring particular benefits. Theme 2, 'Resources in schools are stretched', represented the pressure of school environments, limited resources and need for prevention.

Theme 3, 'Integration, flexibility and relationships as factors for success', highlighted integrating programmes into school practice, enabling flexibility and choice, and building positive relationships between students, school staff and external providers are key for success.

Conclusions: Despite implementation challenges, visual arts-based prevention approaches were viewed positively. We recommend school mental health arts programmes should promote school ownership, include creative freedom, be flexible to schools' and students' 25 needs, and be supported by both internal school staff and external providers. 26

Background Adolescent anxiety and depression are common and are associated with significant impairment, long-term mental health difficulties and education and employment problems (1) (2) (3) (4) (5). Peak age at onset for social anxiety disorder, panic disorder, generalised anxiety disorder, and depressive disorders is in adolescence (6). Primary prevention programmes are needed to reduce the burden and impact of mental illness in young people (7). The World Health Organization (2020) advocates prioritising prevention strategies for adolescent mental health conditions and integrated approaches towards this, including educational and health care services (8). Schools offer one way to implement prevention programmes at scale. There is evidence supporting school-based prevention programmes for primary prevention generally (7) and some evidence supporting programmes preventing anxiety and depression (9, 10). However, many programmes lack longevity (10) and prevention approaches that seem promising in small-scale trials often fail to replicate positive findings in large, well-powered studies (11, 12). Such programmes often focus on using treatment approaches (e.g. cognitive behavioural therapy, mindfulness) in a prevention context (9), whereas a focus on modifiable risk factors for anxiety and depression may be more effective for those who have not yet developed mental health difficulties depression (11). For instance, a whole-school approach using restorative practice and social and emotional learning to target bullying was effective at reducing both bullying and mental health difficulties three years later (13).

There is growing evidence connecting the arts (14) (15) (16) (17) and art therapy (18, 19) to positive mental health and wellbeing outcomes in young people, suggesting arts-based interventions may target risk factors for anxiety and depression. The multimodal nature of arts means that engagement can affect health through diverse psychological, biological, social and behavioural mechanisms (20, 21). Importantly, for children and young people, visual arts (for instance, painting, drawing, crafting, design, photography, sculpture, and textiles (21)) offer an opportunity to communicate without the need for words, a useful mechanism for young people who find it difficult to label emotions and describe experiences. Furthermore, engaging in art and art therapy can increase effective emotion regulation (22) (23) (24), improve self-esteem (14, (25) (26) (27) (28) and reduce stress (29, 30). These factors may, in turn, buffer against development of anxiety and depression (31) (32) (33) (34) (35). Therefore, targeting these protective factors using engagement with visual arts may be a useful prevention strategy. There is preliminary evidence that students with emotional/behavioural problems receiving preventative art therapy in schools from art therapists show improvements in mental health (36, 37), but little is known about using arts as a universal prevention strategy in schools or prevention strategies not delivered by art therapists.

School buy in, support from multiple levels of the school community, and competency of teachers can help school programmes achieve longevity, uptake and success (38) (39) (40) (41).

However, there is some evidence that school staff delivery may be less effective than external provider delivery (10, 42), potentially due to low levels of teacher acceptability and confidence in delivering interventions, alongside inadequate time, training and support (43).

Arts-based programmes, in particular, may face implementation challenges given the evidence that the arts are not valued in the English state education system (44). An in-depth understanding of the experiences and perspectives of staff working in and with schools is needed to determine how to make an art and wellbeing prevention programme in schools successful and sustainable. These staff groups are key for exploring the current cultural attitudes towards visual arts and mental health, and navigating the barriers to implementing arts programmes within schools.

The current study

The aims of this study were to (1) explore current experiences of using visual art to promote student mental health in UK secondary schools and (2) better understand what would be needed to facilitate a school-based art and wellbeing programme to help prevent anxiety and depression. We chose a mixed methods design to enable both breadth and depth in understanding staff's experiences and perspectives. We collected quantitative and qualitative data via an online survey and interviews with staff across the UK from staff who would be closely involved in the organisation or delivery of an art and wellbeing prevention programme.

Methods

Study design

We used a convergent mixed methods design to collect quantitative data (survey) and qualitative (survey and interview) data during one phase of data collection. Survey and interview questions were designed to collect parallel data using similar questions, with the survey enabling views from a wider range of staff and interviews providing more depth and contextualised exploration of the research topic. We pre-registered the Study Protocol prior to data collection on the Open Science Framework: https://osf.io/erq5a/ and used Good Reporting of A Mixed Methods Study (GRAMMS) guidelines (45) ( S1 Table).

Participants and recruitment

We recruited participants who were over 18 years old and worked in a UK secondary school as either an art teacher specialising in visual or fine arts, or in a role supporting student mental health. This second broad category included staff based within schools (for example, mental health leads, teaching assistants and Special Educational Needs Coordinators (SENCOs)) and external professionals (for example, creative arts therapists and Educational Mental Health Practitioners (EMHPs)). Participants were excluded if they did not speak fluent English, or they were public contributors involved in designing the study materials.

Recruitment for surveys and interviews took place between June 2023 and January 2024 by (1) utilising existing networks and contacts to share study information, (2) directly contacting UK schools and external organisations with links to schools to share information with staff, (3) sharing through established school research networks and (4) sharing on social media.

We also asked participants to share study information with other potentially eligible individuals.

Participant recruitment is presented in S1 Figure . Potential participants initially registered their interest by completing a short survey with demographic information, occupation, and preference to take part in the survey and/or interview. Verification of eligibility and genuine participant status was manually conducted by checking staff lists on school websites and work email addresses. All verified and eligible individuals expressing an interest in survey participation were sent a link to complete the online survey, and a reminder email after two weeks if the survey had not been completed. We carried out purposive sampling to ensure a diverse range of views across interview participants. Verified and eligible individuals were invited to book an interview time if they had a job role or demographic characteristics (age, gender, ethnicity, region of work) not yet included in the interview sample, with a reminder email after two weeks if they had not booked an interview time. Participants were remunerated with a £10 voucher for completing the survey and a £25 voucher for completing an interview.

A total of 79 participants completed the online survey, of whom 28 individuals were also interviewed. Separate quantitative and qualitative data analyses were conducted in parallel before being integrated to enable one overall interpretation in the Discussion. Priority was given to the qualitative results which provided depth and context to the quantitative results.

Qualitative data

Qualitative survey and interview results

We constructed three themes from the qualitative data: (1) art is a sanctuary in the school day, (2) resources in schools are stretched, and (3) integration, flexibility and relationships as factors for success. Themes and subthemes are presented in Figure 1, with example quotes in S4 Table. Art is a sanctuary in the school day

The first theme considers the role of art in the school setting, with participants emphasising that art provides more than a lesson for young people, even being seen as a sanctuary in the school day. We split this theme into two subthemes: 'Art and Mental Health' and 'Art Freedom'.

Art and mental health

Staff discussed that art is intrinsically and uniquely linked to mental health: "I think art itself lends itself to that [mental health], doesn't it? That it's kind of a therapeutic lesson" (Interview, Art-Teacher-11). Art was seen as different to other subjects because it teaches more than technical or academic skills: "What is it that art gives to a child, that nothing else does on the curriculum? And it is that place to explore, and experiment safely, and not worry about the outcome" (Interview, Head-of-Art-1). Furthermore, being creative was seen as a way to help students relax, give a calming space and an opportunity to regulate: "our inclusion room is a space that a lot of students with special educational needs use, but we also use it as a quiet space for our students with anxiety, or our students who get overwhelmed quite a lot within the school day […] and a lot of the time I walk in and they're drawing, or they're painting, or they're making little models out of things, and it's all quite creative to keep their minds busy" (Interview, Pastoral-Team-9). Many participants felt that art facilitated communication by providing a distraction or opening to discuss challenging topics: "The conversations around mental health can be really, you know, make them feel really vulnerable. So having something to sort of, distract whilst having those conversations really open -opens them up. […] I use [art and creative methods] all the time in the way of just making the atmosphere a little bit more therapeutic." (Interview, EMHP-3).

"The most genuine kind of conversations I've had with [students] is by sitting by their side and I will draw, I will do the same task as they do." (Interview, Head-of-Art-2).

The inherent link between art and mental health led to ad hoc clubs being organised and facilitated by invested staff: "I have been running art well-being sessions in my school two days a week where small groups of learners can create art while discussing problems and socialising with new people." (Survey, Art-Teacher-10). Combining art and mental health through a novel programme was therefore seen positively due to the perceived strong link, but programmes should be clearly distinguished from standard art lessons: "Sometimes nonspecialists talk about not being good at art and can ruin the work of art teachers…staff need to be aware of the difference between art (building subject knowledge and skills) and art for mental health development" (Survey, Art-Teacher-2).

Art freedom

Participants saw potential benefits for students engaging in the artistic process freely and with choice. However, 'art freedom' can be constrained in school environments because of marking and curriculum pressures and the pressure to do 'good art': "I think that making art promotes positive mental health when students are able to make art with more flexibility. It is a great way for them to express and communicate without having to use words. However, due to the constraints of the curriculum/number of students in lesson/practicality of getting out different types of equipment etc, it isn't possible to do that in art lessons" (Survey, Art-Teacher-9).

Participants discussed how social comparison and the importance placed on the end product can impact students' ability to enjoy and benefit from the process, with older students particularly affected. Consequently, it would be important for any art and wellbeing programme to communicate with young people that the process is important, not the outcome: "Because when they go to art class in a school, you know, they're told what to do, you know, and they compare each other, they compare their work to other's work, you know, so there's that separation, you know, trying to separate school art from wellbeing art" (Interview, Counsellor-3). "You don't have to be a great footballer to enjoy football, you don't have to be a great artist to enjoy drawing something, you know? You can just have a go and enjoy it" (Interview, Pastoral-Team-4). This was noted as challenging in school environments where art freedom is not the norm: "I have to kind of make clear that I, I'm not there in an academic sense. I'm not there to teach them how to do something, but actually this is a space for them to use, in whichever way they want and I suppose they're not used to having so much freedom [laughs]" (Interview, Creative-Arts-Therapist-2).

Resources in schools are stretched

The second theme encompasses discussions of the pressure in school environments and the restriction of available resources in three subthemes: 'Widening support', 'No one in school has time', and 'School staff are overworked and under-supported'.

Widening support

Many participants highlighted that student mental health and school attendance had worsened following the COVID-19 pandemic, resulting in reduced resourcing, limited to those with the highest need: "Because it's so busy -we're so busy firefighting, not noticing the little fires that have started, and then turned into a, you know, massive fire. We're justwe're just leaving it all until it's too late" (Interview, Other-4). Participants discussed the need to widen the support within schools: "I think it's important for everybody to have access to do it, and be able to take part in it, because you never know when they might be affected by something that does affect their mental health" (Interview, Head-of-Art-8) and viewed creating initiatives such as an art and wellbeing prevention programme as a positive step.

Nevertheless, there was a tension between prevention for all and providing a safe space that encouraged engagement within sessions: "I think students would feel more comfortable to express themselves in smaller groups. However I think it's important to equip all students Visual art for the prevention of student mental health difficulties in schools: Mixed-methods analysis of staff surveys and interviews 22 with the tools to deal with any mental health issues they may develop" (Survey, Head-of-Art-10).

No one in school has time

Participants highlighted significant time pressures affecting staff and students, with every minute of the day allocated: "A school day is tightly governed by the clock, tightly governed by, 'You've gotta be here, you've gotta be there,' and you don't -you don't get kids in school with free time, and you don't get adults with free time." (Interview, SENCO-4). This environment would lead to practical barriers of taking students and staff off timetable for a prevention programme: "A counsellor would make sure that their client is 'safe' to leave the session but educators would be unable to do this due to time constraints and a lack of training" (Survey, Art-Teacher-9). "I feel that art teachers would be fantastic at helping with supporting this but this would need to be factored into timetabling as most teachers have a very heavy workload" (Survey, Art-Teacher-9).

Participants noted the need to work with each school on a case-by-case basis to consider what staff would be taken away from to support and deliver the programme and if they would have time: "All staff in schools are allocated roles, asking staff to step out of role to do something different causes disruption and knocks onto other staff" (Survey, SENCO-4).

School staff are overworked and under-supported

Increased expectations of staff was a recurring concern, with many teaching staff reporting a blurring of roles across education and student mental health support: "Rather than a role that is guiding them to create the best possible image, you're stepping into a role that supports them in how they're feeling, and accepting that the way they want to make imagery is the best way for them to journey through that. … The number of conversations that I've had with students over the years about different issues that could or do affect their mental health is too many to count" (Interview, Head-of-Art-8).

While some participants felt mental health support came as part of their role, some participants identified a need for training to support student's mental health: "We haven't actually looked specifically at mental health this year, even though we've got more students school refusing and off for mental health reasons than ever before, so it feels a bit odd that we're not getting more training, to be honest" (Interview, Head-of-Art-8).

Improving the level of support for facilitating staff was seen as key for implementing an art and wellbeing programme, with many participants indicating a need for clinical supervision similar to that received by mental health professionals: "I think It's important to recognise that art therapy and similar programs can be emotionally demanding, and those involved need to be prepared for this aspect of the work, so the appropriate training and supervision would be needed" (Survey, Other-9). Support in the form of protected staff time and an adequately resourced programme would reduce the burden of programme delivery and support its success and sustainability: "I think that as teaching staff we are so busy with day to day school life but having a external support for planning to deliver a programme and prepared resources and materials would be great" (Survey, Art-Teacher-2).

Integration, flexibility and relationships as factors for success

The final theme considers factors that are essential to overcome the challenges that may be faced by a potential new art and wellbeing programme in three subthemes: 'Integration into Schools', 'Flexibility and Choice' and 'Importance of Relationships'.

Integration into school

Participants discussed that previous mental health initiatives they experienced had been short-lived and failed to integrate with the school's practices. Introducing a programme in line with the schools' established safeguarding policies, slowly, with good communication and training was seen as a route to promote integration: "Where we've had success with a lot of the changes that we've made over the last five, if not ten years, is it's almost been like a slow build" (Interview, Other-13). Importantly, to improve integration, participants felt an art and wellbeing programme should not be tokenistic: "I don't ever wanna do something in my lesson that's just ticking a box. If I'm going to do it I want to do it right and really embed it fully" (Interview, Head-of-Art-2).

Simultaneously, to ensure buy-in from senior leadership, it was seen as important that a programme can meet targets and goals, or be viewed positively by government bodies, such as the UK Office for Standards in Education, Children's Services and Skills (Ofsted): "If you get a positive evaluation for a programme, the school, we're going to carry it on because a, it sounds really cynical now, it looks really good for Ofsted, because they've done this programme and it's been successful, and what have you, if they didn't carry it on Ofsted, they'd be saying, 'Well, why have you not carried it on? If it was so successful?' So they kind of have to carry it on" (Interview, Mental-Health-Lead-2).

Participants felt that staff ownership of a programme would support its integration and sustainability. They proposed having multiple staff responsible for organising and promoting the programme internally according to the schools' culture, ensuring the programme continues should a member of staff leave: "I can highlight two or three people that … would ensure that that happened. And then almost that long term, can they train the next group of people? … And if we talked about that observing and kind of relearning from each other, that, that cycle just continues? So I think on the ground is where sustainability happens in schools" (Interview, Mental-Health-Lead-3).

Flexibility and choice

The views of participants varied widely in how an art and wellbeing programme would look due to schools being diverse, each with their own schedules, practices and cultures. A programme would therefore need to provide flexibility and choice for schools to be successful as a 'one size fits all' approach would not be appropriate: "Each school's quite different. So some schools want you to have everything done the way that it's done in their school and some schools are more open to it being done the way it's done in your service" (Interview, Creative-Arts-Therapist-1).

Giving staff and students the ability to make an informed choice to take part, depending on their workload and interests, was seen as key for a programme's success: "You know, teachers are different [laughs] and some people are so set in their ways, but some people would love it" (Interview, Art-Teacher-5).

"Because, a lot of the time, I think, with educational stuff, like if they're doing, like, lunchtime groups, or after school clubs, it's kind of forced upon them by teachers or parents, so just being like, 'This is up to you, if you wanna come,' kind of shows that, you know, they have that -that kind of control, and choice over it" (Interview, EMHP-6).

Importance of relationships

This sub-theme acknowledges the benefits and challenges of existing relationships, and the importance of building new relationships between staff, external providers and students.

Existing relationships between programme facilitators and students were seen as beneficial to provide programme legitimacy, a contact outside of sessions, and a familiar face: "I think you would definitely want someone in the room that they were familiar with. Someone who is a reassuring face, a head of year or an art teacher that they're familiar with or even just seeing the adults in the room" (Interview, Head-of-Art-2). However existing relationships could also perpetuate pre-existing barriers between teachers and young people, or blur the boundaries between the normal lessons and the programme, thereby impacting its delivery: "I would say that if an art teacher is in that position, they need to make it clear on their, their role in that group is not kind of, like, creating good art, or making… I suppose establishing boundaries between what's art class and what this group… Just for clarity for the young people" (Interview, Creative-Arts-Therapist-2).

Participants perceived involving external professionals as an effective method to create separation from the school day and be an opportunity to create new therapeutic relationships: "I think it's good for me, as well, like, external, because I can come in, they don't know me, and I can build those really, like, positive relationships from the start" (Interview, EMHP-6).

Discussion

This mixed-methods study explored experiences of staff working in UK secondary schools around using visual art to promote student mental health and aimed to understand what would be needed to facilitate a school-based visual arts programme to prevent anxiety and depression. Participants felt positively towards using visual arts to support mental health and prevent mental health difficulties in schools and frequently used the arts to support student mental health in practice. Visual art was viewed as strongly and uniquely linked to mental health, and participants felt that enabling art freedom within a school context can be beneficial for mental health when curriculum and marking constraints are lifted.

Nevertheless, challenges of implementation were noted, with school staff and resources being stretched, and increases in demand for student mental health support requiring school staff to provide support with limited support and training. Participant views were mixed on how best to deliver an art and wellbeing programme in terms of when it should be delivered and who should deliver and support the programme, highlighting a "one size fits all" approach may not be appropriate. Our study indicates several ways to overcome these challenges including ensuring programme integration into schools, enabling flexibility and choice, and building effective relationships. These factors should form the groundwork for the development of future programmes using arts to support mental health in schools.

In both the quantitative and qualitative data, art was perceived as important for mental health in schools. Almost a third of participants reported using the visual arts at least weekly to support student mental health and promote mental health conversations. These attitudes and practices align with the growing evidence base indicating engagement in the arts can be beneficial for young people's mental health and wellbeing (14) (15) (16) (17) and echoes recent research highlighting that young people aged 16-25 believe art to be essential for mental health (49). In our study, 'art freedom', or engaging in the creative process freely and with choice, was perceived as particularly beneficial. This focus on process and journey, rather than output, is often central to creative arts therapies but challenging in a school context with social comparison and a focus on achievement. Our study highlights the importance of including art freedom and choice in future programme designs, and the need to disentangle art for wellbeing from art as an academic subject. We found the majority of work combining the visual arts and mental health occurred informally in schools, rather than as stand-alone programmes. Given school staff welcomed the idea of a preventative programme for mental illness within schools, such a programme could help fill the gap between the perceived importance of visual arts and their structured use. With staff already combining art and mental health in schools, and being positive about a potential formalised programme, school staff could act as effective champions for arts-based programmes.

While there was endorsement of a universal preventative approach to poor student mental health, participants also acknowledged practical constraints and pressures that only allow support for students with the highest mental health needs. The lack of time, resources, and programme integration into schools described by our participants are well-documented barriers for mental health interventions in schools (39, 40). Most of our participants felt confident having conversations with students on mental health, but almost 60% reported additional training needs and interview participants noted they would need training to successfully integrate a programme into school practice. School mental health interventions with sufficient training for those delivering them are often more effective and more sustainable (39), and the World Health Organization also stresses the importance of good training (8). Providing adequate training and support for staff delivering programmes is challenging in time-pressured school environments. Participants in the current study suggested that external support and ready-made, well-prepared resources would reduce potential burden for staff delivering programmes. While the evidence on the effectiveness of arts-based prevention programmes for mental health is in its infancy, other prevention and early intervention strategies have been shown to improve young people's mental wellbeing and reduce costs associated with later mental health difficulties (50, 51). Further research into the efficacy and cost-effectiveness of arts prevention programmes is therefore warranted.

Echoing young people's recommendations to embed arts programming in schools (49), our study found school staff saw integration in schools as critical for the success of arts programmes. Participants highlighted the need for a slow build, good communication and ownership of the programme to promote integration with schools. Good communication with the whole school community can improve buy-in and engagement with prevention programmes (52). Participants in the current study indicated that different strategies may be required to ensure integration and support from staff at different levels in the school system: leadership would benefit from evidence of a programme meeting targets and aligning with regulatory frameworks, whereas staff delivering the programme need their involvement to be meaningful and not seen as a tick-box exercise. School and staff ownership was seen as a key facilitator for programme integration, echoing previous research that found staff excitement and engagement can aid successful implementation of school mental health programmes (53). Based on our findings, there is a need to balance provision of a wellprepared programme that reduces staff burden with flexibility and choice to facilitate ownership.

Our quantitative results highlighted mixed views around who should deliver a programme and when it should be delivered. Exploring these perspectives in interviews highlighted the need for programme flexibility and choice. This may be achieved by allowing self-selection for students and facilitators and enabling flexible implementation, while retaining core elements of the programme and programme fidelity. Similar to previous findings that school pastoral and support staff can encourage student engagement in mental health interventions due to perceived trustworthiness (54), our participants recognised internal staff can provide legitimacy, reassurance and an ongoing contact point for students. Participants also acknowledged the benefits of building new relationships with external staff. Our results suggest that allowing each school and individuals to choose who is best placed to support and deliver an art and wellbeing programme will likely increase its chances of success, but that a combination of school staff and external providers can be beneficial. The addition of external staff could also provide additional support for school staff facilitators, or empower them to be able to run the programme.

Despite the desire for a universal preventative approach evident in the qualitative data, the quantitative data indicated a preference for small group delivery over whole class delivery.

Maintaining smaller groups limits the ability to apply a whole school approach and can increase programme resourcing costs. Reasons for choosing to restrict group size centred around student comfort and viewing a smaller group as more conducive for wellbeing discussions. This may be one reason prevention approaches seem promising in small group contexts but fail to replicate in large-scale trials (11). Prevention in schools may therefore benefit from a multi-level approach, that enables universal access for the whole school, smaller groups that feel safer for students, and work at the individual level for those with higher needs. For an arts prevention programme, this may look like regular arts activities accessible to a whole year group through half-day events or tutor time, smaller weekly group arts workshops after school or off-timetable for students who want to engage more and are at risk of developing mental health issues, and one-to-one sessions with a trained creative therapist for individuals with greater mental health needs. School prevention programmes that have trialled multi-level (non-arts) approaches have indicated reduction in depressive symptoms (for example, the SEHER trial (55)), but need extensive integration and maintained funding to be sustainable (56).

Based on our findings, we provide recommendations for teams developing arts-based mental health interventions and prevention programmes in schools in Table 3. We used a mixed-methods design, collecting and analysing rich qualitative data and broader 547 quantitative data. This complementary data analysis enabled a breadth of views to be 548 collected but also in-depth insights in this complex research field. However, the time and resource needed to conduct interviews meant that less time was spent on recruitment and may have resulted in a smaller quantitative sample than if the study had employed surveys alone. While we incorporated the views of a diverse range of professions working within schools in the survey, the smaller sample size and number of job roles precluded the use of inferential statistics. Moreover, most of the sample worked in English schools, therefore the findings may not be relevant to different educational contexts. The survey and interview samples also comprised staff who would likely be involved in the support or delivery of an art and wellbeing programme. These staff are more likely to have positive attitudes towards arts and mental health initiatives than broader staff groups (for instance, teachers specialising in non-arts subjects) and school communities. Therefore, further research is required to ascertain acceptability of arts-based programmes more broadly with school staff with different roles (including school leaders), as well as parents and students. Given the potential importance of art freedom in promoting student mental health, additional research to elucidate facilitators to art freedom in a school context is also warranted to improve effectiveness of school-based arts programmes.

Conclusions

This mixed-methods study found that visual arts were seen as important for supporting and promoting student mental health and were used frequently used for this purpose, despite most staff receiving no training for these activities. A visual arts programme to help prevent anxiety and depression within schools was seen as a positive initiative but would encounter implementation challenges. Our findings indicate that a number of factors are needed to successfully integrate an art and wellbeing programme into a busy school context including art freedom, flexibility, choice, a combination of internal and external programme facilitators, appropriate training and supervision for facilitators, ready-made resources, and school ownership. Future development of visual art prevention programmes should take these factors into account to ensure successful and sustainable implementation.
