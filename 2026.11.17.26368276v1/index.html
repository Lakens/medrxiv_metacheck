How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). Abstract

Health outcomes vary substantially across communities due to multiple factors operating across the socioecological framework. Researchers, health systems, public health and government agencies, and communities often lack integrated data systems that identify communities performing better or worse than expected, characterize factors associated with outcomes, and support action. We propose to develop the Virginia Accountable Health Engagement and Action Dashboard (Va-AHEAD) to identify communities with unexpectedly favorable outcomes (Bright Spots) and the modifiable factors that contribute to their success.

This protocol describes the design and methodology of a statewide, community-engaged translational science initiative to identify structural factors, community assets, and modifiable drivers associated with population health outcomes using a strengths-based approach. The project aims to co-create community health measures, identify bright spot communities and their assets, support use of findings by researchers and stakeholders, and evaluate impact.

Payer Claims Database data, mortality data, census-based measures, and other socioecological datasets. Guided by a coalition of community advisory partners, the project will conduct bright spot analyses and community asset mapping to identify communities performing better than expected and the modifiable community factors that may contribute to their success across 15 . priority health topics. Findings will be disseminated through an interactive dashboard, community products, and a statewide learning community. Measures of success include dashboard use, research productivity, care transformation activities, and public health and community action.

Va-AHEAD addresses a critical need for community-informed data systems that move beyond identifying problems to understanding factors associated with success. By integrating advanced analytics, stakeholder engagement, and dissemination strategies, this project will create a scalable model for identifying and spreading effective community approaches to improving health outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

Health outcomes vary across individuals and communities according to socioeconomic status, geographic location, race, ethnicity, and other factors. 1 Although differential outcomes have been recognized for more than half a century, 2, 3 significant gaps persist, and meaningful progress toward addressing them remains limited. For example, there is a ten year difference in life expectancy for Black and Native Americans compared to others. 4 National quality measures demonstrate that compared to White people, Black people receive worse care on 76 out of 190 (40%) measures and Hispanic people on 58 out of 167 (35%) measures. 5 Black, Hispanic, and Asian people are less likely to receive recommended preventive care and more likely to suffer worse outcomes. 6, 7 Social and structural drivers of health such as poverty, unemployment, inadequate education, and unstable housing all contribute to poor health. [8] [9] [10] [11] [12] [13] Additionally, place matters. 14, 15 The life expectancy of residents can vary by 15 to 20 years across census tracts just a few miles apart (Figure 1), [16] [17] [18] and compared to urban communities, rural communities have higher rates of preventable conditions and unhealthy behaviors. 19 Health outcomes are shaped by a range of factors that affect individuals and communities in different ways. 20 These differences are often preventable and grounded in systemic factors.

Research has shown that the root causes of poor health extend well beyond healthcare, and that behaviors, social and economic factors, and the environment are the primary drivers of health and wellbeing. [21] [22] [23] Improving health outcomes requires addressing these systemic differences across sectors through sustained, longitudinal collaboration at the local, state, and national levels. 24, 25 The Role of Translational Science and the Roadblock Translational science can play an essential role serving as an academic anchor, creating resources for community-driven solutions, and conducting research to inform practice and community investment to address differences in health outcomes. 26 However, addressing multisector systemic differences is challenging and our understanding about how to best do this is limited.

A key barrier to this research is the lack of local data and meaningful community input needed to establish priorities, identify underlying drivers of health disparities, develop targeted interventions, and monitor progress over time. Although large population health datasets are increasingly available, they often lack community-level analyses, are difficult to access and interpret, provide limited insight into the underlying drivers of health outcomes, and rarely incorporate community perspectives. Local data. We know that the US has a worse life expectancy than similar developed countries and that many of the conditions driving this difference are preventable. 23, 27, 28 While knowing these drivers of poor health on a macrolevel is helpful, translational science researchers need to know which communities they serve are disproportionately affected by which problems. This can prioritize what to study locally and who should be included in research. Underlying causes. The factors contributing to and causing different health outcomes are complex, interconnected, and span many sectors. Local data is needed across a wide range of socioecological and life-course factors. Advanced analytic methods for evaluating natural experiments (local and temporal changes from research, clinical care, community investment, or trainings), multivariate geospatial modeling, and longitudinally tracking outcomes can be harnessed to explore relationships between these many variables. This combination of data and analytics can inform the design of interventions that may have the greatest potential for success. Community driven. Data alone can only partially address this translational science roadblock. Deliberative multi-stakeholder engagement is also essential to setting priorities, provide local context to understand how to interpret the data, determine if associations may reflect causality, and identify potential solutions based on local values, preferences, and lived experience. Community engagement helps researchers to interpret data correctly, align data with community-identified priorities, and locally tailor potential interventions.

Innovation

Our proposal has three elements that make it highly innovative and will transform our health outcomes research: 1.

Create a community-determined system for accountability -by clearly identifying specific health outcomes and their consequences, and then linking people and communities to researchers, care teams, policymakers, and learners we will create a clear, measurable, and objective system of accountability.

2. Adopt a strengths-based approach -by focusing on bright spot communities that are thriving and assisting communities with worse health outcomes to develop similar assets and resources, we create a strengths-based and respectful roadmap for change.

3. Include the full range of socioecological variables -by considering all variables that could influence health using a combination of person-level and community-level data, we can better design interventions to address the complex topic of health outcomes.

METHODS

Project Overview

Funded by the National Center for Advancing Translational Sciences (NCATS; Grant RC2TR005115) through its RC2 Specialized Innovation Program (PAR-25-156), this project develops and disseminates a novel specialized innovation to support clinical and translational science, the Virginia Accountable Health Engagement and Action Dashboard (Va-AHEAD). Va-AHEAD displays community-level data generated through our novel bright spot and community asset mapping approach, which operationalizes the socioecological model by accounting for the full range of factors influencing health outcomes. 29, 30 This analytic approach integrates robust socioecological data with local context to identify community-level protective factors and potential drivers of health outcomes. The overall approach is shown in Figure 2.

Throughout the process, we will co-create content with a broad and representative cadre of community partners from across the state. Va-AHEAD will address 15 community identified health priorities (2-4 added per year) and will include community level measures, analytic findings, and tools and products developed by the community. Content will be annually updated and displayed longitudinally. The project consists of seven phases: (1) prioritize and co-create health outcome goals and measures with communities, (2) identify initial bright spot communities that perform better than expected and factors that may contribute to their success, (3) conduct community asset mapping to understand the factors that create bright spot communities, (4) refine the bright spot model to further inform interventions, (5) develop shared meaning, products, and action from the data, (6) disseminate and support use of content, and (7) measure the impact of the approach, change in health outcomes, and evolution of partnerships.

Project Aims

We define success of this project as an increase in impactful health outcomes research, clinical and community care transformation, and state programs; inclusion of more broad and representative people and communities in research; and dissemination of assets and approaches used by bright spot communities to other communities. Project specific aims include: Aim 1 (Creation): Engage a broad range of community partners to co-create the Va-AHEAD measures and analyses. Meet with community partners weekly to monthly to address 15 health outcomes topics. For each topic, we will identify factors that contribute to outcomes, conduct a bright spot and community asset analysis, and design dashboard measures and products. Annually, we will assess the impact of the process on our relationship with community partners. Data sources to create community health metrics or existing community measures used for our bright spot analyses are all de-identified. Meetings with community members will be recorded. Discussions will focus on the priorities and perspectives of the communities they represent and will not include discussions about personal health information or other protected information. Participants will be informed about the purpose of study and nature of discussions prior to participation and will have the opportunity to withdraw from discussions and participation as desired.

Setting

The study will include every census tract, county, and city in Virginia, with the geographic unit varying according to the available data and topic assessed. Virginia is a geographically and demographically representative state that includes urban, suburban, rural, coastal, Appalachian, and medically underserved communities. Virginia's heterogeneity in population characteristics, health systems, socioeconomic conditions, and community contexts closely reflects the heterogeneity seen across many U.S. states, making it an ideal setting for developing and evaluating community health outcomes.

Phase 1: Community Prioritization and Co-Creation

Community engagement is foundational to Va-AHEAD and occurs through three complementary structures: the Community Data Translation Team (CDTT), Topic Teams, and dissemination opportunities. Community members serve as equal partners throughout the research process, helping to establish priorities, interpret findings, develop dissemination products, and guide implementation. We assembled the CDTT at the outset of the study to provide overall governance and ensure that Va-AHEAD reflects priorities from across the Commonwealth. Members were selected to broadly represent Virginia's health interests and were drawn from established community partnerships of the Wright Center for Clinical and Translational Research and VCU. The CDTT meets monthly throughout the five-year project and is responsible for selecting the health topics addressed through bright spot and community asset mapping, identifying Topic Team members, serving as consultants throughout each assessment, supporting dissemination activities, and providing ongoing oversight of the project's scientific and community-engaged direction.

For each priority area, we assembled 7 Topic Teams to oversee the 15 bright spot and community asset mapping assessments. The topics identified by the CDTT include primary care, substance use, mental health, cancer, adverse childhood events, obesity, and cultural arts. Topic Team members were identified with guidance from the CDTT using a snowball sampling approach to recruit key stakeholders from across Virginia with relevant expertise and lived, professional, and organizational experience. Topic Teams meet 6-12 times over the course of each assessment and are responsible for identifying meaningful outcomes, selecting candidate factors for bright spot analyses, interpreting findings, guiding community asset mapping, refining analytic models, developing dashboard and dissemination content, and supporting dissemination.

Collectively, the CDTT and Topic Teams include more than 75 members representing over 63 organizations distributed throughout Virginia, including clinicians, patients, public health, community organizations, government, philanthropy, advocacy organizations, and community members, ensuring that the project reflects broad perspectives from across the Commonwealth.

Biographies of CDTT and Topic Team members are available at https://virginia-ahead.org/.

Phase 2: Identify Bright Spot Communities

The identification of bright spot communities is shown in steps 1-3 of Figure 2. In our approach, each community is characterized along two dimensions: (1) observed outcomes and (2) performance relative to predicted outcomes.

Hot spots and cold spots represent communities with higher and lower observed outcomes, respectively. Bright and challenge spots represent communities performing greater and lower than predicted based on their structural factors and key drivers.

For each topic, the Topic Team identifies one to three outcomes that are meaningful to communities and identifies structural factors and key drivers that may influence these outcomes.

Each outcome serves as the dependent variable for a bright spot analysis, with structural factors and key drivers serving as independent variables. Variable selection is informed by evidence reviews, Topic Team members' knowledge of the communities they represent, and the socioecological and life-course frameworks (Table 1). 23 The socioecological framework accounts not only for the proximal influences of healthcare and behavior on biological outcomes, but also the upstream influences of socioeconomic status and social and physical environments. 11, 12 Lifecourse research recognizes health as a longitudinal process, influenced by interacting social, environmental, and biological factors that shape health trajectories over the lifespan and temporal health trends in communities. 31 The Data and Analysis Team supports variable selection by identifying appropriate census tract-level measures from the Virginia All-Payer Claims Database (APCD), Virginia Department of Health mortality data, HealthLandscape Vital Signs library, and additional data sources as needed. 32, 33 Variables must represent directly observed or measured characteristics rather than synthetic estimates derived from population demographics.

For example, small-area estimates of health behaviors that are modeled using age, sex, race/ethnicity, or other population characteristics are not used as they would merely reflect the demographics of a community Once outcomes and independent variables are identified, the Data and Analysis Team conducts a statewide multivariable regression analysis at the census tract level to predict outcomes based on structural factors and key drivers. Model selection is guided by the distribution and measurement characteristics of each outcome, with generalized linear models used as appropriate for continuous, count, or rate-based outcomes, and Poisson regression for count data. Depending on data availability and the research question, models may incorporate multiple years of observations, interactions with time or other community characteristics, and methods to account for spatial autocorrelation. The statewide model results are applied to each census tract to calculate residuals as the difference between observed and predicted outcomes.

Bright spots are defined as the 30-50 census tracts with the most favorable outcomes relative to predictions, and challenge spots as the 30-50 with the least favorable outcomes relative to predictions. Because structural factors and key drivers identified by Topic Teams may be conceptually related, the Data and Analysis Team also assesses multicollinearity among independent variables. When substantial collinearity is identified, the team conducts sensitivity analyses using alternative models in which collinear variables are entered separately. Decisions about which measures to retain are made jointly with Topic Teams, considering substantive importance, stability of associations, and model performance.

Phase 3: Conduct Community Asset Mapping

From the initial hot spot and bright spot analyses the community asset mapping process identifies the modifiable assets, approaches, and resources that may explain why communities experience different outcomes. The process combines quantitative data with qualitative approaches to understand local context, knowledge, and perspectives. Ultimately, strengths identified through this process can be disseminated and adapted for use by other communities.

First, results from the initial bright spot multivariable analysis are shared with the Topic Team to identify where outcomes differ across Virginia. We create eight maps and two data tables. Four domains are displayed at two geographic levels, city/county and census tract: (1) distribution of hot and cold spots across all communities, (2) 30 to 50 highest-and lowestranking hot and cold spots, (3) distribution of bright and challenge spots across all communities, and (4) 30 to 50 highest-and lowest-ranking bright and challenge spots. The accompanying tables report the outcome, structural factor, and key driver values for each census tract and city/county. City/county analyses correspond to the geographic level at which many policy, programmatic, and investment decisions are made, whereas census tract analyses reveal variation within localities and identify neighborhoods where action may be needed or strengths-based lessons can be learned. In reviewing the data, Topic Teams first assess the external validity of the findings by determining whether the results are consistent with their knowledge and understanding of the communities represented.

Second, we examine what potentially modifiable factors may contribute to these differences. Topic Teams identify additional socioecological factors beyond the structural factors and key drivers included in the initial bright spot multivariable model, emphasizing factors that may be amenable to intervention (Table 1). When data are available for these factors, the Data and Analysis Team summarizes these measures for each census tract and city/county, and Topic Teams qualitatively compare patterns across communities to identify factors that may further explain differences in outcomes. Topic Teams then identify key stakeholders from a range of bright, challenge, hot, and cold communities for interviews by the Community Engagement Team. Interviews explore stakeholders' knowledge and perspectives regarding local factors, assets, resources, and approaches that may contribute to community outcomes, including their perceptions of how these factors influence outcomes and insights into potentially important factors for which quantitative data are unavailable.

Third, the Data and Topic Teams examine for whom and under what circumstances these modifiable factors may differ. We identify community characteristics that may modify these relationships, such as rurality or level of social deprivation, and conduct quantitative subgroup analyses and stakeholder interviews to determine whether communities sharing these characteristics have similar or different assets, challenges, and modifiable factors. For example, factors associated with better-than-predicted outcomes in rural communities may differ from those in urban communities, or approaches that appear important in communities with high social deprivation may differ from those in more advantaged communities.

Finally, we integrate findings across the bright spot analysis, additional quantitative comparisons, subgroup analyses, and stakeholder interviews to identify the estimated associations and community-perceived contributions of assets, resources, and approaches to outcomes. These findings are used to develop tailored sets of potential research, clinical care, public health, community, policy, and programmatic approaches that reflect communities' structural characteristics, modifiable factors, assets, and needs.

Phase 4: Refine Model

Findings from community asset mapping are used to refine the initial bright spot model. The Data and Analysis Team and Topic Team review additional factors identified through quantitative comparisons, subgroup analyses, and stakeholder interviews and determine whether any represent structural factors or key drivers that should have been included in the initial statewide model. Factors are considered for inclusion when they are conceptually related to the outcome, supported by the quantitative or qualitative findings, considered important by the Topic Team, and meet the data requirements described in Phase 2. Selected factors are added to a refined multivariable model, which is reapplied to all census tracts to recalculate predicted outcomes and residuals and determine whether the identification or ranking of bright and challenge spots changes.

Importantly, the purpose of model refinement is not to statistically adjust away the potentially modifiable assets, resources, and approaches that community asset mapping is intended to discover. Variables representing specific community programs, resources, practices, or other potentially transferable approaches are generally treated as explanatory findings rather than adjustment variables in the primary bright spot model. When a newly identified factor could reasonably function as either a key driver of the outcome or a potentially transferable community asset, we compare alternative model specifications with and without the factor. Changes in model coefficients, predicted outcomes, residuals, and bright spot classification are examined and interpreted with the Topic Team. This approach preserves the model's ability to account for important underlying community characteristics while retaining the assets and approaches that may explain how communities achieve better-than-predicted outcomes.

Phase 5: Develop Shared Meaning, Products, and Action (VA-AHEAD Dashboard Design)

The overarching goal of the bright spot model and community asset mapping approach is to identify community assets, resources, and approaches associated with favorable health outcomes and make these findings actionable for researchers, clinical teams, community partners, learners, government and public health officials. To do this, the Topic Teams will assemble key information for each of the seven topic domains into the Va-AHEAD website, https://virginia-ahead.org/. Content will include four major components: (1) data and measures, (2) tools and resources, (3) supported projects and partners, (4) success stories, and (5) team members. Throughout, we will use human-centered design principles to ensure content is accessible, understandable, and usable. 34

Data and Measures.

For each topic domains, the Va-AHEAD data dashboard will display findings for each bright spot model developed by the Topic Teams in three ways: (1) interactive maps identifying hot, cold, bright, and challenge spots at both the city/county and census tract levels; (2) graphs displaying each community along hot versus cold and bright versus challenge characteristics; and (3) community scorecards reporting the outcome measures, structural factors, key drivers, and additional quantitative measures identified through community asset mapping. Users will be able to filter and compare content by geographic region and community characteristics including rurality, poverty, social deprivation, and racial/ethnic composition. City/county-level findings can help guide public health programs, policies, and regional investments, while census tract-level findings can identify neighborhoods where resources and interventions may be needed or where community assets and approaches could inform action elsewhere. Measures will be updated annually to track changes over time and all data will be downloadable.

Tools and Resources.

Va-AHEAD will provide tools and resources to help users understand Bright spot findings, identify evidence-based approaches to improve outcomes, and apply lessons learned from successful communities. Features will include data export, geographic and temporal comparisons, additional analytic tools, training materials, and topicspecific reports documenting the seven phases of the assessment process. Each topic will also include a curated collection of evidence-based interventions, community resources, and promising approaches relevant to the factors and opportunities identified through bright spot and community asset mapping. These collections will summarize supporting evidence, implementation considerations, and links to relevant programs, organizations, and tools. To reach a broad range of audiences, we will develop infographics, one-page summaries, issue briefs, interactive StoryMaps, and other communication products.

Success Stories.

Va-AHEAD will feature stories of bright spot communities highlighting the resources, assets, and approaches that may explain their better-than-expected health outcomes. Stories will describe the community context, successful programs and approaches, partners involved, and lessons learned, with links to supporting evidence, implementation resources, and individuals or organizations with relevant expertise. These examples will help other communities identify promising strategies, connect with experienced partners, and consider opportunities for local adaptation.

Supported Projects and Partners.

Va-AHEAD will highlight projects and partnerships that use bright spot and community asset mapping findings to guide research, community planning, public health, and interventions to improve health outcomes. Each project will include a summary of its goals, partners, activities, findings, and relevant contacts or resources. These examples will demonstrate how Va-AHEAD findings are applied and facilitate collaboration and replication of promising approaches.

Phase 6: Disseminate and Support Use of Content

Our approach to disseminating Va-AHEAD findings and supporting their use will be guided by the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality's Dissemination Planning Tool framework, which emphasizes identifying dissemination content, target audiences, partners, and communication strategies, as well as evaluating and adapting dissemination efforts over time. 35 This systematic approach will help ensure that findings reach intended audiences and are used to inform research, policy, practice, and community action.

Phase 7: Measure the Impact

We will assess three dimensions of impact corresponding to our study aims: (1) creation of Va-AHEAD content through community partnerships, (2) dissemination and use of Va-AHEAD, and (3) effectiveness in supporting interventions that extend bright spot assets and approaches to communities with identified needs. [36] [37] [38] [39] Interviews will explore the nature and impact of partnerships, shared projects, communities reached, and resulting products and outcomes. Together, these sources will allow us to assess how Va-AHEAD is developed, used, and translated into community action.

Outcomes.

For Aim 1 (Creation), we will assess the number and types of measures, bright spot models, community asset mapping findings, tools, and dissemination products developed. We will also evaluate the community co-development process, including participation, contributions to dashboard content, and changes in partnership capacity and community mobilization scores over time. For Aim 2 (Use), we will measure the number and characteristics of researchers, clinicians, policymakers, learners, organizations, and communities reached; frequency and types of dashboard use; and the number and types of research, care delivery, public health, policy, and training activities supported. We will also assess academic and programmatic outputs, including grants, funding, interventions, presentations, publications, and workforce training, comparing productivity during the project with the five years preceding the project.

For Aim 3 (Effectiveness), we will assess whether Va-AHEAD increases interventions that address community-identified health priorities, expands participation of underserved communities in research and programs, and supports the adoption of resources, assets, and approaches identified in bright spot communities. Our primary outcome is the increase in bright spot assets and resources implemented in challenge spot communities. We will use project-supported reports and annually updated community measures to determine whether these approaches are being extended to communities with identified needs. Our secondary outcome is improvement in community-level structural factors, key drivers, and health outcomes. Because meaningful changes in population health may require more than five years, we will assess these outcomes longitudinally while recognizing that some impacts may extend beyond the study period.

Quantitative Analytic Plan.

We will use descriptive statistics to summarize dashboard content, users, dissemination activities, supported projects, and academic outputs. Annual bright spot analyses will assess changes in community characteristics, observed and predicted outcomes, and bright spot classifications over time. We will use generalized linear mixed models to evaluate increases in supported interventions, alignment of projects with identified community needs, inclusion of underserved populations, extension of bright spot assets and resources to challenge spot communities (primary outcome), and improvements in community health outcomes (secondary outcome). Analyses will be conducted at the census tract level, with fixed effects for time, relevant area-level characteristics from the American Community Survey, and area-level random effects to account for spatial correlation and repeated measurements.

Outcomes will be modeled as continuous or count measures, as appropriate, and nonlinear trends will be explored using polynomial or spline terms.

Qualitative Analytic Plan.

We will analyze meeting transcripts, field notes, and annual semi-structured interviews to understand the processes of co-developing, disseminating, and applying Va-AHEAD findings. We will use a combination of template-based coding informed by the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research and emergent coding to identify themes specific to the project. [40] [41] [42] Analyses will examine how community partnerships influence dashboard content, interpretation of findings, development of interventions, dissemination, and community action. We will also identify disconfirming evidence and integrate qualitative findings with quantitative outcomes to better understand the mechanisms and contextual factors contributing to project impact.

Power Estimation.

For each bright spot assessment, our primary outcome is the number of identified bright spot assets and resources implemented in challenge spot communities.

Among the 30 communities with the most severe worse-than-predicted outcomes, assuming no identified assets or resources at baseline and a standard deviation of two, we estimate greater than 80% power to detect an average increase of one asset or resource over time. Pooling approximately 500 challenge spot communities across the 15 planned assessments provides at least 80% power to detect an average increase of 0.25 assets or resources. Improvements in community health outcomes will be assessed as secondary outcomes, recognizing that these changes may require longer follow-up.

DISCUSSION

Va-AHEAD creates a lasting translational science resource to guide and inform research, clinical and community care; public health programs; local, state, federal government action; and training. It also presents a model that can be replicated for other health topics and in other geographic areas. By integrating comprehensive socioecological data with bright spot analyses and community asset mapping, Va-AHEAD moves beyond identifying differences in health outcomes to understanding the resources, assets, and approaches that help communities achieve better-than-expected outcomes. The resulting community scorecards provide a sustainable, datadriven roadmap for improving health outcomes, identifying where needs are greatest, what factors contribute to those needs, and the potentially modifiable approaches that may improve outcomes. Grounded in the full socioecological framework and informed by community partners, Our approach has three main limitations. First, although Virginia's APCD includes approximately 5.3 million of the state's 8.2 million residents, it excludes some veterans, uninsured individuals, and commercial claims. 43 However, the APCD includes complete Medicare and Medicaid claims, populations particularly important for understanding differences in health outcomes. Race and ethnicity are available for approximately 60% of claims and are supplemented by community characteristics from census data. We address incomplete claims through adjustments for missing commercial data and by emphasizing comparisons across geographic areas and trends over time. Second, mortality data may contain inaccuracies, particularly in the assignment of causes of death, although these errors are not expected to systematically bias geographic or temporal comparisons. Third, our long-term goal of improving health outcomes, particularly for people and communities with worse outcomes, is ambitious and requires sustained effort and cross-sector collaboration. Meaningful changes may extend beyond the five-year study period. Nevertheless, Va-AHEAD builds on established infrastructure and partnerships and provides the data, tools, and community engagement processes needed to establish priorities, guide action, and measure progress.

If successful, Va-AHEAD will support an expanding range of research, clinical and community care, policy, and training initiatives. We anticipate that these efforts will become increasingly sophisticated and impactful as we learn from our findings, expand the range and geographic distribution of partners and projects, and continue to update and adapt Va-AHEAD.

LEGEND.

Our bright spot and community asset mapping approach includes 6 steps: 1) identify the socioecological factors that contribute to outcomes, 2) conduct a multivariable analysis to create an overall model (statewide) that will predict outcomes based on socioecological factors, 3) apply overall model to each census tract and do a residual analysis to calculate the difference between actual versus predicted outcomes to identify bright spots with higher than predicted and challenge spots with lower than predicted outcomes, 4) compare characteristics of bright and challenge spot communities to identify factors, assets, and approaches that contribute to outcomes, 5) use findings to design research, transform care, and implement programs that will disseminate bright spot resources to communities in need, and 6) longitudinally track the health outcomes and contributing socioecological factors for each community.

Data Collection.

We will use five complementary data sources to assess project outcomes: (1) Va-AHEAD database, including community measures, bright spot analyses, and website utilization; (2) meeting minutes, recordings, transcripts, and field notes documenting community engagement and supported projects; (3) dissemination and training attendance records; (4) annual and final reports from Va-AHEAD-supported projects; and (5) annual partnership assessments. The partnership assessments, conducted with participating health systems, the CDTT, Topic Teams, and community organizations, will include surveys and follow-up interviews with representatives of each organization.
