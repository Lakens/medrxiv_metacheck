Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

Abstract

Gut microbiota composition has been associated with multiple neurological conditions, and candidate biomarkers have been proposed on the basis of these associations. Their usefulness presupposes that taxa altered in one population are also altered in others, a condition that is rarely verified. This work quantifies the extent to which it holds, and the geographic scale at which it ceases to do so.

Ten public 16S rRNA gene sequencing cohorts of Alzheimer's disease, Parkinson's disease and autism spectrum disorder, collected in eleven cities across six countries, were reprocessed from raw data, comprising 1,185 samples with uniform processing within each condition.

Models trained and evaluated within a single cohort reached a mean AUC of 0.751 in Alzheimer's disease, 0.741 in autism spectrum disorder and 0.673 in Parkinson's disease. When applied to a different population, these values fell to 0.569, 0.542 and 0.551 respectively. The decline was not associated with geographic distance: transfer between cities within the same country was equivalent to transfer between continents. No taxon appeared among the twenty most important features in all cohorts of a given condition, and no correlation between rankings reached significance.

Classifiers trained to predict cohort of origin, disregarding clinical group, reached AUC values of 0.87 to 1.00 using control subjects, and equivalent values using affected subjects. In one cohort with two recruitment centers and a single protocol, controls from both cities were separable (AUC 0.947), models did not transfer between centers (0.562 and 0.515), and their rankings correlated 0.003. Geographic heterogeneity of discriminant taxa operates below the national scale, which constrains the generalization of microbiome signatures derived from individual cohorts.

Introduction

The human gut microbiota harbors hundreds of bacterial genera whose metabolic activity influences host physiology beyond the digestive tract. Over the past two decades, a set of bidirectional communication pathways between the gut and the central nervous system has been characterized, collectively known as the gut-brain axis [1]. Among the described pathways are bacterial production of short-chain fatty acids, which modulate intestinal barrier integrity and microglial activity [2]; tryptophan metabolism toward indolic derivatives and the kynurenine pathway, some of whose products cross the blood-brain barrier [3]; afferent signaling through the vagus nerve [4]; and modulation of systemic inflammation by bacterial wall components [1]. These pathways provide a plausible mechanism by which alterations in microbiota composition might be associated with neurological, neurodegenerative and neurodevelopmental conditions.

On this basis, a considerable body of microbial composition studies has accumulated in patients with Alzheimer's disease [5, 6, 7, 9], Parkinson's disease [8] and autism spectrum disorder [10, 11, 12]. The predominant design is case-control, with 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing and differential abundance analysis between groups. These studies consistently identify taxa whose abundance differs between subjects with the condition and controls from the same population; several propose panels of candidate biomarkers, and some train classifiers that achieve appreciable performance within their own cohort [8, 13]. Throughout this work, "discriminant taxon" is used to refer to a genus whose abundance differs between groups, and "candidate biomarker" when referring to taxa proposed as such in the prior literature; neither term implies diagnostic validation.

Aggregating these findings in reviews and meta-analyses presupposes that taxa altered in one population are also altered in others. This assumption has rarely been tested directly, because doing so requires reprocessing other investigators' cohorts from raw data under uniform criteria.

The dominant expectation in the field takes two forms. The more widespread presupposes that taxa altered in a condition are altered independently of population, and candidate biomarker panels have been proposed on that basis. A second form, present in studies that acknowledge geographic variation in the microbiota, admits differences between regions or countries but implicitly maintains comparability within a single territory. Neither anticipates discordance between cities of the same country, still less between recruitment centers within a single study.

Regional variation in the gut microbiota has already shown consequences for model generalization. In a cohort of 7,009 individuals from fourteen districts of a single Chinese province, host location was the factor most strongly associated with microbial variation, and metabolic disease models derived in one locality failed when applied in another [14]. It remains unknown, however, whether this phenomenon extends to neurological and neurodevelopmental conditions, whether it also operates at international scale, and whether it affects the identity of discriminant taxa in addition to aggregate model performance. A second question overlaps with this one: comparison between independent studies accumulates differences in DNA extraction, library preparation, amplified hypervariable region, platform and sequencing depth [15]. These differences are well known, but verifying them beforehand is not part of standard practice when aggregating cohorts.

External validation in an independent population is, moreover, an established requirement for predictive models in health, set out in current reporting guidelines [21, 22]. In the microbiome literature, however, classification models are usually reported with internal validation only, and the effect of the derivation population on their performance is not quantified systematically. This work addresses both questions through uniform reprocessing of ten public 16S sequencing cohorts, corresponding to three conditions, collected in eleven cities across six countries. We evaluated, first, the capacity to separate cases from controls within each cohort; second, the transferability of that separation when the model is applied to a different population; and third, the concordance between the taxa that sustain the separation in each population. To distinguish the geographic component from the technical one, we separately analyzed a cohort with two recruitment centers in different cities, processed under a single protocol.

Results

Cohort selection and provenance

Searching public repositories identified approximately thirty deposits with 16S sequencing data in the three conditions. An admission protocol was applied to the public metadata before downloading any file, verifying amplified hypervariable region, sequencing platform, sample type, one-subject-per-sample uniqueness and availability of group labels.

Twenty deposits did not meet the criteria. Fourteen were excluded for technical incompatibility: 16S region different from the rest of the condition (n = 4), shotgun sequencing or RNA-Seq (n = 4), obsolete platform or proprietary format (n = 2), no accessible raw data (n = 2), and organism or gene different from that declared (n = 2). Six were excluded for design characteristics: therapeutic intervention or multiple time points per subject, pseudoreplication, and absence of group labels.

Ten cohorts met the criteria: five of Alzheimer's disease, three of autism spectrum disorder and two of Parkinson's disease, totaling 1,185 samples collected in eleven cities across six countries (Figure 1, Table 1). Processing reproduces published effect directions

To verify that uniform reprocessing did not introduce systematic deviations, the effect directions obtained were compared with those reported by the original authors in three Alzheimer's disease cohorts [5, 6, 7]. Of fifteen evaluable comparisons, fourteen agreed in direction, thirteen of them with statistical significance in the reanalysis. The remaining discrepancy corresponded to an annotation error in extracting the published direction, not to divergence in processing. Internal performance obtained in the Turkish cohort (AUC 0.633) likewise matched that published by its authors (0.63) [16].

Separation between cases and controls within each cohort

Models trained and evaluated within a single cohort reached AUC values between 0.583 and 0.955 (Figure 2). The mean was 0.751 in Alzheimer's disease, 0.741 in autism spectrum disorder and 0.673 in Parkinson's disease.

Three cohorts did not reach significance in the permutation test: Kayseri (AUC 0.594; p = 0.233), Xiamen (0.589; p = 0.156) and Iași (0.704; p = 0.053). All three correspond to the smallest sample sizes in the set. Three cohorts showed, by contrast, values above the range usually reported in the literature for these conditions: Shanghai (0.955), Lishui (0.865) and Nanjing (0.916). In two of them, a significant difference in sequencing depth was detected between cases and controls (Shanghai p = 0.007; Lishui p = 0.003), consistent with processing in separate batches by group.

In the case of Xiamen, a sensitivity analysis showed that internal performance depends on processing mode: when the cohort is reprocessed in paired-end mode, the AUC rises to 0.703 (p = 0.003). The reported absence of significance therefore corresponds to the uniform processing adopted for the condition, not to an absence of signal in the cohort.

Separation does not transfer between populations

When each model was applied to a different cohort, the AUC declined in all three conditions. The mean across cross-cohort pairs was 0.569 in Alzheimer's disease (30 pairs), 0.542 in autism spectrum disorder (6 pairs) and 0.551 in Parkinson's disease (2 pairs), compared with internal values of 0.751, 0.741 and 0.673 respectively (Figure 3). In the Alzheimer's disease condition, nine of thirty pairs fell below 0.5.

The magnitude of the decline was not associated with geographic distance. In Alzheimer's disease, the mean AUC between cities of the same country was 0.565, and between different countries 0.571. In autism spectrum disorder, within-country transfer (0.563) likewise did not exceed between-country transfer (0.523).

The taxa that sustain the separation do not coincide

Before comparing across cohorts, the stability of importance rankings within each cohort was evaluated. Spearman correlation between twenty independent fits with different seeds ranged from 0.896 to 0.959, indicating that estimation variability is low and that the discordance observed between cohorts is not attributable to fitting noise.

Across cohorts, no ranking correlation reached significance in any of the three conditions (Figure 4). Overlap among the twenty most important taxa ranged from 0 to 7 of 20 depending on the pair. No taxon appeared among the top twenty of all cohorts of Alzheimer's disease or of autism spectrum disorder. The region-grouped contrast identified few taxa with concordant direction and significance: ten of 211 genera in Alzheimer's disease, two of 178 in autism spectrum disorder and eight of 173 in Parkinson's disease (Table 2). Most correspond to short-chain fatty acid producers. Three, nine and nine confirmed direction reversals were identified, respectively (Figure 5).

Parkinson's disease illustrates that the two properties are independent: eight genera maintained concordant direction and significance between Finland and Romania, the highest directional concordance of the three conditions; nevertheless, the model trained in Finland performed at chance level in Romania (AUC 0.513) and the correlation between rankings was 0.014.

Sample origin predominates over condition

To gauge the magnitude of variation between populations, classifiers were trained to predict the cohort of origin of each sample, disregarding clinical group. Using control subjects only, cohorts were separable with AUC values between 0.867 and 1.000. Using only subjects with the condition, values were equivalent: 0.942 on average in Alzheimer's disease, 0.999 in autism spectrum disorder and 0.999 in Parkinson's disease, with differences relative to controls of -0.026, +0.002 and +0.004 (Figure 6).

In the pairs described above, provenance conflates city, study and protocol, which are not separable from one another. One cohort allows them to be dissociated: the Turkish study recruited in two cities under a single protocol, with the same extraction kits and the same diagnostic criteria, and with no difference in sequencing depth between centers (p = 0.78) [16].

Control subjects from Istanbul and Kayseri were separable with an AUC of 0.947, and subjects with Alzheimer's disease from both cities with an AUC of 0.922. Internal performance was 0.633 at both centers. A model trained in Istanbul reached 0.562 when applied in Kayseri, and the reverse 0.515. The correlation between their importance rankings was 0.003 (p = 0.97), with three taxa shared among the top twenty.

Discussion

The results show that the separation between cases and controls observed within a cohort is not preserved when the model is applied to another population. The magnitude of that loss is unrelated to geographic distance: in Alzheimer's disease, transfer between cities of the same country was equivalent to transfer between continents.

The case hardest to attribute to technical variation is the Turkish cohort. Two recruitment centers some seven hundred kilometers apart, with a single extraction and sequencing protocol and no difference in depth between them, produced control populations separable with an AUC of 0.947 and models that do not transfer to each other. That differences exist between distant populations is an accepted observation in the field; that they appear between two cities of the same country, within the same study, places the scale of heterogeneity below the national boundary. This phenomenon converges with what was observed in a metabolic disease cohort within a Chinese province [14], despite differences in condition, population and geographic scale.

Origin predominates over condition

Comparing the three separability measures puts the phenomenon in perspective. Distinguishing cases from controls within a cohort reached a mean AUC of 0.67 to 0.75 depending on the condition. Distinguishing the cohort of origin of two subjects reached values of 0.94 to 1.00, and did so equivalently using only controls or only subjects with the condition.

The condition does not reduce separability between populations. If the pathology converged toward a common altered state, affected subjects from different populations would be expected to be less distinguishable from one another than controls. No such effect was observed in any of the three conditions. This observation constrains the interpretation of the differential taxa reported in the literature: variation associated with sample provenance is of greater magnitude than that associated with the condition under study.

Computational harmonization does not resolve comparability

The ten cohorts were processed with the same version of the variant inference algorithm, the same taxonomic reference database and a fixed seed. Despite this, they were separable from one another with AUC values between 0.93 and 1.00.

Differences that precede sequencing DNA extraction protocol, library preparation, sequencer chemistry, depth, recruitment criteria are not corrected by downstream bioinformatic processing [15]. In two of the cohorts, a systematic difference in depth between cases and controls was additionally detected, in opposite directions. The practical consequence is that aggregating cohorts from independent studies requires explicit verification of separability and, where appropriate, batch effect correction [19, 20], before interpreting differences between groups as attributable to the condition under study.

The effect of processing decisions on the result is of the same magnitude as that attributed to the condition under study. In one of the autism spectrum disorder cohorts, processing reads in paired-end mode instead of single-end changed the internal AUC by 0.108 points, a value greater than the mean difference between the internal performance of the Parkinson's disease cohorts and that of the Alzheimer's disease cohorts. Comparability between studies therefore requires verifying not only data provenance but also the processing decisions applied to each dataset.

Effect direction and predictive structure are distinct properties

Parkinson's disease offers the clearest contrast. Eight of 173 genera maintained concordant direction and significance between Finland and Romania, the highest directional concordance of the three conditions; nevertheless, the model trained in Finland performed at chance level in Romania and the correlation between importance rankings was 0.014.

That a taxon is altered in the same direction in two populations does not imply that a classifier using it is transferable. Effect direction is a marginal property of each taxon; predictive structure depends on the joint relationships among all of them, which may differ even when individual directions coincide. This distinction is relevant when interpreting the replication of findings across studies: agreement in the direction of a differential taxon does not constitute validation of a model derived from it.

It should be noted, however, that conclusions concerning Parkinson's disease rest on a single pair of cohorts, with sample sizes of 133 and 39 subjects, and that this pair additionally spans two different countries. Unlike the Alzheimer's disease analysis, supported by fifteen cohort pairs of which eight correspond to cities within the same country, the design available for Parkinson's disease does not permit inference of the within-country pattern in that condition.

The functional clue

Most of the taxa concordant across regions belong to a single functional group. In Alzheimer's disease, Lachnospira and Faecalibacterium; in Parkinson's disease, Fusicatenibacter, Blautia, Roseburia, Agathobaculum and Agathobacter. All of these participate in the production of short-chain fatty acids.

This suggests that functional capacity might be conserved across populations more often than the taxonomic composition that sustains it. Alternative interpretations exist: that the relevant pathophysiological pathways differ between populations, or that part of the discordance is technical in origin. Testing this hypothesis requires validated functional inference and, ultimately, direct measurement of metabolites, available in only one of the cohorts analyzed [12].

Limitations

This work evaluates separability between groups and its transferability between populations. It does not evaluate diagnostic capacity, nor does it propose criteria for clinical classification.

In cohort pairs drawn from independent studies, provenance conflates city, study and protocol, which are not separable. Only the Turkish cohort allows them to be dissociated by design. No public cohort records comparable dietary data, so the hypothesis of dietary mediation cannot be tested with this material.

Sample support also differs across conditions. The Alzheimer's disease analysis has fifteen cohort pairs and six cities; autism spectrum disorder, three pairs; and Parkinson's disease, a single international pair.

Statements about the scale at which heterogeneity operates are supported principally by the first. The Alzheimer's disease and Parkinson's disease cohorts use the V3-V4 region and the autism spectrum disorder cohorts the V4 region; magnitudes are not comparable across conditions. The cohorts were moreover designed to answer questions specific to each study, and relevant variables such as gastrointestinal comorbidity, medication and diet are reported in some and not in others, which prevents homogeneous stratification. Gastrointestinal comorbidity, frequent in autism spectrum disorder and a known source of variation in microbial composition [10], is reported with a per-subject score in only one of the three cohorts of that condition.

The decision to process the autism spectrum disorder cohorts in single-end mode, imposed by the deposit format of one of them, reduces the resolution available in the other two. The corresponding sensitivity analysis is presented in the supplementary material.

Three cohorts did not reach significance in the permutation test, and two have reduced sample sizes (n = 35 and n = 39); one of these additionally shows an age difference of 7.3 years between groups, which cannot be adjusted for at that sample size. Finally, analyses by number of features used selection prior to cross-validation, which introduces optimistic bias in those specific estimates.

Implications

The results suggest that deriving microbiome signatures from individual cohorts requires validation in independent populations before their generality can be assumed, and that such validation cannot be replaced by aggregating findings that agree in direction.

Resolving the underlying question what proportion of the observed heterogeneity is population-related and what proportion technical requires cohorts collected under a single protocol in different populations, with homogeneous clinical phenotyping and direct measurement of metabolites. Available public data do not permit that separation except in exceptional cases of multicenter design, such as the one analyzed here.

Methods

Scope of the study

This work evaluates separability between cases and controls in 16S sequencing cohorts and its transferability between populations. It does not evaluate diagnostic capacity, nor does it propose criteria for clinical classification.

Pre-specified hypotheses

Before running any model, two predictions were stated in writing. The first was that the performance published for the Turkish cohort (AUC 0.63) would be explained by having combined two different recruitment centers, and that analyzing them separately would yield higher performance in each. The second was that the taxa most important to the model would not be the same in different populations, even if the direction of their abundance did coincide.

The first prediction was not confirmed: analyzed separately, both centers reached an AUC of 0.633, the same value as the combined set. Heterogeneity between cities did not manifest where it was expected as a loss of performance within each center -but in the transfer between them and in the discordance between their discriminant taxa. The second prediction was confirmed in all three conditions.

Search and admission criteria

The European Nucleotide Archive, the Sequence Read Archive, QIITA and Zenodo were searched using terms relating to the three conditions combined with "microbiome", "gut microbiota" and "16S". The search was complemented with the data availability statements of publications in the field. An admission protocol was applied to each identified deposit, verified against public metadata before downloading sequence files: sample type restricted to feces; 16S amplicon strategy, excluding shotgun metagenomics and RNA-Seq; hypervariable region matching the other cohorts of the same condition; Illumina platform; one sample per subject, or the possibility of selecting one; group labels accessible in public metadata; institution and location of origin confirmed in the deposit metadata; and absence of intervention prior to sampling, or an isolable baseline.

The hypervariable region was verified by inspecting the first bases of the reads in a representative file per deposit. This criterion excluded cohorts widely cited in the field, among them those of Scheperjans [8] (V1-V3 region, 454 platform) and Kang [10] (V2/V3 region, pyrosequencing), as not comparable with the other cohorts of their respective conditions.

Sequence processing

Sequences were processed with DADA2 version 1.34 in R 4.4.2, with the seed fixed at 42.

Where primers were present, they were removed by sequence localization using vmatchPattern from the Biostrings package, allowing degenerate bases and up to two mismatches; only the read pair in which both ends contained the corresponding primer was retained. Fixed-position trimming was not used, given that several cohorts contain heterogeneity spacers of variable length.

Truncation parameters were calibrated per cohort according to the actual read length after trimming. maxN = 0, maxEE = c(2,5), truncQ = 2 and PhiX removal were applied. The error model was learned independently for forward and reverse reads; pair merging used a minimum overlap of twelve bases and chimeras were removed by the consensus method. Taxonomic assignment was performed against SILVA nr99 version 138.2, with search in both orientations; sequence variants were aggregated at genus level and those unassigned at that rank were discarded.

The three autism spectrum disorder cohorts were processed in single-end mode on the forward read truncated to 145 bases. One of them is deposited only as a forward read and therefore does not permit pair merging; processing the other two in paired-end mode would have introduced a processing difference between cohorts of the same condition.

The effect of this decision was evaluated by reprocessing in paired-end mode the two cohorts that allow it. Internal performance increased in one of them (from 0.595 to 0.703; p changed from 0.163 to 0.003) and was maintained in the other (0.691 to 0.694), while transfer between the two declined from 0.530 and 0.524 to 0.447 and 0.418, and between-cohort separability remained unchanged (0.998 and 0.999).

Single-end processing therefore yields higher transfer estimates than paired-end processing, and so constitutes the conservative choice with respect to the conclusion of this work (Table S3).

In the Finnish cohort, of longitudinal design [17], a single sample per subject was retained, the earliest by collection date. In the Italian cohort, only baseline samples were used, prior to the probiotic intervention. In the Shanghai cohort, which additionally included a mild cognitive impairment group [5], only the Alzheimer's disease and control groups were used.

Data preparation and classification models

Genus-level count matrices were converted to relative abundance and transformed by centered logratio, using a pseudocount of 1×10⁻⁶ for zeros. Cross-cohort analyses used the genera common to all cohorts of the condition, filtered by a minimum prevalence of 20% in each of them.

The pseudocount value was set at 1×10⁻⁶, below the lowest detectable relative abundance given the sequencing depths of the set. A sensitivity analysis over the range 1×10⁻⁷ to 1×10⁻⁴ produced mean variations of 0.025 AUC points, without altering the significance of any cohort (Table S1).

Random forests were used with 500 trees, max_features = sqrt and balanced class weighting. Internal separability was estimated by stratified ten-fold cross-validation, repeated twenty times with different seeds, and the mean is reported. Confidence intervals were obtained by bootstrap with 1,000 resamples of the out-of-fold predictions, and the null hypothesis was tested by permutation test with 300 permutations of the labels. Transfer was evaluated by training the model on the whole of one cohort and applying it to the whole of another, restricted to shared features. Between-cohort separability was estimated by training a classifier to predict cohort of origin, disregarding clinical group, separately on controls and on subjects with the condition.

All performance evaluation was carried out on out-of-sample predictions; values obtained on training data are not reported.

Feature importance and effect size

SHAP values were calculated with TreeExplainer over twenty models fitted with independent seeds, averaging the absolute value per feature. Within-cohort stability was evaluated by Spearman correlation between all pairs of rankings obtained with different seeds; between-cohort concordance, by Spearman correlation between the averaged rankings and by the overlap among the twenty most important features.

Effect size between groups was quantified by Cliff's delta on relative abundances, with confidence intervals from 500 bootstrap resamples, and significance by the Mann-Whitney U test. For the regional contrast, the cohorts of each condition were grouped by region and the effect was calculated separately in each group. A genus was considered concordant if it had the same sign in both regions, magnitude equal to or greater than 0.10 in both, and significance in both; a confirmed reversal was defined as a genus with opposite sign, magnitude equal to or greater than 0.10 in both regions, and non-overlapping confidence intervals.
