Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Objective: Interruptions are frequent in clinical care globally and associated with inefficiency, cognitive burden, and patient safety risks. Existing research into interruptions reflects paper-based workflows, and "digital distraction" studies often focus on personal device use. There is little research regarding interruptions produced by mandated digital infrastructures such as Electronic Health Records (EHRs) and clinical messaging platforms. This study aims to examine how mandated digital systems shape the form, experience, and meaning of interruptions in English outpatient care, and how doctors attempt to manage these demands.

We conducted a qualitative analysis of focus group and interview data from a larger study of interruptions in outpatient care. Participants were 42 UK outpatient care doctors across specialties and experience levels, recruited via a screener survey and stratified into six groups based on experience level and self-reported patient complexity (trainee, 0-19 years, and 20+ years' experience, each divided into high and low complexity). Data were analysed using Reflexive Thematic Analysis, with interpretation informed by the combined lens of Cognitive Load Theory and Social Identity theory; a pairing not previously applied together to interruptions, capturing both the cognitive and social-relational dimensions of digital interruptions. Analysis as structured deductively around these theories while remaining open to inductive, novel insights.

Results: Four themes captured digitally mediated interruption. Mandated systems normalised constant availability, making interruption a persistent condition of work. Digital tools acted as cognitive amplifiers, increasing extraneous cognitive load through fragmented interfaces, multi-window navigation, and technical unreliability, with perceived safety risks around patient-context switching. Digital coordination occurred without social cues, weakening visibility and contextual signals that support judgements of relevance, urgency, and responsibility, contributing to interpersonal friction. Doctors sought to reclaim control through individual strategies, including limiting exposure to channels and selectively embracing digital prompts where they supported safety or care quality.

Conclusion: Mandated digital infrastructures transform interruption into continuous, cognitively disruptive, and socially ambiguous demands. Mitigation requires system-and organisation-level solutions beyond individual workarounds.

Introduction

Interruptions are a pervasive feature of clinical practice, defined as a break in the performance of a task, initiated by an external stimulus, that temporarily diverts attention from the primary activity before it is resumed (1). Interruptions are recognised globally as a source of inefficiency and mental burden for healthcare workers, and increases patient safety risks. Interruptions may originate from multiple sources, including other healthcare professionals, patients and their families, alarms and technology, or administrative demands (2, 3). Empirical research across hospital settings consistently demonstrates that staff are frequently interrupted during core clinical activities, including patient care, medication administration, laboratory tasks, and surgery (4). Such interruptions have been documented across a range of environments, including critical care (5), inpatient wards (6), and medication administration contexts (7), suggesting that an interruption is not incidental but embedded within the everyday coordination of clinical work (8). Importantly, frequent interruptions have been associated with increased medication errors (9), divided attentional resources (8, 10), and delayed task completion (4, 11), positioning interruptions as a persistent and hazardous feature of contemporary healthcare delivery.

However, most existing interruptions literature has developed within clinical contexts organised around paper-based systems and relatively stable models of work (Rivera & Karsh, 2010; Teigne et al., 2023). In these accounts, interruptions are typically conceptualised as discrete external events (e.g., a phone ringing, a colleague approaching, an alarm sounding) that momentarily disrupt a bounded primary task, such as a medication round or documentation episode (6, 12). This framing assumes neatly divided clinical work in which a primary task can be clearly identified, temporarily paused, and then resumed. Yet these insights rest on assumptions about workflow that no longer reflect the fluid, digitally mediated nature of contemporary clinical practice Over the past two decades, healthcare systems have undergone large-scale digital transformation, with Electronic Health Records (EHRs) now central to documentation, communication, and care coordination globally (13, 14). Clinical work is increasingly conducted through digitally mediated systems that structure how tasks are initiated, prioritised, paused, and resumed (15). As a result, interruptions no longer occur solely as physical interpersonal or environmental disruptions, but also through the routine operation of digital infrastructures.

These digitally mediated interruptions differ in important ways from traditional, face-to-face disruptions. From a cognitive perspective, interruptions embedded within digital systems often occur alongside complex interface navigation, fragmented documentation, and ongoing task switching, all compounding attentional demands and making task resumption more effortful (16, 17). From a social perspective, digital interruptions frequently lack the contextual cues that accompany in-person interactions, obscuring intent, urgency, and relational context (18, 19). The persistence and visibility of digital communication channels also shape emerging norms around responsiveness, subtly reinforcing expectations of constant availability as part of professional identity (20, 21). In this way, digital systems not only increase the frequency of interruptions; they also change how interruptions are cognitively processed and socially interpreted.

Early empirical evidence supports this shift. Studies have demonstrated that the introduction of EHRs is associated with increased task switching and workflow fragmentation, altering both the frequency and form of interruptions experienced by healthcare professionals globally (15, 18, 22). Similarly, the expansion of instant messaging and alert-based communication systems has introduced persistent channels of contact, enabling interruptions to occur asynchronously, repeatedly, and across spatial boundaries (23, 24).

Despite this emerging evidence, most literature addressing "digital distraction" in healthcare has focused on personal device use, such as smartphones accessed for non-clinical purposes during shifts (25) (26) (27). This body of work often frames disruption in terms of professionalism, boundary management, or inappropriate technology use. This emphasis has had the unintended effect of obscuring a distinct and increasingly salient phenomenon: interruptions generated through mandated digital systems that are integral to clinical care.

Mandated technologies, such as EHRs and clinical messaging platforms, are organisationally sanctioned, embedded within formal care processes, and are needed for documentation, communication, and decision-making (28). As such, interruptions arising from these systems cannot be readily understood as optional, avoidable, or individual in nature (8). Yet comparatively little attention has been paid to how these mandated digital infrastructures shape interruption patterns, cognitive demands, and doctors' everyday experiences of work.

To address this gap, the present study offers an original qualitative examination of digitally mediated interruptions in outpatient care across England, with particular attention to those generated through mandated systems such as EHRs and clinical messaging platforms. At their core, these interruptions are cognitive events, imposing attentional and processing demands that disrupt task performance. These processes are well documented in Cognitive Load Theory (CLT) (29, 30), which holds that working memory (the limited mental system that temporarily holds and manipulates information during active tasks) has a finite capacity, and that demands placed on it beyond the primary task risk degrading performance. The study draws on CLT to examine how system complexity, fragmented workflows, and frequent tasks switching shape doctors' attentional demands. However, these cognitive effects do not occur in isolation. Whether, when, and how an interruption is attended to is shaped by the social environment in which it occurs, including expectations placed on doctors to be reachable, responsive, and accountable within their professional role (8). To capture this, the study also draws on Social Identity Theory (SIT) (31), which holds that individuals derive part of their sense of self from the groups and roles they belong to, and regulate their behaviour to match the norms and expectations attached to those roles. SIT is used here to examine how digitally mediated interruptions contribute to evolving norms of responsiveness and availability that become embedded within contemporary clinical identities. This study was guided by the following research question:

In what ways do mandated digital systems shape the form, experience, and meaning of interruptions in everyday outpatient care settings, and how can they be managed?

Methods

Design

This study reports a qualitative secondary analysis of data generated as part of a larger study examining interruptions in outpatient clinical work, involving focus groups and interviews with 42 outpatient doctors in England. The parent study was analysed using Framework Analysis, with broader findings reported in our companion papers (8, 32). The present analysis focuses specifically on digitally mediated interruptions arising from EHRs, instant messaging systems, electronic task lists, and other mandated digital platforms. Ethical approval was obtained from UCL Research Ethics Committee (ID: 1132).

Participants and Recruitment

A screener survey was distributed via a large medical defence organisation, capturing demographic and professional characteristics, including experience, perceived job complexity, interruption frequency, and interruption management strategies (33). Participants who expressed interest in follow-up qualitative participation provided contact details and consent for re-contact. Participants volunteered with no prior relationship to the research team, and were informed only that the study concerned interruptions and their management. Survey data were used to stratify participants into six groups by experience level (trainee; 0-19 years post-qualification; 20+ years post-qualification) and self-reported patient profile complexity (high; low), producing a 3 × 2 stratification matrix (see Table 1). The thresholds used to define the experience groups were based on existing research identifying the point at which experience begins to influence clinical performance. This purposive stratification, rather than convenience sampling, was informed by prior evidence that experience and complexity both shape the cognitive impact of interruptions (34, 35), and supported a heterogenous sample across seniority and case-mix.

Participants within each stratum attended group-specific focus groups or, where scheduling did not permit this, individual interviews. In total, 42 participants were recruited across the six strata, contributing to 19 data collection sessions (see Tables 1 & 2). Data were collected through focus groups (2-5 participants) and individual semi-structured interviews (approx. 55 minutes) conducted online via Microsoft Teams in 2025, and facilitated by SB (Cognitive Psychologist) and CS (Social Psychologist). Theme 1: Normative Availability as a Feature of Everyday Clinical Work

Participants described working in environments where availability was widely assumed as a routine feature of everyday clinical practice. Rather than being limited to specific roles or moments, digital communication created an expectation that doctors could be contacted at any point during the working day. In this account, availability is not explicitly demanded but inferred through system presence, with digital visibility overriding contextual cues about workload or competing responsibilities. This expectation of reachability was reinforced by multiple communication platforms operating in parallel -phones, bleeps, electronic messaging systems, and email -often without shared guidance about which channel should be used in which circumstances. The absence of agreed communication norms redistributed the problem of triage onto individual doctors who could no longer rely on any single channel as the place where urgent information would surface. This left many feeling compelled to monitor ever channel simultaneously for fear of missing something important: "Sometimes you think, Oh my God, if I don't have all 7 methods of communication open at once. I'm going to miss something because actually somebody could contact me about something quite important and urgent on any one of these devices" (Catrina, Paediatric Oncology Consultant, Group 3).

It is through these everyday practices that mandated digital systems help produce a pervasive culture of immediacy, in which availability is normalised and difficult to refuse, even in the absence of explicit organisational expectations.

Theme 2: Digital Systems as Cognition Amplifiers

Within this context of constant incoming demands, participants described a marked increase in the cognitive effort required to navigate mandated digital systems. Thus, while interruptions were disruptive in their own right, participants emphasised that digital systems amplified the cognitive workload involved in managing them: "So what was previously either piece of paper or few clicks on the computer?

Now it takes much longer and I've many more windows for single patient […] Not allowing me to focus on one thing […] and then if I've opened a window because I was listing the previous patients for operation, and now I'm not listing. I need X-ray. Then it means closing this window, open another window or a swap in between windows and then I tend to find myself a little bit lost. Have I done the letter or not? Have I booked in for this MRI or not? And this is this is mentally exhausting" (Baxter, Oncology Consultant, Group 6).

Several participants contrasted this with paper-based work, where information could be kept visible and interruptions managed without dismantling the entire workspace. In digital systems, however, rather than simply pausing one task to respond to another, doctors described having to close down multiple screens, switch between systems, and later actively reconstruct where they had been in their original task, placing additional demands on attention and working memory. This combination of interruption and system navigation introduced new risks around task resumption: "It just takes your flow and it makes you feel undervalued because you shouldn't have some of these bureaucratic or administrative problems to deal with. And there are some specific risks, you know. So if you've got six IT systems and you've put Mr X on all of them ready for the clinic or the consultation, but then the radiographer comes in to ask about Mr Y and you quickly look him up in one of the systems […] 20 minutes later when you're back talking to Mr Jones, do you miss that you've got the wrong one on the screen" (Owen, Clinical Oncologist, Group 6).

In this way, interruptions created a dual cognitive burden. Doctors had to manage the clinical problem and the additional work of navigating complex digital environments. This dual complexity made task-switching more effortful and recovery more uncertain than in paper-based work.

Theme 3: Coordination Without Social Cues

Participants described how digitally mediated communication reshaped the social coordination of interruptions by stripping away social context. This weakened cues for judging urgency, relevance, and appropriateness that had previously helped regulate interruptions: "I suppose you can't really tell until you physically get your phone out and look at it or you click on the relevant secure chat message […] We have a secure chat function […] you'll probably find that the username you've attached to that specialty has got 10 or 15 users inside that.

So what becomes a very simple question can very quickly become a whole stream of responses […] and you don't really know, at that point, who it's for or who's meant to pick it up" (Mason, Emergency and Acute Medicine Consultant, Group 4).

This ambiguity was compounded by multi-user chats that blurred intent and responsibility, often making it harder to judge who a message was directed at or how urgently it should be treated. Participants also described how digitally mediated work removed visible social cues that had previously signalled clinically significant, non-interruptible activity: "We do basically a virtual clinic where we'll go through sort of 30-40 patients in the morning. To other members of the team we just look like we're sitting in front of a laptop. So you're ripe to be interrupted, but actually you are engrossed in decision making and you just don't happen to have a patient in front of you. And having the cue of a patient in front of me is easier to get back to, than when I'm on a computer and then interrupted" (Megan, Urology Consultant, Group 6).

Without the social protection afforded by a visibly present patient, digitally mediated clinical work became easier to misrecognise as interruptible, weakening the social pressure that might otherwise restrain interruptions. These accounts suggest that digitally mediated communication reshapes coordination by eroding the cues that previously helped regulate interruption and responsibility.

Theme 4: Reclaiming Control Over Digital Interruptions

Participants described two main strategies for managing digital demands: 1) limiting exposure to digital channels; and 2) selectively embracing the benefits of digital tools. These strategies were largely individual and situational rather than formally supported by organisational systems.

For many participants, the most immediate response was to limit exposure by silencing devices, closing email, or reducing notifications during clinical work. These actions were framed not as disengagement, but as necessary measures to preserve focus. Some also made their availability more legible to others through informal signals: "My current secretary's former colleague […] he used to sit at his desk, and if he was in a mood to take a question he would be sitting there normally.

But if his tie was draped over his left shoulder, that was his sign for saying do not talk, walk away […] some people do put on their e-mail signatures 'this is how to get me, this is my timetable' […] I quite often shut my emails when I'm seeing patients in clinic" (Owen, Oncologist, Group 6). These strategies were often fragile, dependent on constant personal vigilance rather than being supported by system design or organisational norms. Alongside boundary-setting, some participants also described a more forward-looking approach, embracing interruptions from digital tools that were perceived to enhance patient care. This included distinguishing interruptions that disrupted workflow from those that introduced safety checks or decision support: "There can be positive digital interruptions as well, so electronic prescribing, there'll be a pop-up box that'll come up with: 'Do you mean to prescribe this? This has already been prescribed? Is this a continuing course?' Explain your rationale. So there can be safety checks in digital interruptions" (Darren, Obstetrician & Gynaecologist, Group 4). Others adopted a more pragmatic stance, acknowledging the disruptive potential of digital devices while also trying to incorporate them into clinical encounters in ways that might improve communication or care: "I suppose it does interrupt things. I say, right, get your phone out. I'm now going to e-mail you. And of course, you tell the parents to get their phone out and they're going to mention they're going to answer all their WhatsApp's and all their other notifications. So yeah, you know, so it is a bit difficult, but I'm also trying to use the technology to improve the way I consult with people. So I think that's swings and roundabouts" (Charlotte, Consultant Paediatrician, Group 6). These accounts suggest that managing digital interruptions did not always mean reducing them. Instead, doctors sought to reclaim control by carving out pockets of protected attention and by reframing digital systems as tools for safer or more effective care.

Discussion

This study is the first to explore how outpatient doctors experience mandated digital interruptions, applying a combined cognitive and social theoretical framework to specify why these interruptions generate greater burdens than the face-to-face interruptions that characterised former healthcare delivery. This joint CLT-SIT lens offers a transferable framework for researchers examining how technology-mediated demands intersect with professional identity in other highaccountability settings.

From Episodic to Ambient Interruption

Prior work has largely treated interruption as an episodic disruption, escapable by changing location or context (2,11). Our findings extend this by showing that mandated digital systems create a sustained condition of availability that doctors cannot step outside of, even by physically leaving a ward or working from home. This reframes interruption not as a discrete event, but as a background condition of digitally mediated work; a shift in kind rather than degree from how the phenomenon has previously been conceptualised.

From Information Overload to Systemic Cognitive Disruption

Our findings align with, and extend, earlier work on workflow fragmentation in EHRmediated work (15, 18, 22) in three ways. First, whereas prior research has largely focused on single settings, such as emergency medicine (15) or primary care (22), our findings suggest these dynamics are a general feature of digitally mediated clinical work rather than being confined to particular specialties.

Second, rather than a narrow subset of digital functions such as inbox management, our findings speak to the wider ecology of mandated systems. Interruption risk arises from how multiple systems interact, not from any single tool, positioning digital interruption as a systemic property of contemporary clinical work rather than a problem localised to specific departments.

Third, where existing accounts largely frame the problem in terms of information volume (18), our findings show the burden lies not only in how much information doctors receive, but in the structure of the cognitive work required to manage it: task switching, navigating fragmented interfaces, and verifying that the correct patient is in view. This shift from quantity to structure helps explain why digital interruptions can be effortful and error-prone even when their informational content is simple, and links this cognitive burden directly to safety risk, particularly around patientcontext switching.

From Communication Breakdowns to the Erosion of Social Regulation

Existing literature has noted fragmentation in digital healthcare communication (15, 18, 22) and coordination challenges in digital channels more broadly (19, 21), but has not directly theorised how the social norms and visible cues that once regulated interruption are altered when work is digitally mediated. Our findings address this gap. In face-to-face settings, interruption is regulated through visibility, timing, and shared situational awareness (5, 11), whereas digital interruptions frequently arrive stripped of these signals, with multi-user chats further obscuring who is being addressed. From a SIT perspective, this matters because professional norms of responsiveness are enacted through everyday interactional cues (31). When these are absent, doctors are left to infer expectations and manage accountability individually. This illuminates a novel avenue through which digitally mediated interruption disrupts clinical workflows: not only as a problem of information overload, but of social regulation.

From Reduction to Managing Digital Interruption in Practice

Finally, participants also described two broad strategies for managing digital demands: limiting exposure to digital channels, and selectively embracing tools perceived to enhance safety or care quality. Neither strategy reversed the conditions described above. Rather, both represent attempts to make digitally saturated work more tolerable within existing constraints, echoing prior observations that doctors often rely on local workarounds rather than system-level solutions when facing EHRrelated burden (15, 17). This is consistent with evidence that EHRs often shift interruption management onto doctors rather than embedding protections into workflows (15, 18), reinforcing sociotechnical arguments that poorly aligned digital systems externalise coordination and safety work onto users rather than absorbing it into system design (15, 18). In practice, doctors experience the steady addition of new digital systems as an accumulation of cognitive load and fragmented attention, layered onto persistent expectations of constant availability, with no organisational imperative to help them manage it (39). This highlights the need for organisational approaches to interruption management.

Strengths, Limitations, and Future Directions

This study extends interruption research beyond paper-based, task-bounded models (2, 11), and narrow foci on personal device distraction (25, 26), by examining how mandated digital infrastructures shape interruption across outpatient care. By integrating cognitive (e.g., CLT) and social (e.g., SIT) perspectives, it moves beyond accounts of information overload (18) to show how digital systems transform both the cognitive work of task management (16, 17) and the social regulation of attention and responsibility (19, 21). The research team's complementary professional backgrounds, spanning cognitive psychology, clinical practice, health psychology, and social psychology, allowed data to be interrogated through multiple analytic lenses, reducing the risk that findings reflect a single disciplinary standpoint. Finally, emerging findings and draft recommendations were shared with a subset of participants to assess resonance with their clinical experience, a form of member checking that supports the credibility of the analysis (38).

Two limitations should be noted. First, this study draws on qualitative accounts rather than direct observation or system log data. Future research could complement this approach with ethnographic studies or EHR trace data, as used in studies of workflow fragmentation and inbox work (15, 22). Second, although the sample spans multiple specialties and experience levels, it is situated within English outpatient care and within particular EHR and messaging infrastructures. However, given that EHR adoption reflects a shared international policy priority rather than a UK-specific phenomenon (40), the mechanisms identified here are likely to extend to other health systems with comparable digital infrastructures, though comparative studies across settings and platforms would help to establish this directly given system configuration and organisational context strongly shape interruption patterns (18, 28).

Future research should test intervention strategies at the level of system design and organisational policy, examining how alert hierarchies and task interfaces shape cognitive load (16, 17). and how team-level norms might reintroduce shared regulation of interruption (21).

Implications for Policy and System Design

The findings have important implications for digital health policy and EHR design. Current approaches often frame interruption as an unavoidable consequence of connectivity, or as a problem of individual professionalism (25, 26). Our findings instead align with sociotechnical critiques showing that EHR-related burden is produced through design choices and organisational arrangements, not merely user behaviour, meaning solutions cannot rest solely on doctors managing their attention better (15, 18, 28).

There is a need for organisational strategies that reintroduce meaningful boundaries and shared norms for digital communication, echoing calls for system-level approaches to workflow fragmentation (3, 17). This includes clearer signalling of urgency and responsibility, better support for task resumption, transparent management of multi-user channels, and protected time for cognitively demanding work (18, (20) (21) (22) . Without such changes, responsibility for coping with digital interruption will continue to fall disproportionately on individual doctors.

Conclusion

This study shows that mandated digital systems do not merely add new interruptions to existing clinical workflows (2, 11). They transform interruption itself: from episodic events into ambient conditions of work, from momentary disruptions into sources of systemic cognitive strain (16, 17), and from socially regulated interactions into ambiguous demands stripped of shared cues and norms (19, 21). While doctors develop pragmatic strategies to cope with these conditions, these remain individualised and weakly supported by system design (15, 18). By conceptualising digital interruption as both a cognitive and social phenomenon, this study advances current debates beyond information overload and personal device distraction. It highlights the need to treat interruption as a design, organisational, and policy problem, not simply an individual one, if digitally mediated healthcare is to support, rather than systematically undermine, attention, coordination, and safety.
