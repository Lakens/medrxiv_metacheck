Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Abstract

The apnea-hypopnea index (AHI) summarizes respiratory events by frequency, but was not designed to reflect how strongly an individual's brain responds to them. We developed a deep-learning model that estimates AHI from a single EEG channel (AHI brain ), developed using 22,044 polysomnography recordings and validated in an internal test set (r = 0.88) and three external test sets (r = 0.50-0.62). From AHI brain , we derived two complementary metrics that characterize the EEG-Respiratory Gap: ∆AHI brain , reflecting whether a patient's AHI brain is higher or lower than expected for their measured AHI, and AHI brain,event-free , estimated from epochs without scored respiratory events. Both were associated with sleep macro-, micro-, and epoch-level EEG features, suggesting that this discrepancy is not solely attributable to model error. Among participants free of cardiovascular disease, both metrics were associated with all-cause and non-CVD mortality after adjustment for AHI and clinical risk factors. After accounting for the same covariates, AHI brain,event-free was also associated with CVD mortality in the highest-versus-lowest quartile comparison. These findings support the EEG-Respiratory Gap as an interpretable EEG-derived measure associated with prognostic information beyond AHI.

Introduction

Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is estimated to affect nearly one billion people worldwide 1 and is associated with longterm mortality 2 , yet the optimal metric for assessing its severity remains unclear 3 . The apnea-hypopnea index (AHI) remains the most widely used severity metric and summarizes OSA primarily by the frequency of scored apnea and hypopnea events. However, individuals with similar AHI can differ in the physiological consequences associated with those events [3] [4] [5] . This heterogeneity has motivated complementary measures that characterize dimensions of OSA not represented by event frequency alone. For example, ventilatory and hypoxic burden measures incorporate information from airflow reduction or event-related hypoxemia and have shown asso-ciations with cardiovascular and mortality outcomes beyond those observed with AHI 6 . These measures provide a richer characterization of respiratory and oxygenation burden, but remain primarily derived from respiratory or oximetric signals [7] [8] [9].

Respiratory disturbances may also impose cumulative physiological burden on end organs, particularly the brain. OSA has been associated with accelerated cognitive decline and an increased risk of mild cognitive impairment and dementia [10] [11] [12] . Neurocognitive vulnerability also varies across individuals and is influenced by traits such as the respiratory arousal threshold, which reflects the propensity to arouse in response to a respiratory event, rather than by event frequency alone 13, 14 . Consistent with the potential importance of arousal-related physiology, arousal burden, defined as the cumulative duration of scored arousals normalized to total sleep time, has been associated with long-term all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in MrOS and SHHS, cohorts also analyzed in the present study 15 . Respiratory drive and arousal responses to airway obstruction are generated and modulated by brainstem and cortical circuits 16, 17 . EEG may therefore provide a means to characterize neural responses associated with respiratory stress, complementing conventional metrics derived from respiratory signals.

Several studies have shown that AHI can be estimated from EEG alone. Alongside broader deep-learning approaches to AHI prediction 18, 19 , several studies have used EEG to detect respiratory events or estimate AHI. Kokkalas et al. 20 detected individual apnea events with a convolutional-recurrent network and reported strong agreement between estimated and polysomnography-derived AHI (r ≈ 0.88). Cheng et al. 21 jointly modeled sleep staging and OSA-event detection, while Wan et al. 22 combined a CNN, Transformer, and CRF to link sleep-stage sequences with OSA-severity regression. Barnes et al. 23 used an explainable CNN for single-channel EEG apnea detection and Mahmud et al. 24 detected apnea from sub-frame EEG feature variation. However, in these studies, estimation accuracy was the primary goal, and the discrepancy between the EEG-derived estimate and the measured AHI was treated as residual error rather than as information. The present study takes the comparable internal agreement (r = 0.88) as a starting point rather than an endpoint and asks what information this discrepancy itself may contain.

Treating a predicted-observed discrepancy as an individual vulnerability marker, rather than noise, is an established strategy across modalities and target variables. A sleep-EEGderived brain-age index -predicted minus chronological ageindependently predicted reduced life expectancy in SHHS 25 . A multimodal-PSG extension similarly linked age-estimate error to all-cause and cardiovascular mortality 26 . The same logic extends beyond sleep and age: ECG-based BMI-estimate error independently predicted future cardiometabolic disease 27 , while ECG-based age-estimate error independently predicted mortality 28 . Mortality provides an objective, clinically consequential endpoint with long-term follow-up available in large, well-characterized cohorts, making it a natural first test of the prognostic relevance of such a discrepancy. We apply this logic to OSA, defining the EEG-Respiratory Gap as the discrepancy between EEG-estimated (AHI brain ) and measured (AHI GT ) AHI. This construct is characterized using two complementary metrics: ∆AHI brain , which quantifies the AHI-adjusted discrepancy between AHI brain and AHI GT , and AHI brain,event-free , which captures EEG-derived AHI information remaining in epochs without scored respiratory events.

Recent sleep foundation models have demonstrated that EEG and multimodal PSG contain clinically relevant information beyond conventional sleep summaries [29] [30] [31] [32] [33] . SleepFM, for example, evaluates AHI-based severity classification as a downstream benchmark and separately uses learned representations for disease-risk prediction 29 . However, it does not model AHI as a continuous prediction target or examine whether deviation from conventionally scored AHI is itself informative. Our objective is therefore distinct from broad representation learning or direct risk prediction. By making AHI the explicit prediction target and treating its discrepancy from conventionally scored AHI as the object of study, we ask whether EEG captures a component of respiratory burden not represented by event frequency alone. We test this AHIreferenced construct against established EEG physiology and long-term mortality, providing an interpretable link between conventional OSA severity and latent information encoded in sleep EEG.

This study therefore (1) develops and externally validates a model that estimates AHI from EEG alone, (2) tests whether the resulting EEG-Respiratory Gap reflects sleep-EEG physiology beyond residual model error, and (3) evaluates whether these two complementary metrics are associated with longterm mortality after adjustment for established risk factors.

Dataset and baseline model

This study draws on 22,044 polysomnography (PSG) recordings from a multicohort collection comprising five datasets: the diagnostic-night subset of the Korea Image based Sleep Study dataset (KISS diag ), the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis (MESA), the Osteoporotic Fractures in Men Study (MrOS), the Sleep Heart Health Study (SHHS), and the CPAP titration-night subset of Korea Image based Sleep Study (KISS cpap ) (Fig. 1a). Records with total sleep time (TST) <180 minutes, missing AHI, or missing basic demographic information (age, sex, or BMI) were excluded before partitioning. The Training, Validation, and Internal Test partitions were drawn from pooled KISS diag + MESA + MrOS data and stratified by dataset and AHI severity category (Supplementary Fig. 1a,b). Repeat visits from the same individual (e.g., the same subject serial number recorded at both MrOS1 and MrOS2) were assigned to a single partition, ensuring that no participant contributed data to both model development and test sets.

Model performance

At the epoch level, Phase 1 recovered both respiratory-event and sleep stage information from EEG alone. In Internal Test, macro-F1 was 70.2% for respiratory-event detection and 74.4% for sleep stage classification (Table 2, Supplementary Fig. 3a,b). Across the external validation cohorts, sleep stage classification consistently outperformed the EEG-CLNet baseline, while respiratory-event detection was broadly comparable to the baseline despite some decline from Internal Test. These results indicate that EEG contains substantial information about respiratory-event occurrence that generalizes across independent cohorts. This epoch-level information translated into patient-level estimates of respiratory-event frequency. AHI brain was strongly correlated with AHI GT in Internal Test (r = 0.88, p < 0.001, MAE = 9.26 events/hour; Fig. 1c). Significant correlations were also observed in the two external diagnostic cohorts, SHHS1 (r = 0.60, p < 0.001, MAE = 9.57 events/hour) and SHHS2 (r = 0.62, p < 0.001, MAE = 9.39 events/hour; Fig. 1d,e). SHHS1 and SHHS2 had narrower and lower AHI GT distributions than Internal Test (Supplementary Table 1), consistent with restriction of range. Although correlations were lower in these cohorts, estimation errors remained similar across the three diagnostic cohorts.

KISS cpap was evaluated separately as a CPAP titrationnight cohort rather than as a conventional diagnostic externalvalidation cohort. Agreement was weaker and MAE was higher in this cohort, accompanied by systematic upward bias in AHI brain (Table 2). Together, these results are consistent with prior EEG-based studies showing that respiratory-event frequency is substantially represented in the EEG.

Two EEG-derived metrics of the respiratory gap

A notable observation in KISS cpap suggested that this EEG representation may extend beyond scored events themselves. KISS cpap consists of CPAP titration-night polysomnograms, in which increasing airway pressure suppresses respiratory events. If AHI brain reflected only these events, its estimates would be expected to decrease accordingly. Instead, AHI brain remained correlated with AHI GT (r = 0.50, p < 0.001; Fig. 2b) and showed systematic overestimation. The median difference (AHI brain -AHI GT ) was +11.81 events/hour in KISS cpap , compared with only +0.33 events/hour in Internal Test, and the discrepancy increased with AHI GT (proportionalbias slope +0.496, p < 0.001). Notably, 23.0% of participants with AHI GT < 5 events/hour had AHI brain ≥ 15 events/hour. In contrast, SHHS1 and SHHS2 showed only small biases (-1.64 and -2.40 events/hour; Supplementary Table 2). This discrepancy suggested that EEG may retain respiratory-related information beyond the occurrence of discrete scored events.

Motivated by this observation, we defined two complementary metrics from the trained Phase 2 model to characterize this EEG-respiratory discrepancy (Fig. 2a). ∆AHI brain represents the discrepancy between EEG-derived and conventionally measured AHI. A positive ∆AHI brain indicates that AHI brain is higher than expected for a patient's AHI GT , whereas a negative value indicates that it is lower than expected. Rather than using the simple difference between AHI brain and AHI GT , we quantified this discrepancy as the residual from regressing AHI brain on AHI GT , thereby removing the linear dependence of the discrepancy on measured AHI. By construction, ∆AHI brain is therefore uncorrelated with AHI GT (r ≈ 0.00 in Internal Test; Supplementary Fig. 4a).

Association with sleep macro-and microstructure

Partial correlations (controlling for AHI GT , age, BMI, and sex; Benjamini-Hochberg FDR-adjusted q-values within each cohort × target combination, q < 0.05) between ∆AHI brain /AHI brain,event-free and a panel of sleep macroarchitecture and EEG-microstructure features showed a broadly consistent pattern across Internal, SHHS1, SHHS2, and KISS cpap (Fig. 3). Both metrics were positively associated with lighter, more fragmented sleep macro-architecture (e.g., higher N1%, higher stage-transition and N2-to-N1 transition rates, higher arousal index) and negatively associated with deeper, more consolidated sleep (higher N3%/REM%, higher sleep efficiency, longer TST). Within each cohort, ∆AHI brain and AHI brain,event-free pointed in the same direction for nearly all features, indicating a shared association with sleep fragmentation at the group level despite their nonredundancy at the individual level. Across cohorts, most macro-architecture associations were directionally consistent, although a few features, such as relative theta power in REM, showed opposite directions across cohorts (positive in SHHS1/SHHS2, negative in Internal and KISS cpap ; Fig. 3). Associations with EEG-microstructure features (relative sigma, delta, and theta power; slow-oscillation and spindle density) were generally weaker and less consistently significant across cohorts than the macro-architecture associations described above.

These analyses were descriptive and were used to visualize the organization of the Phase 1 representations.

Bridging epoch-level features to patient-level metrics

The preceding analysis (Fig. 4a) showed that EEG feature values varied systematically with the model's predicted event probability at the epoch level. However, a pattern observed across pooled epochs does not necessarily explain inter-individual differences in patient-level ∆AHI brain and AHI brain,event-free . We therefore asked whether the same EEG features could bridge these two levels of analysis. We defined each patient's mean feature value across true-negative (TN) epochs as their baseline and performed two complementary analyses using the selected eight-feature panel (precise definitions in Methods).

Connection 1 tested whether inter-individual differences in these TN baseline values were associated with AHI brain,event-free after adjustment for AHI GT (Fig. 4c). Several features, including REM relative alpha and delta power and N1 relative delta and fast-delta power, showed directionally consistent and statistically significant associations across all four cohorts. Other features, including N2 theta/alpha ratio, N2 slow-oscillation amplitude, and N3 relative slow-delta power, showed weaker or cohort-inconsistent associations.

Overall, Connection 1 showed that patient-specific baseline EEG characteristics during TN epochs were systematically related to AHI brain,event-free independently of AHI GT.

Connection 2 tested whether patient-specific EEG reactivity was associated with ∆AHI brain . Using each patient's TN mean as the baseline, we quantified how strongly feature values deviated from this baseline as predicted event probability increased. These associations were weaker and less consistent, with no feature showing consistent replication across the three diagnostic cohorts (Supplementary Fig. 14).

Discussion

This study asked what information the AHI may miss. AHI quantifies respiratory events but treats events as equivalent regardless of their physiological consequence for an individual. We trained a deep-learning model to estimate AHI from a single EEG channel and validated it in an internal test set and three external test datasets. We then examined whether the discrepancy between EEG-derived and conventionally scored AHI carries meaningful information beyond residual model error. We characterized this EEG-Respiratory Gap using two complementary metrics. ∆AHI brain is the residual of the full-recording model output after accounting for AHI GT and is therefore linearly independent of AHI GT by construction. In contrast, AHI brain,event-free is derived from epochs without scored respiratory events and remains correlated with AHI GT . Both metrics were associated with epoch-level EEG features and sleep architecture in physiologically consistent directions. Among participants free of cardiovascular disease at baseline, the two metrics showed broadly similar associations with all-cause and non-CVD mortality; AHI brain,event-free was addi-tionally associated with CVD mortality in the highest-versuslowest quartile comparison when adjusted for conventionally scored AHI and standard clinical covariates. A natural concern is that the EEG-Respiratory Gap may reflect imperfect AHI estimation rather than meaningful interindividual variation. ∆AHI brain was designed to quantify this discrepancy while removing its linear dependence on conventionally scored AHI. Specifically, it represents how much a patient's AHI brain is higher or lower than expected for their AHI GT , rather than the simple prediction error between the two values. Whether this residual variation is meaningful must therefore be established through its associations with independent physiological and clinical outcomes.

AHI brain,event-free provides a complementary perspective by estimating AHI brain using only epochs classified as non-event. This metric should not be interpreted as completely independent of respiratory events. Brief event overlap can remain because of the epoch-level labeling criterion, and physiologically meaningful responses may extend beyond the eventpositive epoch itself. However, such overlap was limited, and AHI-related patterns persisted when progressively wider regions surrounding respiratory events were excluded (Supplementary Fig. 5a,c-e). Arousal activity also remained elevated immediately after respiratory events (Supplementary 9/18 .

Additional controls, including models trained using event-free epochs alone, further showed that AHI-related information could be learned from the retained EEG itself and was not explained by input-length differences alone (Supplementary Fig. 6). The physiological and prognostic associations of these metrics provide further evidence that the EEG-Respiratory Gap is not simply uninformative estimation noise. Both metrics were associated with sleep macrostructure and, less consistently, EEG microstructure after adjustment for AHI GT . Epoch-level analyses further showed systematic EEG feature changes with predicted respiratory-event probability, including in nonevent epochs. Finally, both ∆AHI brain and AHI brain,event-free remained associated with all-cause and non-CVD mortality after adjustment for AHI GT and standard clinical covariates. Together, these findings support the interpretation of the two metrics as complementary measures of EEG-Respiratory discrepancy rather than simple measures of AHI prediction error.

The clearest epoch-level substrate for AHI brain,event-free came from a patient's own baseline EEG state across sleep stages. The direction of these associations formed a coherent rather than a scattered pattern. Higher AHI brain,event-free was consistently linked to lower delta-band power at a patient's own baseline during epochs without scored respiratory events: lower relative delta and fast-delta power in N1, lower relative slow-delta power in N3, and lower relative delta power in REM. At the same time, higher AHI brain,event-free was associ- . ated with greater faster-rhythm activity, specifically higher relative theta power in N3 and higher relative alpha power in REM.

Together, these findings describe a baseline EEG shifted away from slow, high-amplitude activity and toward faster rhythms across multiple stages. This pattern was most marked in REM, where elevated alpha content is itself atypical. REM sleep is normally characterized by low-voltage, desynchronized cortical activity. Alpha intrusion into REM, together with reduced REM delta, is therefore more consistent with a persistently lighter, more wake-adjacent REM state than with ordinary REM physiology. The prominence of this pattern in REM may also be relevant to cardiovascular physiology. Independent of overall AHI, REM sleep carries the greatest autonomic and hemodynamic instability of the night, and respiratory events concentrated in REM are an established predictor of hypertension and impaired nocturnal blood-pressure dipping 35, 36 . A baseline tendency toward a lighter, more arousable REM state, of the kind captured here, may therefore reflect autonomic vulnerability relevant to cardiovascular risk. Consistent with this possibility, AHI brain,event-free was the only one of the two gap metrics to show an association with CVD mortality, although this was limited to the highestversus-lowest quartile comparison and was not significant in the continuous analysis. Severity metrics beyond simple event counting, such as hypoxic-burden-based measures, have likewise been associated with cardiovascular mortality 7, 8 . This interpretation is also compatible with the observation that AHI brain,event-free remained elevated in KISS cpap even though scored respiratory events were actively suppressed by titration. Patients undergo CPAP titration because they already carry a clinical diagnosis of significant OSA. A single night of pressure normalization can suppress discrete, fully scored events without necessarily eliminating more subtle flow limitation or reversing EEG changes that may have accumulated over a longer period of untreated disease. EEG alterations of this kind could therefore persist during a titration night even as the scored event burden is substantially reduced.

In contrast, ∆AHI brain was associated with all-cause and non-CVD mortality but not CVD mortality. Non-CVD mortality encompasses multiple causes, including cancer, respiratory disease, and neurodegenerative conditions, making this association difficult to attribute to a specific physiological pathway. To explore a potential physiological correlate of ∆AHI brain , we examined whether it might relate to interindividual differences in EEG reactivity to model-predicted respiratory events, including arousal-related responses. However, the corresponding feature-reactivity analysis showed limited consistency across cohorts (Supplementary Fig. 14), and respiratory arousal threshold was not measured directly in this study. A reactivity-based interpretation of ∆AHI brain therefore remains a hypothesis requiring further investigation.

Our metrics are conceptually related to, but distinct from, other EEG/PSG-derived measures relevant to OSA. Arousal burden, defined as the cumulative duration of scored arousals normalized to total sleep time, has been associated with long-term all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in MrOS and SHHS 15 , both of which were included in the present study. However, a separate analysis found no significant associations of arousal burden with cardiovascular outcomes or allcause mortality in MESA, while in MrOS an association was observed for cardiovascular mortality but not for all-cause mortality or incident CVD 37 . Overall, these findings suggest that the prognostic associations of arousal burden may vary across cohorts and outcomes. In addition, arousal burden is defined directly from scored arousal events and does not distinguish respiratory from non-respiratory arousals. In contrast, ∆AHI brain quantifies the discrepancy between EEG-estimated and measured AHI after removing its systematic dependence on AHI GT , whereas AHI brain,event-free is estimated specifically from epochs without scored respiratory events. Continuous EEG-derived measures such as the odds ratio product 38 similarly characterize aspects of cortical state and sleep depth, but were not designed to estimate AHI or its discrepancy from conventionally scored values. The extent to which ∆AHI brain and AHI brain,event-free overlap with arousal burden, the odds ratio product, or other established EEG-derived measures remains to be determined.

More broadly, the sleep-microstructure analyses showed that spindle and slow-oscillation density were associated with both discrepancy metrics, alongside the macro-architectural findings described above. These features have been implicated in restorative functions of NREM sleep, including memory consolidation 39 , synaptic homeostasis 40 , and processes related to glymphatic clearance 41 . Their associations with the discrepancy metrics suggest that the physiological information reflected by the EEG-Respiratory Gap may extend beyond cardiovascular processes and may also be relevant to broader health outcomes. Recent work in the same MrOS and SHHS cohorts similarly showed that deep-learning-derived EEG power-band features were independently associated with all-cause mortality 42 , providing complementary evidence for the broader prognostic relevance of EEG-derived measures.

Taken together, the associations with sleep physiology and long-term outcomes suggest that the EEG-Respiratory Gap may provide information complementary to conventionally scored AHI. Both metrics remained associated with all-cause and non-CVD mortality after adjustment for AHI GT and standard clinical risk factors. From a practical standpoint, they are derived from EEG already recorded during standard overnight polysomnography, and therefore require no additional sensors or patient burden. Their clinical utility, however, will require prospective validation and evaluation of whether they provide actionable information beyond established risk factors.

Several limitations should be considered. First, this observational study establishes association rather than causation, and the physiological pathways linking the EEG-Respiratory Gap to mortality were not directly tested. Key measures relevant to our physiological interpretations, including arousal threshold, blood pressure, and heart-rate variability, were not directly assessed. Spindle and slow-oscillation features were also not 11/18 . evaluated in relation to mortality, and outcome specificity was not formally tested using interaction analyses. Second, some EEG associations varied across cohorts. For example, relative theta power in REM showed opposite associations with the discrepancy metrics in SHHS compared with our internal and KISS cpap cohorts. Such heterogeneity may reflect differences in study populations, recording devices, or scoring criteria and warrants caution when interpreting individual EEG features across cohorts. Third, the survival analyses were limited to SHHS, an older community-dwelling cohort without CVD at baseline, which may limit generalizability to other populations. Non-CVD mortality was a heterogeneous residual category comprising deaths not classified as CVD mortality and was not further decomposed by underlying cause; the specific conditions driving this association therefore remain to be identified. KISS cpap could not be included in the survival analyses because of its titration-night design. Finally, computing the metrics currently requires a full overnight EEG recording and inference through a trained model. Clinical implementation will therefore require prospective validation and workflow integration. Cognitive and neurodegenerative outcomes were beyond the scope of this study and are being investigated separately.

In summary, ∆AHI brain and AHI brain,event-free provide complementary measures of the discrepancy between EEGderived and conventionally measured AHI. Their associations with sleep architecture, EEG characteristics, and long-term mortality suggest that this discrepancy contains structured information beyond simple AHI prediction error. These findings support the EEG-Respiratory Gap as an interpretable complement to conventional AHI and suggest that EEG already collected during polysomnography may contain clinically relevant information not captured by standard respiratory-event frequency alone.

Methods

Data sources and ethics

Five polysomnography (PSG) datasets were used. KISS is an internal, multi-cohort dataset collected across four institutions, comprising two subsets: a diagnostic-night PSG cohort (KISS diag ) and a CPAP titration-night PSG cohort (KISS cpap ). The remaining three datasets -MESA 43, 44 , MrOS 43, 45 (visits 1 and 2), and SHHS 43, 46 (visits 1 and 2) -are open datasets available through the National Sleep Research Resource (NSRR). Detailed dataset descriptions are provided in Supplementary Note 1.

KISS cpap consists of single-night titration studies in which airway pressure is adjusted in real time to suppress respiratory events. Its within-night AHI GT distribution is therefore markedly compressed relative to the diagnostic-night cohorts (Supplementary Table 1), and KISS cpap was treated separately when interpreting model performance and downstream analyses.

This study -including analysis of KISS together with the publicly available MESA, MrOS, and SHHS datasets -was approved by the responsible Institutional Review Board (IRB approval number: E-2510-019-1681), which covers large-scale secondary analysis across these datasets rather than the original collection of KISS itself. The original collection of KISS was separately approved by the institutional review boards of Seoul National University Hospital (C-2007-179-1143) and Chuncheon Sacred Heart Hospital, Hallym University College of Medicine (2021-03-005), with informed consent waived because the data were de-identified 47 . Use of MESA, MrOS, and SHHS data was additionally conducted under the data use agreements of the respective parent studies, each of which obtained institutional review board approval and participant informed consent at the time of original data collection 43 . All data were de-identified prior to analysis.

Per-cohort baseline characteristics are reported in Supplementary Table 1.

Data preprocessing

A single central EEG derivation (C4-A1), among the channels commonly used in sleep research 48 , was used as model input for every cohort. Raw single-channel EEG was preprocessed identically across datasets. A 60 Hz notch filter was applied to remove line noise, followed by a 0.5-35 Hz band-pass filter (8th-order Butterworth, zero-phase), and the signal was resampled to 128 Hz. The filtered signal was decomposed into five sub-bands (0.5-4, 4-8, 8-12, 12-15, and 15-35 Hz), stacked, and z-score normalized per recording before being used as model input. Recordings were trimmed at the sleepperiod boundary by removing wake periods exceeding 30 minutes immediately before sleep onset or after sleep offset, retaining the sleep period plus up to 30 minutes of adjacent wake. Signals were then segmented into consecutive, nonoverlapping 30-second epochs for sleep-stage and respiratoryevent labeling and prediction.

Sleep-stage scoring conventions differed across datasets. KISS was scored using AASM criteria, whereas MESA, MrOS, and SHHS were originally scored using Rechtschaffen & Kales (R&K) criteria. To harmonize sleep-stage labels, R&K stages 3 and 4 were merged into a single N3 stage, matching the five-class scheme (Wake, N1, N2, N3, REM) used throughout this study. Sleep-stage ground truth was otherwise taken directly from each cohort's native per-epoch scoring.

Ground-truth AHI (AHI GT ) reflects the AASM 2012 alternative hypopnea rule, requiring either ≥3% oxygen desaturation or an arousal, in each dataset's official derived-variables file. However, individual hypopnea annotations in SHHS, MrOS, and MESA were originally scored by flow reduction alone without this requirement. Event-level hypopnea annotations in these cohorts were therefore re-scored according to AASM 2012 criteria using a standardized annotationrefinement pipeline 49 ; KISS already followed AASM criteria . at the event level.

An epoch was labeled as an event epoch if apnea or hypopnea occupied at least 10 seconds of the 30second epoch; otherwise, it was labeled as a non-event epoch. This criterion was applied identically across all five datasets.

Model architecture

brAHInet (Fig. 1b) comprises two stages. Phase 1 is a multitask deep neural network operating on each 30-second central (C4-A1) EEG epoch. The five-band-filtered signal forms an input tensor X ∈ R 5×3840 (5 frequency bands × 128 Hz × 30 s). X is encoded by a ModernTCN backbone 34 , a pureconvolutional time-series architecture that alternates depthwise temporal convolutions with pointwise cross-channel convolutions across successive downsampling stages, yielding a feature map F ∈ R M×D×N (M=5 frequency bands, D feature dimension, N temporal patches). A self-attention block applies multi-head self-attention 50, 51 over F (reshaped to N × MD) with a residual connection, giving updated features Z. A sleep-staging head maps Z to per-epoch stage logits s ∈ R 5 (Wake/N1/N2/N3/REM). An additive-attention module then combines Z with softmax(s) as context, allowing the respiratory-event head to incorporate the model's sleepstage prediction. The event head outputs binary event logits e ∈ R² , corresponding to event and non-event classes, together with an intermediate event representation φ ∈ R 30×80 for each epoch. Here, the 30 positions correspond to one-second temporal representations within the 30-second epoch, and 80 is the hidden feature dimension of the event head. Phase 1 is trained with a combined loss L = L stage + L event , where L stage is cross-entropy on the five-class stage label and L event is classweighted cross-entropy on the binary event label. A detailed layer-by-layer diagram, including tensor shapes, is provided in Supplementary Fig. 2.

Phase 2 predicts patient-level AHI brain from the temporally ordered sequence of Phase 1 event representations. For a patient with T epochs, the representations are assembled as X p = [φ 1 , . . . , φ T ], where each φ t ∈ R 30×80 . Each epoch representation is flattened to a 2,400-dimensional vector and linearly projected to a 256-dimensional embedding. Sinusoidal positional encoding is added, and a learnable [CLS] token is prepended before the sequence is passed through a three-layer Transformer encoder 50 with four attention heads and dropout of 0.1. The encoded [CLS] representation is layer-normalized and passed through a linear regression head to obtain the patient-level prediction. Phase 2 is trained to predict log(1 + AHI GT ) using a weighted mean absolute error loss that assigns greater weight to the lower and higher ends of the AHI distribution, with the model output transformed back to the AHI scale for evaluation.

Phase 1 was optimized using Adam with a learning rate of 1 × 10 -3 and a batch size of 512. Training was performed for up to 30 epochs with early stopping (patience 7) based on validation performance across the respiratory-event and sleep-stage tasks. Phase 2 was optimized using AdamW with a learning rate of approximately 2 × 10 -5 , weight decay of 0.01, and a batch size of 1. Training was performed for up to 50 epochs with early stopping (patience 10), with gradient clipping at a maximum norm of 4.0. The checkpoint with the best validation performance was retained for subsequent analyses.

Phase 1 and Phase 2 models were implemented in Python 3.11.13 using PyTorch 2. For comparison, EEG-CLNet 21 , a baseline convolutional model that performs sleep staging and respiratory-event detection via a shared convolutional feature extractor with crosstask knowledge distillation between the two tasks, was trained and evaluated on the identical data splits as a comparator for both epoch-level tasks (Table 2). EEG-CLNet does not include a patient-level AHI-regression component.

Bias correction and null-benchmark control for ∆AHI brain and AHI brain,event-free ∆AHI brain is the residual of an OLS regression of AHI brain on AHI GT . For Internal Test, the regression's slope and intercept were fit on the held-out Validation partition and applied to Internal Test, so that ∆AHI brain 's orthogonality to AHI GT there reflects an out-of-sample correction rather than a trivial consequence of fitting and evaluating on the same data, consistent with recommended procedures for correcting regression-based prediction bias in similar predicted-observed discrepancy frameworks 52, 53 . For each External Test cohort (SHHS1, SHHS2, KISS cpap ), no comparable held-out validation subset exists, so the regression was instead fit and applied within that same cohort.

To distinguish the effect of selectively removing respiratoryevent epochs from that of simply reducing the amount of EEG input, we constructed a length-matched null control. For each patient, we first calculated the proportion of epochs retained in the event-free analysis. We then selected the closest matching condition from the input-length reduction analysis (Supplementary Fig. 6a), in which a similar proportion of the recording was retained without selectively removing respiratoryevent epochs. The resulting control prediction was residualized against AHI GT in the same manner as AHI brain,event-free . We compared the AHI GT -adjusted associations of ∆AHI brain with AHI brain,event-free and with this length-matched control using five-fold cross-validated ridge-regression R² . Differences in R² and 95% confidence intervals were estimated using 1,000 bootstrap samples (seed= 0).

EEG feature extraction

Candidate EEG microstructure features were extracted using the YASA toolbox (v0.6.5) 54 , applied to the same C4- 13/18 A1 derivation used by the Phase 1 model.

Relative spectral power (delta, slow-delta, fast-delta, theta, alpha, sigma, beta bands) and the theta/alpha ratio were computed directly per 30-second epoch (N1, N2, N3, REM). Sleep spindles and slow oscillations were instead detected on the continuous wholenight signal (YASA's default detectors), and each detected event was assigned to the 30-second epoch containing its onset, from which epoch-and stage-level (N2, N3) density and amplitude were derived.

Epoch-level feature analysis

Epoch classification, feature panel construction, person-meancentering, dose-response binning, monotonicity-based feature selection, and the Connection 1/2 and FDR procedures were performed as described below. Extended cohort-specific analyses and complete candidate-feature results are provided in Supplementary Note 2.

Dose-response binning.

For each of the four sleep stages, model-predicted event probability was divided into ten bins; the two most extreme bins (nominal centers 0.05 and 0.95) were excluded to avoid instability from sparsely populated tails, leaving eight analysis bins. For each patient and sleep stage, the mean feature value across true-negative (TN) epochs was defined as the patient-specific baseline (TN anchor). Each epoch's feature value was expressed as a deviation from this baseline 55 , thereby emphasizing within-patient changes rather than differences in overall EEG level between patients. Within each probability bin, these deviations were standardized to obtain the effect sizes shown in Fig. 4a. Within each stage, the two features with the highest monotonicity (rank correlation between probability bin and centered effect size), among those showing a consistent direction across cohorts, were retained for downstream analyses: N1 -relative delta power and relative fast-delta power; N2 -theta/alpha ratio and slowoscillation amplitude; N3 -relative slow-delta power and relative theta power; REM -relative alpha power and relative delta power. Connection 2 (TP+FP deviation slope vs. ∆AHI brain ). Connection 2 tested whether patient-specific EEG feature reactivity was associated with ∆AHI brain . For each patient, epochs with model-predicted event probability > 0.5 (TP+FP) were used to quantify feature deviations from the same TN baseline. Patient-specific reactivity was estimated as the Theil-Sen slope 56 of feature deviation against predicted event probability, restricted to patients with at least 10 such epochs and at least two distinct probability values. The resulting slopes were correlated with ∆AHI brain separately for each feature and cohort.

Epoch embedding analysis.

For each patient with T epochs, low-dimensional UMAP embeddings were computed from the Phase 1 event-detection representations of shape (T, 30, 80), corresponding to 30 one-second sub-epoch positions × 80-dimensional feature vectors. Separate embeddings were constructed using all epochs and event-free epochs to examine whether the learned representations contained structure related to AHI GT , ∆AHI brain , and AHI brain,event-free.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed in Python 3.11.13 (pandas 2.3.1, numpy 1.26.4, scipy 1.16.0, statsmodels 0.14.5, pingouin 0.6.1, lifelines 0.30.3, matplotlib 3.10.0). Unless otherwise noted, correlation and regression statistics used two-tailed tests at α = 0.05 (or BH-FDR-corrected q < 0.05, where stated). Confidence intervals for correlation and R² comparisons were computed by patient-level bootstrap resampling with fixed random seeds.

Multiple-comparison correction. BH-FDR correction was applied in exactly two places: the Fig. 3 partial-correlation forest plot (corrected within each of 4 cohorts × 2 targets, q < 0.05) and the Connection 1/Connection 2 analysis (corrected jointly across all 64 comparisons, q < 0.05). All other statistical comparisons in this manuscript are either single comparisons, small pre-specified nested-model comparisons interpreted via confidence-interval overlap, or explicitly uncorrected exploratory comparisons.

Survival analysis

Survival analyses were performed in a baseline CVD-free dataset drawn from SHHS. Participants with prevalent myocardial infarction, stroke, congestive heart failure, or angina were excluded to evaluate prognostic associations in individuals without pre-existing cardiovascular disease. Participants with non-positive follow-up time were also excluded, yielding a final analytic cohort of 4,694 participants. Detailed cohort construction is provided in the Supplementary Information.

We evaluated three outcomes separately: all-cause mortality, CVD mortality, and non-CVD mortality. CVD mortality was defined using the cardiovascular-or coronary-heartdisease death classification provided by SHHS, and non-CVD mortality as all-cause death not classified as CVD/CHD death. For each outcome and each predictor (∆AHI brain , AHI brain,event-free ), Cox proportional-hazards models were fit with four levels of progressive covariate adjustment and with the predictor parameterized separately as quartiles and as a continuous per-SD term.

To compare their mortality associations with that of conventional AHI, crude and fully adjusted models were also fit separately for AHI GT , ∆AHI brain , and AHI brain,event-free using the same clinical covariates. Discrimination was assessed using Harrell's C-index, and the proportional-hazards assumption was evaluated using Schoenfeld residuals. Additional cohort-construction details and complete model-specific results are provided in Supplementary Note 3.
