Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

BACKGROUND: Active case finding (ACF) in India screens large populations to find few cases. AI-NETRA predicts neighbourhood-level tuberculosis (TB) risk from routine notification data and generates field microplans. OBJECTIVE: To compare the yield and efficiency of AI-guided and standard ACF, and frontline acceptability. DESIGN: Prospective quasi-experimental evaluation in four TB Units of Pune district, comparing twoweek AI-guided sweeps (April to May 2026) with the routine campaign in the same units (December 2024 to January 2025). Primary endpoint: bacteriologically confirmed pulmonary TB per persons screened; secondary: all-forms yield, presumptive yield, referral precision, number needed to screen (NNS) and cost per case. Acceptability: 16 focus groups (n=100) and a survey of 101 ASHAs.

RESULTS: AI-guided teams screened 66,095 people and confirmed 10 pulmonary cases, against 9 among 177,322 in the standard campaign (rate ratio 2.98, 95% CI 1.09 to 8.29); NNS was 6,610 versus 19,702 and cost per case 66% lower. All-forms detection did not differ (15 versus 32; RR 1.26, 95% CI 0.63 to 2.39); the standard campaign had mobile X-ray. 96% of ASHAs reported finding the same presumptive cases while screening fewer people.

CONCLUSION: Geospatial targeting raised confirmed yield per person and lowered NNS; total detection did not differ. A concurrent, like-for-like trial is warranted.

Introduction

India carries about a quarter of the global tuberculosis (TB) burden, and the National TB Elimination Programme (NTEP) has made active case finding (ACF) a central instrument for reaching people who do not present to care, most recently through the 100-day intensified campaign launched in December 2024. [1- 3] ACF is effective but inefficient when screening is spread evenly across a population: national analyses report a median number needed to screen (NNS) in the thousands, with yield below programme targets in most states. [4, 5] In Pune district, a routine campaign screens about half a million people to detect roughly 100 cases, an NNS near 5,000. [6] TB is not evenly distributed. It clusters in identifiable neighbourhoods, and WHO screening guidance asks programmes to prioritise where transmission concentrates. [7] [8] [9] The geospatial tools now used in Indian programmes resolve risk to the block or ward, which helps allocate effort across a district but offers no guidance inside a dense ward, where a whole-ward campaign is rarely feasible. [10] AI-NETRA (Froncort.AI, Pune) addresses the gap below the ward: it scores TB risk for each cell of the H3 hexagonal grid from routine Nikshay notification data and assembles the highest-risk cells into a microplan that a frontline worker can follow. [11] In retrospective validation against three Pune campaigns, its prioritised cells captured 89% of campaign-detected cases from under one-third of the screened population, and its taluka-level rank correlation with observed cases rose from 0.55 to 0.78 across successive campaigns. [6] Whether that translates into yield in the field had not been tested.

We report a prospective quasi-experimental evaluation comparing AI-guided ACF with the standard campaign in four TB Units of Pune district, with a pre-specified bacteriological primary endpoint, and a mixed-methods assessment of acceptability and usability among the workers who executed the plans. The study corresponds to an early-stage live evaluation of an AI decision support system and is reported against TREND, with COREQ for the qualitative component. [12] [13] [14]

Methods

Design and setting

This was a prospective, quasi-experimental, controlled before-after comparison of two ACF planning strategies in the same geographies: AI-guided micro-targeting versus the standard blanket campaign. It contrasts planning strategies, not exposures, and is reported as a non-randomised evaluation. Four TB Units (TUs) of Pune district, Maharashtra, were selected to span the rural and urban ACF context and the range of predicted yield: Bhor and Shirur (rural) and Haveli PMC and Haveli PCMC (urban, within the Pune and Pimpri-Chinchwad municipal corporations).

The planning tool

AI-NETRA represents the district as H3 hexagons and scores each hexagon's TB risk from the Nikshay notification register. The version 1 model was trained on bacteriologically confirmed notifications from 2022 to 2025 geocoded to residential address; about 92% of records were geocoded, 16% to building precision and 72% to area precision, and all were retained. Features computed for each hexagon and month combine recent case history with temporal decay, case characteristics weighted towards pulmonary disease, spatial spillover from adjacent hexagons, cold-start inference from structural proxies where notification history is sparse, and programmatic vulnerability indicators; a two-stage gradient-boosted hurdle model, a calibrated probability that the hexagon will notify any case in the following month multiplied by a Poisson expected count, ranks hexagons for the month ahead. Hexagons in the top 10% of scores are classed high priority and those between the 10th and 30th percentiles medium priority. Navigation hotspots are listed at H3 resolution 10 (about 123 x 123 m equivalent) and the screening population, the NNS denominator, is computed at resolution 9 (about 325 x 325 m) for operational feasibility. Each microplan lists hotspots by landmark, address and map link, with the residential clusters they contain, cases notified in the previous six months, a predicted case range and the number of people to screen (Supplementary Appendix S1). A single locked model version generated the plans before the sweep. An open-source implementation of the model is available under the Apache-2.0 licence (Data and code availability). The tool decides where teams go; it does not change who is screened within a household or how presumptive TB is identified, sampled, tested or treated, which followed the NTEP algorithm.

AI-guided arm

In each unit, ASHAs and TB staff trained before deployment conducted a two-week sweep of the microplan areas between April and May 2026 (rural units in April, urban units in May). All high-priority hexagons were covered comprehensively; about one-third of medium-priority hexagons were covered, selected by rank with geographic stratification; and non-hotspot areas adjacent to the plan served as an opportunistic specificity check. Teams applied the NTEP symptom screen house to house, and presumptive TB was referred for sputum collection and nucleic acid amplification testing (NAAT; Truenat or CBNAAT). The district's mobile chest X-ray van was not deployed with the sweep; chest radiography was available only on referral to a health facility.

Standard arm

The comparator was the routine district ACF campaign conducted in the same four units from December 2024 to January 2025 (14 days): house-to-house symptom screening of the whole target population, sputum microscopy for presumptive TB with NAAT for a subset, and mobile chest X-ray available. Unit-level counts of persons screened, presumptive TB identified and tested, and cases diagnosed by category were taken from the campaign's TB-4 reporting (Supplementary Table S2).

Outcomes

The pre-specified primary endpoint was bacteriologically confirmed pulmonary TB per persons screened, analysed on the as-screened population. In the AI arm this was NAAT-positive pulmonary TB, the NTEP standard definition, and every bacteriologically confirmed pulmonary case in that arm was NAAT-positive; in the standard arm it was the TB-4 reporting category of sputum smear-or NAAT-positive pulmonary TB, which also admits smear-only diagnoses and is therefore, if anything, the broader of the two. Secondary endpoints were all-forms TB (bacteriologically confirmed or clinically diagnosed, pulmonary or extrapulmonary), which served as the total-case-detection measure and as a conservative sensitivity analysis favouring the standard arm; presumptive yield; referral precision (confirmed pulmonary cases per presumptive tested); NNS; and cost per confirmed case, applying the programme's ACF unit cost per person screened to each arm's screening effort and yield (Supplementary Appendix S1). Cases in the AI arm were attributed to the TB Unit in which they were identified during the sweep.

Sample size and statistical analysis

The field window fixed the achievable number of confirmed cases, so the study was powered for estimation rather than hypothesis testing: at 80% power, detecting a rate ratio of 1.25 would need about 640 confirmed cases, 1.5 about 200, 2.0 about 75 and 3.0 about 35, and an exact 95% interval around 10 events spans 4.8 to 18.4. Yields are reported per 10,000 screened with exact (Garwood) Poisson intervals, and NNS as persons screened per case with intervals obtained by inverting the Poisson interval. [15] Rate ratios comparing arms used the exact conditional (binomial) form of the Poisson test with exact intervals; presumptive yield used Fisher's exact test and log-binomial regression; rural and urban contrasts were pre-specified subgroups. Pre-specified sensitivity analyses were leave-one-out omission of each confirmed case and reconstruction of the urban denominators, which were population-covered counts rather than household-enumerated screens. Analyses used Python 3 (SciPy 1.11).

Acceptability and usability

Three instruments were used. Sixteen focus group discussions (FGDs) with 100 frontline workers were held across the four units between 17 April and 11 May 2026, facilitated in Marathi by implementationpartner staff around a hands-on demonstration of a microplan. A moderator and a note-taker recorded responses on a structured template covering current ACF practice, the AI plan, eight mobile-usability statements rated from 1 to 5, field context, workflow and supervision, trust, a 17-item feature-priority vote and overall acceptability, with the group as the unit of analysis; reporting follows COREQ (Supplementary Appendix S4). A time-and-motion study timed 57 workers through the five steps of opening a plan and starting navigation to a hotspot. A structured post-campaign survey of 101 ASHAs, administered in Marathi as an online form between 3 and 30 June 2026 after field use, is treated as the primary in-practice acceptability signal. Quantitative and qualitative strands were integrated in a joint display following GRAMMS. [16] A pre-deployment survey of programme leadership is reported separately. [6]

Ethics and registration

The prospective phase was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Jehangir Clinical Development Centre, Pune (JCDC/BHR/26/005, 13 March 2026). Routine programme data were used under NTEP datagovernance arrangements, and FGD and survey participants gave informed consent. The protocol and statistical analysis plan, including the primary endpoint, were fixed before the sweep. The study evaluated a planning method within routine programme operations and did not alter individual clinical management; it was not registered as a clinical trial.

Results

Screening cascade

Across the four units, AI-guided teams visited 17,817 households and screened 66,095 people, identifying 326 presumptive TB cases (0.49%; 1.83 per 100 households), of whom 325 (99.7%) were tested (Table 1). Fifteen TB cases of all forms were diagnosed (4.6% of those tested): 11 pulmonary, of which 10 were NAAT-positive (7 by Truenat, 3 by CBNAAT) and 1 was diagnosed on chest radiography, and 4 extrapulmonary (3 pleural, diagnosed radiologically, and 1 abdominal, on cytopathology). The 10 NAATpositive pulmonary cases met the primary definition: Shirur 1, Bhor 0, Haveli PMC 7 and Haveli PCMC 2. Cases were aged 14 to 70 years (median 34), 9 were male, 10 were notified as residents of urban slums, and all 15 started treatment within three days of diagnosis (Supplementary Table S3b). The AI arm screened 37% as many people as the standard campaign had in the same units (177,322), and each sweep was completed within two weeks.

Primary endpoint

The pooled yield of bacteriologically confirmed pulmonary TB was 1.51 per 10,000 screened (95% CI 0.73 to 2.78), an NNS of 6,610 (95% CI 3,594 to 13,783); unit-level yields were imprecise, as expected at these counts (Table 1). Against the standard campaign (9 cases among 177,322 screened; 0.51 per 10,000), the rate ratio was 2.98 (95% CI 1.09 to 8.29; exact p=0.019), and the NNS was 6,610 versus 19,702, 66% lower (Table 2; Figure 1). The advantage was carried by the urban units (9 of 59,287 versus 7 of 114,816; RR 2.49, 95% CI 0.83 to 7.87), was large but imprecise in Shirur (1 versus 1; RR 11.5) and absent in Bhor (0 versus 1). Omitting any single AI-arm case reduced the rate ratio to 2.68 (95% CI 0.94 to 7.63; p=0.058).

Total case detection

Under the all-forms definition the AI arm diagnosed 15 cases and the standard campaign 32 (RR 1.26, 95% CI 0.63 to 2.39; p=0.51): Shirur 2 versus 4, Bhor 0 versus 2 and Haveli 13 versus 26. Of the standard campaign's 32 cases, 9 were bacteriologically confirmed pulmonary TB, 5 were pulmonary TB diagnosed on chest X-ray or other clinical grounds and 18 were extrapulmonary. That campaign performed 444 chest X-rays among its 746 presumptive cases and confirmed mainly by sputum microscopy (NAAT in 95), whereas the AI sweep had no mobile X-ray, its protocol specified NAAT for every presumptive, and 4 of its 15 cases were diagnosed radiologically after referral to a facility (Supplementary Table S2).

Presumptive yield and referral precision

The presumptive rate was 0.49% in the AI arm versus 0.42% in the standard campaign (RR 1.17, 95% CI 1.03 to 1.34; p=0.018). The difference was confined to the rural units (0.79% versus 0.36%; RR 2.22, 95% CI 1.62 to 3.00; p<0.001) and absent in the urban units (0.46% versus 0.46%; RR 1.01, 95% CI 0.87 to 1.17; p=0.94). Where the urban advantage appeared was in referral precision: 3.3% of tested presumptives in the urban AI arm (9 of 271) were bacteriologically confirmed pulmonary TB, against 1.3% in the urban standard campaign (7 of 523), a 2.5-fold difference (95% CI 0.93 to 6.59; Fisher p=0.067); pooled across the four units the proportions were 3.1% and 1.2% (10 of 325 versus 9 of 746; 2.6-fold, 95% CI 1.05 to 6.22; p=0.043). The all-forms proportions were similar (urban 4.8% versus 5.0%; pooled 4.6% versus 4.3%).

Efficiency and cost

The AI arm screened 6,610 people per confirmed pulmonary case against 19,702 in the standard campaign (66% fewer). At the programme's ACF unit cost of about Rs 140 per person screened, cost per confirmed case fell from Rs 27.6 lakh to Rs 9.3 lakh (66% lower).

Acceptability and usability

The 100 FGD participants were 86 ASHAs with supervisory staff, multipurpose workers and TB health visitors; 32 had more than 10 years of field experience, all owned smartphones, 87% used Google Maps and all preferred Marathi-language outputs. In 12 of the 16 groups participants said they would use the plan in their next campaign (two further groups were split about 70:30 in favour, one was undecided and one, in a dense urban PCMC area, was opposed); 15 of 16 would recommend it to colleagues; 12 of 16 scored its usefulness at the maximum of 5, with a group median of 5 for both usefulness and ease of use; and 15 of 16 rated it better than the paper-based method. Two problems recurred. The people-to-screen target shown in each plan was endorsed by only 3 of 16 groups (19%), who read it as a quota on an already heavy workload, and turn-by-turn navigation failed for 5 of 7 workers in one dense urban PMC group. In the timeand-motion study the five plan-access steps, spread across three separate applications, took a median of 54 seconds per hotspot (IQR 42 to 69) among the 37 workers for whom every step was timed.

In the post-campaign survey (n=101; Table 3), 96% reported finding the same presumptive cases while screening fewer people (60% fully, 36% partly), 94% found navigation easy, 92% reported reduced workload, 86% time saved and 98% adequate training; 83% agreed the hotspots were higher-risk areas, but only 41% perceived more presumptive cases in hotspots than in general areas. Field barriers were also reported: 62% still encountered stigma, 37% found it very difficult to persuade people to attend for testing, and only 49% said referred persons gave sputum on time (Supplementary Table S8). Table 4 integrates the quantitative and qualitative findings.

Discussion

Three conclusions follow. First, the operational case is made: AI-guided planning concentrated screening into pockets with about three times the standard campaign's confirmed yield per person, cut NNS and cost per confirmed case by two-thirds, and was accepted by the workers who used it. Second, superiority in total case detection is not established: the all-forms comparison is a null, and in absolute terms the standard campaign found twice as many cases. Third, the primary effect is real but fragile and confounded, and should be read as hypothesis-generating.

The two yield measures are reconciled by the study conditions rather than by the targeting. The standard campaign ran with a mobile X-ray van and detected clinically diagnosed and extrapulmonary disease that a symptom screen with sputum NAAT cannot: 23 of its 32 cases were X-ray-based pulmonary or extrapulmonary diagnoses, against 5 of the AI arm's 15, a profile consistent with the large subclinical fraction of TB now recognised in prevalence surveys. [17, 18] Ascertainment also differed in the opposite direction. The AI arm's protocol specified NAAT for every presumptive, whereas the standard campaign relied mainly on sputum microscopy, so part of the per-person bacteriological advantage may reflect the more sensitive test rather than better targeting. Half of the surveyed ASHAs reported that referred persons did not give sputum on time, so sputum-based confirmation without imaging was itself a constraint on what the AI arm could detect. The comparator was drawn from an earlier period, and urban denominators were constructed differently in the two arms; reconstructing the AI arm's urban denominator from households visited lowers the pooled rate ratio to 2.71 (95% CI 0.99 to 7.54). None of these is a property of the algorithm, and none can be separated from it in this design.

The primary advantage arose in the urban units, where nine of the ten confirmed cases were found. There it took the form of precision rather than volume: the presumptive rate did not rise, but a tested presumptive in the AI arm was 2.5 times as likely to be bacteriologically confirmed. This is the setting where administrative vulnerability layers offer least, since they resolve to the block or ward and a whole-ward campaign inside a dense city is not feasible. [10] Sub-ward targeting complements such layers, which allocate effort across a district, rather than replacing them. The usability failures were also urban: navigation failed in one dense unit, and the plan-access overhead fell on exactly these teams. Within the urban arm, Haveli PMC yielded 7 cases from 27,047 screened and Haveli PCMC 2 from 32,240; whether that reflects implementation fidelity or underlying epidemiology cannot be resolved here.

Two further points bear on deployment. A notification-trained model is weakest where notifications are sparse, which are often the least-served areas, so an environmental cold-start layer is needed before such targeting can claim to reach them. And prioritisation alone does not raise total detection: the all-forms null is the expected result of prioritising without then saturating the prioritised zones with complete coverage, imaging and molecular testing, the approach that community-wide screening has been shown to require to reduce prevalence. [19] AI-NETRA is best read as the first layer of that sequence. Earlier geospatial TB work has largely operated at district or province level without field validation, [7, 20] and community ACF reviews emphasise that yield depends as much on the diagnostic package as on where teams go. [21] Strengths include a pre-specified bacteriological endpoint applied to both arms, programme conditions, a mixed-methods design and full reporting of the null. The limitations are substantial: a non-concurrent comparator exposed to secular and seasonal trends; unmatched case ascertainment in both directions; population-covered urban denominators; an intervention bundled with training and a new workflow; small counts, so that the primary result loses conventional significance with the omission of a single case; a single district; self-reported acceptability subject to social desirability, with FGDs facilitated by implementationpartner staff and documented by note-taking rather than transcription; and manual geolocation of cases, which limits within-hexagon spatial analysis.

The decisive next study is a concurrent cluster-randomised or stepped-wedge trial in which both arms run simultaneously with harmonised case definitions, equal access to X-ray and NAAT and equivalent training, with co-primary outcomes of total cases found and cases missed in deprioritised areas, sited in a lownotification district and including a dense urban arm. Routine export of the geo-coordinates already held in Nikshay would allow hexagon-level attribution without bespoke geocoding.

Conclusion

AI-guided planning raised bacteriologically confirmed yield per person screened about threefold, lowered NNS and cost per confirmed case by two-thirds and was well received by frontline workers, but total case detection did not differ from a standard campaign that had mobile X-ray and ran at a different time. The efficiency case for geospatial targeting as a layer within ACF is strong, and strongest in dense urban settings; the case that it finds more cases in absolute terms requires a concurrent, like-for-like trial.
