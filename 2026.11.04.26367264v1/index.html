Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. Abstract

Malaria parasite genotyping and molecular force of infection inference improves standard malaria intervention efficacy analysis.

INTRODUCTION

Malaria remains a significant public health threat, causing over 600,000 deaths and 250 million clinical cases each year (1). The disease burden is greatest in Sub-Saharan Africa, where Plasmodium falciparum is the dominant species of malaria parasite. Following a decade of stagnating progress in malaria control, new momentum is expected from the recent implementation of the malaria vaccines RTS,S/AS01 and R21/Matrix-M in Africa, primarily aimed at reducing disease burden among young children, and from a recent surge in the development of new countermeasures suitable for wider age groups as well as during pregnancy. New biomedical interventions in development include next-generation vaccines, long-acting monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?0mmNwB and long-acting injectable small-molecule drugs (LAIs) (3). These strategies are intended to complement mosquito vector interventions, which continue to represent the mainstay of malaria control (4).

Traditionally, early-phase clinical trials that are designed to assess the efficacy of candidate malaria interventions in endemic regions have often relied on "time-to-first event" analysis using Cox proportional hazards models. These may use time-to-first malaria infection (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?Fb8wuv) based on thick blood smear microscopy (TBS) or PCR-based methods performed on serially sampled blood as primary endpoints (8, 9, 11).

The use of time-to-first-event endpoints in malaria intervention trials has several important limitations.

First, TBS and PCR-based methods cannot distinguish new vs. persisting infections. The approach assumes that time to first infection adequately reflects the overall longitudinal risk of infection during the entire trial period. Second, censoring participants after their first infection can introduce bias by decreasing the size of the study population and changing its risk profile over time. For example, individuals in the control arm who are at high risk of infective mosquito bites, such as those living near mosquito breeding sites, are more likely to be infected and censored early in the trial, while those in the intervention arm(s) who are at equally high risk of infective mosquito bites may remain in the at-risk population longer if the intervention is protective. The control arm is thus expected to become enriched for individuals at lower risk, resulting in an artefactual decline in intervention efficacy over time. Beyond distorting time-specific efficacy estimates, the time-to-first event approach can bias the composite efficacy metric by violating the proportional hazards and constant risk composition assumptions that underlie Cox models and similar survival regression methods (12, 13).

The above limitations of time-to-event analysis can be addressed by including parasite genotypic endpoints to distinguish new from persistent infection states and thereby resolve total infection incidence. This approach enables all participants to remain in the at-risk population throughout the trial follow-up period, allowing for a more complete picture of the infection burden prevented by the intervention. In addition, strainand allele-level analysis of breakthrough infections can identify parasite polymorphisms linked to intervention 'escape', providing insight into the mechanisms underlying intervention failure.

Here, we explored the utility of including parasite genotypic endpoints in clinical trials of malaria interventions by applying this approach to a previously described phase 2 trial of the long-acting mAb CIS43LS (6, 14). CIS43LS targets a conserved 'junctional' epitope spanning the N-terminal and central repeat regions of the P. falciparum circumsporozoite protein (PfCSP) (15). As previously reported, a single intravenous dose of 40 mg/kg of CIS43LS provided 88.2% protective efficacy against the first P. falciparum infection detected by TBS (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?Z54Rn3) and 87.4% efficacy against the first infection detected by qRT-PCR (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?IchNJN) during a six-month malaria season. We re-analyzed this trial using genotypic endpoints generated via PCR amplicon sequencing (AmpSeq) at seven polymorphic P. falciparum targets, estimating the number of distinct strains newly acquired per follow-up visit per participant. We used the genotypic data to calculate three CIS43LS efficacy estimates. The first is efficacy against the molecular force of infection (molFOI)https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?vMDBh3 the number of newly acquired parasite strains over time (16) , which we denote as EffmolFOI. EffmolFOI is defined as one minus the ratio of incidence of new strains in the treated vs. control arms and incorporates the number of events over follow-up, but does not consider time-to-first event.

The second genotyping-informed estimate is the proportion reduction in the incidence of new malaria infections (where one "infection" may include multiple strains) over the course of follow-up. We denote this EffIRR for the incidence rate ratio.

The third estimate, EffFH, integrates time to first infection together with the number of strains at first infection, but it does not consider subsequent infection events. Proposed by Follmann and Huang, this efficacy estimate is defined as the proportional reduction in mean number of strains that develop into a blood-stage infection from an exposure (17). EffFH is estimated as one minus the product of the Cox-estimated hazard ratio (HR) multiplied by the ratio of the mean number of strains at first infection in treated vs. control arms. We compare these genotyping-informed efficacy estimates to the time-to-first event-based efficacy estimates previously used to evaluate CIS43LS efficacy in this trial (6, 14). These include efficacy calculated as 1 -HR based on thick blood smear microscopy, efficacy as 1 -HR based on qRT-PCR, and efficacy as 1 -risk ratio for the risk of at least one infection detected by qRT-PCR during the follow-up period.

Relationship between baseline P. falciparum infection-status and subsequent molFOI, clinical malaria risk, and CIS43LS efficacy

To evaluate differential changes between arms in risk set composition, we explored the relationship between P. falciparum infection-status at study enrollment, as determined by qRT-PCR, and the subsequent molFOI, risk of clinical malaria, and CIS43LS efficacy. After clearance of baseline infection with AL, we found that monthly estimates of molFOI were higher among participants who were enrollment-positive (EP, n = 133) versus enrollment-negative (EN, n = 194) (Fig. 5A). EP participants also acquired their first new P. falciparum infection significantly earlier than EN participants (Fig. 5B, p = 0.026), resulting in more rapid depletion (i.e. censoring) of EP participants from the study population included in time-to-first infection analysis. This accelerated depletion effect was apparent across all three study arms during early follow-up (Fig. S2A), suggesting it may result from infections that were at the hepatic stage during enrollment. For example, from day 0 to 21, the proportion of EP participants declined by 18.5% in the placebo arm, 7.4% in the 10 mg/kg arm, and 10.9% in the 40 mg/kg arm. The proportion of EN participants increased, respectively, by 14.2%, 4.8%, and 7.8%. In contrast, the initial decline in EP proportion in the risk set for TBS positivity was similar between treatment groups, but the EP proportion declined more steeply in controls starting around Day 120. (Fig. S2B). P. falciparum infection at enrollment may indicate a higher overall risk of infection, and as a result, a greater degree of acquired immunity to symptomatic malaria caused by blood stage parasites (18).

Accordingly, despite the higher risk of infection among EP participants (Fig. 5B, p = 0.026), we observed a trend toward lower risk of clinical malaria among those who became infected (Fig. 5C) and lower median parasitemia when infected compared to EN participants (9.9 parasites/μl vs. 78.9 parasites/μl, p < 0.001, Fig. S3A). Finally, for all qRT-PCR-detected infections that occurred after study agent administration, we sequenced the PfCSP junctional region (JR) epitope targeted by CIS43LS (PfCSP amino acid positions 101 to 115 covered by the Nterm assay) to determine whether breakthrough infections in CIS43LS recipients were associated with polymorphisms in this epitope, and no polymorphisms were detected. DISCUSSION

Here we explored the use of P. falciparum genotypic analysis in a clinical trial of a candidate malaria intervention to estimate efficacy and to detect parasite polymorphisms that might escape the intervention. Specifically, we genotyped P. falciparum infections that were detected in a phase 2 trial of the anti-malaria mAb CIS43LS and compared efficacy estimates based on molFOI to traditional methods that use PCR-or TBS-based infection endpoints and time-to-first infection (HR) analysis. Overall, we found that parasite genotypic analysis provided a more comprehensive picture of intervention efficacy than is possible with qRT-PCR or TBS microscopy alone, and critically, the genotypic analysis did not reveal associations between breakthrough infections that occurred in CIS43LS recipients and polymorphisms within or near the PfCSP epitope that CIS43LS targets.

We found that the aggregate 6-month CIS43LS efficacy estimates were similar in magnitude and precision when determined by the molFOI method and the standard HR method. However, when analyzed by month, the precision of efficacy estimates was higher with the molFOI method, and that precision increased with time relative to the HR method as trial participants experienced their first infections and were removed (censored) from the population at risk that contributed to the HR efficacy estimate. Notably, the lower bound of the 95% confidence interval for protective efficacy remained above 77% by molFOI as opposed to 3% by HR at month 6 in the 40 mg/kg CIS43LS arm, underscoring the ability of molFOI to assess efficacy with greater precision over the entire follow-up period, a feature that could have implications for future trial designs (e.g. smaller sample size) and for improving the accuracy of efficacy predictions beyond the follow-up period.

Importantly, by capturing total infection incidence with molFOI, and not censoring participants after their first infection, the size and risk profile of the study population included in the molFOI efficacy analysis was maintained throughout the follow-up period, a feature precluded by HR analysis that censors participants after their first infection, potentially introducing bias into efficacy estimates. For example, with HR analysis we found that censoring participants after their first infections resulted in more rapid depletion of participants who were infected at enrollment, a marker of higher infection risk. The depletion effect was relatively small in this trial and did not bias EffHR estimates. It will be important to assess the impact of depletion effects in future malaria intervention trials, particularly those involving children who have less acquired immunity to bloodstage malaria and are therefore more likely to experience patent parasitemia and symptomatic malaria earlier in a trial. Limited ability to control parasitemia (e.g., median 7,690 parasites/µl for first infections in 4-6 yearolds in the region (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?EqAJ7Y) may also strengthen the positive association between baseline infection risk and time to first detectable infection. This could accelerate the depletion of higher-risk individuals from less-protected study arms within a time-to-first infection analysis framework, possibly leading to biases in EffHR unobserved in the present study. As evidence of the potentially greater risk of encountering bias in EffHR in pediatric trials, a previous longitudinal study conducted at the same study site reports approximately two-fold higher molFOI in children and substantially greater inter-individual variability relative to that observed in this study (21).

Higher re-infection rates and greater heterogeneity in infection susceptibility may further reduce the extent to which time to first infection serves as a proxy for cumulative incidence over follow-up. These factors may have contributed to the observation of greater EffmolFOI than EffHR (0.52 vs. 0.46) in a study of infants receiving RTS,S (22). Although the EffmolFOI estimate was less precise, this may be because only the first TBS positive sample per child was genotyped in that study.

We also found that the efficacy estimate of CIS43LS against the mean number of infecting strains per exposure (EffFH) was similar to the HR efficacy estimate, primarily because the mean number of strains at first infection was similar in the placebo and CIS43LS arms. EffFH was less precise than EffHR because EffFH accounts for uncertainty in estimating the mean strain count in the two arms. Previous analyses of two trials that evaluated genotyping endpoints in African infants receiving the RTS,S vaccine or a control vaccine estimated a lower strain count at first infection in RTS,S recipients (the mean ratio of strains at first infection in RTS,S versus control vaccine recipients was 0.88 (23) and 0.86 (22)). The accompanying statistical power analysis in the second study found that even with a mean ratio of 0.86, EffFH is only slightly more powerful than EffHR in some scenarios and is less powerful in others. In this study, the mean ratios near 1 may be because the treatments extend time to first infection, so treated participants become infected later in the trial when malaria transmission is higher. Thus, EffFH does not provide a clear advantage to EffHR although it requires more data.

The collective findings emerging from TBS microscopy (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?4guQLl qRT-PCR (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?aOBlLj and molFOI-based analyses of this CIS43LS trial offer several lessons for the design of future malaria intervention trials. First, for interventions that aim to block sporozoite infection, qRT-PCR offers greatly enhanced sensitivity relative to TBS, and superior sensitivity to genotyping. Interpretive ambiguities related to the high sensitivity of qRT-PCR, for example distinguishing new infection from recrudescence following drug treatment of baseline parasitemia, may be clarified by genotyping (9). Second, the improved precision and potentially reduced bias in efficacy estimation offered by molFOI may warrant genotyping, particularly as new malaria interventions are deployed and clinical trials contend with measuring protection against the backdrop of an escalating standard of care. Third, genotyping only the first infection (the Follmann-Huang approach) is not advised, because the seasonal timing of first infections may differ systematically between study arms. Therefore, if a genotypic estimate of molFOI is planned, we recommend genotyping all infected samples collected during followup, ideally with infected samples first identified using qRT-PCR for cost-effectiveness. In summary, while molecular endpoints of infection such as qRT-PCR and genotyping carry additional costs beyond clinical disease or microscopy-based endpoints, they can uniquely inform whether candidate malaria interventions may support disease elimination by decreasing transmission risk, in addition to reducing clinical disease burden.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Trial setting and temporal design

The double-blind, placebo-controlled, randomized phase 2 trial of CIS43LS (NCT04329104) involved healthy adults residing in the rural neighboring communities of Kalifabougou and Torodo, Mali, where P. falciparum transmission typically occurs from July through December (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?7CyPG4. As described previously (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?wdlj4G 330 eligible adult participants were randomized and received a single intravenous (IV) infusion of normal saline placebo (n = 110) or CIS43LS at a dose of 10 mg/kg (n = 110) or 40 mg/kg (n = 110) between between May 6th and August 6th, 2021. All participants received a standard treatment course (AL) at enrollment, 7-15 days prior to study agent administration ('day 0'). DBS and TBS sampling was scheduled for collection at enrollment and study days 0, 1, 3, 7, 14, 21, 28, 42, 56, 60, 84, 98, 112, 126, 140, 154, and 168 and during unscheduled illness visits when malaria was suspected. Follow-up visits were realized on exact scheduled dates in 30.2% of cases, with realized dates averaging 1.5 days before or after scheduled dates. In our analyses we therefore generally refer to the actual number of days elapsed since day 0 instead of the originally scheduled date. TBS microscopy was only conducted in real time when study participants exhibited signs or symptoms of malaria, and all participants who developed symptomatic malaria during the trial were provided standard treatment.

The trial followed the International Council for Harmonization Good Clinical Practice guidelines and applicable regulations in Mali. The participating communities granted permission to conduct the trial, and all participants provided written informed consent. The trial protocol and informed-consent forms were approved by the ethics committee at Faculté de Médecine et d'Odonto-Stomatologie and Faculté de Pharmacie at the University of Sciences, Techniques and Technologies of Bamako and by the national regulatory authorities of Mali.

Microscopy was performed for all TBS samples, while qRT-PCR testing was only performed on the subset of DBS needed to determine when the first qRT-PCR-detectable infection occurred. Because the qRT-PCR testing did not include all DBS samples, genotyping was triggered by TBS or qRT-PCR positivity at the timepoint of interest or before in the same person, and also included enrollment samples with positive or missing qRT-PCR.

Genotyping consisted of two separate AmpSeq assays: 1) an 'Nterm' PCR monoplex containing the CIS43LS epitope in the junctional region (JR) of PfCSP, and 2) a PCR multiplex called 'SIMPLseq' (24) which targets six highly polymorphic P. falciparum gene fragments. For both assays, we extracted total DNA from DBS using KingFisher Ready DNA Ultra 2.0 Prefilled Plates (ThermoFisher Scientific) and followed a standard nested PCR approach followed by paired-end sequencing on the Illumina MiSeq platform. We resolved microhaplotypes (alleles) from sequence output using DADA2 (v.1.14.0) (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?Ni0vwN) and the malaria amplicon processing pipeline available at https://github.com/broadinstitute/malaria-amplicon-pipeline.git.

Details on sample inclusion, library preparation, and microhaplotype processing are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

To classify alleles as new or persistent at each detection timepoint per sample, we applied a Bayesian framework called DINEMITES (26). The model treats longitudinal allele observations as outcomes of latent infection states and estimates, via posterior inference, the probability that each observed allele reflects either persistence of an existing strain or acquisition of a new strain. For each participant timepoint, new allele presences inferred per locus are summarized into an expected number of newly acquired strains. DINEMITES also estimates the number of distinct infection events for each subject. An "infection event" is defined as transmission of malaria parasites from a mosquito to a human, potentially involving multiple strains, and is assigned to the sampling timepoint rather than the actual transmission time (expected to have occurred approximately 10 days earlier, based on the P. falciparum incubation period (27)). DINEMITES assignment of infection events accounts for the possibility that alleles introduced by a single infection become detectable over several visits due to imperfect detection and temporal variation in parasite density. At most one infection event is inferred per sampling timepoint, as multiple transmission events occurring within a sampling interval cannot be distinguished. DINEMITES included imputation for qRT-PCR positive samples with missing genotyping data; 50 augmented datasets were generated including expected new strain counts for each person-timepoint. See Supplementary Materials for further details about DINEMITES.

Statistical analyses (molFOI modelling and efficacy estimation):

Efficacy estimates were calculated for five methods, described below, for each of the 50 augmented datasets and then combined using Rubin's rules (28). The first two efficacy estimation methods are commonly applied and analyze only the time to first malaria infection, without incorporating data from later samples. The latter three methods incorporate information obtained from genotyping. 1. Risk ratio (RR) efficacy, denoted EffRR, is estimated as one minus the risk ratio (1-RR), which is the CIS43LS-induced proportion reduction in probability of malaria infection during follow-up. In this analysis, malaria infection was defined as qRT-PCR positivity.

2. Hazard ratio (HR) efficacy, denoted EffHR, against malaria infection is estimated as one minus the hazard ratio (1-HR), which is the CIS43LS-induced proportion reduction in hazard of malaria infection during follow-up. This measure incorporates information about the time to first infection but does not use information about later infections or number of strains at first infection. EffHR was calculated both for the hazard of qRT-PCR positivity, and for the hazard of TBS positivity during follow-up.

3. Follmann-Huang (FH) efficacy, denoted EffFH, is defined as the CIS43LS-induced proportion reduction in number of infecting strains per exposure. In field trials, we do not observe exposure events of participants, so we cannot calculate mean strains per exposure. Follmann and Huang demonstrated that under randomization (which ensures similar exposure distributions in the study arms), we can estimate EffFH as where and are mean numbers of strains for observed infections (rather than exposure events) in treated and controls, respectively, and HR is the hazard ratio for time to first observed infection obtained from a Cox model. The rationale for this is that a treatment that reduces the number of strains per exposure will convert some infections to non-infections (by converting exposure events with non-zero strains to exposure events with zero strains), which pushes back the time to first infection. This measure incorporates the time to first infection and number of strains present at the first infection. This is why, although the estimand is one minus the ratio of means, a valid estimator is one minus the HR times the ratio of truncated means, where the truncation excludes all exposures with zero infecting strains as these are unobservable.

4. Molecular force of infection (molFOI) efficacy, denoted EffmolFOI, is estimated as one minus the incidence of strains per unit time in treated vs. control participants. This measure incorporates information about the number of strains per participant acquired during follow-up but discards the timing information.

5. Incidence rate ratio efficacy, denoted EffIRR, is estimated as one minus the incidence of new malaria infections determined by DINEMITES, where one "infection" may include multiple strains.

RR values were estimated by modified Poisson regression (29). In general, HR values and associated variance were estimated with Cox regression, except for the HR for time to first positive TBS, which was estimated with interval-censored survival analysis with a Weibull distribution to align with the primary analysis (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?eCfRix. FH estimates were calculated using the empirical mean number of strains at first infection and the HR estimated from Cox regression. molFOI was estimated by a Tweedie model with log-transformed total time-at-risk as an offset, using the glmmTMB() package (30, 31) in R. Tweedie models are a class of models appropriate for random variables X that are discrete when X=0 and continuous when X>0 (32). EffIRR (where an infection may include multiple strains) was calculated analogously to molFOI.

A similarly stratified analysis was performed for time from first infection to clinical malaria to assess whether participants with higher exposure have more natural immunity to symptoms.
