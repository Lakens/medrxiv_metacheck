Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background. Myocardial infarction (MI) outcomes depend on time to treatment, and fatigue and sleep disturbance are often reported in the weeks before the event. Whether wearable-measured sleep changes within individuals before MI, and whether such change is specific to MI, is unknown.

In the All of Us Research Program, adults with a first MI code and at least 180 valid Fitbit sleep nights in the preceding 360 days were matched to up to 10 controls on sex, comorbidities, sleep apnea, calendar time and a propensity score. The full matched cohort of all cases was the primary cohort, and cases with diabetes and/or hypertension formed a subgroup. The main endpoints were the within-person change in night-to-night variability (SD, RMSSD, CV) of the REM-sleep fraction at days 30-16 relative to each person's baseline (days 360-121) and from days 30-16 to days 15-1, tested against 10,000 randomly placed index dates with allowance for the choice of window (max-T). The same pipeline was applied to cases without recorded symptoms or treatment, to an index date at the first angina code, to a cohort including heart failure and to ischaemic stroke and admissions for heart failure, pneumonia, fracture or appendicitis and any cause, and the analysis was adjusted for nightly heart rate, wear and device.

Results. The full matched cohort comprised 45 cases and 450 controls. REM-fraction variability changed transiently before MI, decreasing at days 30-16 and returning towards baseline over days 15-1; allowing for the choice of window, this decrease-thenreturn pattern was rarely matched at 10,000 randomly placed index dates (max-T p<0.001). Introduction

In the United States, a myocardial infarction (MI) occurs about every 40 seconds, and ischaemic heart disease caused about 9 million deaths worldwide in 2021 1 . Outcomes depend on time to treatment: each 30 minutes of delay to primary angioplasty was associated with a 7.5% relative increase in 1-year mortality 2 . In a Swedish registry of 89,155 patients with ST-elevation MI followed over 20 years, the median pre-hospital delay was 150 minutes; each hour of delay independently increased 14-day and 1-year mortality, and delay did not decline over the two decades 3 . In a Japanese registry of 4,625 patients, 63.3% arrived 2 hours or more after symptom onset, and older age, diabetes and night-time onset were associated with longer delay 4.

Many patients report symptoms before their MI. In a study of 515 women, 95% reported new or different symptoms more than a month before the event, most often unusual fatigue (70%) and sleep disturbance (48%) 5 . In a cross-sectional study of 242 patients, 41.3% reported warning symptoms, including unusual fatigue (23%) and sleep disturbance (22%) 6.

Sleep is closely linked to cardiovascular health. In a dose-response meta-analysis of 71 cohorts comprising 3.8 million participants, sleep duration showed a U-shaped association with cardio-cerebrovascular disease, with the lowest risk at about 7.5 hours per night 7 . In the All of Us Research Program, sleep measured with consumer wearables over a median of 4.5 years in 6,785 participants was associated with a range of incident chronic diseases: irregular sleep with obesity, hyperlipidaemia, hypertension, major depressive disorder and generalised anxiety disorder, and REM and deep sleep inversely with atrial fibrillation 8 . Among US mobile phone users, 28% reported using an app to track their sleep 9 . Against polysomnography, a current Fitbit device showed no significant bias in total sleep time, while agreement for sleep stages was moderate (four-stage agreement 70.9%, κ 0.55) 10 . Continuous wearable monitoring relative to a person's own baseline has anticipated acute events in other conditions: in patients with heart failure, a multisensor patch predicted rehospitalisation with 76-88% sensitivity and 85% specificity at a median of 6.5 days before admission 11 , and a wrist-worn device detected SARS-CoV-2 infection earlier than symptom reporting, although with low specificity 12 . To our knowledge, within-person changes in wearable-measured sleep in the weeks before MI have not been examined; existing wearable studies have related habitual sleep to longterm disease risk 8 or detected short-term change before other acute events 11, 12.

In the All of Us Research Program, heart-rate and step-count features from Fitbit devices predicted incident cardiovascular disease (area under the curve 0.80) and allcause hospitalisation in 14,157 participants 13 , and in the period from 90 days before to 30 days after a diagnosis of incident heart failure, the percentage of REM sleep decreased and that of light sleep increased, whereas matched controls did not change 14.

Using long-term Fitbit sleep data linked to electronic health records in the All of Us Research Program, we examined within-person changes in night-to-night variability of REM sleep in the weeks before incident MI, compared with matched controls, and tested whether the changes were specific to MI by applying the same analysis to other acute events.

Methods

Data source

We used the All of Us Research Program Registered Tier Dataset v9 (curated data repository R2025Q4R6), which links Fitbit data to electronic health records (EHR). See Declarations for ethics and data availability.

Cases

The index date was the first EHR code for acute or unspecified MI (ICD-10-CM I21.0-I21.4, I21.9, I21. A9; ICD-9-CM 410.x0/410.x1). Participants were excluded if a code for prior MI (I25.2, 412), post-MI or subsequent MI (I22-I23, 410.x2, 411.0, 429.7x) or type 2 MI (I21. A1) was recorded before the index date. Cases were required to have age and sex recorded, at least 180 valid sleep nights in the 360 days before the index date, a body mass index (BMI; latest value within 3 years) and baseline sleep available, and no atrial fibrillation or flutter (I48; 427.31/427.32) or heart-failure (I50.x; 428.x) code before the index date. Atrial fibrillation and heart failure were excluded because they disturb sleep architecture and heart-rate-based sleep staging; a sensitivity analysis included heart failure (Secondary analyses).

Sleep data

A valid night was a main-sleep record with sleep stages and at least 180 minutes asleep; nights with zero REM sleep (under 1% of nights) were retained with a REM fraction of 0. The REM fraction was REM minutes divided by minutes asleep. Deep and light fractions, total sleep time, the wake fraction (minutes awake divided by time in bed) and sleep efficiency were derived for exploratory analyses. The baseline period was days 360-121 before the index date; at least 30 valid baseline nights were required.

Controls and matching

Candidate controls had no MI-class code and a 360-day window of Fitbit data ending within ±365 days of the case's index date. For each control, one candidate reference date was set in every calendar week, on a pseudo-random day of that week, irrespective of whether sleep was recorded that night, so that the window end did not depend on device wear; for each case, the candidate nearest to the case's index date was used. Controls were matched exactly on sex, diabetes (E08-E13; 250), hypertension (I10-I16; 401-405) and sleep apnea (G47.3; 327.2, 780.51/53/57) status, and by a propensity score for age, BMI and baseline sleep duration within a caliper of 0.5 SD of the logit 15 , taking 5-10 controls per case, nearest first, without replacement. The same exclusions as for cases applied. The full matched cohort was the primary cohort; matched sets whose case had diabetes and/or hypertension (subgroup) and sets without either condition (sensitivity) were also analysed. The number of cases in the subgroup is not reported because, together with the full matched cohort, it would reveal a count of 20 or fewer (the sets without diabetes or hypertension).

Endpoints

For each person, night-to-night variability of the REM fraction was summarised in 15night windows by the SD, the root mean square of successive differences between consecutive calendar nights that were both valid (RMSSD) and the coefficient of variation (CV). Endpoint 1 was the change at days 30-16 relative to the person's baseline: the median, over all same-length baseline windows, of the difference between the target window and each baseline window. Endpoint 2 was the change from days 30-16 to days 15-1, and endpoint 3 the change at days 15-1 relative to baseline. Windows required at least 60% valid nights and were compared with equal numbers of valid nights. SD and RMSSD changes were divided by the person's nightly scale (1.4826 × median absolute deviation of the baseline); CV changes were used as plain differences. SD and RMSSD are related but capture different timescales: SD reflects overall spread within the window, including slow drifts, whereas RMSSD reflects only differences between consecutive nights (RMSSD² ≈ 2•SD²•(1 -lag-1 autocorrelation)).

Endpoint selection. The direction and approximate timing of the change were first seen in a previous All of Us data release (CDR v8), which overlaps with the present cohort and so does not provide independent validation. In an analysis reported on 7 August 2026, the 10-30 days before MI (near period) were compared within each case with the 20 days with the most data earlier than 50 days before MI (far period), each period with at least 5 days of data; among activity, heart-rate and sleep features, the SD of daily REM minutes (minute_rem_std) separated the two periods most strongly and was lower in the near period in every case (Wilcoxon p<0.01; the number of cases is not shown, 20 or fewer). Variability of the nightly REM fraction was carried forward from analyses of that release: across them, measures of night-to-night variability (SD, RMSSD, CV) consistently separated MI cases from controls, whereas the mean REM fraction did not. The within-person method (each window scored against the person's own same-length baseline windows, with nights equalised) and the 15-night window length were also fixed on v8. In the present data, the decrease seen in v8 was refined to the windows of days 30-16 and 15-1 after an exploratory analysis. We therefore refer to them as main rather than primary endpoints, treat them as exploratory and tested them against randomly placed index dates (Robustness checks).

Secondary analyses

Cases without recorded symptoms or treatment. A case was considered symptomatic if, in days 90-1 before the index date, a beta-blocker, nitrate, antiplatelet or calcium-channel blocker was newly started (no record of the same class in the preceding 365 days) or an angina (I20.x) or chest-pain (R07.x) code was recorded, and asymptomatic otherwise. Matched sets with an asymptomatic case were analysed with the statistics of the main endpoints.

Other acute events.

To test whether the changes were specific to MI, the pipeline (data requirements, matching and endpoints) was applied unchanged to ischaemic stroke and to admissions for heart failure, pneumonia, fracture or appendicitis (an event without a physiological prodrome) and any cause, each with its own matched controls. For stroke, the index date was the first acute ischaemic stroke code (I63; 433.x1, 434.x1) within an acute visit (inpatient, emergency or intensive care) containing that day or starting up to 3 days before it; earlier stroke or stroke-history codes, lacunar or cerebral venous codes in the index episode, MI before the index date and atrial fibrillation or flutter before or within 365 days after it excluded a person. For heart failure (I50; 428), pneumonia (J12-J18; 480-486) and fracture or appendicitis (S02, S12, S22, S32, S42, S52, S62, S72, S82, S92, K35; 800-829, 540), the person's first code of the group had to lie in such an acute visit, and the index date was the start of that visit; for any admission, it was the first inpatient admission. For pneumonia and any admission, a person was excluded if an acute MI code (I21-I22; 410) was recorded during the index admission, from its start to its discharge (or to the first pneumonia code, if later). In the admission groups, an MI or stroke code before the index date and, except for heart failure, atrial fibrillation or flutter excluded a person. Controls had no code of the group before the reference date or within 365 days after it. A heart-failure code before the index or reference date excluded cases and controls in every group, as for MI. Power to detect the effect seen in MI (δ -0.30) was estimated with the Noether approximation 16 . MI was compared with each admission group directly by the difference in Cliff's δ of endpoint 1 (MI minus group), with a 95% CI from 2,000 bootstrap resamples in which the matched sets of MI and of the group were resampled independently. Persons included in both the MI analysis and a comparison analysis, as a case or a control, were kept in both (43 for pneumonia, 53 for fracture or appendicitis and 213 for any admission).

Heart failure. The whole analysis, including extraction and matching, was repeated in a cohort in which heart failure neither excluded participants nor was used for matching; the size of this cohort is not reported because, together with the main cohort, it would reveal the number of people with heart failure. In that cohort, controls with and without a heartfailure code were compared, the per-person scores of MI cases were compared against each and the conditional logistic regression was repeated with heart-failure status as a covariate.

Nightly heart rate, wear and device. Minute-level heart rate (25-220 beats per minute) within the main-sleep segments gave, for each valid night, the mean heart rate and the share of main-sleep minutes with a heart-rate value (wear fraction). The changes at days 30-16 of the nightly mean heart rate and of its 15-night SD, computed with the same within-person method as the REM endpoint, were always included as covariates. The valid-night fraction and the wear fraction at days 30-16 and the Fitbit device (the wrist device recorded on the valid nights at days 30-16 or the following day, scales and phone tracking excluded, grouped as Charge, Inspire, Versa, Sense, other or no record; per person, the family with the most such nights, ties going to the most recent) were compared between cases and controls by the standardised mean difference with controls weighted by 1/(controls in the set), and those of 0.1 or more in absolute value were added, the largest first, up to four variables in all; for the device the largest absolute SMD over its categories was used, and it was entered as indicator variables against the most common family. A missing wear fraction was set to the mean. The conditional logistic regression was fitted with and without these covariates in the people with both heart-rate covariates. Index date at the first angina code. For cases whose first-ever angina code (I20.x, including unstable angina) lay in days 90-1 before the MI index date, the whole matched set was re-indexed: the case's index date was set to that code and the controls' reference dates were moved back by the same number of days, and all windows were recomputed. The first angina or chest-pain code (I20.x or R07.x) was used as a secondary definition, and the analysis was repeated with these sets removed.

Composite indices.

From the per-person z scores, we formed the dip (-z of days 30-16), the rise over days 15-1 (z of days 15-1 minus z of days 30-16, or the z score of the change from days 30-16 to days 15-1 against the person's changes between adjacent baseline windows), their conjunction (the minimum of dip and rise) and a flag (dip z ≤ -1 and rise ≥ +1), and computed the AUC for MI cases against their own controls and against the cases of each other event group. Secondary analyses were not adjusted for multiplicity.

Statistical analysis

Changes were compared between cases and controls with Cliff's δ and the Mann-Whitney test 17 , conditional logistic regression within matched sets (odds ratio per SD) 18 and a within-set permutation test (1,000 permutations). Each group's change from zero was summarised by the matched-pairs rank-biserial correlation with the Wilcoxon signed-rank test 19 . To allow for residual imbalance after matching, the conditional logistic regression was repeated with age, BMI and baseline sleep duration (standardised) as covariates.

Per-person detection. Each person's change was expressed as a z score against their own distribution of baseline windows (median and 1.4826 × MAD). We report the AUC, the odds ratio for being flagged (|z| ≥ 1.645) and, for continuous monitoring, the number of false alarms per control-year over days 120-1.

Exploratory segment analysis. For every sleep measure, nine window features (mean, median, SD, CV, median absolute deviation, minimum, maximum, RMSSD and lag-1 autocorrelation) were computed in disjoint 15-, 30-and 60-night segments over days 120-1, each compared with same-length baseline windows and with the preceding segment, using the same statistics as the main endpoints.

Exploratory analyses. Bubble entropy 20 , distribution entropy 21 (pairs at lags ≤ 10 nights), local variation (Lv) 22 over 14 and 30 nights, Bayesian online changepoint detection 23 and a Kalman-filtered time-varying vector autoregression 24 were computed for each sleep measure and compared as above. Person-level logistic regression models combining endpoint features were evaluated by leave-one-matched-set-out cross-validation, and day-level classifiers (logistic regression and random forest) by 5fold cross-validation grouped by matched set. Exploratory p values are not adjusted for multiplicity.

Robustness checks. To test whether a comparable decrease and increase could arise at any time, each matched set (the case and its controls together) was assigned a random earlier index date, drawn from the offsets of 60-354 days (7-day steps) at which its case met the same data requirement as at the real index date and had an endpoint-1 value; a control was included at that date only if the preceding 360 days held at least 180 valid nights, and sets whose case had no such offset were not used (the observed values were recomputed in the same sets). Endpoint 1, endpoint 2 and a combined decreasethen-increase statistic, min(-δ1, δ2), were recomputed with the same pipeline in 10,000 draws, a number chosen for precision. Single-window p values were two-sided (twice the smaller tail of the draws). To allow for the window having been chosen after the fact, the same statistics were computed at each of the eight 15-night windows in days 120-1 in every draw; each window's statistic was standardised by the mean and SD of its own random-date distribution, and the observed standardised value at the paper's window was compared with the largest across the windows in each draw; two-sided, the largest absolute value was used for endpoints 1 and 2 and, for the combined statistic (seven window positions), the larger of it and its mirror, min(δ1, -δ2) (max-T). In a cluster permutation test, in the same matched sets, the 14-night rolling SD of Figure 1b was compared between cases and controls on each of days 120-1 on which both groups had more than 20 people with a value (Mann-Whitney); runs of consecutive days with p<0.05 and the same sign formed clusters, and the mass of the largest decrease cluster overlapping days 30-16 (the sum of the daily differences) was compared with the largest absolute cluster mass over days 120-1 in each of the same 10,000 draws. As secondary analyses, the cluster test was repeated one-sided (decrease clusters against the largest decrease-cluster mass) and with a forming threshold of p<0.01, and max-T was computed one-sided and over the three windows of days 45-31, 30-16 and 15-1; the one-sided versions and the three-window range rest on the decrease seen in the previous data release (Endpoint selection). In a split-half check, the matched sets were divided at random into two halves 1,000 times; in one half the window with the largest |δ| among the eight was chosen, and δ was measured at that window in the other half, with its sign reversed when the chosen change was an increase, so that a negative value means the chosen change repeated. The check was repeated with a shorter baseline (days 240-121, random dates 60-116 days earlier), applied to real and random dates alike. Endpoints 1-3 were re-estimated in persons with all three available. For the index date, inpatient and emergency visits within 30 days of the first MI code were identified. Where the visit began before the code date and the diagnoses recorded before the code indicated an MI presentation (chest pain, angina, tachycardia or palpitations, dyspnoea, pain, hypertensive urgency), the index date was moved to the visit start; cases with a separate acute illness recorded first (stroke, thyrotoxicosis) were kept at the code date. In addition, all windows were moved 3 and 5 days earlier, leaving out the final nights before the index date. Missing nights were classified as no main-sleep record, under 3 hours asleep, no sleep stages or valid with zero REM sleep (retained as a REM fraction of 0).

Results

Study population

The study design is shown in Figure 1a and the participant flow in Figure S1. Of 790 participants with a first acute or unspecified MI code and Fitbit and EHR data, 692 remained after excluding prior, post-MI or type 2 MI codes. After requiring at least 180 valid sleep nights in the year before the index date, the remaining exclusions and matching, the full matched cohort comprised 45 cases and 450 matched controls (10 per case; Table 1). Matching removed the pre-matching imbalance in age (standardised mean difference 0.91 before vs 0.05 after), diabetes (0.59 vs 0.00) and hypertension (0.86 vs 0.00) in the full matched cohort, in which BMI differed by -0.11 after matching (Table 1); in the subgroup with diabetes and/or hypertension, BMI differed by -0.36 and baseline sleep duration by +0.17; adjustment for these is reported below.

Cases had more valid nights than controls over the year (median 290 [IQR 238-323] vs 244 [184-319]; δ 0.22, p=0.017), with a higher share of valid nights at baseline (78.2% vs 66.7%, p=0.012); the share of valid nights in the endpoint windows is given under Missing nights below.

Main endpoints

REM-fraction variability changed transiently before MI, decreasing at days 30-16 and returning towards baseline over days 15-1; allowing for the choice of window, randomly placed index dates rarely reproduced this decrease-then-return pattern (max-T, p<0.001; Random index dates, below). At days 30-16, variability decreased in cases relative to their baseline and to controls (SD: δ -0.30, p=0.004; OR per SD 0.53, 95% CI 0.35-0.80; RMSSD: δ -0.25, p=0.018; OR 0.55, 0.36-0.83), whereas changes in controls were small (|r| ≤ 0.08; Table 2, Figures 1b and 2). Before adjustment, the median SD of the nightly REM fraction in cases was 0.059 at baseline, 0.053 at days 30-16 and 0.062 at days 15-1, against 0.059, 0.060 and 0.059 in controls; for RMSSD, 0.080, 0.069 and 0.085 against 0.081, 0.081 and 0.082 (Table S1). From days 30-16 to days 15-1, variability increased in cases (RMSSD: δ +0.36, p<0.001; OR per SD 2.09, 1.32-3.31; SD: OR 1.90, 1.22-2.95; CV: OR 1.70, 1.13-2.55). Relative to baseline, variability at days 15-1 was higher in cases, but not significantly (endpoint 3: SD δ +0.14, p=0.17; RMSSD δ +0.17, p=0.10; CV δ +0.16, p=0.13; subgroup with diabetes and/or hypertension, δ +0.14 to +0.20, p≥0.086); the increase over the final 15 days thus largely reflects a return from the decrease at days 30-16. The decrease was not confined to cases with diabetes or hypertension: it was of the same size in the subgroup with either condition (SD δ -0.31, p=0.008) and in the same direction in sets without either condition (Table 2). Baseline-adjusted trajectories are shown in Figure 1c. Withinset permutation tests (1,000 permutations) gave similar results (SD: endpoint 1, p=0.003, endpoint 2, p=0.006; RMSSD: endpoint 1, p=0.006, endpoint 2, p=0.002; CV: endpoint 1, p=0.005, endpoint 2, p=0.014). Adjusting the conditional logistic regression for age, BMI and baseline sleep duration did not change the odds ratios (SD endpoint 1, 0.52, 0.34-0.79; RMSSD endpoint 2, 2.09, 1.32-3.33), nor did adjusting for nightly heart rate, wear and device (SD endpoint 1, 0.53, 0.34-0.83, against 0.54, 0.35-0.83, unadjusted in the same people; Table S2).

Per-person detection

In the full matched cohort, per-person z scores discriminated cases from controls with AUCs of 0.51-0.66; the best was RMSSD with either a drop at days 30-16 or a rise at days 15-1 (AUC 0.66; OR for being flagged 3.44, 1.34-8.85). In the subgroup with diabetes and/or hypertension the same rule reached an AUC of 0.64 (OR 2.28, 0.75-6.92). With continuous monitoring over days 120-1, controls had about 2.3 false alarms per year (2.2 in the subgroup). Sensitivity and the false-positive rate could not be reported because, together with the odds ratio, they would reveal counts of 20 or fewer (Table 3).

The dip alone separated MI cases not only from their own controls but also from the cases of other acute events (Table S3). In the full matched cohort, the dip in SD discriminated MI cases from their own controls with an AUC of 0.61 (95% CI 0.51-0.71) and from the cases of pneumonia, fracture or appendicitis and any admission with AUCs of 0.65, 0.65 and 0.66; the dip in RMSSD gave 0.62 (0.51-0.73) against own controls and 0.71 (0.60-0.81) against fracture or appendicitis. Composite indices that also required a subsequent rise performed similarly (SD: 0.62, 0.51-0.73, against own controls).

Other acute events

The decrease in SD at days 30-16 was not seen at the same time before the other acute events examined (Table S4, Figures 1b and 1d). In the full matched cohort, endpoint 1 for SD was δ -0.30 (95% CI -0.47 to -0.12) before MI, against -0.05 (-0.22 to +0.11) before pneumonia admission (69 cases, 690 controls), +0.04 (-0.07 to +0.15) before fracture or appendicitis admission (130 cases, 1,298 controls) and +0.02 (-0.03 to +0.06) before admission of any cause (859 cases, 8,247 controls); pneumonia and any admission excluded admissions with an acute MI code. Compared directly, the change before MI was larger than before each (difference in δ -0.25, -0.49 to -0.01; -0.34, -0.55 to -0.13; -0.32, -0.49 to -0.14). RMSSD also tended to decrease before pneumonia, by less than before MI (δ -0.17, -0.31 to -0.01; MI δ -0.25; difference -0.08, -0.33 to +0.18), so RMSSD was not used as evidence of specificity. The increase over days 15-1 was shared with pneumonia (SD endpoint 2: δ +0.26, p=0.005; RMSSD: δ +0.27, p=0.005) but not with fracture or appendicitis or any admission (Table S5).

Before ischaemic stroke (22 cases, 220 controls) and heart-failure admission (27 cases, 270 controls), endpoint 1 estimates were close to zero or positive (δ about 0 to +0.1), but power to detect an effect of the size seen in MI was at most 0.64 and 0.73. Results were similar in the subgroup with diabetes and/or hypertension (pneumonia, SD endpoint 1: δ -0.07, -0.26 to +0.11).

Cases without recorded symptoms or treatment

In the full matched cohort, SD decreased at days 30-16 in matched sets whose case had no angina or chest-pain code and no newly started cardiovascular drug in the 90 days before the index date (δ -0.30, p=0.011), and variability increased over days 15-1 (SD: δ +0.24, p=0.050; RMSSD: δ +0.34, p=0.006) (Table S6). In the subgroup with diabetes and/or hypertension, 20 or fewer cases had no recorded symptoms or treatment; in these matched sets, REM-fraction SD and RMSSD decreased at days 30-16 (δ about -0.3, p<0.05). The changes were therefore of similar size in cases without recorded symptoms or treatment started before the event.

Heart failure

When cases and controls with a heart-failure code were included (heart failure neither excluded nor matched on), the main endpoints were unchanged (full matched cohort: SD endpoint 1, δ -0.29, p=0.004; RMSSD endpoint 2, δ +0.35, p<0.001; Table S7), and adding heart-failure status as a covariate did not change the odds ratios (SD endpoint 1, 0.51 unadjusted and 0.51 adjusted; RMSSD endpoint 2, 2.04 and 2.05). In that cohort, controls with a heart-failure code did not differ significantly from those without in any endpoint change (p≥0.05). For the per-person SD-drop score, MI cases were separated from controls without heart failure (AUC 0.64) but not from controls with heart failure (AUC 0.5, rounded to one decimal because 20 or fewer controls with heart failure had a value).

Exploratory analyses

In leave-one-set-out cross-validation in the full matched cohort, a model with REMfraction RMSSD changes alone (model A) reached an AUC of 0.63 (0.53-0.71); adding total-sleep variability and lag-1 autocorrelation (model B) gave 0.53 (0.43-0.64), and further adding 30-night distribution entropy of the light and REM fractions (model C) 0.61 (0.52-0.72). The SD, RMSSD and CV of the REM fraction (model D) gave 0.66 (0.57-0.76), and model E, combining models C and D, the highest AUC, 0.67 (0.57-0.77). An L1-penalised model including all 224 features did not discriminate (AUC 0.44, 0.38-0.49). In the subgroup with diabetes and/or hypertension, model C gave the highest AUC (0.69, 0.57-0.81) (Table 3).

In the exploratory segment analysis of the full matched cohort (756 tests, 44 with p<0.05, about 38 expected by chance), the mean REM fraction did not change (days 30-16: δ -0.08, p=0.46), so the change was in variability rather than level. The wake fraction of time in bed changed before the event: its minimum fell over days 60-31 (δ -0.27, p=0.008), its lag-1 autocorrelation tended to rise at days 30-16 (δ +0.17, p=0.094) and its minimum rose again over days 30-1 relative to days 60-31 (δ +0.37, p=0.001). Results were similar in the subgroup with diabetes and/or hypertension (39 of 756 tests with p<0.05; minimum of the wake fraction over days 60-31, δ -0.30, p=0.011).

Among complexity measures, 2 of 80 contrasts had p<0.05 in the full matched cohort (about 4 expected by chance): 30-night bubble entropy of total sleep increased at days 30-16 (δ +0.22, p=0.044), and 30-night distribution entropy of the light-sleep fraction fell over days 15-1 (δ -0.25, p=0.041) after a non-significant rise at days 30-16 (δ +0.15, p=0.17). In the subgroup with diabetes and/or hypertension, 3 of 68 contrasts had p<0.05 (about 3 expected), among them an increase in 30-night distribution entropy of the light-sleep fraction at days 30-16 (δ +0.28, p=0.029; OR per SD 1.46) and in the changepoint probability of the light-sleep fraction over days 15-1 (δ +0.26, p=0.013). Lag-1 autocorrelation of total sleep over the 21 nights before the index date did not differ (full matched cohort, δ -0.09, p=0.50). Day-level classifiers predicting the event 1-14 days ahead did not discriminate (AUC 0.44-0.56).

Robustness checks

Random index dates. Randomly placed index dates did not reproduce the observed pattern. In the full matched cohort, endpoint 1 for SD was δ -0.28 against a mean of +0.03 across random dates (central 95%, -0.14 to +0.20; p=0.0012). Allowing for the choice of window among the eight possible positions, the decrease-then-increase in SD (max-T, p<0.001), the decrease alone (SD, p=0.0034) and the increase in RMSSD (p=0.0035) remained significant (observed values refer to the matched sets with at least one usable random date). Max-T standardises each window by the mean and SD of its own random-date distribution (Table S8); without standardisation, the decrease alone gave p=0.047 (0.11 in the subgroup with diabetes and/or hypertension), because the random-date means differed between windows (-0.08 to +0.04) while their SDs were similar (0.088-0.096). One-sided, max-T gave p=0.0019 for the decrease alone, p<0.001 for the decrease-then-increase and p=0.0019 for the increase in RMSSD; over the three windows of the range suggested by the earlier data release (days 45-31, 30-16 and 15-1), it gave p=0.0013, p<0.001 and p=0.0016. Day by day, the decrease was not detected as a continuous change: in a cluster permutation test of the 14-night rolling SD over days 120-1, the larger of the two clusters of days with p<0.05 within days 30-16 lasted 2 days (days 17-16; mass 0.21; p=0.58), against a median largest cluster of 3 days (95th percentile 13) at random dates. One-sided, the decrease cluster (days 20-15) had p=0.41, and with a forming threshold of p<0.01 no cluster formed. Adjacent days of a 14-night rolling window are strongly correlated, which lengthens clusters arising by chance, and the change itself lasted less than two weeks, so this test had little power. In random halves of the matched sets, the window with the largest change in one half was days 30-16 in 776 of 1,000 splits, and the change was preserved in the other half (median δ -0.25, 95% interval -0.41 to +0.23). The decrease in RMSSD was attenuated in the held-out halves (median δ -0.08, 95% interval -0.28 to +0.15). With the shorter baseline, the decrease in SD was also rarely matched at random dates (single window, p<0.001).

Consistency across endpoints.

Restricted to persons with all three endpoints, endpoints 1 and 2 were similar (SD endpoint 1, δ -0.30, p=0.005; RMSSD endpoint 2, δ +0.36, p<0.001).

Index date. The first MI code was usually recorded on the day the visit began; where the visit had begun 1-3 days earlier with an MI presentation, moving the index date to the visit start did not change the results (SD endpoint 1, δ -0.30, p=0.004; RMSSD endpoint 2, δ +0.37, p<0.001). Moving all windows 3 days earlier left both endpoints largely unchanged (SD endpoint 1, δ -0.22, p=0.032; RMSSD endpoint 2, δ +0.35, p=0.002). Moving them 5 days earlier attenuated both endpoints (SD endpoint 1, δ -0.16, p=0.12, partly because the window then covers days 35-21; RMSSD endpoint 2, δ +0.19, p=0.083). Index at the first angina code. In matched sets whose case had a first angina code in the 90 days before the MI, the whole set was re-indexed to that code. The results were unchanged (full matched cohort: SD endpoint 1, δ -0.31, p=0.003; RMSSD endpoint 2, δ +0.39, p<0.001; original index, δ -0.30 and +0.36), as they were with the first angina or chest-pain code and with these sets removed (SD endpoint 1: δ -0.32, p=0.003) (Table S9). Too few cases were re-indexed to tell whether the timing of the changes follows the angina code or the MI. Missing nights. At days 30-16, the share of valid nights did not differ between cases and controls (73.3% vs 77.3%; δ -0.07, p=0.46), so the decrease in variability is not explained by selective data loss. At days 15-1, cases had somewhat fewer valid nights (67.6% vs 75.4%; δ -0.15, p=0.086), with more nights without a main-sleep record (23.6% vs 17.9%; δ +0.13, p=0.13), but neither difference was significant. Nights with zero REM sleep made up under 1% of nights in every window and are included as a REM fraction of 0; RMSSD uses only pairs of consecutive calendar nights.

Discussion

In this matched case-control study using long-term Fitbit data in the All of Us Research Program, night-to-night variability of the REM-sleep fraction changed within individuals in the weeks before MI: it decreased relative to their own baseline at days 30-16 and returned towards baseline over the final 15 days, while changes in matched controls were small. Scored against each person's own baseline, these changes discriminated cases from controls with AUCs of about 0.6-0.7, although these are probably optimistic because the best rule used RMSSD, whose decrease was attenuated in held-out data.

The decrease in REM-fraction SD was not seen at the same time (days 30-16) before admissions for pneumonia, fracture or appendicitis or any cause, it was larger before MI than before each of them and it persisted in cases without recorded symptoms or treatment and when the index date was moved to the first angina code, which argues against a non-specific effect of acute illness, hospital admission, contact with health care or treatment. The increase over the final two weeks, in contrast, was also seen before pneumonia and probably reflects acute illness more generally. RMSSD also decreased before pneumonia, and its difference from MI was not significant, so only the SD of the REM fraction supports specificity. Comparisons with ischaemic stroke and heart-failure admission were too small to be informative. When people with heart failure were included in the MI analysis, the changes were unchanged, and MI cases could not be distinguished from controls with heart failure. The decrease was of the same size in the subgroup with diabetes and/or hypertension and in the same direction in the sets whose case had neither condition, so it may be a feature of MI itself rather than of the comorbid state.

Variability, rather than the mean level, has been the signal in every analysis of these data: between groups in the earlier data release, and within persons here. What changed before MI is therefore best described as the night-to-night consistency of wearable-estimated sleep architecture rather than its average composition. The difference between groups in the earlier release was later found to reflect diabetes and hypertension; the evidence that the change is linked to the MI itself comes from the within-person analysis, whose decrease-then-return pattern held against randomly placed index dates and was absent before other acute events. Reduced variability has been linked to cardiovascular risk on other timescales: reduced heart-rate variability predicts mortality after MI 25 and cardiac events in the general population 26 , and a loss of variability and complexity of physiological signals, measured for example by entropy, has been proposed as a general sign of failing regulation 27 . These measures concern beat-to-beat fluctuations over seconds to minutes, whereas the change here was in the night-to-night variation of a wearable-estimated sleep stage, so the parallel is one of form rather than of mechanism. Because Fitbit estimates sleep stages partly from heart rate and movement, a change in nocturnal heart-rate dynamics could contribute to the staging output; adjusting for nightly heart rate (its mean and night-to-night variability), wear and device did not change the result, and further heart-rate analyses are the subject of ongoing work.

In the same data source, the percentage of REM sleep fell before incident heart failure 14 . Before MI, in contrast, the mean REM fraction did not change, whereas its night-tonight variability did.

These changes match the prodromal literature in timing: fatigue and sleep disturbance are reported weeks before MI 5, 6 , and we found within-person changes in sleep architecture over a similar window, 2-4 weeks before the event, in cases with and without recorded symptoms. They were detectable with simple, surface-level measures -the SD and RMSSD of the REM fraction -whereas more complex dynamical measures and day-level classifiers added little. Wearables thus complement long-term risk profiling from habitual sleep 8 with short-term, within-person change.

REM sleep is accompanied by surges of sympathetic activity 28 that may contribute to nocturnal cardiovascular events, so changes in REM regulation are plausibly linked to cardiovascular instability. One interpretation of the decrease at days 30-16 is a period of steadier sleep preceding the event; in the exploratory analyses the wake fraction of time in bed also tended to become more persistent from night to night over the same window (δ +0.17, p=0.094), although this was one of many exploratory contrasts. Plaque instability frequently precedes occlusive coronary thrombosis by days to weeks 29 , but how an unstable plaque could be followed by such a sequence is not known, and the following are hypotheses only. Our main hypothesis is that inflammation in the plaque and the circulation shifts autonomic balance, including through the vagal antiinflammatory reflex, and that cytokines act on sleep regulation; an alternative is that silent ischaemia or microembolism during plaque instability alters afferent input from the heart to brainstem networks that govern arousal and transitions between REM and non-REM sleep. Both need direct testing.

The change was detectable at the level of the individual with simple measures scored against the person's own baseline (AUCs of about 0.6-0.7 from a single feature). These AUCs are probably optimistic: the best rule used RMSSD, whose decrease was attenuated in held-out halves of the matched sets, whereas the decrease in SD was preserved. Whether a person-specific rule of this kind can contribute to wearable-based early warning needs larger cohorts and prospective studies.

Limitations

The full matched cohort was small (45 cases). Requiring at least 180 valid nights in the year before MI excluded most of the 692 eligible cases, so participants with sparse wearable use are not represented, and cases wore the device more consistently than controls at baseline (78.2% vs 66.7% valid nights), which may be related to health status. MI and comorbidities were identified from EHR codes; sleep apnea and heart failure are likely under-recorded, and the index date is the date of the first code rather than symptom onset, although moving it to the start of the acute visit or to the first angina code did not change the results. Fitbit sleep staging agrees only moderately with polysomnography 10 , the proprietary staging algorithm may have changed over the study period and, because staging uses heart rate and movement, our findings concern wearable-estimated rather than polysomnographic REM sleep. Cases had somewhat fewer valid nights than controls in the final 15 days, although not significantly; windows were compared with equal numbers of nights, but data missing before the event may not be missing at random. The endpoint windows were chosen after an exploratory analysis of these data, refining a decrease seen in an earlier, overlapping data release, although randomly placed index dates did not reproduce the finding even after allowing for the choice of window; the timing of days 30-16 is therefore exploratory and is to be confirmed, with the window fixed in advance, in new cases of a later All of Us data release. Secondary and exploratory analyses were not adjusted for multiple testing. There was no external validation cohort, and Apple Watch data in All of Us included too few MI cases with sleep data for validation. The results come from All of Us participants who shared Fitbit data and wore the device on most nights; they were older, mostly White and often obese, and other devices and populations were not studied. Under the All of Us dissemination policy, sensitivity and several descriptive characteristics could not be reported. A small number of cases had a separate acute illness recorded before the MI code and may have had the MI during a hospital stay. Comparisons with ischaemic stroke and heart-failure admission were underpowered.

Conclusions

In adults who use consumer wearables, a first MI was preceded by a transient change in night-to-night REM-sleep variability, a decrease at days 30-16 and a return over the following two weeks. The decrease was not seen at the same time before other acute admissions, did not depend on recorded symptoms or treatment, did not appear to be confined to people with diabetes or hypertension and was not explained by nightly heart rate, wear or device.
