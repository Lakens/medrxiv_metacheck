Show details

Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 2 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Language production provides a window into cognitive development, social cognition, and mental health. To translate this potential into clinical practice, we developed a scalable speech-based platform integrating graph-theoretical, psycholinguistic, and emotional features from brief spontaneous narratives to support developmental monitoring, mental wellness screening, and clinical decision-making. Normative developmental trajectories were derived from 2149 participants spanning 0-23 years of formal education. Narratives elicited using positively valenced pictures were transformed into word graphs to derive two composite indices: Narrative Complexity (NC), reflecting structural language organization, and Emotional Narrative Complexity (ENC), integrating narrative structure and emotional content. Both indices were modeled using asymptotic functions calibrated by schooling and converted into percentile-based normative curves. NC (R² = 0.29, τ = 7.77) and ENC (R² = 0.31, τ = 6.97) increased with schooling, approaching saturation around middle school. These trajectories provide educationally calibrated references for evaluating linguistic and socioemotional development. Clinical utility was investigated in a transdiagnostic sample of 120 participants, comprising healthy controls (n = 38), mood disorders (n = 28), affective psychosis (n = 36), and non-affective psychosis (n = 18). Although ENC alone did not discriminate between symptom-risk or diagnostic groups, classifiers combining its constituent variables with education and sex achieved AUCs of 0.726 for symptom risk and 0.800 for diagnosis, increasing to 0.774 and 0.858, respectively, with additional linguistic features. Machine-learning models integrating graph-derived, psycholinguistic, and emotional features further estimated symptom dimensions assessed by PANSS, GAD-7, and PHQ-9, achieving AUCs of 0.74-0.92 across severity thresholds while maintaining sensitivity ≥ 0.80. INTRODUCTION

Human language is one of the most complex forms of human behavior and provides a privileged window into cognitive, emotional, and social functioning. The way individuals use language reflects aspects of their thought organization, emotional states, and social behavior, providing a rich behavioral signal for studying human cognition and behavior (Mihalcea et al., 2024; Mota, Weissheimer, et al., 2023). Narrative production engages multiple cognitive processes involved in the expression of experiences, including lexical retrieval, working memory, executive control, temporal organization, and social and emotional processing (B. L. C. Malcorra et al., 2022; Mota, Weissheimer, et al., 2023).

Although oral language emerges naturally during development, the ability to produce coherent, hierarchically organized, and emotionally nuanced narratives is shaped by both developmental and environmental factors, including formal education and literacy experience (Mota, Ribeiro, et al., 2023; Mota, Weissheimer, et al., 2023). Schooling provides sustained exposure to increasingly complex linguistic structures, literacy practices, abstract concepts, and social interactions, which may contribute to the progressive refinement of communicative and narrative skills. Accordingly, narrative organization evolves across educational attainment and reflects the maturation of cognitive abilities as well as the acquisition and refinement of social-cognitive competencies involved in understanding, interpreting, and communicating experiences effectively (Gardner-Neblett, 2024; Grau-Husarikova et al., 2024; B. L. C. Malcorra et al., 2022; Mota et al., 2018 Mota et al., , 2025;; Mota, Weissheimer, et al., 2023; Suggate et al., 2018).

Emotional information constitutes a fundamental component of this developmental process. Beyond describing events, individuals must attribute emotional meaning to experiences, recognize affective states, and communicate them coherently and in a contextually appropriate manner. These abilities are closely related to social cognition and are essential for successful interpersonal functioning. Consequently, integrating emotional content into narratives may provide important insights into both cognitive development and emotional well-being (Grau-Husarikova et al., 2024; Mota, Ribeiro, et al., 2023). Understanding how emotional narrative abilities evolve across schooling is, therefore, critical for distinguishing typical developmental variation from potential indicators of psychological distress.

The developmental trajectory of language and social cognition unfolds within broader social and epidemiological contexts that also shape mental health. Social disadvantage, including poverty, educational deprivation, exposure to violence, and other forms of adversity, can constrain developmental opportunities and is associated with increased vulnerability to mental health problems across the life course (Lund et al., 2018; Marmot et al., 2012; Patel et al., 2008). Mental health conditions are among the leading causes of disability worldwide, and epidemiological studies estimate that around 30% of children and adolescents experience clinically relevant psychological difficulties (McGorry et al., 2024). These challenges highlight the need for scalable approaches that can monitor cognitive and emotional development across large populations while supporting early identification of individuals at risk.

Language-based assessment represents a promising strategy to address this need. Recent advances in computational psychiatry and natural language processing have demonstrated that spontaneous speech contains measurable markers of cognitive organization, emotional functioning, and psychopathology (Malgaroli et al., 2023; Palaniyappan, 2021). Automated analysis of speech has been successfully used to identify thought disorder, predict psychosis onset, characterize mood disorders, and monitor cognitive decline (Bedi et al., 2015; B. L. C. Malcorra et al., 2021; Morgan et al., 2021; Mota et al., 2012 Mota et al., , 2014 Mota et al., , 2017 Mota et al., , 2025;; Mota, Ribeiro, et al., 2023). Because speech can be collected quickly, remotely, and non-invasively, it represents an attractive candidate for large-scale screening and longitudinal monitoring. Among computational approaches, graph-theoretical analysis of speech has emerged as one of the most robust and interpretable methods for quantifying thought organization (Mota et al., 2012 (Mota et al., , 2014 (Mota et al., , 2017 (Mota et al., , 2025;; Mota, Ribeiro, et al., 2023). In this framework, words are represented as nodes and their sequential relationships as edges, generating mathematical representations of discourse structure (Mota et al., 2014; Mota, Weissheimer, et al., 2023). Graph attributes quantify dimensions such as lexical diversity, recurrence patterns, connectivity, and organizational complexity. Unlike semantic approaches that depend heavily on language-specific corpora and high-dimensional embeddings, graph-based methods capture structural properties of discourse that are largely independent of content, facilitating scalability and cross-cultural applicability (Mota, Weissheimer, et al., 2023). Over the past decade, graph measures of speech connectedness have consistently demonstrated sensitivity to psychosis, mood disorders, neurodevelopmental conditions, and cognitive impairment (Mota, Weissheimer, et al., 2023).

Despite these advances, an important challenge remains: language-based markers require developmental context for meaningful interpretation, as linguistic differences associated with educational experience may otherwise be confounded with psychopathology. Establishing normative developmental trajectories can therefore help distinguish expected variation from clinically relevant deviations. At the same time, the increasing use of complex machine-learning models in mental health raises concerns about clinical interpretability, highlighting the value of language-based markers that combine predictive performance with theoretically meaningful and clinically understandable features (Joyce et al., 2023).

The Mobile Brain framework was developed to address these complementary challenges. Building upon pioneering work on speech connectedness and graph-theoretical analysis of language (B. Malcorra et al., 2024; Mota et al., 2012 Mota et al., , 2017 Mota et al., , 2018 Mota et al., , 2025;; Mota, Ribeiro, et al., 2023), the framework combines graph-derived measures of discourse organization with psycholinguistic and emotional features extracted from brief spontaneous narratives. Rather than relying exclusively on black-box predictions, Mobile Brain generates interpretable indicators linked to meaningful dimensions of cognitive development, social cognition, emotional functioning, and mental health. The framework is designed to support a continuum of applications ranging from developmental monitoring and population-level screening to symptom-oriented clinical assessment.

The present study evaluates three complementary levels of language-based assessment. First, we characterize normative developmental trajectories of structural aspects of narrative and emotional expression across formal education using a large Brazilian sample spanning the full range of schooling, from individuals without formal education to highly educated adults. To achieve this objective, we introduce two composite language-based indices calibrated by years of formal education. The Narrative Complexity (NC) index captures structural aspects of language organization derived from graph attributes associated with lexical diversity, graph size, and short-and long-range recurrence patterns. The Emotional Narrative Complexity (ENC) index extends this framework by incorporating emotional expression and reflecting the integration of affective content into narrative organization. Our hypothesis is that both indexes develop according to formal education in an asymptotic trajectory, and must mature at around high school level, as observed in (Mota et al., 2018). Second, we investigate the utility of ENC as a scalable indicator of emotional well-being and mental health screening in a clinical and transdiagnostic population. We hypothesize that levels below the 25 percentile in the normative curve of ENC would be able to predict case-control participants in the clinical sample with sensibility higher than 80%. Third, we develop and validate machine-learning models that estimate symptom severity from spontaneous speech using clinically interpretable linguistic markers. The main hypothesis is that a specific combination of language markers would predict the risk of psychotic, depressive and anxious symptoms measured by psychometric scales. Together, these analyses establish a unified framework spanning developmental assessment, mental wellness screening, and symptom-informed clinical decision support, providing a practical pathway from large-scale monitoring to individualized mental health evaluation via a brief, scalable speech-elicitation protocol.

METHODS

This study consisted of two cohorts: one spontaneously collected by Mobile Brain users (study approved by the ethics committee -CAAE nº 91594425.8.0000.5282 with de-identified data), hereby referred as the large-scale cohort, with a broad educational attainment and age range, and the other is a clinical sample from mental health specialized centers (study approved by the ethics committee -CAAE nº 86286024.5.1001.5263 with participant-consented personal data), hereby referred as the symptoms cohort. The collection, processing and analysis of these datasets were done as described in the following sections.

1. Large-scale and symptom datasets description

The large-scale dataset comprised 2149 participants, spanning a broad developmental spectrum from early childhood to adulthood. The participants used Mobile Brain Platform as consumers of educational or mental health screening products by Mobile Brain from different projects around the country (Brazil). Participants had a mean educational attainment of 6.40 years of formal schooling (SD = 4.78; range = 0-23 years), including individuals with no formal education and those with advanced educational backgrounds. Regarding biological sex, 669 participants (31.1%) were classified as female, 719 (33.5%) as male, and 761 (35.4%) had no sex information available. Among participants with identified sex (n = 1388), 48.2% were female, and 51.8% were male. Mean years of education were comparable between female (mean = 6.95, SD = 5.44) and male participants (mean = 7.00, SD = 5.29).

The second dataset (symptoms dataset) consists of individual-level observations, incorporating spontaneous speech samples alongside demographic and clinical parameters. The sample includes 130 data points derived from 120 unique individuals (58 females and 62 males). All participants were clinically evaluated by a mental health specialist, and diagnostic criteria from DSM V were used to categorized groups as follows: healthy controls (n = 38), individuals with mood or anxiety disorders (n = 28), affective psychosis like bipolar disorders (n = 36), and non-affective psychosis like schizophrenia (n = 18). Their ages ranged from 18 to 79 years (mean 41.5 years), and their educational attainment ranged from 2 to 22 years of formal schooling (mean 12.7 years).

2. Speech elicitation protocol and text preprocessing

Speech samples were collected using the Happy Thoughts protocol (Mota, Ribeiro, et al., 2023). The protocol consists of the sequential presentation of three positively valenced images. For each stimulus, participants were instructed to observe the image for 15 seconds. After the image disappeared from the screen, they were asked to tell a story about what they had seen. This procedure was repeated for all three images within the same assessment session. The stimuli depicted three thematic categories (babies, dogs, and desserts) selected from the International Affective Picture System (IAPS) (Lang et al., 2020) due to their positive affective valence. To minimize order effects, the image presentation sequence was randomized across sessions. Beyond the Happy Thoughts protocol, symptom participants afterward answered psychometric scales GAD-7 and PHQ-9, followed by an application of the PANSS psychometric scale by a trained mental health specialist (exclusively for the second sample of participants).

Audio recordings were de-identified prior to analysis and automatically transcribed using the Whisper large language model developed by OpenAI (Radford et al., 2022). The transcriptions of the three narratives elicited during a session were concatenated into a single text, which served as input for subsequent computational analyses. This procedure allowed the extraction of multiple linguistic indicators from a standardized speech sample while preserving the ecological validity of spontaneous narrative production. The Happy Thoughts protocol, as well as the mental health questionnaires, de-identification and preprocessing of recordings were implemented and run end-to-end automatically through a web-based application developed by Mobile Brain S.A.

3. Speech markers

Narratives were converted into directed word graphs in which each unique word represented a node and the temporal succession between words was represented by edges. From these graphs, structural attributes related to lexical diversity, short-range recurrences, long-range recurrences, and graph size were computed.

Graph-theoretical analyses were performed using a proprietary algorithm designed to generate word recurrence graphs. In these directed graphs, nodes represent unique words, and edges represent the temporal sequence connecting consecutive words throughout the narrative. Because graph measures are sensitive to differences in text length and participants naturally vary in speech verbosity, graph attributes were calculated using a moving-window approach. Specifically, graphs were constructed from windows containing 30 words, with steps of 15 words, guaranteeing 50% of overlap between consecutive graphs. Final graph metrics were obtained by averaging the measures across all windows within each narrative sample. The complete list of graph attributes is provided in Table 1.

4. Statistical modeling of educational attainment and psychiatric symptoms a. Large-scale sample and construction of developmental curves Based on previous studies demonstrating their association with cognitive maturation and formal education (Mota et al., 2018; Pinheiro et al., 2020), the graph-derived measures were combined in a linear regression, defined as Narrative Complexity (NC) index, calibrated by years of formal education. Higher NC values indicate increasingly organized and developmentally mature narrative structures.

To account for the affective dimension of discourse, emotional valence information derived from the narratives was incorporated into the structural model, yielding the Emotional Narrative Complexity (ENC) index. It calculated the proportion of positive and negative emotional words used in the narrative. As the narrative were based on positive affective pictures, the proportion of positive emotional words were considered as a congruent emotional expression, and the proportion of negative emotional words were considered as an incongruent emotional expression, revealing negative bias. Both proportions were combined with narrative graph attributes in a linear regression calibrated by years of formal education.

To establish normative developmental trajectories, NC and ENC scores were modeled as a function of years of formal education using an asymptotic exponential growth function: where (f₀) represents the initial value of the index, (f∞) represents the asymptotic maximum value reached at developmental saturation, (t) corresponds to years of formal education, and (τ) is the characteristic time constant of the trajectory.

The parameter (τ) quantifies the educational pace of development, indicating how rapidly narrative abilities approach their asymptotic level as schooling increases. This function is the solution of the first-order differential equation: df/dt = (1/τ)(f∞ -f), subject to the initial condition (f(0) = f₀). This modeling approach was selected based on the ontogenetic assumption that cognitive development follows nonlinear growth dynamics characterized by periods of rapid change followed by progressive stabilization (Mota et al., 2018; Pinheiro et al., 2020). Model parameters were estimated from the normative sample, yielding fitted developmental curves that represent the expected maturation of narrative complexity and emotional narrative organization across educational levels. The fitted functions were subsequently used to generate percentile-based normative curves, allowing individual scores to be positioned relative to the expected developmental trajectory for a given level of schooling.

Finally, percentile bands were derived from the fitted models to create normative developmental curves for both NC and ENC. These percentile curves function analogously to pediatric growth charts, allowing individual performance to be compared against expected values for a given educational stage. The resulting reference framework enables the identification of individuals presenting narrative development below, within, or above the expected range for their level of schooling. It supports the use of these indices as large-scale screening tools in typically developing populations.

Having established the normative developmental trajectories of narrative and emotional complexity across formal education, the subsequent objective was to evaluate the clinical utility of these linguistic markers. To determine whether speech-derived metrics can identify deviations associated with psychological distress, we applied the Mobile Brain framework to a transdiagnostic psychiatric cohort. The following analyses investigate the efficacy of these structural and emotional speech features in screening for general mental wellness, distinguishing among diagnostic groups, and mapping specific symptom-severity dimensions, including anxiety, depression, and psychosis, onto continuous language parameters. b. Assessing the triage performance of the Emotional Narrative Complexity index Leveraging the clinical dataset, we evaluated the discriminative capacity of the Emotional Narrative Complexity index to effectively stratify participants into distinct clinical categories. This was done using a case-control framework to distinguish healthy controls from individuals with any psychiatric diagnosis present within the cohort. To ensure statistical equivalence in comparative analyses, the clinical subgroup underwent a random downsampling procedure, repeated 10 times, to match the control cohort's dimensionality. The final predictive performance of the linguistic indicator was subsequently determined by calculating the mean classification accuracy across these 10 balanced iterations. We also tested a risk of symptoms outcome: the risk group is defined by a participant with a score above any of the clinical scale cutoffs: 10 points for PHQ-9, 10 points for GAD-7, 14 points for PANSS positive, 14 points for PANSS negative or 60 points for PANSS total.

c. Symptom-level modeling of psychiatric scales At the symptom level, the discriminative power of individual language markers was first assessed using Spearman's rank correlation analyses between speech-derived metrics and standardized clinical scores, with Benjamini-Hochberg corrections for multiple comparisons. Because the PANSS was primarily designed to monitor symptom severity in clinical populations rather than as a primary screening instrument, we established triage benchmarks based on literature-derived criteria for clinical remission and psychological well-being (Lasser et al., 2007; Leucht et al., 2005). To evaluate the predictive utility of language-based markers, we developed a classification framework to identify symptom-severity dimensions with high sensitivity. We implemented a correlation-based network analysis using the qgraph framework in R (Epskamp et al., 2012) to reduce dimensionality. Initially, Pearson correlation coefficients were computed across a set of 37 structural, lexical, and syntactic features. These variables were then visualized using a force-directed layout based on the Fruchterman-Reingold algorithm, enabling topological inspection of feature clusters and identification of redundant metrics. Based on these observed aggregations, we derived composite indices by linearly combining correlated variables. The formulas for these integrated indicators are detailed in Table 5.

Using these integrated indices, we implemented a supervised machine-learning architecture designed for clinical screening applications. Initially, continuous variables were standardized as z-scores to ensure uniform scaling across diverse linguistic indicators. Predictive modeling was conducted through Bayesian logistic regression via the rstanarm framework, targeting the binary classification of symptom severity (Normal vs Risk) across all clinical outcomes and diagnostic thresholds. To mitigate overfitting and facilitate feature selection, we employed a Bayesian LASSO prior for coefficient estimation. The modeling strategy included interaction terms between composite speech indices and biological sex, while adjusting for potential confounders, including years of formal education. To enhance the framework's effectiveness as a screening instrument, decision boundaries were determined using the pROC package and Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) analysis. We adopted a constrained optimization procedure that prioritized diagnostic sensitivity over standard default parameters; specifically, thresholds were calibrated to maximize specificity while maintaining a mandatory sensitivity floor of 80% (Bujang, 2026). The model's performance was quantified using Area Under the Curve (AUC), optimized sensitivity and specificity metrics, and confusion matrix distributions for each psychiatric dimension.

RESULTS

The resulting Narrative Complexity (NC) index was positively associated with years of formal education (Pearson-r = 0.516, R² = 0.266, p << 0.001), indicating progressively more organized and developmentally mature narrative structures with increasing educational attainment. Incorporating emotional valence into the structural model yielded the Emotional Narrative Complexity (ENC) index, which showed a similar positive association with years of formal education (Pearson-r = 0.530, R² = 0.281, p << 0.001). Given the positive valence of the eliciting stimuli, positive emotional language represented congruent emotional expression, whereas negative emotional language represented incongruent expression and negative affective bias. Together, these findings indicate that both structural and affective dimensions of spontaneous narratives vary systematically with educational attainment, supporting the subsequent modeling of NC and ENC normative developmental trajectories.

1. Asymptotic Growth of Narrative and Emotional Narrative Complexity as a Function of Educational Attainment

Narrative Complexity (NC) and Emotional Narrative Complexity (ENC) increased with educational attainment, consistent with the expected asymptotic developmental pattern described by the fitted exponential growth model. In both cases, rapid gains were observed during the early years of schooling, followed by a progressive deceleration toward a plateau in later educational stages.

For Narrative Complexity, the asymptotic model provided a significant fit to the data (R² = 0.29, MSE = 4.33). The estimated initial value was f₀ = 3.93, corresponding to the beginning of formal literacy acquisition (approximately 4 years of schooling), while the asymptotic value reached f∞ = 9.04. The characteristic time parameter was τ = 7.77 years of schooling, indicating that most developmental gains occurred by approximately the eighth year of education, corresponding to the final years of Brazilian middle school. After this point, increases in Narrative Complexity became progressively smaller, suggesting a saturation process in the maturation of discourse organization.

A similar developmental trajectory was observed for Emotional Narrative Complexity.

The fitted model explained a comparable proportion of the variance (R² = 0.31, MSE = 4.45), with an estimated initial value of f₀ = 3.69 and an asymptotic value of f∞ = 8.94. The characteristic time parameter (τ = 6.97 years of schooling) indicated a slightly faster developmental trajectory than that observed for Narrative Complexity, with emotional narrative organization approaching its asymptotic range around the seventh year of schooling (also during middle school). As observed for NC, the growth rate decreased substantially after the elementary school years and approached a stable plateau during later educational stages. Based on these fitted trajectories, percentile-based normative curves were generated for both measures across the entire educational range represented in the sample. These curves provide educationally calibrated reference values that allow individual performance to be positioned relative to a large normative population spanning from individuals without formal schooling to highly educated adults. Consequently, NC and ENC can be interpreted as developmental indicators, analogous to growth charts used in physical health, enabling the identification of individuals performing below, within, or above the expected range for their level of educational attainment.

Overall, the results indicate that both structural and emotional dimensions of narrative production exhibit systematic developmental growth across schooling, with the most pronounced gains occurring during the years immediately following literacy acquisition and approaching asymptotic stabilization by the end of elementary education. These findings support the use of NC and ENC as normative markers of cognitive and emotional development in typical populations.

2. Predictive power of ENC as screening tool for mental wellbeing

Having established this normative developmental baseline, we sought to determine if deviations in the Emotional Narrative Complexity (ENC) index could serve as an initial marker for psychological distress. Neither the ENC index, nor its percentile values based on the development curve proved to be a viable indicator for general clinical assessment, either for a case-control framework or as a risk of symptoms (Table 4). The low performance observed in this scenario indicates that despite being an easy-to-implement, simple radar for mental wellness, this framework is limited to assess the normative development of speech, and other complimentary tools are needed to understand the linguistic features of mental suffering.

3. Individual speech variables are distinct between symptom severity levels

To achieve symptom-level specificity, we first evaluated the linear and monotonic relationships between continuous speech-derived metrics and standardized clinical scores using Spearman correlations (Figure 3). Some cross-diagnostic patterns emerged across multiple dimensions.

Greater anxiety symptom severity (GAD-7) was associated with lower frequencies of nouns, proper nouns, and adpositions, shorter words, and a higher frequency of pronouns. A similar pattern was observed for depressive symptom severity (PHQ-9), with the additional finding that greater severity was associated with more frequent punctuation (Figure 3). Psychotic symptoms showed a broader pattern of linguistic associations. Greater overall psychotic symptom severity (PANSS total score), as well as greater severity across all three PANSS subscales, was associated with fewer words per sentence, lower noun and adposition frequencies, smaller largest connected components (LCCs) in the word recurrence graphs, more frequent punctuation, and greater use of the present tense (Figure 3). Despite these shared patterns, symptom-specific associations were also observed. Greater severity of negative symptoms was uniquely associated with a lower frequency of second-person pronouns and smaller LSC and ASP values (Figure 3). In contrast, greater severity of positive and general symptoms was associated with higher pronoun frequencies, particularly first-person pronouns, as well as shorter words and lower determiner frequencies (Figure 3). These findings suggest that individual speech variables do not merely drift uniformly under generalized stress, but rather break down into granular, distinct structural patterns that are highly specific to individual psychiatric symptom dimensions, and that this can be used to differentiate mental suffering into symptom categories.

5. Stability of model performance and shift in feature importance across severity thresholds

An important finding of our predictive architecture is the decoupling of overall classification performance from individual feature dynamics. While the classifier maintains a remarkably stable discriminative capacity across a continuous spectrum of clinical cutoffs, the underlying linguistic markers driving those predictions shift dynamically with symptom severity and biological sex. As illustrated in the continuous threshold analyses, the model's Area Under the Curve (AUC) remains resiliently bound within highly accurate tracking bands. For instance, across the entire operational range of the GAD-7 and PHQ-9, the AUC consistently hovers between 0.74 and 0.85, with no critical drop-offs even at the extreme boundaries (Supplementary Figures 1 2 3 4 5). This macro-level stability is similarly mirrored in psychotic symptom tracking, where the PANSS positive subscale maintains a steady baseline AUC of roughly 0.73 to 0.80, and the PANSS total curve remains bounded within a high-performance plateau of 0.75 to 0.92.

However, inspecting the internal parameter assignments reveals that the lasso-regularized feature importance is highly sensitive to symptom severity, with distinct linguistic indicators taking precedence at different stages of clinical progression (Supplementary Figures 6 7 8 9 10). In affective screening models, low-severity thresholds are heavily driven by temporal orientation indices. For the GAD-7 at 5 points, idx_time_present acts as the dominant positive predictor, particularly for male participants, whereas at the severe 15-point cutoff, this metric diminishes significantly, and affective load indicators like idx_negative, with positive correlation, and idx_positive, marked by an inverse, negative correlation, emerge as the primary drivers of classification. A parallel severity-dependent flip is observable in the PHQ-9 models, where conversational focus indicators, idx_on_topic and idx_2nd_singular, dominate early screening, but give way to a rise in the importance of negative emotion markers and a drop in first-person plural usage as symptoms intensify to the 15-point threshold.

This rearrangement of feature importance is even more pronounced within psychotic symptom strata, where severe symptom manifestations change the model's linguistic tracking profile. At low-severity cutoffs (e.g., PANSS Positive at 10 points or PANSS Negative at 10 points), classification relies on localized egocentric speech metrics, marked by high positive values for idx_self_focus. However, as psychotic severity escalates to the highest thresholds of 21 points for both scales, these early markers drop out of the top rankings. For severe positive symptoms (PANSS+ 21 points), the model pivots toward conversational markers, shifting idx_2nd_singular to a positive predictor and idx_3rd_focus to a negative predictor. For severe negative symptoms (PANSS-21 points), idx_negative increases exponentially, dominating the model's feature space and registering a scaled importance near 0.75. Collectively, these results demonstrate that while the machine learning framework serves as a reliable, stable triage instrument across all continuous cutoffs, keeping its AUC above 0.7 in any scale cutoff with representativity above 8% in the study population, and it does so by mathematically adapting to a shifting linguistic phenotyping architecture that evolves with clinical severity.

DISCUSSION

The present study introduces a comprehensive framework for assessing cognitive development, socioemotional functioning, and mental health through the analysis of spontaneous speech. By combining graph-theoretical measures of discourse organization with psycholinguistic and emotional features, we demonstrate that language can be quantified along developmental trajectories that reflect expected educational maturation and subsequently leveraged for mental health screening and symptom-informed clinical assessment. Rather than proposing isolated language markers, our findings support a hierarchical pipeline in which language serves as a digital marker across the continuum from developmental monitoring to preventive screening and clinical decision support.

Our first objective was to establish normative developmental references for language organization across formal education. Both Narrative Complexity (NC) and Emotional Narrative Complexity (ENC) exhibited nonlinear growth that was accurately described by asymptotic exponential functions. This replicates in large scale what was demonstrated in Mota et al. (2018) in a population of 135 typical participants. Moreover, it demonstrates that emotional content also develops with educational attainment, a correlation also described in a first-episode psychosis population (Mota, Ribeiro, et al., 2023). This finding supports the hypothesis that language organization develops through rapid gains during early schooling followed by gradual stabilization, consistent with general models of cognitive development and educational learning.

The estimated characteristic times (τ = 7.77 years for NC and τ = 6.97 years for ENC) indicate that narrative abilities approach their mature organization during the final years of Brazilian elementary education. The asymptotic behavior suggests that schooling progressively refines linguistic organization until reaching a relatively stable level, reflecting the cumulative acquisition of literacy, syntactic complexity, discourse planning, and abstract reasoning. Importantly, these parameters should not be interpreted as developmental endpoints, but rather as indicators of the educational pace through which increasingly sophisticated narrative structures emerge.

These findings reinforce the view that language organization is not solely determined by biological maturation. Instead, formal education appears to play a central role in shaping the cognitive mechanisms required for producing coherent, hierarchically organized narratives. Exposure to written language, increasingly complex texts, classroom discourse, and metacognitive activities likely contributes to the progressive refinement captured by NC and ENC.

Although both indices followed similar developmental trajectories, they represent distinct aspects of language production. Narrative Complexity primarily captures the structural organization of discourse. By integrating graph attributes related to lexical diversity, graph size, and long-and short-range recurrences, NC reflects the ability to organize ideas coherently over the course of a narrative. Rather than measuring vocabulary size or verbal fluency alone, the index quantifies an emergent property of discourse organization that reflects planning, working memory, and logical integration of information (Mota et al., 2016 (Mota et al., , 2018 (Mota et al., , 2020;; Mota, Weissheimer, et al., 2023).

Emotional Narrative Complexity extends this structural representation by incorporating emotional valence information. These results replicate in a Brazilian large-scale population the association between emotional expression and schooling already mapped in other cultural contexts (Yuan et al., 2024). Consequently, ENC captures the degree to which affective information is coherently integrated into narrative production. This additional emotional dimension likely reflects higher-order socioemotional processes, including emotional regulation, autobiographical integration, and social cognition.

A major contribution of this work is the establishment of percentile-based normative curves calibrated by educational attainment. Because language performance is strongly influenced by schooling, interpreting raw speech measures without accounting for educational level may lead to substantial diagnostic bias. Lower narrative complexity in individuals with limited educational exposure may simply reflect expected developmental variation rather than pathology, whereas highly educated individuals may compensate for subtle cognitive impairment through preserved linguistic abilities.

The normative curves proposed here address this limitation by providing developmental expectations analogous to pediatric growth charts. Just as anthropometric curves allow clinicians to determine whether physical growth follows expected developmental trajectories, NC and ENC percentiles enable the assessment of whether language organization and socioemotional narrative development are consistent with an individual's educational history. This normative approach substantially increases the interpretability and potential scalability of language-based biomarkers. Instead of relying on absolute values, clinicians and researchers can interpret each individual's performance relative to an expected developmental distribution, facilitating early identification of atypical developmental trajectories.

ENC fails to discriminate between individuals with preserved mental wellness from those presenting clinical symptoms. Although the observed AUC values were low, the consistently high sensitivity suggests that ENC is particularly suitable as a first-line screening tool for mental wellness, identifying atypical developmental trajectories.

From a public health perspective, screening instruments prioritize sensitivity over specificity to minimize missed cases requiring further evaluation. The relatively modest specificity observed in the present study indicates that ENC should not be interpreted as a standalone diagnostic instrument. Rather, it serves as an efficient population-level radar system that identifies individuals who may benefit from a more comprehensive clinical assessment.

This interpretation aligns with the conceptual purpose of the framework. Rather than replacing standardized psychiatric evaluation, ENC provides an objective, scalable, and low-burden method for identifying individuals whose emotional narrative organization deviates from expected developmental patterns. Such an approach may prove particularly valuable in educational systems, primary care, and occupational health settings, where rapid large-scale monitoring is often necessary, but specialist availability remains limited.

The second major contribution of this study is the demonstration that spontaneous speech contains clinically meaningful information that extends beyond general screening. Machine-learning models that combine graph-derived structural measures with psycholinguistic and emotional features achieved AUC values ranging from 0.74 to 0.92 across multiple psychometric scales and symptom-severity thresholds, while maintaining high sensitivity.

Importantly, these models estimate symptom dimensions rather than categorical diagnoses. This distinction is consistent with contemporary dimensional approaches to psychopathology, in which psychiatric symptoms exist along continuous spectra rather than discrete diagnostic boundaries (Conway et al., 2022; Cuthbert & Insel, 2013; Kotov et al., 2017). Depressive and anxiety syndromes appear to share overlapping alterations in speech content, broadly characterized by reduced verbal informativeness and shorter words. Depressive syndromes may additionally be associated with more frequent punctuation, more straightforward sentence constructions, reduced linguistic complexity, and potentially longer or more frequent pauses. In contrast, psychotic syndromes appear to affect both the structure and content of discourse more substantially. These alterations include reduced lexical richness and linguistic complexity, lower verbal informativeness, shorter sentences, more frequent punctuation-suggesting simpler syntactic structures and potentially more frequent pauses-and a greater use of the present tense.

Negative psychotic symptoms further reproduce the marked reduction in the connectivity and size of word recurrence graphs described by Mota, Weissheimer, et al. (2023) alongside a lower frequency of second-person pronouns, suggesting reduced linguistic orientation toward the interlocutor. Conversely, positive and general psychotic symptoms are associated with greater self-referentiality, reflected in more frequent use of first-person pronouns, as well as shorter words.

Taken together, these findings suggest that speech patterns may reflect the phenomenological similarities between depressive and anxiety syndromes, while psychotic syndromes are associated with more pronounced alterations in discourse organization and communicative structure. This distinction highlights the potential of linguistic features to capture both shared and syndrome-specific dimensions of psychopathology.

Furthermore, the observation that individual linguistic variables differed across symptom-severity levels suggests that distinct speech features may capture partially dissociable dimensions of psychopathology. Previous computational studies have shown that linguistic characteristics such as syntactic complexity, semantic coherence, referential cohesion, lexical choices, and pronoun use are associated with clinically relevant features of psychiatric disorders, particularly psychosis (Corcoran et al., 2020; Low et al., 2020; Morgan et al., 2021). These findings support the interpretation of structural organization, emotional content, temporal references, and self-referential language as complementary linguistic dimensions that may reflect different aspects of psychological functioning, although their correspondence to partially independent neurocognitive mechanisms remains to be established. Such interpretability represents a potential advantage of feature-based approaches over less interpretable black-box models, as it allows computational findings to be related to phenomenological constructs routinely assessed during psychiatric interviews (Malgaroli et al., 2023; Viduani et al., 2026).

Taken together, these findings support a unified framework that integrates developmental monitoring, preventive screening, and clinical assessment through spontaneous speech analysis. Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the normative curves were derived from cross-sectional rather than longitudinal data. Although the large sample provides robust estimates of developmental trajectories, longitudinal studies will be necessary to characterize individual developmental dynamics more precisely. Second, educational attainment was used as the primary developmental reference. While schooling represents one of the strongest determinants of language organization, additional variables-including literacy quality, socioeconomic status, bilingualism, and cultural background-may further influence narrative development and should be incorporated into future normative models.

Third, although the clinical sample included multiple psychiatric diagnoses, its size remains relatively modest for developing highly specialized diagnostic models. Independent multicenter validation involving larger and more diverse clinical populations will be essential to establish the generalizability of symptom prediction across healthcare settings. Finally, the moderate specificity observed for ENC indicates that the index should be interpreted as a screening measure, with its greatest value when integrated with complementary clinical assessments.

Future work should focus on longitudinal monitoring of individuals across educational and clinical settings, external validation in diverse linguistic populations, and integration with digital health infrastructures. Because the proposed framework relies on brief speech samples collected through low-cost protocols, it offers considerable potential for implementation in schools, primary healthcare services, telemedicine platforms, and population-based mental health surveillance programs.

This study demonstrates that spontaneous speech contains sufficient information to characterize normative cognitive and socioemotional development, identify deviations from typical trajectories associated with mental wellness, and estimate clinically relevant symptom dimensions. The establishment of percentile-based developmental curves for Narrative Complexity and Emotional Narrative Complexity provides interpretable normative references analogous to growth charts for language development. When combined with machine-learning models calibrated to standardized psychiatric scales, these language-based biomarkers constitute a unified framework spanning developmental monitoring, preventive mental health screening, and symptom-informed clinical decision support. Collectively, these findings establish spontaneous speech as a scalable, interpretable, and clinically meaningful digital biomarker with broad applications in education, public health, and mental healthcare.
