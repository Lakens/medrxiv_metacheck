Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

The exposure and health benefits of clean cooking depend on sustained, exclusive use, yet few studies have assessed fuel use after trial support ends. We conducted a prospective follow-up of the Household Air Pollution Intervention Network (HAPIN) trial cohort to assess cooking fuel use for 4 years post-trial in India, Guatemala, and Rwanda.

Methods: Intervention households in the HAPIN trial received a free liquefied petroleum gas (LPG) stove, fuel, and behavioral support for 18 months. At trial completion, control participants in Guatemala and Rwanda were offered a free LPG stove or alternative compensation, and in India, a free LPG stove and 18 months of free fuel. We surveyed 2,169 participants annually for four years on exclusive and any LPG use, examined post-trial use trajectories, and assessed factors associated with post-trial LPG use using logistic regression.

Results: Despite near-exclusive use during the trial, post-trial, few intervention participants used LPG exclusively (29% in India, <3% in Guatemala, near 0% in Rwanda). By year 4, 82% (India), 50% (Guatemala), and 10% (Rwanda) of intervention participants used any LPG. Sustained use was highest in India; non-use was most common in Rwanda; delayed use (starting years 3-4) was most common in Guatemala. Intervention assignment (aOR 1.8 [1.3-2.7], driven by Guatemala), socioeconomic status (up to aOR 40.9 [15.7-106.6] for Q4, relative to Q1), perceived ability to use LPG (aOR 3.9 [2.6-5.7]), household size (aOR 0.86 [0.79-0.93] per member), and country (aOR 5.4 [2.3-12.5] for India; aOR 1.9 [1.3-2.7] for Guatemala, relative to Rwanda) were significantly associated with post-trial LPG use. Only 27% of Guatemalan and 7% of Rwandan control participants chose an LPG stove (or electric, in Guatemala) as compensation, citing unaffordability and inaccessibility.

Conclusions: A time-limited intervention, though successful in promoting LPG preference and sustained non-exclusive use in India and Guatemala, is unlikely to sustain the exclusive clean fuel use needed for long-term environmental and health benefits without policies supporting affordability and accessibility.

Introduction

Household air pollution (HAP) remains a major public health challenge, particularly in low-and middle-income countries (LMICs). 1 More than two billion people still rely on solid fuels such as wood, charcoal, and dung for cooking. 2 HAP has far-reaching negative consequences, contributing to poor health outcomes, environmental degradation, lost economic and livelihood opportunities-particularly for women-and increased climate-warming emissions such as black carbon and carbon dioxide. 3, 4 The World Health Organization classifies liquefied petroleum gas (LPG) as a clean and scalable alternative to biomass for cooking. 5 Although the evidence on health benefits is mixed, 6, 7 exclusive or near-exclusive LPG use can dramatically reduce HAP emissions and exposures, and may yield significant time savings, welfare, environmental, climate, and other benefits. 8 Modeling indicates that near-exclusive use of LPG (i.e., using only LPG for all cooking needs) is necessary to achieve potential health benefits. 9 However, exclusive LPG use over the long-term remains uncommon in many contexts, due in part to limited accessibility and recurring fuel costs. 10 Rogers' Diffusion of Innovation Theory identifies five factors driving long-term technology adoption: relative advantage (perceived benefit over existing alternatives), compatibility (fit with current practices), trialability (opportunity for low-risk experimentation), observability (visibility of results), and complexity (ease of understanding and use). 11 Some evidence from clean cooking trials supports this framework. In Peru, a 12-month LPG intervention led to high levels of sustained, though not exclusive, LPG use in the year following the trial. 12 In Uganda, offering a free trial period with flexible payment options increased uptake of fuel-efficient cookstoves. 13 In Bangladesh, households were more likely to retain their LPG stove rather than exchange it for cash after a trial period. 14 These findings suggest that free trial periods can support long-term adoption by enabling households to experience LPG's advantages firsthand. However, rigorous, multi-year data are needed to assess long-term usage patterns and the role of household, social, market, and other contextual factors.

Our study, the Household Air Pollution Intervention Network (HAPIN) randomized controlled trial, addressed these gaps by providing an LPG stove and an 18-month supply of LPG and tracking participants for four years after the end of the trial. During the HAPIN trial, intervention participants almost completely abandoned their traditional stoves (biomass was used on 0.4% of monitored days); 96% of intervention participants reported exclusive LPG use on the prior day (n=984 of 1,029) compared to only 1% in the control group (n=9 of 1,058). Most traditional stove use in intervention households was due to running out of LPG, other household members using the traditional stove, or issues with LPG stove functionality, with very little due to cultural or social reasons. 15 In this paper, we quantify the impact of the intervention on long-term LPG use, compare post-trial LPG use between intervention and control groups, and identify factors associated with post-trial LPG use across three diverse settings in Latin America, Africa, and Asia.

Methods

Study population and sites

This study examined fuel use in participants enrolled in the HAPIN study, a multi-country randomized controlled trial designed to assess the health impacts of LPG fuel use during pregnancy and early childhood. Detailed descriptions of the study design have been published elsewhere. 16, 17 In brief, the trial enrolled approximately 800 pregnant women between 9 and 19 weeks of gestation in each of four rural research sites: Jalapa, Guatemala; Puno, Peru; Kayonza, Rwanda; and Tamil Nadu, India (stratified between Villupuram and Nagapattinam districts). Eligible participants primarily used biomass fuels for cooking at enrollment. Those randomized to the intervention group received a free LPG stove and fuel refills delivered as needed, regular behavioral support to encourage exclusive LPG use, and stove maintenance services as necessary. At the end of the trial, participants in the intervention group retained their LPG stove and one cylinder, but free LPG refills were discontinued. In India, all control group participants received an LPG stove along with 18 months of free fuel as needed after the trial concluded, although they did not receive any behavioral reinforcement for LPG use as was provided to intervention participants during the trial. In Rwanda and Guatemala, control participants were offered a choice between receiving an LPG stove and a small supply of fuel or an alternative form of compensation. 18

Trial sites

We conducted annual post-trial follow-up assessments in Guatemala, India, and Rwanda; Peru was not included for logistical and financial reasons. In Guatemala and Rwanda, national estimates indicate nearly universal reliance on biomass as the primary cooking fuel in rural areas (82.7% and 99%, respectively). 19 Guatemala has made progress expanding LPG infrastructure and awareness, but access remains challenging in rural areas, and no government programs currently support LPG financing. Rwanda's national energy strategy emphasizes LPG primarily for urban areas, resulting in limited infrastructure and availability in rural areas, and the country lacks government programs to support LPG affordability. In India, government subsidies and sustained promotion of LPG-through the ongoing Pradhan Mantri Ujjwala Yojana (PMUY), which expanded the LPG distribution network, provided digital subsidy transfers to women's bank accounts, and conducted extensive public awareness campaigns-have contributed to substantially lower rural reliance on polluting fuels (34.3%). 19 However, "stacking" (use of both clean and polluting fuels) is common even among those with access to affordable clean fuels; thus, primary fuel reliance does not mean exclusive use. 20

Follow-up data collection

Post-trial fuel use data were collected between March 2021 and May 2025. Stove and fuel use were assessed among both intervention and control participants using two survey-based methods. First, participants were asked to list all stove types used in their household over the last year and estimate the number of hours each stove was used per week. Second, participants completed a 24-hour recall survey indicating which stove(s) had been used during the previous day and for how many hours (see Table S1 for a comparison of data between these methods). Both surveys were administered at the trial baseline visit and then repeated at annual follow-up visits after completion of the trial, when the child was 24 (year 1), 36 (year 2), 48 (year 3), and 60 (year 4) months of age; assessments of exposure and health outcomes were also conducted annually. The 24-hour recall survey was also conducted at the trial endline visit, when the child was 12 months old. At the same visit, a separate survey was administered to assess participants' intentions for LPG use following the trial. This included questions on preferred and expected stove use, and LPG stove perceptions. Control participants' reasons for not selecting an LPG stove as compensation were collected in Rwanda.

Data analysis

Participant and household characteristics were summarized for all participants with data at one or more post-trial follow-up visits (years 1-4). Wealth status, educational attainment, and age were assessed at trial baseline; preference for and perceived ability to use LPG were from trial endline. Number of household members was averaged across follow-up years with available data; the baseline value was used if missing (n=30). Receipt of a clean cooking stove from the trial was determined using study administrative records.

Indicators of fuel use. We examined two indicators of fuel use: exclusive clean fuel use (LPG or electric stove only) and any clean fuel use (LPG or electric stove, alone or in combination with biomass). These indicators were compared across timepoints, groups, and countries as described below.

LPG use among intervention participants over the post-trial follow-up period. At each annual follow-up visit (years 1-4), participants self-reported which fuel(s) they used for cooking over the prior year. Trends over time were assessed by country and compared to baseline values through numerical and visual examination of the data.

Comparison of post-trial LPG use between intervention and control participants.

Analyses differed by country based on the type of compensation control participants received at trial completion. In Guatemala and Rwanda, control participants were offered a choice between an LPG stove and starter fuel package or an alternative form of compensation, including an electric stove in Guatemala; not all participants selected the LPG stove. In these two countries, we descriptively compared exclusive and any LPG use at each post-trial visit between intervention and control participants. In India, all control participants received an LPG stove and 18 months of free fuel starting at trial end, but without the behavioral support component of the intervention. To isolate the contribution of behavioral support to initial LPG adoption in India, we compared prior-day LPG use among control participants at year 1 to prior-day use among intervention participants at trial endline. Exclusive and any LPG use were then compared between intervention (years 1-4) and control participants (years 2-4) in India.

Proportion of cooking done with LPG by mixed-fuel users.

For each post-trial annual follow-up, we identified mixed fuel users who reported using clean fuel and biomass over the prior year. For these mixed users, we calculated the proportion of self-reported total cooking time spent using LPG by dividing hours of LPG cooking by total hours of cooking across all stoves. This metric characterizes the extent to which LPG was used as a primary versus supplementary fuel among mixed users.

LPG use trajectories.

For each post-trial year, participants were first classified as LPG users if they reported using any LPG over the prior year (either exclusively or in combination with biomass). Next, participants were classified as following one of five trajectories based on their pattern of annual LPG use across years 1-4: sustained adopters (LPG use reported at all followup years); late abandoners (LPG use reported in first one or two years post-trial but discontinued); delayed users (no LPG use in follow-up years one and/or two, with adoption in years three or four); intermittent users (inconsistent LPG use across follow-up years); and nopost trial use (no LPG use reported at any follow-up year). Households were included if they had stove-use data in at least two of the four post-trial years. India control households were excluded in year 1 when they were receiving free LPG; they were included if they had at least two years with stove use data in years 2-4. Trajectory distributions were compared across countries and treatment arms descriptively.

Predictors of post-trial LPG use.

To identify factors associated with post-trial LPG use, we fit a multivariable logistic regression model with any LPG use across years 1-4 post-trial as the outcome (excluding year 1 for control households in India when free fuel was provided). Predictors were selected a priori based on variables hypothesized to influence fuel use behavior, drawing on existing literature and investigator expertise from each study country. Predictors included treatment assignment, socioeconomic status (SES), self-reported preference for LPG, perceived ability to use LPG, maternal age, number of household members, and country site. An SES index was developed based on factor analysis of self-reported household wealth indicators (housing materials, water and sanitation access, durable asset ownership, and maternal education), with values scaled to the range among study participants, as described previously. 21, 22 We categorized SES into quartiles, pooled across countries. In India, where most participants continued using some LPG post-trial, we fit a separate logistic regression of exclusive LPG use at year 4 on LPG accessibility, LPG affordability, SES, household size, district of residence, maternal age, and treatment assignment. Model fit was evaluated using the pseudo-R², area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), and Wald χ². Variance inflation factors were calculated for all predictors to assess multicollinearity.

Results

1. Participant characteristics and fuel preferences

Of the 2,218 participants with trial endline data, 2,137 (96.3%) completed at least one post-trial fuel use survey and 2,012 (90.7%) completed the Year 4 survey. A further 32 households without a trial endline record also provided post-trial data, giving 2,169 households with any follow-up data (1,070 intervention participants and 1,099 control participants). Demographic characteristics at baseline of the HAPIN trial were similar for intervention and control participants with follow-up data (Table 1). Upon trial enrollment, none of the participants used primarily LPG or electric stoves. Households had on average 5 people (SD 1.6) and most of the participating women had primary school education or less. Socioeconomic status was highest in India (70% of households were in the highest wealth quartile) and lowest in Rwanda (68% of households were in the lowest wealth quartile). Educational attainment was highest in India (36% of women had completed secondary or higher) compared to 17% in Rwanda and 14% in Guatemala. Maternal age was slightly higher in Rwanda (27 years, SD 4.4) than in India (24 years, SD 3.8) or Guatemala (25 years, SD 4.5). *73 participants received an LPG stove (2 of these also received an electric stove), and 27 participants received an electric stove.

In India, at trial endline, >95% of both intervention and control participants said they would prefer to cook with LPG and that they would be able to continue doing so after the HAPIN trial (Table 1). Most intervention participants in Guatemala (95%) and Rwanda (85%) also said they would prefer to keep using LPG after the trial, but fewer said they would be able to continue to do so (80% in Guatemala and 62% in Rwanda). Control participants in Guatemala and Rwanda reported lower preference for LPG (59% in Guatemala and 15% in Rwanda), and much lower ability to continue using LPG (10% in Guatemala and 6% in Rwanda) than intervention participants.

Across countries, 977 of 1,762 participants (55.4%) reported planning to continue using LPG after the trial, of whom 484 (49.5%) planned exclusive use and 493 (50.5%) planned nonexclusive use. Among those planning non-exclusive use, the most cited reasons were that LPG refills are expensive (n=401, 81.3%) and that biomass is free (n=85, 17.2%), cheap (n=103, 20.9%), or easier to get (n=47, 9.5%); none cited dissatisfaction with LPG as a reason. Participants who said they did not plan to use LPG at all were not asked about their reasons.

2. Control group stove/fuel choices at trial endline At the conclusion of the trial, all control participants in India received an LPG stove and 18 months of free fuel, per trial design, while in Guatemala and Rwanda, only 100 (27%) and 25 (7%) of control participants, respectively, selected an LPG stove (or electric, in Guatemala) as compensation for their trial participation (Table 1). In Rwanda, primary reasons for choosing the LPG stove were hearing good things from other HAPIN trial participants (n=9), saving money (n=7), and saving time collecting fuel and cooking (n=7). Primary reasons for not selecting LPG among control participants in Rwanda were unaffordability of LPG (n=200) and inaccessibility of LPG refills (n=71). Participants in Guatemala were not asked about their reasons for choosing LPG or not.

3. Post-trial LPG use among intervention participants

Following the end of the trial period, LPG use by intervention participants dropped substantially. Very few participants continued using LPG exclusively in years 1-4 after the intervention ended (Figure 1): 2-3% in Guatemala, 29% in India (43% in the first year of follow-up but only 21-27% in years 2-4), and near 0% in Rwanda (2% in the first year of follow-up and 0% thereafter).

After the trial, intervention participants had higher rates of 'any' LPG use (i.e., using LPG either exclusively or in combination with biomass). Reported use of any LPG in Guatemala dropped to 35% in year 1, then increased over years 2-4 post-trial to 50% in year 4. Use of any LPG decreased over time in Rwanda (from 32% in year 1 to 10% in year 4) and remained fairly stable in India (from 78% in year 1 to 82% in year 4).

4. Post-trial LPG use among intervention compared to control participants

Across the post-trial follow-up visits in both Guatemala and Rwanda, reported LPG use was higher among intervention participants (who received an LPG stove and 18 months of free fuel) compared to control participants (who were offered an LPG stove with limited starter fuel or other compensation at the conclusion of the trial) (Figure 1). In Guatemala, 35% of intervention and 13% of control participants reported any LPG use during the first post-trial year. In Rwanda, 32% of intervention and 8% of control participants reported any LPG use over the first year.

In Guatemala, intervention participants maintained higher use of any LPG compared to control participants after the trial, though both intervention and control groups increased LPG use over years 1-4 post-trial: by the fourth year post-trial, 50% of intervention and 28% of control participants reported any LPG use over the prior year. In contrast, in Rwanda, LPG use diminished over time. By year 4, only 10% of intervention participants and 4% of control participants reported using any LPG in the prior year. Reported exclusive LPG use remained very low across all post-trial years in both Guatemala and Rwanda for both intervention and control participants. Only 24 participants reported electric stove use post-trial; 15 did so without LPG at some follow-up visit (2 exclusively in India intervention households, and 13 in combination with biomass -9 in Guatemala control households, 3 in India intervention households, and 1 in Guatemala intervention household). None used only electricity across all follow-up years.

In India, during year 1 post-trial (when control participants were receiving free fuel without behavioral reinforcement), control participants demonstrated similar LPG adoption as intervention participants did during the trial. During year 1 when control participants received free fuel, 85% reported exclusive LPG use and 8% reported LPG use in combination with biomass on the day prior to the survey, for a total of 93% reporting any LPG usecomparable to the 99% of intervention participants reporting any LPG use on the prior day while receiving free fuel at trial endline (Figure S1). In years 2-4 post-trial (after fuel subsidies ended), control participants in India maintained similar post-trial use of any LPG fuel as intervention participants did post-trial, with around 81% reporting any LPG use over the prior year, compared to approximately 80% of intervention participants (Figure 1). Slightly more intervention participants continued using LPG exclusively than control participants (by year 4 post-trial, 27% of intervention participants exclusively used LPG during the prior year, compared to 21% of controls).

5. Amount of cooking done with LPG among mixed stove users

Mixed stove users in Guatemala and India reported using LPG for slightly less than half of their cooking tasks (Figure 2). By year 4 post-trial, mixed stove users in the intervention group used LPG for 45% of cooking in India (n=196) and 38% in Guatemala (n=159); mixed stove users in the control group used LPG for 45% of cooking in India (n=218) and 40% in Guatemala (n=92). The few mixed stove users in Rwanda used LPG for a lower percentage of cooking tasks: 19% by control (n=13) and 23% by intervention (n=31) participants at year 4.6. Trajectories of LPG use LPG use trajectories varied greatly across participants in the three countries (Figure 3; overall by country in Figure S2; n = 2,128 households with stove use data from at least two follow-up years). Rwanda had the greatest number of participants who reported no post-trial LPG use (81% across intervention and control groups) and the least number of participants who used LPG for the entire post-trial follow-up period (3%). Some intervention participants in Rwanda continued using LPG before abandoning it (17%) or continued using LPG intermittently (7%). In Guatemala, more control participants (67%) than intervention participants (34%) had no posttrial LPG use, and intervention participants had a greater proportion of sustained adopters (21% vs 8%) and intermittent LPG users (19% vs 8%). Delayed use of LPG was most common in Guatemala (17% across groups), while sustained LPG use for the entire follow-up period was highest in India (63% overall). 7. Factors influencing post-trial LPG use After listwise deletion of covariates with missing values, 1,759 (81%) of the 2,169 participants with any post-trial fuel use data were included in the fully adjusted logistic regression model. The fully adjusted multivariable logistic regression model demonstrated good discrimination, with a pseudo-R² of 0.46, area under the ROC curve (AUC) of 0.91, and a Wald χ²(10) = 515.97 (p < 0.001). Variance inflation factors (VIF) for all predictors were below 5.1 (mean VIF = 2.43).

We saw a significant effect of treatment assignment, socioeconomic status, perceived ability to use LPG, number of household members, and country (Figure 4). Maternal age was not significantly associated with LPG use during the follow-up years. Assignment to the intervention arm was associated with 84% higher odds of LPG uptake compared to the control arm (adjusted OR [aOR] 1.84, 95% CI 1.26 -2.69). Compared to the lowest SES quartile-a composite index that included maternal education along with other household assets and characteristicsparticipants in SES Q2 had nearly four times the odds of LPG use (aOR 3.74, 95% CI 2.47 -5.66), those in Q3 had nearly 14 times the odds (aOR 13.73, 95% CI 8.78 -21.47), and those in the highest SES quartile (Q4) had approximately 41 times the odds of post-trial LPG use (aOR 40.94, 95% CI 15.72 -106.62). Participants who reported their ability to use LPG at the trial endline visit had approximately four times the odds of post-trial LPG use compared to those who did not (aOR 3.87, 95% CI 2.63 -5.69). In contrast, stated preference for LPG at trial endline was not significantly associated with post-trial LPG use (aOR 1.26, 95% CI 0.86 -1.84). Greater household size was significantly associated with lower odds of LPG use (aOR 0.86 per additional household member, 95% CI 0.79 -0.93). Relative to Rwanda, participants in India had markedly higher odds of LPG use (aOR 5.38, 95% CI 2.32 -12.51); participants in Guatemala also had higher odds compared to Rwanda (aOR 1.88, 95% CI 1.29 -2.73). There were some notable differences in country-specific models (Figures S3, S4, S5). Treatment assignment was only associated with post-trial use of any LPG in Guatemala (aOR 2.08, 95% CI 1.31-3.31) (Figure S3). In both Guatemala and Rwanda, SES and perceived ability to use LPG were significant predictors; stated preference for LPG and maternal age were not. Larger household size was associated with lower odds of any LPG use in Guatemala (aOR 0.83, 95% CI 0.76-0.91) but not Rwanda (Figures S3, S4).

In India, where only 42 participants (5.7%) never used LPG post-trial, we examined predictors of exclusive LPG use at year 4 (24% of participants with year 4 data used LPG exclusively) (Figure S5). Perceived LPG accessibility and affordability were each associated with approximately twice the odds of exclusive use (aOR 2.05, 95% CI 1.34-3.13 and aOR 2.09, 95% CI 1.35-3.24, respectively). SES was not independently associated. Larger households (aOR 0.66, 95% CI 0.55-0.80), residence in Villupuram versus Nagapattinam (aOR 0.34, 95% CI 0.23-0.52), and older maternal age (aOR 0.95, 95% CI 0.90-1.00) were each associated with lower odds of exclusive use. Post-trial exclusive LPG use was too rare in Guatemala and Rwanda to model.

Discussion

While almost all intervention participants used LPG fuel exclusively during the HAPIN trial when fuel was free and delivered directly to the home, exclusive use was limited after the trial, when households had to pay for and procure fuel. By year 4 post-trial, only about a quarter of participants in India were still using LPG exclusively and almost none in Guatemala or Rwanda were. The trial did, however, result in substantial non-exclusive use of LPG post-trial in India and Guatemala, but not in Rwanda. LPG use trends in the four years post-trial varied by site: LPG use increased in Guatemala, remained stable in India, and declined in Rwanda. In India, behavioral reinforcement may have led to slightly higher exclusive use of LPG post-trial among intervention participants compared to control, but similar levels of non-exclusive use.

Differences in post-trial LPG use across countries were likely shaped by broader country contexts, including infrastructure, policies and political support, subsidization, average per capita income, and social norms. In India, government subsidies and active promotion of LPG created both financial support and social pressure to use it, likely contributing to higher sustained use. The PMUY program in India expanded LPG access through a network of cylinderrefill distributors, digital delivery of subsidy credits to women's bank accounts, and public awareness campaigns promoting LPG use; however, the scope and level of subsidy support fluctuated over the study period and varied across states. In contrast, Rwanda's low LPG use likely reflects the absence of government programs to support LPG affordability, and a national energy strategy that emphasizes LPG for urban areas only, resulting in limited LPG infrastructure and availability outside major cities. In Guatemala, LPG infrastructure is expanding, reflected in a growing presence of LPG companies and advertising in rural areas. These trends may be driving greater awareness of and interest in adopting LPG. Nevertheless, access remains challenging in many rural areas, and no government programs currently support LPG financing in Guatemala. Differences in SES across sites also likely influenced post-trial LPG use, with higher SES in India enabling continued purchase of subsidized LPG, low SES in Rwanda limiting purchasing power, and intermediate SES in Guatemala contributing to moderate continued LPG use.

Other trials have similarly found near-exclusive use of LPG during the intervention when it was provided and delivered for free, with lower post-trial use that was higher in areas with LPG subsidies. In Ghana, where LPG fuel subsidies were not available, the GRAPHS study found that no households reported LPG stove use three years after an 18-month LPG trial in which free fuel was delivered. 23 The CHAP study in Peru, where LPG subsidies were available, found that intervention participants continued using LPG on 85% of monitored days but only exclusively for 51% of days after a 12-month LPG trial with free LPG fuel and deliveries. 12 Other studies have found that pay as you go schemes with lower, flexible incremental prices also increase LPG use. 24 These studies suggest that cost, not social or cultural preferences, is the primary driver of LPG use, and that efforts to subsidize LPG and create a more accessible payment structure are most likely to facilitate its adoption.

At an individual level, factors influencing post-trial LPG use align with other literature demonstrating higher use in households with greater socioeconomic status and maternal education, and lower use in larger households. 10 Although the majority of intervention participants preferred to continue using LPG after the trial ended, fewer said they would be able to do so, primarily due to the ongoing cost of fuel refills. Our regression results also showed that lower SES and larger household size were associated with lower post-trial LPG use. Other studies have similarly found affordability to be a main driver of sustained clean fuel use. 25 Notably, the HAPIN, GRAPHS and CHAP trials found no protective effects across a range of primary health outcomes, making it unclear whether real or perceived health gains motivated continued use in any setting. Other work has found low post-trial adoption of interventions with demonstrated health benefits that require significant resources, as well as high post-trial adoption of "ineffective" interventions with null health benefits, suggesting health motivation alone is insufficient to sustain behavior change in the absence of structural support. 26

Strengths and Limitations

This study has several strengths. Longitudinal follow-up allowed assessment of the longer-term effects of an 18-month free LPG fuel, home delivery, and behavioral reinforcement intervention on fuel use patterns. The multi-country design (Guatemala, India, Rwanda) captures diverse sociocultural, economic, and infrastructural contexts. High retention (96% of participants with trial endline data completed at least one post-trial fuel use survey and 91% completed the year 4 survey) and repeated measures enable analysis of trends and trajectories over time. Participants' long-standing trust in field staff increased confidence in self-reported data. These findings have clear policy relevance, demonstrating that even short-term provision of free LPG can substantially increase long-term LPG use where infrastructure is sufficient, although exclusive use remains limited.

Several limitations should also be noted. First, post-trial fuel use was measured annually at the household level, which limits the ability to capture within-household seasonal variation; however, because surveys were administered across different households throughout the year, the pooled data capture fuel use across all seasons. Second, all data were self-reported, introducing potential recall and social desirability bias. Participants may have overreported LPG use to align with perceived study expectations or underreported LPG use to emphasize financial need in hopes of receiving further benefits from the project. Ongoing contact through health and exposure monitoring may have heightened this bias, or improved recall. Although retention was high, participants lost to follow-up may differ systematically from those who remained in the study. Control participants may not represent the general population, as their prior trial participation likely increased awareness of LPG benefits. Third, in Guatemala and Rwanda, few control participants selected an LPG stove as compensation for trial completion. Thus, our analyses in these countries compare the LPG intervention to the option of receiving an LPG stove rather than to actual stove provision; this low uptake likely reflects limited prioritization of LPG and suggests that sustained use may have remained low even with stove distribution. Those who elected to receive the LPG stove may be different from those who chose another compensation option. Fourth, we did not collect qualitative data to contextualize our results or confirm our interpretation of the data. Finally, since HAPIN trial participants received free fuel delivered to their homes during the trial, they may have developed an expectation that they should not have to pay or arrange for the delivery of LPG post-trial, influencing their fuel usage behaviors.

Conclusion

The 18-month HAPIN trial was successful in achieving awareness and stated preference for cooking with LPG, as well as continued use of LPG in combination with biomass in India and Guatemala over four years post-trial. However, exclusive use of LPG four years after the trial was limited to about one quarter of participants in India, and very few participants in Rwanda continued using any LPG. Fuel access and affordability were key barriers to long-term adoption. Additional efforts beyond a short subsidization and behavioral support period provided during the trial, such as national-level policies that enhance LPG accessibility and subsidize fuel costs, are likely needed to achieve greater sustained and exclusive LPG use.
