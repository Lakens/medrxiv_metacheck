How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background: Electronic health record (EHR) data can help identify patients who would benefit from blood pressure reassessment after an elevated reading.

Objective: To develop and internally validate a model to predict the absence of a recorded blood pressure measurement after an elevated reading among adults with hypertension; evaluate the potential yield of targeted outreach and model performance across subgroups; and determine whether recorded contact patterns provide additional information for predicting subsequent cardiovascular hospitalization.

Methods: This retrospective cohort study used MIMIC-IV data. Adults with hypertension, a qualifying systolic blood pressure reading >140 mmHg, defined as the first such reading on or after index, and 90 days of subsequent database coverage and survival were included. The primary outcome was the absence of a recorded blood pressure measurement within 90 days of the index reading. Gradient-boosted trees incorporating 62 demographic, clinical, and contacthistory features were compared with logistic regression models using a random 6:2:2 training, validation, and test split, with isotonic recalibration. Nested cross-validation was used to assess the tree model. Model discrimination, calibration, simulated outreach yield, and subgroup performance were evaluated.

Results: Among 30,942 patients, 10,899 (35.2%) had no recorded blood pressure measurement within 90 days. In the test set (n = 6,189), the gradient-boosted tree model achieved an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) of 0.740 (95% CI, 0.727-0.753), compared with 0.698 for penalized logistic regression and 0.687 for two-variable logistic regression. The calibration slope was 0.91, and the area under the precision-recall curve was 0.568 (95% CI, 0.546-0.593). Selecting the 20% of patients with the highest predicted risk identified 34.2% (95% CI, 32.7-35.7) of patients without a recorded measurement, with a positive predictive value of 60.4% (95% CI, 57.4-63.2). In out-of-fold subgroup analyses using a common selection threshold corresponding to an overall selection rate of 20%, sensitivity was 21.5% among Black patients, 23.3% among Hispanic patients, and 37.2% among White patients. Among 15,200 participants in the landmark analysis, 664 cardiovascular hospitalizations occurred. Increasing and sustained contact patterns were associated with modestly higher hazards of cardiovascular hospitalization, with the association primarily attributable to heart failure hospitalization. For sustained versus minimal contact, the hazard ratio for the composite outcome was 1.40 (95% CI, 1.06-1.85). However, adding contact gaps and trajectories improved the C-statistic by only 0.0023 (95% CI, -0.0006 to 0.0056).

The model showed potential for prioritizing blood pressure reassessment outreach, although sensitivity varied across racial and ethnic groups. Recorded contact patterns provided little incremental discrimination for cardiovascular hospitalization.

INTRODUCTION

Hypertension remains a leading modifiable risk factor for myocardial infarction, stroke, and heart failure [1, 2]. Yet blood pressure control remains suboptimal among patients diagnosed with hypertension [3]. Effective management requires repeated engagement with the health system: blood pressure must be measured, antihypertensive medications initiated or adjusted, and elevated readings reassessed. [4] Clinical guidelines recommend reassessment approximately 1 month after treatment initiation or adjustment in response to elevated blood pressure and followup every 3 to 6 months once blood pressure is controlled [5]. When elevated readings are not followed by timely reassessment and appropriate management, treatment intensification may be delayed, potentially increasing cardiovascular risk [6].

Reassessment after an elevated reading is therefore a clinically actionable step in hypertension management. In electronic health record (EHR) data, the absence of a subsequent recorded blood pressure measurement may indicate a missed reassessment, although it may also reflect measurement outside the health system. The period following an elevated reading thus represents a potential opportunity for targeted outreach, such as a care-coordinator call to arrange reassessment or follow-up by a nurse or pharmacist. Because clinic outreach capacity is limited, a predictive model could help prioritize patients unlikely to have a recorded reassessment, using information already available in the EHR, including visit history, blood pressure measurements, and prescribing data. Evaluating such models requires more than assessing discrimination. Decision curve analysis can help determine whether model-guided decisions offer net benefit across clinically relevant thresholds [7], while operational evaluation should quantify how many patients without a recorded reassessment could be identified for a given outreach capacity. Equity is also essential because outreach prioritization allocates a limited health care resource. [8] Performance should therefore be assessed across racial and ethnic, insurance, sex, and age groups. Algorithms trained on patterns of health care utilization may reproduce disparities in access rather than differences in clinical need [9]. Moreover, when outcome prevalence differs across groups, a model can be well calibrated within each group yet exhibit unequal sensitivity [10].

Recorded contact patterns may also contain information about subsequent cardiovascular risk, although their interpretation is not straightforward. The concept of retention in care, developed in HIV medicine [11], suggests that patients who disengage from health care may experience worse outcomes. In hypertension care, retention has also been operationalized as a return visit within a specified interval; the Hypertension Treatment in Nigeria Program evaluated follow-up within 37 days of registration [12]. In EHR studies, longitudinal contact has been characterized using trajectory classes [13] or intervals between encounters. [14] However, evidence linking these patterns to cardiovascular outcomes remains limited. Greater continuity of care has been associated with lower cardiovascular risk in national claims data [15], but continuity is conceptually distinct from the frequency of recorded encounters. Studies of EHR-based contact patterns also face several methodological challenges. First, patients receiving care outside the observed health system may be indistinguishable from those who discontinue care; therefore, recorded contact measures utilization within the data source rather than retention in care itself. Second, defining cohorts according to whether patients return for subsequent care conditions inclusion on the behavior being studied and may introduce selection bias [16, 17]. Third, measuring exposure during the same period in which outcomes accrue can introduce immortal time bias [18, 19], while care prompted by an emerging clinical condition may itself generate additional encounters. Finally, because greater illness burden often leads to more frequent health care encounters, higher recorded contact may reflect greater underlying morbidity rather than better care [20].

In this study, we used MIMIC-IV, a deidentified EHR database [21], to develop and internally validate a model predicting the absence of a recorded blood pressure measurement within 90 days after an elevated reading among adults with hypertension. We evaluated its potential to prioritize outreach under fixed clinic-capacity constraints and assessed performance across racial and ethnic, insurance, sex, and age groups. As a secondary objective, we examined whether recorded contact patterns, characterized by trajectory classes and gaps between encounters, were associated with subsequent cardiovascular hospitalization. To establish temporal ordering and reduce bias arising from outcome-related contact and exposure ascertainment, we used a landmark design in which cohort eligibility was defined at the index date, contact patterns were measured before the landmark, and covariates were measured before the index date.

METHODS

Data source and study population

We used the hospital module of MIMIC-IV v2.2 [21], which contains hospital admissions, diagnoses, laboratory results, and prescription orders from a single academic medical center in Boston, as well as outpatient measurements, including blood pressure and body mass index. We also used the emergency department module (MIMIC-IV-ED) [22], to identify emergency department visits that did not result in hospital admission and to determine the dates of emergency department contacts. We included adults with an ICD-9 or ICD-10 diagnosis of hypertension and a subsequent outpatient blood pressure measurement (codes are provided in Table S1). The index date was the date of the first blood pressure measurement on or after the first hypertension-coded admission. After excluding patients with a surgically correctable secondary cause of hypertension or death within 90 days after index, 48,628 patients were eligible for the study (Figure 1A). Cohort entry was based on database coverage rather than subsequent return visits. Some analysis-specific eligibility criteria, however, depended on information observed after the relevant index date: the prediction cohort required database coverage and survival for 90 days after the prediction date, whereas the primary landmark cohort required a record after day 730 to confirm survival (Supplementary Methods; sample denominators for all analyses are reported in Table S9). We distinguished three sources of information: database coverage determined cohort entry, evidence of survival determined eligibility for the landmark analysis, and death ascertainment determined the end of followup. Prediction of no recorded blood pressure measurement

For each patient, predictions were generated at the first systolic blood pressure reading greater than 140 mmHg on or after the index date. Patients were included in the prediction cohort if database coverage extended at least 90 days beyond the prediction date and they survived through day 90. The outcome was the absence of a recorded blood pressure measurement within 90 days of the prediction date. Because measurements obtained outside the observed health system are not captured, this outcome reflects absent documentation rather than necessarily indicating a missed clinical reassessment. The prediction trigger was based on systolic blood pressure alone. Among qualifying readings, 26.7% were accompanied by a diastolic pressure of at least 90 mmHg. In addition, 9.7% of patients had an earlier post-index reading with a diastolic pressure of at least 90 mmHg and a systolic pressure of 140 mmHg or lower; these readings did not trigger the prediction window. Guidelines recommend reassessment approximately 1 month after treatment initiation or adjustment and within 3 to 6 months when blood pressure is managed with lifestyle modification alone [5]. We selected a 90day prediction window to accommodate the recommended early reassessment while allowing additional time for scheduling. In this cohort, the median time to the next recorded blood pressure measurement was 35 days (interquartile range [IQR], 14-91). Alternative outcome definitions using 30-, 180-, 270-, and 365-day windows, as well as analyses treating death before any subsequent blood pressure measurement as an outcome or as a competing event, are described in the Supplementary Methods.

The model incorporated 62 features derived exclusively from information available at the prediction time: demographic characteristics, insurance, Elixhauser comorbidities, laboratory values, body mass index and associated missingness indicators, blood pressure level and longitudinal variability and trend, medication count, prior encounters, time since the last visit, and visit regularity. [24] Contact trajectory class was excluded because it was measured after the prediction date for most patients. Patients were randomly assigned, with stratification by outcome, to training (60%), validation (20%), and test (20%) sets; thus, all model evaluation was internal. We compared a two-variable logistic regression model using time since the last visit and prior encounters, an L2-penalized logistic regression model, and a gradient-boosted tree model [25]. Hyperparameters were tuned using five-fold cross-validation within the training set. The selected models were recalibrated using isotonic regression on the validation set and evaluated once on the held-out test set.

We assessed discrimination using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) and the area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC) [26]. Calibration was evaluated using the Brier score and calibration intercept and slope [27]. Paired AUROC differences were estimated, and 95% confidence intervals were obtained using 1,000 bootstrap samples. Shapley values were used to characterize feature contributions to model predictions [28]. To assess the robustness of the results to the random data split, we also performed nested five-fold cross-validation to generate out-of-fold predictions for every patient (Supplementary Methods). Reporting followed the Transparent Reporting of a Multivariable Prediction Model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis (TRIPOD) guidelines [29].

Because outreach costs depend substantially on the number of patients a clinic can contact, we evaluated model performance at fixed outreach capacities. Specifically, we flagged the 5%, 10%, 20%, or 30% of patients in the test set with the highest predicted risk. At each capacity level, we calculated the proportion of patients without a recorded blood pressure measurement who were flagged (sensitivity), the proportion of flagged patients who had no recorded measurement (positive predictive value), and the number of outreach contacts required per patient identified. We estimated 95% confidence intervals using bootstrap resampling. Decision curve analysis [7] was also performed to estimate net benefit across risk thresholds, with 95% bootstrap confidence bands.

For the equity audit, we flagged the 20% of patients with the highest out-of-fold predicted risk [9]. Within racial and ethnic, insurance, sex, and age groups, we assessed AUROC, calibration (observed-to-predicted ratio and calibration slope), the proportion flagged, true-positive and false-positive rates, and positive predictive value. We calculated bootstrap 95% confidence intervals for each measure and for differences in true-positive rates relative to a reference group [30]. Because a model can be calibrated within each group yet achieve different detection rates when outcome prevalence varies across groups [10], we additionally evaluated group-specific decision thresholds designed to equalize detection rates across groups.

Landmark and follow-up for the association analysis

The association analysis began with the at-index cohort, comprising patients whose database coverage extended through day 730 after index. Because both contact measures were derived from the first two years after index, cardiovascular outcomes were ascertained from a landmark at day 730 [18, 19]. The primary landmark population excluded patients with a recorded cardiovascular hospitalization before index or during the interval from index through day 730. This requirement relied on information obtained after the landmark and excluded 1,476 patients whose survival to day 730 was known but who had no subsequent record. To assess the potential effect of this selection, we repeated the cause-specific analyses in a day-730information sample, in which survival at the landmark was confirmed solely by hospital or emergency department contact during days 366-730 (Figure 1A; Table 4). This alternative sample was also subject to selection because inclusion required recorded contact during the second year after index. Follow-up ended at the earliest of the end of database coverage, day 1,460 after index, 365 days after the last hospital or emergency department contact, or death. The same follow-up end date was used for cardiovascular hospitalization and death. Accordingly, the results apply to patients who were alive, free of prior cardiovascular hospitalization, and eligible for follow-up based on recorded data two years after index. Contact measures

Contact was measured in consecutive 91-day quarters as the number of unique days on which any encounter, blood pressure measurement, or antihypertensive prescription order was recorded. Multiple types of contact on the same day counted as a single contact day. This measure captured the frequency of recorded contact rather than the number of records generated and did not assume that more contact represented better care. Contact trajectories were derived from quarters 1-7 (days 91-727), ensuring that all trajectory measurements preceded the day-730 landmark. Let Uiq denote the number of unique contact days for patient i in quarter q. The trajectory models used the transformed contact measure y(i,q) = log[1 + U(i,q)], q = 1, …, 7.

Group-based trajectory models [13] were fitted with the lcmm package [31] in the landmark population, using only pre-landmark contact data. Solutions containing one to six classes were compared using model selection criteria, without reference to outcome data [32]. Because the information criterion continued to improve as the number of classes increased, we selected the smallest adequate solution: a four-class model. This was the fewest-class solution that distinguished rising from declining contact trajectories while maintaining adequate classification quality across all classes (Table S2; Supplementary Methods). Each patient was assigned to the class with the highest posterior probability of membership.

To account for uncertainty in class assignment, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using each patient's posterior class-membership probabilities in place of the assigned class, allowing patients to contribute proportionally to each class according to their estimated membership probabilities. A five-class solution, which further divided the sustained-contact trajectory into moderate-and high-contact classes, was examined in a second sensitivity analysis.

For the contact-gap measure, let di(1) <⋯<di(m) denote the ordered days from day 0 through day 730 on which patient i had a recorded blood pressure measurement or hospital admission, with the index reading defined as day 0. The longest interval without recorded contact was defined as This definition included the interval from the last recorded contact to the landmark, thereby allowing a gap to be measured without requiring a subsequent return. The longest gap was modeled as a continuous exposure using a restricted cubic spline [33], with a 90-day interval as the reference. Gaps exceeding 180, 270, and 365 days were examined in secondary analyses because no universally accepted threshold defines a clinically meaningful gap in care [11]. A gap represented an absence of recorded contact within the observed health system, not confirmed disengagement from health care.

Outcome and covariates

For the association analysis, the outcome was the first hospitalization after the day-730 landmark with myocardial infarction, stroke, or heart failure recorded as the principal diagnosis. We used the principal diagnosis to improve the specificity of the hospitalization endpoint, particularly because chronic heart failure may be coded during admissions for unrelated conditions [34]. Accordingly, the outcome represented cardiovascular hospitalization rather than incident cardiovascular disease. Death from any cause before cardiovascular hospitalization was treated as the competing event because cause-specific mortality information was unavailable.

Covariates included age, sex, race and ethnicity as recorded in the EHR, insurance, systolic and diastolic blood pressure, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), glycated hemoglobin, body mass index, Elixhauser comorbidity score [35, 36], prior emergency department visits, prior inpatient admissions, total prior encounters, antihypertensive medication count, and characteristics of the index episode. All covariates were derived from information available on or before the index date. Blood pressure measurements were taken from the index date, whereas laboratory values and body mass index were taken from the measurements closest to index within the preceding 365 days. eGFR was calculated using the 2021 CKD-EPI equation without a race coefficient. Age, systolic blood pressure, eGFR, body mass index, Elixhauser score, prior encounters, prior inpatient admissions, and antihypertensive medication count were modeled using restricted cubic splines [33].

Diastolic blood pressure and glycated hemoglobin were modeled as linear terms. Missing values for eGFR (5.6%), glycated hemoglobin (60.9%), and body mass index (30.9%) were handled using multiple imputation by chained equations, with 30 imputed datasets [37, 38]. Estimates were combined using Rubin's rules [39]. Sensitivity analyses used missing-indicator methods [40], complete-case analysis, and delta-adjusted imputation to assess the robustness of findings to assumptions about missing data [41] (Supplementary Methods).

Association analyses

Cumulative incidence was estimated using the Aalen-Johansen estimator and compared across groups using Gray's test [42]. Cause-specific Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate associations with cause-specific event rates, and Fine-Gray subdistribution hazards models were used to assess associations with cumulative incidence in the presence of competing mortality [43, 44]. The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using scaled Schoenfeld residuals [45]. Doubly robust estimates combined stabilized inverse probability weights, truncated at the 1 st and 99 th percentiles, with covariate adjustment [46] [47] [48]. To account for potentially informative loss to follow-up, we additionally applied inverse probability-ofcensoring weights. The probability of remaining under follow-up was estimated at 91-day intervals using pooled logistic regression incorporating covariates and both contact measures. The resulting stabilized weights were truncated at the 1 st and 99 th percentiles [46]. To assess whether contact patterns provided information beyond the covariates, we compared a covariateonly model with models additionally including the contact-gap measure or trajectory class. Incremental information was assessed using pooled Wald tests and the change in Uno's Cstatistic at 730 days [49]. All models were fitted separately in each imputed dataset, and estimates were pooled across imputations. Sensitivity analyses are summarized in Table S3. We also report the minimum detectable hazard ratio at 80% power [50].

All tests were two-sided, with a significance threshold of α = 0.05. No adjustment was made for multiple comparisons. Analyses of component outcomes were considered exploratory and comprised 12 comparisons: one gap contrast (greater than 365 days versus the reference) and three trajectory-class contrasts, each evaluated for myocardial infarction, stroke, and heart failure hospitalization. Software details and a supplementary analysis of comorbidity patterns are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

RESULTS

Study population

Among 50,946 adults with a hypertension diagnosis and a subsequent blood pressure measurement, 48,628 met the index-cohort eligibility criteria. The prediction cohort comprised 30,942 patients, while the at-index cohort comprised 37,183 patients, of whom 15,200 were included in the primary landmark population (Figure 1A). The largest exclusion from the landmark population comprised 14,361 patients whose survival to day 730 could not be confirmed because they had neither a hospital or emergency department contact during days 366-730 nor a subsequent record. Consequently, the landmark analysis excluded a substantial number of patients without evidence of continued contact within the observed health system. An additional 1,476 patients were known to be alive at day 730 but had no subsequent record; they were excluded from the primary landmark population because inclusion required a record after day 730. Among the 32,101 patients with a systolic blood pressure reading greater than 140 mmHg, 711 had no recorded data after the qualifying reading, 254 had insufficient database coverage for the 90-day prediction horizon, and 194 died within 90 days. These patients were excluded from the prediction cohort, leaving 30,942 patients for analysis (Table S9).

Prediction of no recorded blood pressure measurement

Among the 30,942 patients in the prediction cohort, 10,899 (35.2%) had no blood pressure measurement recorded in the health system within 90 days of the qualifying elevated reading. The median interval between index and prediction was 22 days. Patients without a recorded measurement were broadly similar to those with a subsequent measurement in age, sex, race and ethnicity, insurance, blood pressure measurements at and before the qualifying reading, laboratory values, and comorbidity burden; all standardized differences were ≤0.13. However, patients without a recorded measurement had fewer prior encounters (median, 4 vs 8), a longer interval since their last visit (median, 55 vs 22 days), and more frequently had insufficient prior readings to estimate blood pressure variability (45.1% vs 23.7%) (Table 1; Table S4).

In the test set (n = 6,189; 35.3% without a recorded measurement), the gradient-boosted tree model achieved an AUROC of 0.740 (95% CI, 0.727-0.753) and an AUPRC of 0.568 (95% CI, 0.546-0.593). Discrimination was higher than that of penalized logistic regression (AUROC difference, 0.042; 95% CI, 0.033-0.052) and the two-variable logistic regression model (AUROC difference, 0.054; 95% CI, 0.044-0.063) (Table 2A). Nested cross-validation across all 30,942 patients yielded similar performance, with an AUROC of 0.746 (95% CI, 0.741-0.751) and an AUPRC of 0.578 (95% CI, 0.569-0.589). Model predictions were reasonably well calibrated: the test-set Brier score was 0.193, compared with 0.228 for the prevalence-only benchmark, and the calibration slope was 0.91 in the test set and 0.98 across cross-validation folds (Figure S1).

The interval since the last visit was the largest contributor to predictions, accounting for 30% of the total Shapley value contribution, followed by the average interval between visits, prior encounter frequency, and systolic blood pressure variability. Longer intervals since the last visit and fewer prior encounters were associated with higher predicted risk. Outreach yield and decision curve analysis

Flagging the 20% of patients with the highest predicted risk identified 34.2% (95% CI, 32.7-35.7) of patients who subsequently had no recorded blood pressure measurement. Among flagged patients, 60.4% (95% CI, 57.4-63.2) had no recorded measurement, corresponding to approximately 1.7 outreach contacts per patient identified, compared with 2.8 contacts under random selection (Table 2B; Figure 2A). Across selection thresholds ranging from the top 5% to the top 30% of predicted risk, positive predictive value remained between 58% and 66%, suggesting that outreach capacity could be adjusted within this range without substantial variation in yield. The full model identified more patients without a recorded measurement than either simpler model at every capacity level. In decision curve analysis, the model yielded greater net benefit than the strategies of contacting everyone or no one across the evaluated risk thresholds of 0.05-0.70. The lower bound of the 95% confidence interval for net benefit also exceeded both reference strategies over thresholds of 0.15-0.58. Results were consistent across alternative prediction horizons of 30-365 days, for which AUROC values ranged from 0.737 to 0.791 (Table S5). Death before a subsequent recorded blood pressure measurement was uncommon across all predicted-risk fifths, ranging from 0.1% to 0.4%.

Performance by groups

Table 3 presents the full set of performance measures across subgroups. Discrimination was similar across groups, with AUROC values ranging from 0.71 to 0.78. Mean predicted risk was also consistent with observed outcome prevalence across groups, with observed-to-predicted ratios ranging from 0.96 to 1.03. Calibration slopes ranged from 0.87 to 1.09; their 95% confidence intervals included 1 in all groups except Black patients (95% CI, 0.82-0.98) and patients younger than 50 years (95% CI, 0.78-0.97). Despite broadly similar discrimination and average calibration, outcome detection differed substantially across groups (Figure 2B). When the 20% of patients with the highest predicted risk were flagged, the model identified 37.2% of White patients without a recorded measurement, compared with 21.5% of Black patients (difference, -0.16; 95% CI, -0.18 to -0.14) and 23.3% of Hispanic patients (difference, -0.14; 95% CI, -0.18 to -0.10). The corresponding detection rate was 52.3% among patients categorized as having other or unknown race. Detection also varied by age: it was 22.2% among patients younger than 50 years (difference, -0.15; 95% CI, -0.18 to -0.12), 28.3% among those aged 50-64 years, and 48.4% among those aged 80 years or older. The difference for Medicaid beneficiaries was smaller, with a detection rate of 31.1% (difference, -0.01; 95% CI, -0.05 to 0.03). Differences in detection by race and ethnicity exceeded the corresponding differences in outcome prevalence. The proportion of patients without a recorded measurement was 31.9% among Black patients and 30.6% among Hispanic patients, compared with 35.8% among White patients. However, the proportion selected for outreach was also lower among Black and Hispanic patients (12.5% and 13.4%, respectively) than among White patients (21.3% ).

Thus, similar overall discrimination and average calibration did not translate into comparable detection at a common outreach capacity. In an additional analysis, we selected separate thresholds for each racial and ethnic group to achieve a common sensitivity equal to the overall detection rate of 35.2% (Table S6). This approach maintained the overall proportion of patients flagged at 20.0% but increased the false-positive rate among Black patients from 0.08 to 0.13 and among Hispanic patients from 0.09 to 0.13, while positive predictive values remained similar. Contact patterns and cardiovascular hospitalization

Within 730 days after the landmark, 664 patients (4.4%) experienced a cardiovascular hospitalization, whereas 1,077 (7.1%) died before experiencing the outcome. An additional 3,556 patients (23.4%) were censored before the end of follow-up, at a median of 443 days after the landmark. Of these, 3,028 were censored when death ascertainment ended and 528 when database coverage ended. Early censoring was modestly more frequent among patients classified as having Declining, Rising, or Sustained Contact than among those with Minimal Contact (odds ratios, 1.09-1.35) and among patients with a contact gap than among those without one (odds ratio, 1.11; 95% CI, 1.01-1.23). Inverse probability weighting for censoring did not materially change the estimates for the association between a contact gap and cardiovascular hospitalization (hazard ratio [HR], 0.86; 95% CI, 0.71-1.05) or between Sustained Contact and the outcome (HR, 1.39; 95% CI, 1.04-1.84; Table S3).

Four distinct contact patterns were identified (Figure 3A): Minimal Contact (49.8% of the landmark population), Declining Contact (22.5%), Rising Contact (17.6%), and Sustained Contact (10.1%). The Bayesian information criterion (BIC) decreased with each additional class through the six-class solution (Table S2); therefore, BIC alone did not identify a definitive number of classes. The final four-class solution was selected according to the prespecified classification criteria described in the Methods. It was the smallest solution that distinguished rising from declining contact while maintaining adequate classification performance across all classes, with average posterior probabilities ranging from 0.74 to 0.92 and odds of correct classification of at least 9.9. The five-class solution subdivided Sustained Contact into moderate-and high-contact groups, whereas the six-class solution introduced two additional classes, each comprising less than 4% of the population, and further subdivided Rising Contact without identifying a clearly distinct additional pattern. Despite these classification metrics, uncertainty remained for a subset of patients: 23.0% had a maximum posterior classmembership probability below 0.70. Incorporating posterior probabilities into the outcome model, rather than relying solely on assigned classes, yielded a similar estimate for Sustained Contact (HR, 1.42; 95% CI, 1.05-1.93, versus HR, 1.40 in the assigned-class analysis; Table S3). The five-class solution likewise produced a similar estimate for Sustained Moderate Contact (HR, 1.41; 95% CI, 1.11-1.79). Differences between classes were more pronounced in clinical characteristics and prior health care utilization than in demographic characteristics (Table S7). Median Elixhauser comorbidity scores ranged from 4 in the Minimal Contact class to 7 in the Sustained Contact class, with a maximum standardized difference of 0.49. Median numbers of prior health care encounters ranged from 3 to 9, with a maximum standardized difference of 0.71. In contrast, standardized differences for age, blood pressure, laboratory measures, sex, race, and insurance status were 0.17 or lower. The availability of clinical measurements also varied across classes. For example, body mass index was missing for 38.8% of patients in the Minimal Contact class, compared with 13.2% in the Sustained Contact class.

The two measures largely identified the same patients (Figure 3B). Among the 3,777 patients (24.8%) with a contact gap exceeding 365 days, 96% belonged to the Minimal Contact class, and none belonged to the Sustained Contact class. The gap definition did not require a subsequent return; however, inclusion in the primary landmark cohort required a record after day 730. The cumulative incidence of cardiovascular hospitalization varied modestly across contact-pattern classes, ranging from 4.4% in the Minimal Contact class to 5.8% in the Rising Contact class at 730 days (Gray's test, p = 0.023). The corresponding incidence was 4.2% among patients with a contact gap and 5.1% among those without one (Gray's test, p = 0.042). In contrast, death without prior cardiovascular hospitalization differed more substantially across classes, increasing from 403 deaths (5.3%) in the Minimal Contact class to 212 (7.9%) in the Rising Contact class and 258 (16.8%) in the Sustained Contact class (Gray's test, p < 0.001; Table S7). These differences in competing mortality are consistent with the greater comorbidity burden and health care utilization observed among patients with more sustained contact (Figure 3C-D). Adjusted estimates are presented in Table 4. A recorded contact gap was not associated with an increased hazard of cardiovascular hospitalization. Furthermore, the duration of the longest interval without recorded contact showed no clear dose-response relationship (overall p = 0.26; Figure S2). Relative to a 90-day interval, the estimated HRs were 0.93 (95% CI, 0.76-1.13) for 180 days, 1.03 (95% CI, 0.81-1.31) for 366 days, and 0.73 (95% CI, 0.51-1.04) for 730 days. All corresponding 95% CIs included the null value of 1. Similarly, a contact gap exceeding 365 days was not associated with a statistically significant increase in the hazard of cardiovascular hospitalization in either the cause-specific model (HR, 0.86; 95% CI, 0.71-1.05) or the Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard model (HR, 0.86; 95% CI, 0.71-1.05). The doubly robust estimate was below 1 (HR, 0.81; 95% CI, 0.66-0.98). Gaps exceeding 180 days (HR, 0.94; 95% CI, 0.79-1.11) or 270 days (HR, 1.03; 95% CI, 0.86-1.22), as well as gaps within the Minimal Contact class (HR, 0.92; 95% CI, 0.72-1.17), were also not associated with higher hazards. Taken together, these findings do not support a consistent association between longer contact gaps and increased cardiovascular hospitalization risk, although the estimates do not establish the absence of an association. The overall association between contact-pattern class and cardiovascular hospitalization was borderline (p = 0.05). Compared with Minimal Contact, Sustained Contact was associated with a higher cause-specific hazard in the covariate-adjusted model (HR, 1.40; 95% CI, 1.06-1.85) and the doubly robust model (HR, 1.43; 95% CI, 1.10-1.85). However, the corresponding Fine-Gray subdistribution HR was less precise and included the null (HR, 1.28; 95% CI, 0.97-1.70). This difference across estimands is relevant given the substantially higher incidence of death without cardiovascular hospitalization in the Sustained Contact class. Rising Contact was associated with a more modest increase in the causespecific hazard (HR, 1.26; 95% CI, 1.02-1.56), whereas its Fine-Gray estimate was compatible with no association (HR, 1.23; 95% CI, 0.99-1.52). No statistically significant association was observed for Declining Contact relative to Minimal Contact (HR, 1.08; 95% CI, 0.88-1.33).

After weighting, standardized differences were 0.12 or lower across contact-pattern classes, approaching the prespecified balance target of 0.10; the standardized difference for contact-gap status was 0.22. The doubly robust estimates additionally incorporated direct covariate adjustment. Proportional hazards diagnostics provided no strong evidence of violations, with the smallest p value for an exposure term being 0.07. In the day-730-information sample, which excluded post-landmark information, the results were directionally similar but less precise (n = 7,649; 318 cardiovascular hospitalizations). The HR was 0.77 (95% CI, 0.55-1.09) for a contact gap exceeding 365 days and 1.33 (95% CI, 0.92-1.93) for Sustained Contact. The overall association between contact-pattern class and cardiovascular hospitalization was not statistically significant in this restricted sample (p = 0.34; Table 4). Neither contact measure materially improved discrimination beyond the baseline covariates (Table 4B). The covariateonly model had a C-statistic of 0.708. Adding contact-gap status increased the C-statistic by 0.0006 (95% CI, -0.0011 to 0.0023; Wald p = 0.14), whereas adding contact-pattern class increased it by 0.0021 (95% CI, -0.0006 to 0.0053; p = 0.05). Including both measures yielded an increase of 0.0023 (95% CI, -0.0006 to 0.0056). Sensitivity analyses and outcome components

The findings were generally consistent across sensitivity analyses (Table S3). Excluding the complete-case analysis, hazard ratios (HRs) for a contact gap exceeding 365 days ranged from 0.77 to 0.93, whereas estimates for Sustained Contact ranged from 1.28 to 1.43. The overall test for differences across contact-pattern classes yielded p values ranging from 0.02 to 0.34, indicating some variability in the statistical evidence across model specifications. Point estimates were similar across most alternative specifications; the complete-case analysis yielded a lower and imprecise estimate. The HR remained similar when covariates were averaged over the period from 365 days before to 90 days after index (HR, 1.42), when continuous covariates were modeled linearly (HR, 1.39), and when the missing-indicator method was used (HR, 1.40). Delta-adjusted imputation likewise yielded estimates ranging from 1.39 to 1.40. The estimate was also similar when patients with cardiovascular events before index were retained (HR, 1.41; 95% CI, 1.09-1.83; n = 16,446; 798 events) and when posterior classmembership probabilities were used in place of assigned classes (HR, 1.42; 95% CI, 1.05-1.93).

The complete-case analysis was the principal exception, yielding an HR of 1.11 (95% CI, 0.71-1.72) for a contact gap exceeding 365 days and 0.88 (95% CI, 0.53-1.48) for Sustained Contact. This analysis was restricted to the 28% of patients with complete data on all three incompletely observed clinical measures-estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), glycated hemoglobin, and body mass index-and included 184 events. The confidence intervals were wide and included the corresponding primary estimates, limiting the strength of any inference that these results differed from the primary findings. Observed and imputed means were similar for eGFR (76.4 vs. 77.5 mL/min/1.73 m²), body mass index (30.5 vs. 30.2 kg/m²), and glycated hemoglobin (6.69% vs. 6.73%). The fraction of missing information was 0.01 or lower for every estimate, indicating limited loss of statistical information attributable to missingness under the fitted imputation models. Nevertheless, similarity between observed and imputed means and low fractions of missing information do not, by themselves, establish the validity of the missingdata assumptions. With 664 cardiovascular hospitalization events, the study had 80% power to detect an HR of 1.29 (or 0.78) for a contact gap exceeding 365 days and HRs ranging from 1.33 to 1.47 for Declining, Rising, and Sustained Contact relative to Minimal Contact. These calculations suggest that the study was better positioned to detect larger associations than modest ones; therefore, smaller associations cannot be excluded.

In exploratory analyses of individual outcome components (Table S8; Figure S3), neither contact gaps nor contact-pattern classes showed evidence of an association with myocardial infarction or stroke. In contrast, the higher hazards observed for Rising and Sustained Contact were concentrated in heart failure hospitalization. Relative to Minimal Contact, Rising Contact was associated with an HR of 1.66 (95% CI, 1.17-2.35), whereas Sustained Contact was associated with an HR of 2.14 (95% CI, 1.38-3.30), based on 283 heart failure hospitalization events. These associations persisted after adjustment for prior heart failure diagnoses and among patients without a documented heart failure diagnosis before the landmark. In the latter subgroup, the HRs were 1.67 (95% CI, 1.01-2.75) for Rising Contact and 2.07 (95% CI, 1.07-4.02) for Sustained Contact. Although these findings suggest that the observed associations were not explained entirely by previously documented heart failure, the exploratory nature of the analyses and the relatively wide confidence intervals warrant cautious interpretation. When diagnoses recorded in any diagnostic position were included, the number of composite outcome events increased more than threefold, and the estimated associations increased with contact intensity. This pattern is consistent with the possibility that patients with more frequent health care encounters have greater opportunities for diagnostic evaluation and coding. Accordingly, the stronger associations observed under this broader outcome definition may partly reflect differential ascertainment rather than differences in underlying disease incidence alone.

DISCUSSION

In this cohort study of adults with hypertension receiving care within a single academic health system, the absence of a recorded blood pressure measurement within 90 days after an elevated reading was common and could be predicted using information available in the EHRs at the time of the elevated reading. More than one-third of patients had no recorded measurement within this interval. A prediction model relying primarily on health care contact history achieved an AUROC of 0.74 and an AUPRC of 0.57, compared with an outcome prevalence of 0.35. At an outreach capacity of 20%, approximately six in ten patients selected for outreach subsequently had no recorded measurement. In contrast, analyses of cardiovascular hospitalization revealed a different pattern: a prolonged gap in recorded contact was not consistently associated with a higher hazard of cardiovascular hospitalization, whereas Rising and Sustained Contact were associated with modestly higher cause-specific hazards, largely attributable to heart failure hospitalization. When contact measures and covariates were defined before the landmark, incorporating contact patterns or gaps into models based on baseline covariates yielded little improvement in discrimination for cardiovascular hospitalization. Collectively, these findings suggest that EHR-derived contact history may help prioritize blood pressure follow-up, but they do not establish that model-guided outreach improves care or that contact history materially enhances cardiovascular risk prediction.

The most influential predictors of absent recorded follow-up were measures of prior health care utilization, including time since the last visit, the number and spacing of previous encounters, and blood pressure variability. The latter requires multiple measurements and may therefore be unavailable or less informative for patients with limited prior engagement. A parsimonious model using only time since the last visit and the number of prior encounters achieved an AUROC of 0.69, indicating that useful risk stratification may be feasible without a more complex machine learning model. [51] Nevertheless, the full model identified more patients without a subsequent recorded measurement at each outreach-capacity level and yielded positive net benefit across a wider range of decision thresholds. Its discrimination was within the range reported for models predicting missed appointments [52], although the outcomes are not directly equivalent: absence of a recorded blood pressure measurement after an elevated reading reflects an undocumented step in a care process and does not necessarily indicate a missed appointment or failure to obtain care elsewhere. Discrimination improved over longer prediction windows, which may primarily reflect the identification of patients who cease appearing in the health system altogether. The 90-day window may nevertheless be more actionable because it aligns more closely with the recommended interval for reassessment after an elevated reading [5].

Evaluating performance in relation to outreach capacity helps translate discrimination into a potential operational strategy. At outreach capacities of 5% and 30%, approximately six in ten patients selected for hypothetical outreach by the model subsequently had no recorded measurement, compared with approximately one in three patients contacted at random. These results suggest that contact history could help direct limited outreach resources toward patients more likely to lack documented follow-up. [53] However, the outcome used for model development was the absence of a recorded measurement, not the absence of measurement in all care settings, and a positive prediction does not establish that an intervention is necessary or beneficial. Implementation findings from the same program also describe patient registries, team-based care, and improved medication access as components of a broader hypertensioncare strategy [54]. The study did not evaluate whether model-guided outreach increased followup, improved blood pressure control, or reduced cardiovascular events. Establishing clinical utility will therefore require prospective evaluation, ideally through a randomized trial or a welldesigned quasi-experimental study comparing model-guided outreach with usual care.

The subgroup analyses revealed a trade-off that aggregate calibration metrics alone do not capture. Although average predicted and observed risks were concordant across racial and ethnic groups, the model identified 22% of Black patients and 23% of Hispanic patients without a recorded measurement at the evaluated threshold, compared with 37% of White patients. Differences in outcome prevalence can contribute to differences in classification performance even when a model is calibrated within groups [10]. In this study, however, outcome prevalence differed by approximately 4-5 percentage points, whereas detection rates differed by 14-16 percentage points. This disparity suggests that differences in the distribution or recording of predictors, including health care contact history, may also have contributed, consistent with concerns about algorithms that rely on patterns of health care utilization [9]. Applying groupspecific thresholds brought detection rates closer together but increased false-positive rates among Black and Hispanic patients. For a relatively low-burden intervention such as a scheduling call, false-positive classifications may carry limited direct costs, although they can still impose burdens on patients and health systems. Using race-specific thresholds nevertheless raises important legal and ethical questions. Alternatives that do not explicitly use race, including expanding outreach capacity or incorporating better measures of access to care, warrant evaluation. We therefore present group-specific thresholds as an illustration of the trade-offs between equity objectives and classification errors, rather than as a recommendation for race-specific deployment. Patients categorized as having another or unknown race or ethnicity-including those whose race was not recorded-had the highest prevalence of absent recorded follow-up and were flagged most frequently. Because missing demographic information may itself be associated with patterns of health care contact, this finding also highlights the potential importance of data completeness when evaluating algorithmic fairness. [55] For cardiovascular hospitalization, the results suggest that recorded contact history reflects clinical complexity and patterns of health care utilization more directly than it reflects future cardiovascular risk. Contact-pattern classes differed relatively little in age, blood pressure, and laboratory measurements but substantially in comorbidity burden, prior health care utilization, and mortality. Patients with gaps exceeding 365 days were concentrated in the Minimal Contact class, which had the lowest comorbidity burden and fewest prior encounters. For some patients, therefore, a prolonged interval without a recorded encounter may reflect stable disease, lower clinical need, or care received outside the health system rather than inadequate care. The absence of a consistent dose-response relationship between gap duration and cardiovascular hospitalization, together with the limited incremental discrimination provided by contact measures, argues against interpreting a prolonged EHR contact gap as a stand-alone marker of cardiovascular risk. These findings were broadly consistent across alternative estimators and missing-data approaches, and estimates were similar in the sample restricted to information available at the landmark.

These findings differ from previous studies linking patterns of care to clinical outcomes. Greater continuity of care was associated with fewer cardiovascular events in Korean national claims data [15], delayed diagnosis following elevated blood pressure readings was associated with higher cardiovascular risk in a large US health system [6], and missed visits were associated with higher mortality in HIV care, where missed visits were also interpreted as a marker of risk rather than necessarily its cause [56]. Differences in outcome and exposure definitions, population selection, and data capture may help explain the apparent discrepancies. National claims data can capture encounters across multiple providers, making a prolonged gap more informative about overall health care utilization than a gap observed within a single health system. The Korean study also required at least four visits during the first two years, selecting a population with established health care contact, although our landmark design similarly conditioned on evidence of survival through day 730. Delayed diagnosis following elevated blood pressure is more directly related to inaction within a care process than is an EHR-defined gap in contact. In addition, interruption of HIV treatment may have relatively rapid clinical consequences, whereas the cardiovascular consequences of inadequate hypertension follow-up may require a longer period to become apparent. These distinctions underscore the importance of defining contact gaps in relation to the data source, clinical process, and outcome under investigation.

The modestly higher cause-specific hazards observed in the Rising and Sustained Contact classes were largely confined to heart failure hospitalization and persisted after adjustment for prior heart failure diagnoses and among patients without a documented heart failure diagnosis before the landmark. These findings warrant cautious interpretation. The component analyses were exploratory and involved 12 comparisons: contact-gap status and three class contrasts for each of three cardiovascular outcome components. In addition, the association for Sustained Contact was attenuated and less precise in the Fine-Gray model, which estimates the subdistribution hazard in the presence of competing events. One plausible explanation is that increasing contact reflects emerging cardiac disease that prompts additional encounters before a heart failure hospitalization. More frequent encounters may also increase opportunities for heart failure recognition and coding [34]. The persistence of the association among patients without a previously documented diagnosis does not exclude subclinical or previously unrecognized disease. Taken together, these findings are more consistent with contact intensity acting as a marker of underlying or emerging illness and differential diagnostic ascertainment. These associations may reflect underlying illness and diagnostic ascertainment and should not be interpreted causally.

Finally, the study highlights how cohort construction can influence analyses of retention and follow-up using routinely collected EHR data. [57] Studies of retention frequently define eligibility according to whether patients return for subsequent care, thereby conditioning on the behavior under investigation [16, 17]. In our analyses, restricting the prediction sample to patients with subsequent recorded contact reduced the 270-day prevalence of absent recorded blood pressure follow-up to 9.4%, compared with 27.9% among patients excluded by that restriction. This substantial difference illustrates how requiring later contact can select a population with a markedly different follow-up profile and underestimate the frequency of undocumented follow-up in the broader eligible population. Explicitly distinguishing database coverage, evidence of survival, and death ascertainment makes these selection mechanisms more transparent and provides a framework that may be useful in other single-system EHR studies. Defining the cohort at the index reading, however, does not eliminate selection. The cardiovascular analysis remained conditioned on evidence of survival through the day-730 landmark, while the prediction analysis required 90 days of database coverage after the elevated reading. These design choices define the populations to which the respective findings apply and should be considered when interpreting their generalizability.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, care received outside the study health system was not captured; therefore, the absence of a recorded blood pressure measurement does not necessarily indicate absent follow-up care. The single-center MIMIC-IV population may also limit generalizability. Second, the landmark design selected patients with evidence of survival through day 730, and survival ascertainment required subsequent hospital or emergency department contact. Analyses using day-730 information yielded similar but less precise estimates, although selection related to health care utilization remained. Third, uncertainty in contact-pattern classification, missing clinical measurements, limited socioeconomic data, and unavailable cause of death may have affected estimates. With 664 cardiovascular hospitalization events, moderate associations cannot be excluded. Finally, the prediction model underwent internal validation using a random split and nested cross-validation, without temporal or external validation, and incomplete race and ethnicity data limited the assessment of algorithmic fairness.

Future studies should validate the model temporally and across health systems, evaluate model-guided outreach in randomized or quasi-experimental studies, and investigate approaches to reduce disparities in detection without relying on race-specific thresholds. SUPPLEMENT Supplementary Methods

Cohort definition. MIMIC-IV shifts patient dates and provides calendar time only in three-year groups; the latest hospital and emergency records were from 2020. Database coverage was defined using the latest date guaranteed to be available within each patient's calendar group. Initial eligibility was based on the index criteria and estimated database coverage. Additional survival and record-availability requirements were applied separately to the prediction and landmark analyses, as detailed in Table S9. Overall, 7.3% had an admission beginning on the index date, and 99% of recorded deaths occurred within 355 days of the last hospital or emergency contact.

Prediction targets and model development. Alternative prediction targets were defined as the absence of a blood pressure measurement within 30, 180, 270, or 365 days, each with targetspecific coverage and survival requirements. Competing-risk cumulative incidence of measurement and death before measurement was assessed across predicted-risk fifths. Predictors were restricted to information available at prediction. Training and test assignments were aligned across the 90-and 270-day targets. Logistic regression and tree-based models were tuned using five-fold cross-validation and AUPRC. Imputation and standardization were fitted within training folds; tree models handled missing values directly. Nested cross-validation used five outer folds and three-fold inner tuning, with isotonic recalibration that excluded each patient's own outcome. Subgroup confidence intervals were estimated using 500 bootstrap samples.

Trajectory models.

Trajectory models used quadratic time trends, class-specific means, Gaussian errors, and no random effects. One-to six-class solutions were fitted using 20 random starts. Selection considered the Bayesian information criterion, class size, posterior classification probabilities, and classification odds. The four-class solution was the smallest that distinguished rising from declining contact while meeting prespecified classification criteria. Alternative definitions and expanded cohorts were evaluated in sensitivity analyses without refitting the original model.

Covariates and missing data. Index characteristics, comorbidities, blood pressure, prior encounters, medication counts, and laboratory measures were derived from records available within specified observation windows. Continuous covariates were modeled using restricted cubic splines or linear terms, as appropriate. Multiple imputation incorporated covariates, contact measures, event indicators, and Nelson-Aalen cumulative hazards. Imputed values were restricted to observed-value percentiles, and delta-adjusted imputation and alternative covariate specifications were evaluated in sensitivity analyses.

Gap measure and association analyses.
