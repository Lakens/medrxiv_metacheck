What we found

We found 3 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background: Previous work demonstrated that route previewing improves adaptive gait performance in older adults. However, it is unknown if specific gaze behaviours during previewing are associated with these benefits. Specifically, little is known about how older adults visually scan future walking routes during a given previewing period and whether different gaze behaviours are associated with subsequent walking performance.

Methods: Gaze and gait data were collected from 29 older adults (Mage = 73.9, SD = 8.1 years; n = 19 female) during dual-task adaptive walking, preceded by a 10-second period in which they were instructed to preview their walking route. Eye-tracking measures across defined areas of interest during previewing and subsequent gait measures were analysed using linear and generalized mixedeffects models to determine whether previewing gaze behaviours predicted gait outcomes and cognitive dual-task cost.

Results: Greater gaze sequence complexity (LZC_task) during previewing was associated with lower cognitive dual-task cost during walking, Larger gaze transition sizes predicted slower walking speed while greater duration of distal fixation allocation was associated with an increased likelihood of risky stepping behaviours. No previewing gaze behaviours predicted more than one gait outcome, and no significant relationships were observed between gaze behaviours and stepping errors.

Conclusions: Different aspects of visual previewing were associated with distinct locomotor and cognitive outcomes. Greater gaze-sequence complexity was associated with lower cognitive dual-task cost. In contrast, larger gaze transitions predicted slower walking. Together, these findings suggest that effective previewing may depend on integrating proximal and distal route information into a coherent prospective stepping plan, rather than simply looking further ahead. Understanding how older adults visually plan upcoming routes may help inform approaches to reducing dual-task-related impairments in adaptive walking.

1. Introduction

Adaptive walking relies on using visual information for both feedforward route planning and feedback-based control (Patla, 1997; Reynolds & Day, 2005; Matthis et al., 2016). Older adults appear increasingly reliant on visual input for stability and may perform walking tasks poorly with additional cognitive load that competes for cognitive resources (Pieruccini-Faria et al., 2014; Dominguez-Zamora et al., 2020). When dual-tasking, older adults show more stepping errors (Ellmers et al., 2016; Cocks et al., 2025) likely due to a reduction in attentional resources available for task-relevant visuo-motor control processes (Ellmers et al., 2016). Cognitive dual-tasking has previously been shown to shift gaze towards the immediate walking path and away from future stepping targets (Ellmers, Cocks, & Young, 2020) or other more distant environmental features potentially reducing visuo-motor planning (Zukowski et al., 2021). These findings highlight the importance of identifying strategies that help older adults maintain optimal gaze behaviour even under cognitive load, as this may support safer and more stable adaptive gait.

One such strategy is route previewing. Recent work from our group demonstrated that allowing older adults to preview an adaptive walking route before movement initiation mitigated the negative effects of dual-tasking on stepping performance (McVeigh-Mellor et al., 2026). These findings suggest that previewing reduced the cognitive load associated with online visual search, freeing up attentional resources that can be allocated to secondary task performance (and thereby attenuating dual-task interference). However, this previous study focused primarily on the behavioural consequences of previewing and did not examine participants' gaze behaviour during the previewing period itself. Consequently, it remains unclear whether there are specific gaze behaviours through which previewing supports subsequent gait performance.

Previewing is thought to help older adults map environmental constraints and plan future actions in advance (Curzon-Jones and Hollands, 2018), but this has not yet been explicitly tested. If so, the benefits of previewing may depend not only on where older adults allocate gaze, but on how they scan future stepping constraints (e.g., how much they move their gaze between different areas of the walking path). Most locomotor eye-tracking studies describe visual behaviour by measuring where participants look and for how long for but fail to capture how gaze shifts between these. This sequence of fixations may provide additional insight into how visual information is gathered and organised, which may be particularly important before gait initiation (Amor et al., 2016). For example, gazesequence complexity may reflect how visual information is organised during exploration, with higher entropy indicating less predictable and potentially less structured visual sampling (Shiferaw et al., 2019). However, it is unknown whether the complexity of gaze fixation sequences during previewing, or other characteristics reflecting how visual information is sampled and distributed across the route, are associated with subsequent adaptive walking performance.

The present secondary analysis therefore examined whether specific gaze characteristics during route previewing predict subsequent adaptive gait and cognitive outcomes in older adults. We assessed three complementary aspects of visual exploration: (i) the overall organisation of scanning behaviour, indexed by gaze complexity and maximal gaze transition size; (ii) the extent of visual sampling across future route locations; and (iii) the allocation of visual attention towards distal route information, indexed by the proportion of fixation time on the latter half of the walkway Hypothesis:

Smaller maximal gaze transitions and greater relative proportion of distal fixation duration towards the latter half of the walkway will improve subsequent walking performance.

2. Method

This analysis used gaze and gait data previously collected as part of a larger cross-sectional, experimental study (McVeigh-Mellor et al., 2026) that examines the effects of route previewing on adaptive walking and gaze behaviour under cognitive dual-task conditions in older adults. The study protocol was approved by Brunel University of London's College of Health, Medicine and Life Sciences' Research Ethics Committee (reference: 45431-A-Sep/2024-52891-1).

2.1 Participants

The study sample included twenty-nine community dwelling older adults aged 60-87 years (Mage = 73.9, SD = 8.1) who are a sub-sample from a larger crosssectional study conducted by the research team (McVeigh-Mellor et al., 2026). Participants were recruited through the Brunel Older people's Reference Group, a longstanding local public involvement and engagement group. Participants were eligible if they were aged 60 years or older, had neither a musculoskeletal condition affecting balance nor progressive neurological diagnosis, were able to walk unaided for 10 meters, stand unsupported for at least 2 minutes, and had (corrected-to) normal vision. Participants were excluded if the MoCA score was <22 (Carson et al., 2018).

2.2 Experimental Setup

Please see McVeigh-Mellor et al., (2026) for a comprehensive overview of the study protocol. The protocol was adapted from Cocks et al., (2025). Adaptive walking trials were conducted on an 8-metre instrumented walkway (Zeno Walkway system, ProtoKinetics, Havertown, PA, USA) marked with four coloured 10×10 cm stepping targets (red, blue, yellow and green) that defined different winding routes (see Figure 1). Each route was purposefully designed to have similar complexity. For each condition, all 4 coloured routes were used, with two routes used twice for a total of 6 trials per condition (single and dual-task, with and without previewing), and 24 trials in total. All participants walked the same routes in a pre-set order but never walked the same-coloured route on consecutive trials. Participants were asked to follow a designated coloured path at their preferred walking speed, contacting each target with any part of their foot, but without needing to step precisely on its centre (as in : Cocks et al, 2025). A video camera positioned at the end of the walkway recorded all trials to allow offline coding of stepping errors and cross-steps.

Participants wore mobile eye-tracking glasses (SensoMotoric Instruments, Teltow, Germany) that sampled gaze position at 50 Hz. A scene camera integrated into the frame recorded the participant's field of view at 25 Hz with a resolution of 1920 × 1080 pixels. Prior to each experimental condition, a threepoint calibration was performed. Calibration quality was subsequently verified between trials using a visual target check, with recalibration undertaken if accuracy was deemed insufficient. The walkway was divided into seven task-relevant Areas of Interest (AOIs) spanning the near to distal regions of the route, with an eighth AOI representing task-irrelevant areas outside the walkway (see Fig. 1). Task relevant AOIs included in the analysis contained the stepping targets and were used to quantify visual sampling behaviour during route previewing. For analysis purposes, AOIs 1-3 were classified as near fixations, representing immediately upcoming stepping constraints and the 2/3 step lookahead window previously described by Patla and Vickers (2003) and Matthis & Fajen (2014). AOIs 4-7 were therefore classified as far fixations, representing future route information (see Fig. 1). Fixations were identified using the SMI automated dispersion-based algorithm which identified fixations as groups of consecutive points within a maximum separation of 1 degree of the visual angle, and automated minimum fixation duration of 80ms (Salvucci & Goldberg, 2000) and each fixation was assigned to an AOI for subsequent analysis.

Figure1. Schematic representation of the adaptive walking route. The walkway was divided into seven task-relevant Areas of Interest (AOIs 1-7), extending from proximal to distal (AOI 4-7) locations along the route. The red dashed line indicates the boundary between near and far AOIs. AOI 8 (white) any taskirrelevant areas outside the walkway.

2.3 Walking and Dual-Task Procedure

Participants completed adaptive walking trials under single-task and cognitive dual-task conditions in a set order (McVeigh-Mellor et al., 2026). In dual-task trials, participants performed verbal serial subtraction aloud in steps of seven, beginning from a random three-digit number (100-300) (Hausdorff et al., 2008). A seated 30-second subtraction baseline was completed separately at the end of the walking conditions to compute cognitive dual-task cost. The correct number of responses was divided by trial duration, to determine the correct response rate per second. This allowed direct comparison of cognitive task performance during walking trials and the baseline.

In previewing conditions, participants were first instructed which coloured route to follow while facing away from the walkway. Once told "begin previewing now" participants turned 180° to face the walkway and initiated a 10 second stationary previewing phase (timed using a stopwatch, starting once they had completed their turn and were facing the walkway; based on Curzon-Jones and Hollands (2018). The participants were not explicitly instructed to preview the route in any specific way but simply told to use the 10 seconds to look at the route they would be walking, allowing individuals to spontaneously adopt their own previewing strategy. Following a "Stop previewing" instruction, they turned away from the walkway, received the serial subtraction number to start from (dual-task only) followed by a "Go" cue to commence walking and counting. This ensured that previewing occurred before any cognitive load was introduced. The same procedure (bar the use of the dual-task instruction) was used for single-task conditions to ensure the turning away and back to the walkway procedure was consistent across conditions.

2.4 Outcome measures 2.4.1 Data Preparation

We only included data from (i) participants who had sufficient data available for ≥4 trials in the dual-task previewing condition and only included (ii) trials for which a minimum of 50% of the identified fixations had position data (Ellmers et al., 2016). In total, 10 participants from the original dataset (N=39) were therefore excluded from the analysis; three participants were excluded due to complete failure to capture eye-tracker data due to operator error or calibration failure. For the remaining 7 participants, fixation position could not be identified for >50% of trial duration of >4 trials, leaving 29 participants' data to be analysed.

2.4.2 Gaze Variables

Only task-relevant fixations (fixations to the walkway) were included in the analysis of the gaze behaviour during the previewing phase. We used Lempel-Ziv complexity (LZC_task) to quantify the complexity of each participants' gaze behaviour by measuring how many patterns were encountered within the given previewing period (Lempel & Ziv, 1976). To compute LZC_task, AOI fixation sequence was parsed sequentially, each time a new subsequence appeared it was added to an index of possible patterns, with the total number of unique patterns reflecting the sequence's complexity (Lounis et al., 2020). This count was then normalised by sequence length to allow comparison across signals to summarise how varied the sequence of gaze locations was during the preview period. Higher LZC_task values indicate greater randomness or unpredictability, whereas lower values reflect a more repetitive structure (Harris et al., 2023). This measure has previously been used to distinguish differences in visual scanning behaviour between novice and experienced pilots (Lounis et al., 2020). The maximum AOI transition size was used to describe the largest shift in gaze between route regions. Larger transitions may indicate that participants display less granular visual sampling of the route. Maximum AOI transition size per trial (MaxAOI_Transition) was calculated as the largest change between consecutive fixations within the previewing phase (calculated as the change from one AOI to the next [e.g., a transition from AOI 1 to AOI4 would be noted as a change size of 3]). Distal allocation of gaze (FarFix) was quantified as the percentage of total fixation duration directed at the furthest areas (AOI 4-7) of the walkway (see Fig. 1). FarFix was selected as the primary fixation metric because it was considered to most directly reflect distal visual previewing. Greater percentage of fixation duration beyond the immediate walkway was expected to reflect the acquisition of information relevant to future stepping actions.

2.4.3 Performance variables

Walking and cognitive performance were assessed using four dependent variables. Walking speed (m/s per trial), step errors (number of missed route targets per trial), cross-step proportion (rate of cross-steps per trial, calculated as number of cross-steps divided by number of total steps). Cross-steps were defined as steps in which one foot crossed the midline or lateral border of the contralateral stance foot (Cocks et al., 2025) and are considered a potential marker of unsafe stepping strategies particularly for older adults at risk of falls (Cocks et al., 2025). Cross-step behaviour was analysed using a two-part approach: firstly, a binary measure (cross-step occurrence) indicating whether a cross-step occurred in a given trial, and (ii) a continuous cross-step proportion measure for trials in which cross-stepping occurred. Motor (speed) and cognitive dual-task cost (DTC) were included as a measure of performance during walking, cognitive DTC was measured as proportional change in response rate from seated single-task baseline serial subtraction to dual-task waking with serial subtraction in the previewing condition (Single-task -Dual-task preview) / Singletask x 100 per trial) (Montero-Odasso et al., 2012).

2.4.4 Statistical Analysis

All analyses were conducted in MATLAB (MathWorks, Natick, MA) using restricted maximum likelihood estimation for linear mixed-effects models and maximum pseudo-likelihood estimation for generalized linear mixed-effects models. Statistical significance was set at p < .05. Linear mixed-effects modelling was used to examine associations between previewing gaze behaviour and adaptive walking performance outcomes in the dual-task previewing condition. Mixed-effects approaches were selected because the dataset contained repeated trial-level observations nested within participants, requiring models capable of accounting for within-subject dependency while retaining trial-level variability. Participant was therefore included as a random intercept in all models. 1Cross-step behaviour was analysed using a two-part modelling approach. Initially, to understand if these gaze-related variables were associated with the emergence of this stepping strategy, cross-step occurrence was examined using a generalized linear mixed-effects model with a binomial distribution and logit link function, where trials were coded according to whether a cross-step occurred (0 = no cross-step,1 = cross-step present). Subsequently to further probe the strength of this association the occurrence frequency of cross-steps was examined using a linear mixed-effects model restricted to trials in which crossstepping occurred, to investigate whether previewing variables predicted the frequency of cross-step behaviour.

Separate linear mixed-effects models were constructed for walking speed, step errors, and cognitive DTC. Cognitive DTC values (n=6) exceeding ±300% were excluded prior to analysis because these values were considered likely to reflect very small single-task denominators rather than meaningful behavioural variation. A sensitivity analysis retaining these extreme values was conducted to assess whether their exclusion materially affected the conclusions.

4. Discussion

Previous work has shown that route previewing can reduce dual-task related gait impairments in older adults (McVeigh-Mellor et al., 2026). The present analysis explored the gaze characteristics underlying these behavioural benefits by examining which specific gaze behaviours during previewing predict subsequent gait and cognitive performance under dual-task conditions. We hypothesised efficient visual exploration, characterised by smaller maximal gaze transitions and greater relative proportion of distal fixation duration towards the latter half of the walkway, would support feedforward planning and improve subsequent walking performance. Further, gaze-sequence complexity was explored as a novel indicator of visual planning whereby greater complexity was predicted to be associated with worse walking performance.

Notably, larger maximal gaze shifts (i.e. greater transition size of consecutive fixations between AOIs) predicted slower walking speed, but were unrelated to stepping errors or cross-stepping. This suggests that making larger gaze shifts during previewing may reduce the efficiency of route encoding before movement begins. Larger transition size also predicted greater walking speed dual-task costs, indicating that larger gaze shifts during previewing were associated with greater dual-task-related reductions in subsequent walking speed. Participants who shifted gaze between widely separated areas of interest may have encoded the route as a series of isolated stepping locations rather than as a continuous sequence of future foot placements, leading to a more cautious walking strategy (Boschker et al., 2002). This interpretation is consistent with evidence that the organisation of visual search can be behaviourally meaningful. Amor et al. (2016) examined the temporal organisation of eye movements and found that participants adopted a systematic reading-like scanning strategy. Therefore, how gaze progresses between locations, may provide information about the organisation of visual exploration, beyond simply where fixations are directed. More specifically within locomotion research, effective feedforward control has been shown to depend on sampling terrain within an optimal look-ahead window of approximately two step lengths (Patla & Vickers, 2003; Matthis, 2018). If route previewing facilitates the formation of action-relevant chunks (Boschker et al., 2002), large transitions between distant route locations may disrupt the sequential organisation of these chunks, leading to slower subsequent walking speed as previously reported (McVeigh-Mellor et al., 2026). However, maximum transition size alone does not provide information about the broader organisation of visual exploration. In this context a large transition may also reflect a purposeful shift to inspect distal route features before returning to more proximal locations. Consequently, caution is warranted when interpreting larger transitions as inherently less efficient. It is possible that the benefits of previewing depend less on the availability of preview and more specifically on how route information is encoded into a prospective movement plan before gait initiation (Pezzulo et al., 2010).

Among all gaze measures examined, only gaze-sequence complexity was associated with cognitive dual-task cost. This suggests that the organisation of visual exploration during previewing may be particularly relevant to the cognitive demands experienced once walking begins. In the present study, greater gaze sequence complexity (LZC_task) was associated with lower cognitive dual-task costs alone, not with differences in gait performance itself. These results may suggest that scan-path organisation primarily influenced the cognitive resources required for route retention rather than the direct control of stepping movements itself (Ellmers et al., 2016). Because working memory resources are shared across locomotor and cognitive processes, this benefit may have emerged primarily as reduced cognitive dual-task cost. A potential interpretation is that more complex visual exploration facilitated the construction of an internal route representation, potentially reducing the visuospatial working-memory demands associated with route retention and online planning during walking (Baddeley, 2003; Pezzulo et al., 2010). It is possible that sampling the route in a more complex manner supported gait planning/performance by reducing the online demands associated with this, thus increasing the relative attentional capacity available for the secondary cognitive task.

Contrary to our hypothesis, greater distal fixation allocation was not associated with improvements in gait or cognitive dual-task performance. Instead, greater distal previewing increased the likelihood of cross-step occurrence.. This finding was unexpected; in our previous work we showed that the presence of a cognitive dual-task increased the frequency of cross-stepping, (McVeigh-Mellor et al., 2026). Therfore, we expected distal previewing to reduce the occurrence of cross-steps as an automatic stepping response when attentional resources were divided. This association between distan fixation and cross-step frequency suggests that the availability of advance visual information has a less distinct relationship with subsequent stepping behaviour.The current finding could be consistent with Cocks et al., (2025), who demonstrated that reduced cognitive resources increase reliance on risky automatic stepping strategies in older adults. However, participants who allocated a greater proportion of gaze towards distal route features during previewing remained more likely to adopt cross-steps, indicating substantial individual variability in how previewed information was incorporated into locomotor planning. Instead, in the context of previewing, looking further ahead may reflect the detection of perceived route challenge rather than more effective feedforward planning. This is not dissimilar with findings from climbing research demonstrating that visual exploration increases when participants encountered environmental features that approach the limits of their action capabilities, reflecting a search for functional movement solutions rather than straightforward route planning (Van Knobelsdorff et al., 2020). Pezzulo et al. (2010) proposed that successful previewing involves evaluating how each upcoming movement constrains the next, while Russo et al. (2025) demonstrated that performing serial-7 subtraction while walking reduced fixations on task-relevant stepping targets and restricted visual exploration between proximal and distal areas of the walkway suggesting that cognitive load may constrain the visual search processes required for feedforward gait planning. Together, these findings suggest that the behavioural benefits of previewing depend on efficiently integrating visual information across the upcoming route to support feedforward locomotor planning, rather than simply increasing attention towards more distant stepping locations.

An important observation from the present study was that no single gaze characteristic consistently predicted all behavioural outcomes. Instead, larger gaze transitions predicted walking speed, greater distal fixation allocation predicted cross-step occurrence, and gaze-sequence complexity predicted cognitive dual-task cost indicating that different aspects of previewing were selectively associated with different dimensions of adaptive walking. This pattern aligns with theoretical accounts of visually guided locomotion, which propose that vision supports multiple complementary functions including environmental sampling, route planning and movement regulation rather than a single visual strategy (Patla, 1997; Reynolds & Day, 2005; Matthis et al., 2017).

4.1 Limitations

Ten participants (25.6% of the recruited sample) were excluded from the present analysis due to insufficient eye-tracking data. Although data-quality-related participant loss is a recognised challenge in eye-tracking research (Holmqvist et al., 2023), the resulting reduction in sample size and potential for exclusion bias should be acknowledged. The Cognitive DTC findings were robust to the exclusion of extreme DTC values, as retaining these observations did not alter the overall pattern of statistical inference (supplementary data Table 1). However, coefficient estimates and standard errors were substantially inflated when extreme values were retained, supporting caution when interpreting percentage-based DTC measures where small single-task denominators can generate extreme values. Interpretation of fixation duration remains complex, as prolonged viewing may reflect either effective information acquisition or uncertainty-driven monitoring. The absence of associations between distal fixation duration (FarFix) and gait outcomes suggests that duration alone is not a reliable indicator of previewing efficiency in this context. Nieuwenhuys (2008) discussed how in the investigation of professional climbers more fixations did not necessarily mean better visual behaviour, instead it reflected reduced processing efficiency. This is consistent with evidence from visual expertise research indicating that successful performance depends less on the amount of time spent viewing particular locations than on selectively directing gaze towards taskrelevant information (Gegenfurtner et al., 2011). Together, these findings suggest that the organisation and relevance of visual sampling may be more informative than fixation duration alone. Although, we cannot determine what information was acquired during preview or how participants used it while walking. The fixed route may also limit generalisability of findings, as it is unclear if these findings could apply to navigation tasks where participants can choose which route to follow (rather than this being prescribed).

Finally, this analysis focused solely on pre-walking previewing, it did not attempt to determine how previewed information interacts with online gaze behaviour during adaptive gait. Although the observational design limits causal inference, previous research has demonstrated that gaze training can modify visual search behaviour and improve aspects of motor performance in other populations (Wood et al., 2017; Gunn et al., 2019). These findings suggest that targeted gaze training may be feasible, although its effectiveness for improving adaptive gait in healthy older adults remains uncertain. Future research could investigate whether prompting older adults to systematically integrate proximal and distal route information during previewing improves subsequent stepping performance, particularly during cognitive loading conditions.

5. Conclusion

Overall, the findings show that different aspects of older adults' visual previewing behaviour support different locomotor and cognitive outcomes. Importantly, higher gaze-sequence complexity was linked to lower cognitive dual-task cost, suggesting that flexible and information-rich visual exploration may increase availability of cognitive resources during subsequent locomotion. Larger gaze transition sizes during previewing predicted slower walking speed, while greater duration of distal fixation allocation was associated with an increased likelihood of cross-step occurrence. Together, these findings may indicate that effective previewing depends not simply on sampling more distant route information (i.e. planning further ahead), but on integrating proximal and distal route information into a useful sequence of upcoming stepping actions. Large shifts between areas of interest, or preferential allocation of gaze towards distal areas, may be less beneficial when this information is not integrated with the intervening step/path information. When considered alongside previous findings that route previewing reduces dual-task costs, understanding how older adults visually plan upcoming walking routes may help inform interventions aimed at improving adaptive walking and reducing dual-task-related adaptive walking performance.
