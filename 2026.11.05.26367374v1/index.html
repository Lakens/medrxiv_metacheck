To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 2 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Seasonal influenza follows annual cycles, but variation in epidemic magnitude and timing complicates preparedness. We applied periodic mean-reverting stochastic differential equations (SDEs) to seven seasons of weekly Texas influenza surveillance data. Missing observations were imputed using MissForest and compared with four alternative methods. Stochastic variability was modeled as σpX(t) p dW (t), with p fixed at 1/2, 2/3, 5/6, and 1, representing square-root, intermediate sublinear, and multiplicative diffusion. Each specification was evaluated for direct influenza modeling and within a susceptible-exposed-infectious-recovered model incorporating waning immunity, where the SDE governed transmission. Parameters were estimated using least squares and approximate maximum likelihood for direct models and sample-proportion weighted least squares for stochastic SEIR models. Model-based parametric bootstrap assessed direct-model parameter uncertainty. For direct modeling, p = 1/2 achieved the lowest RMSE (712.8) and AIC (5383.0), with similar AIC for p = 2/3 (5383.2). For stochastic SEIR modeling, p = 1 achieved the lowest RMSE (0.02926), compared with 0.03506 for p = 1/2. The deterministic SEIR model yielded R0 = 1.158. Transmission peaked approximately 10 weeks before infectious prevalence, while stochastic transmission generated variation in epidemic peak timing. Bootstrap analysis identified dependence between mean-reversion and diffusion parameters. Overall, the appropriate diffusion structure depended on the epidemiological quantity modeled, supporting flexible characterization of seasonal transmission variability while requiring prospective validation before operational forecasting.

1 Introduction

Seasonal influenza remains a major global public health challenge, causing substantial morbidity, mortality, and healthcare burden each year [2, 26]. Influenza epidemics exhibit strong annual periodicity, with predictable seasonal peaks driven by climatic conditions, host behavior, viral evolution, and population immunity, e.g., [47, 54, 73]. Despite these recurring patterns, epidemic timing, magnitude, and duration vary considerably because of stochastic environmental, demographic, and epidemiological factors. Accurate characterization of both seasonal structure and random variation is therefore essential for understanding disease transmission, improving outbreak forecasting, evaluating intervention strategies, and informing public health preparedness. Statistical and mechanistic models that jointly capture periodicity and stochasticity provide a robust framework for influenza surveillance and epidemiological inference.

Analyzing complex periodic data is essential for identifying and understanding recurring patterns in time series data, providing valuable insights into trends, seasonality, and anomalies [27]. Effective mathematical models for simulating systems that naturally exhibit randomness over time are stochastic differential equations (SDEs). SDEs have a long history in finance and economics, e.g., [13, 21, 42, 51, 65]. More recently, they been applied to ecology, epidemiology, and physiology [4, 30, 34, 35, 52, 58]. Mean-reverting SDE models that account for seasonal fluctuations are common in financial economics, energy markets, and commodity price modeling, e.g., [28, 29, 61, 53]. Also, mean-reverting SDEs with fluctuations in the mean reversion level have been fit directly to temperature and seasonal influenza data, e.g., [54, 78].

Seasonal disease forecasting relies on recognizing periodic patterns, most notably annual or subannual cycles driven by climate, host behavior, and pathogen biology, which shape predictable peaks such as winter influenza or rainy-season malaria [6, 31, 68]. However, it is even more critical to address stochasticity, since random fluctuations in weather, human behavior, pathogen evolution, and reporting noise can dramatically alter outbreak timing and size [62]. Ignoring this randomness leads to overconfident predictions, whereas stochastic models provide more realistic forecasts with uncertainty bounds, making them far more useful for guiding public health decisions.

A variety of statistical and mathematical approaches have been used to model seasonal influenza dynamics. Important in the statistical approaches are the inclusion of data to test reliability of modeling outcomes. Discrete time-series models such as a seasonal autoregressive integrated moving average model capture autocorrelation and seasonal variation in weekly influenza incidence [14, 38]. Inclusion of exogenous predictors, such as meteorological variables, Google Trends, and mobility data, improve forecasting accuracy [80]. Although computationally efficient for short-term prediction, these approaches remain primarily descriptive and lack epidemic stages [14]. Structural and state-space models extend classical time-series methods by distinguishing disease processes from noisy observations [25]. Dynamic linear and Bayesian state-space models have been widely applied to influenza surveillance, accommodating measurement error, missing data, and multiple surveillance streams [36, 37, 75], with Kalman and particle filtering enabling efficient real-time inference [66]. Likewise, Bayesian methods provide a framework for uncertainty quantification and data integration, supporting hierarchical surveillance models, transmission parameter estimation, and probabilistic epidemic forecasting [2, 15, 64, 76]. Because influenza incidence is observed as count data, generalized linear models (GLMs) are also widely used to estimate disease burden, characterize seasonality, and assess environmental and demographic risk factors [17, 36, 50]. However, these discrete-time methods do not model epidemic dynamics explicitly.

Many deterministic and stochastic mathematical models have captured some of the complex temporal and spatial dynamics of seasonal influenza in specific settings, e.g. [7, 10, 12, 16, 24, 26, 40, 47, 49, 57, 69, 73, 77, 79]. The majority of these epidemic models have an underlying Susceptible, Exposed, Infectious, and Recovered (SEIR) structure that include important characteristics of seasonal influenza spread and prevention (seasonal variability, waning immunity, multiple circulating viral types/subtypes, and vaccination). Some of the earliest mathematical investigations on seasonal infectious diseases applied SIR ordinary differential equations (ODEs) with a periodic transmission rate to study recurrent outbreaks of childhood diseases [9, 23]. A review of seasonal modeling studies by Altizer et al. [6] provides a comprehensive review of models for the spread of human and animal infectious diseases and presents some of the challenges in defining the seasonal drivers. To our knowledge, there has been no epidemic modeling applications using seasonal surveillance data to estimate parameters for periodic mean-reverting SDEs in epidemic models. However, a few studies have applied constant mean-reverting SDEs in parameters for disease transmission, death rate, or vector emergence in SEIR/SIR or epidemiological vector-host models to verify compatibility with routinely collected surveillance data [16, 49, 56, 63, 69].

Estimation of the mean-reverting parameters from discrete observations is important and can be facilitated by various numerical methods such as Euler-Maruyama and likelihood-based approaches [3, 39, 41, 72]. Other estimation methods include method of moments [3, 33] and filtering [55]. All of these methods are computationally intensive as they require a large number of numerically simulated sample paths. Among the most well-known mean-reverting diffusion process is the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) process, which models random fluctuations around a long-term equilibrium with a Gaussian distributed density [42].

Mean-reverting SDEs provide a useful stochastic framework for modeling epidemic dynamics as well as seasonal variability and long-term behavior, making them particularly well suited for studying influenza dynamics beyond short-term forecasting. Also, the time-continuous mean-reverting process may account for temporal correlation in social behavior of individuals [49]. In the stochastic SIR/SEIR literature, periodic mean-reverting processes have been largely unexplored. Only in a simplified setting, without the epidemic structure, were parameters for mean-reversion and volatility fit directly to influenza data [54]. Therefore, our primary goals are to fill this gap, to provide a foundational study in epidemiological modeling by extending this research to several new seasonal mean-reverting processes, formulating a new SEIR compartmental model with seasonal mean-reverting transmission, and fitting the mean-reversion and volatility parameters of these models to seasonal influenza data. In the next section, we define and discuss the properties of the periodic mean-reverting SDEs.

A key limitation of the OU process is that its stationary distribution is normal with unbounded support, which means the process does not remain strictly positive and can cross into negative values even when centered around a positive mean [22]. This feature makes the OU process unsuitable in settings where the underlying quantity is naturally nonnegative.

Nonnegative solutions are an important property in biological systems. If β(t) ≡ β is constant with p ∈ (1/2, 1], then X(t) > 0 for t > 0 and if p = 1/2, then X(t) ≥ 0 for t > 0 [42]. The SDEs in Equation (1) with positive and periodic β(t) and the state-dependent diffusion terms have nonnegative solutions provided r p β(t) + β ′ (t) ≥ 0 [5]. To ensure the numerical methods generate nonnegative sample paths, we set X(t) = 0, if X(t) ≤ 0 for t > 0.

Our bootstrap-based confidence regions reflect how sensitive parameter estimation is to this nonlinear structure, and help distinguish between models with similar drift but differing stochastic dynamics.

4.2 Confidence Ellipses for Model Parameters

Assuming that the SDEs in Equation (1) are the data-generating models for the original data, we apply the parameter estimates in Table 1 to test these models. One hundred sample paths for seven years are generated from each of the four SDEs in Equation (1) (p = 1/2, 2/3, 5/6, 1) to obtain 100 estimates for the five model parameters.

Parameter uncertainty was quantified using a model-based parametric bootstrap designed to preserve the temporal dependence induced by the fitted SDE. Let θ = ( a 1 , a 2 , a 3 , r p , σ p ) ⊤ denote the parameter vector estimated from the observed time series. Conditional on θ, we generated B = 100 independent bootstrap trajectories for T = 7 years from the fitted SDE model.

Thus, the bootstrap is based on simulated stochastic paths rather than on resampling individual residuals or observations. For each bootstrap replication b, the initial condition was randomly selected from the corresponding annual starting values of the observed series, X * (b) 0 ∈ {X 0 , X 52 , X 104 , . . .}, thereby allowing uncertainty associated with the initial state to contribute to the bootstrap distribution. Conditional on this initial value, a complete seven-year trajectory was generated using Milstein's numerical scheme [3] with integration step ∆t = 1/520. The simulated trajectory was subsequently sampled at weekly intervals, t j = j/52, j = 0, . . . , 364, so that each bootstrap dataset had the same sampling frequency and duration as the observed series. Importantly, observations within a bootstrap series were not independently resampled. Instead, each trajectory was generated recursively from the fitted stochastic process. The shift in the peaks in the influenza data from December/January to February/March may be due to a shift in the dominant viral type or subtype (A H1N1/H3N2 or B).

As a final example, we compare sample paths of I(t) for the mean-reverting SEIR SDE model and the mean-reverting SDE model from Section 4. In the left panel of Figure 8 is graphed the influenza data. The center panel is a sample path of I(t) for the mean-reverting SEIR SDE and in the right panel for the mean-reverting SDE X(t). One obvious difference between these two models is the variability. Sample paths of X(t) are highly variable and those of the SEIR SDE I(t) are smooth. The variability in the influenza data lies between these two extremes. Although, the SEIR SDE I(t) does not always reach the peaks, the variability in the timing of the peaks, the range of the peaks, and the stages in the structured SEIR model provide a realistic and meaningful setting for interpretation of the epidemiological results over many seasons.

6 Discussion

The novel periodic mean-reverting SDEs in Equation ( 1) provide a realistic framework for capturing seasonal variation in a variety of environmental and biological systems. Although such models are common in financial and economic applications, e.g. [28, 29, 61, 53], they have not been investigated in an epidemic setting.

This investigation focuses on periodic mean-reverting SDEs that incorporate state-dependent diffusion which effectively accounts for the complexities of seasonal processes and ensures solutions are nonnegative. Seasonal influenza data are used to estimate model parameters in two different settings. First, the meanreverting processes in Equation ( 1) are fit directly to the seasonal influenza data, and second, they are used as a model for the transmission rate, where the infectious cases I in an SEIR epidemic model are fit to the influenza data.

In the first setting, the Euler-Maruyama approximation provides a stable computationally efficient likelihood-based framework for estimation of parameters when exact transition densities are unavailable. For the more complex SEIR structured models, a sample proportion weighted least squares method (SP-WLS), fit to the proportional data, helps to ensure the peaks of the seasons are adequately represented. Because the influenza observations are available at weekly intervals, some discretizations error may nevertheless remain. Alternative approaches include quasi-maximum likelihood and continuous-discrete filtering methods, such as the extended Kalman filter, which are particularly useful for discretely observed continuous-time stochastic processes [28, 44, 55]. These methods provide potential extensions for assessing sensitivity of parameter estimates to the transition-density approximation, while the Euler-Maruyama and weighted least squares approaches adopted here offer transparent and consistent frameworks for comparing the alternative diffusion specifications.

Understanding the dynamics of the periodic mean-reverting SDE in Equation ( 1) and the relation to the parameter estimates are important because it directly informs how uncertainty and stability evolve in the modeled system. The diffusion term σ p X p governs the strength of the stochastic perturbations, increasing markedly as p increases (Figure 4) indicating that the system becomes more sensitive to random fluctuations. This has significant implications for modeling biological and physical systems where noise plays a critical role. Higher values of p (close to one) may represent regimes of reduced predictability or control. Furthermore, the observed decline in the correlation between r p and σ p for midrange values of p = 2/3, 5/6 suggests a decoupling of the systems' reversion dynamics from its stochastic volatility, allowing for more precise tuning of each component.

Through leverage of bootstrapping techniques, we constructed confidence regions for parameter estimation, improving the robustness of inference and revealing trends in parameter relations. The demonstrated ability to fit parameters of seasonal mean-reverting SDEs and to obtain confidence regions for the parameters, enhances the flexibility and applicability of these time series models.

The transmission rate in epidemiological models is one of the most common parameters to estimate but the parameters for mean reversion r p and volatility σ p are hard to quantify in epidemiological terms. In a COVID-19 epidemic model with a constant mean-reverting transmission, the mean-reversion rate was interpreted as a response to restrictions (e.g., social distancing and masks) imposed during the pandemic [63]. Our model was able to capture some of the variability seen in the influenza data, including the timing and variability of the peaks and the average annual seasonality (Figures 6 and 7). In addition, the SEIR model showed that the peak transmission rate can precede the peak influenza cases by several months (Figure 6, right panel); it was approximately 10 weeks from the peak of β(t) to the peak of the ODE I(t). This has implications for public health practitioners, highlighting the need for early interventions. Also, as shown in Figure 3, the parameters r p and σ p can be highly correlated. In the best fit models, the SEIR SDE model has a longer average time to return to the mean, 364/r1 ≈ 37.3 days (p = 1) versus the direct fit of the mean-reverting SDE Equation (1) to the data, which has a return time of 364/r 1/2 ≈ 28.4 days (p = 1/2). A longer duration allows for a longer span of time above or below the mean. Model fit can be improved with more data on the impact of social behavior and climate variability on mean reversion. Also, data on the circulating viral types and subtypes (A H1N1/H3N2, B), the duration of immunity, and the vaccine coverage will improve model predictability, e.g., [8, 19, 26, 45, 73]. This foundational study of periodic mean-reverting processes serves as a basis for further investigation of more complex structured epidemic models and for application of alternative statistical methods to fit long-term data sets that capture the important features of seasonality. Insights from these models are essential for calibrating parameters, enabling better forecasts, risk assessment, and control strategies in complex stochastic environments.

Moreover, an important consideration is the potential identifiability between the deterministic seasonal component and stochastic diffusion component, since both can explain temporal variation in influenza activity. In particular, excessive flexibility in the periodic mean may absorb short-term stochastic variation, whereas a large diffusion coefficient may obscure the underlying seasonal signal. Our two-stage estimation strategy helps separate these components by first estimating the systematic periodic structure and subsequently estimating the stochastic parameters conditional on that structure. Nevertheless, uncertainty in the estimated seasonal component may propagate to the diffusion parameter estimates, motivating sensitivity and bootstrap analyses.

Overall, in public health, the ability to anticipate and respond to disease outbreaks hinges not only on recognizing the seasonal cycles that shape predictable patterns of transmission, but also on effectively incorporating stochastic models that capture uncertainty and variability in real-world dynamics. By embracing stochastic approaches, epidemiology moves beyond rigid forecasts to provide probabilistic insights that reflect the inherent randomness of disease spread, offering health authorities better tools for risk assessment, preparedness, and resource allocation. These new approaches help ensure that interventions are guided by a realistic understanding of both expected seasonal trends and the unpredictable shocks that often define epidemic behavior. 3.
