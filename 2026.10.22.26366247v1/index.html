How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Full text

Abstract

Anemia affects over two billion people globally. Screening individuals for anemia in community settings currently involves blood sampling, either a capillary or venous blood sample for running a point of care test or a laboratory grade blood test for hemoglobin, respectively. The need for a blood sample, and laboratory equipment and accessories are barriers to widespread screening for anemia in lower resource settings. We present a non-invasive, artificial intelligence (AI)-driven method for measuring hemoglobin (Hb) and detecting anemia using smartphone-captured images. We collected images of conjunctivae, as well as demographic and clinical data from patients seeking care at SEWA Rural hospital in rural, indigenous population of Gujarat, India. Ground truth Hb values were obtained using venous blood on a Sysmex XN-550 autoanalyser. Age and gender specific WHO reference cutoffs were used for classification of anemia severity. The dataset (n=5,176) was partitioned (70:15:15 ratio) for training (n = 3,623), validation (n = 776) and testing (n = 777). The deep learning based AI model used age, gender, pregnancy status and images of conjunctivae and predicted Hb values which were thresholded for various anemia classification tasks. Overall Hb prediction mean absolute error was 1.12 http://g/dL. The sensitivity and specificity of the model for classification of any anemia were 70% (95%CI 66-74) and 70% (95%CI 64-76) respectively. Sensitivity (90%, 95% CI 82-94) and specificity (80%, 95% CI 77-83) were higher for detecting severe anemia versus mild, . moderate, or no anemia. The accuracy of the model was sustained in a variety of lighting conditions, skin tone and smartphone types. Our findings demonstrate the viability of smartphone based detection of anemia, especially severe anemia, in lower-resource settings and motivate further improvements and validation for use in community settings. The dataset comprising conjunctiva, tongue, nail bed images and smartphone-captured photoplethysmogram (PPG) signal is also released for community research use.

Introduction

Anemia is a global health problem characterized by insufficient blood hemoglobin (Hb) concentrations to meet physiological needs (1). It disproportionately impacts vulnerable populations: globally, 40% of children under five years of age, 37% of pregnant women, and 30% of all women of reproductive age are affected (2, 3, 4). In India, the burden is particularly high, with the National Family Health Survey (NFHS-5) reporting prevalence rates of anemia as high as 67% in children and 57% in women (5).

Despite its high prevalence, anemia remains underdiagnosed particularly in lower resource settings, due to the lack of awareness, the relative inaccuracy of clinical features, and challenges of accessing hemoglobin tests using automated hematology analysers (6, 7, 8).

There is strong evidence for interventions to correct anemia when it is detected, underscoring the value of detection particularly among women and children (9, 10). Although several point of care tests using fingerstick capillary blood samples are in use, these invasive methods require skilled personnel, supply-chain logistics, and reagent stability across diverse environmental conditions, hindering their scaling and sustained implementation in rural healthcare settings (11) (12) (13) (14) (15). Non-invasive hemoglobin estimation methods therefore potentially improves the last mile reach of hemoglobin testing, and could be effective tools in anemia control programmes in low and middle income countries (16).

The widespread use of smartphones has prompted several efforts to utilize sensors such as the camera to detect pallor in various areas of the body as a way of estimating Hb (16). However, research in this area has been limited by small, homogenous datasets (often of limited availability to the wider research community) or lack rigorous clinical laboratory reference standards; these have produced encouraging but limited evidence of accuracy (17) (18) (19) (20) (21) (22) (23) (24). This study addresses these gaps by first developing a multimodal AI model trained on a large, rural Indian population, and second introducing and making available the associated dataset 25.

Methods

Study Design & Setting

A prospective cross-sectional study was conducted in rural indigenous areas in Gujarat, India by the Society for Education, Welfare and Action-Rural (SEWA Rural), a voluntary development organization involved in health and development activities. The SEWA Rural hospital delivers outpatient and inpatient secondary level health services for adults and children across a range of specialties, and is accessed by individuals from more than 2,500 surrounding villages.

Before initiating the study, expectations about the target product profile, consisting of performance of eventual model and solution design were elicited from multiple stakeholders. These stakeholders included state program officers and public health practitioners and not for profit organizations. These deliberations, informed by other organizations involved in this area, concluded that given the more accessible and non-invasive nature of the target smartphone AI technology, sensitivity and specificity of at least 80% each for various categories of anemia, especially for severe anemia, would meet minimum performance for meaningful use (26). The model also should provide predictions within 30 seconds of inputs, preferably without internet connectivity, and the inputs required for obtaining predictions should be feasible for a community health worker to obtain in community settings after minimal training using a range of low cost smartphones.

Participants and sampling

Eligible participants were adults, pregnant women and children (1 to 89 years of age) who had a complete blood count (CBC) ordered by a clinician during routine outpatient and inpatient care. Once CBC results were available to the research team, a list of potentially eligible participants were approached and informed about the study in a language of their choice. All participants (and parents/caregivers, where appropriate) provided written informed consent.

Potential participants were selected from four categories of Hb level (normal, and mild, moderate, and severe anemia) based on the CBC result, with attempts were made to enrich for patients with severe anemia to improve data diversity for AI model development. Participants who had any material on nailbeds (e.g henna) which could not be removed, or who could not provide consent for the study, were not enrolled.

After the development of the final model, its performance was assessed on both the held-out test set (described below) and a field-representative validation dataset. This field-representative validation dataset included images obtained in settings within SEWA Rural hospital campus that mimic community settings by the same set of research staff using the same aforementioned procedures. The settings within SEWA Rural hospital campus included various indoor and outdoor sites with a variety of lighting, rooms, and wall colors. The participants were selected in the same manner as for the training, validation & testing datasets.

Procedures

Participant demographics and medical history, including details of health care encounter, pregnancy status and presence of symptoms were collected by research staff based on self-report (or from parent/caregivers) after obtaining an informed consent. A member of research staff measured vital signs including height and weight, temperature (Medtech TMP11 Portable Digital Thermometer, Medtech Life Pvt Ltd, Mumbai, India), heart rate and oxygen saturation (OXYGARD, Pulse Oximeter OG 05, Medtech Life Pvt Ltd, Mumbai, India), and blood pressure (adults only) (Novacheck BP-18, Medtech Life Pvt Ltd, Mumbai, India). Skin tone was measured on the forehead using a spectrophotometer (CAPSURE, Pantone RM200QC Portable Spectrocolorimeter , Panton, Carlstadt, NJ, USA), whereby the L* and b* parameters were converted to an individual typology angle (ITA°) as defined by the function [arctan(L* -50)/b*] x 180/3.14159 (27). Further, based on the ITA° values, skin tones were categorized as very light (>55°), light (41° to <55°), intermediate (28° to <41°), tan (10° to <28°), brown (-30° to <10°), and dark (<-30°). This standardized classification was used to categorize participants according to their measured skin pigmentation. The research staff also examined the conjunctivae, tongue and nail beds for signs of anemia; subsequently, the staff noted the presence or absence of anemia based on their clinical examination. Data collection occurred in an interior room where ambient lighting was recorded daily (TESTO 540 Pocket sized Luxmeter, Testo, West Chester, USA). Data was recorded using a digital data collection tool to improve data consistency, completeness and quality.

For each patient, one of three smartphones were used for data collection: Samsung A05, Samsung A14 & Samsung S24 (Samsung Electronics Co Ltd, Suwon, South Korea) to provide a range of image quality based on device cost (low to high respectively). Investigators obtained images of conjunctiva from one eye while the participant gently pulled their lower eyelid, tongue in a protruding manner, fingernail views (from any one hand) with the hand pronated flat on a surface ("open hand") as well as supinated with fingers gently flexed("closed hand"), and a 30 second video of photoplethysmogram (PPG) from the index finger obtained with finger placed over the camera. A sheet of white paper was used as a reference object and placed above the eyeball while taking an image or underneath the hand for fingernail images (Appendix 1). The camera flash was used consistently for all image acquisitions, irrespective of ambient lighting conditions, to provide a more uniform illumination across subjects and minimize variations in image intensity, color, and shadows arising from differences in ambient lighting. In cases where individuals had removable materials (e.g nail polish) on their index finger, these were removed using nail polish remover.

A point of care device was also used to measure Hb (HemoCue Hb 301 System, HemoCue AB, Angelholm, Sweden) using blood remaining from the venous sample provided for their CBC, and using capillary samples obtained using a finger prick (given this is the standard practice in community settings). The reference Hb test was performed at the SEWA Rural Laboratory using a Sysmex XN-L series cell counter (Sysmex XN-550, Sysmex Corporation, Kobe, Japan) which employs an internal quality control system to ensure calibration at three levels of Hb (5.5, 12, and 16.5 g/dL) and coefficient of variation of the assay at < 1%. The presence of hemoglobinopathies was noted from self-report and/or previous reports noted in the laboratory records.

All images obtained by the research staff were made available to a pathologist (co-author KJ) through a web interface, who assessed the quality of images. Images that were of low quality or revealed identity of participants were excluded. All data were stored in a secure India based cloud server storage owned by SEWA Rural. A copy of de-identified data was made available to Google for AI model development.

Algorithm development

To utilise the correlation between Hb concentrations and the chromatic properties of the target body sites (e.g conjunctiva), all images were subjected to a targeted, heuristic-based preprocessing pipeline. Specifically, a localized crop of the conjunctival region was computationally extracted by leveraging the white paper edge visible in the image as a reference alongside Canny edge detection, effectively isolating the region of interest from extraneous peripheral data. The curated dataset was subsequently partitioned into training, validation, and testing subsets utilizing a 70:15:15 split. The field-representative validation dataset was set aside and was independent from the curated dataset. Prior to model ingestion, these cropped 256x256 pixel images were mapped into the CIELAB (L*a*b*) color space to optimize the representation of visually perceptible color differences.

To mitigate the risk of overfitting inherent to modestly sized datasets, a two-stage ResNet-variant architecture was developed based on the residual learning framework proposed by He et al (28). Each stage comprises a singular convolutional block, the internal architecture of which is detailed in Appendix 2. The high-dimensional visual feature maps extracted by this ResNet backbone are subsequently concatenated with discrete demographic and clinical metadata -namely, the patient's age, sex, and pregnancy status. The resulting multimodal feature vector was then fed into a multilayer perceptron (MLP)-based fusion network to produce a continuous-valued estimate of Hb concentration. The complete architectural schematic is provided in Appendix 3.

The model performed a continuous regression task to predict absolute Hb levels. The model was trained from a random initialization, without any pretrained weights, and optimized using the Adam optimizer with a batch size of 64, an initial learning rate of 0.02 and an exponential decay schedule with a rate of 0.97. At inference, the model's Hb predictions were subsequently used for classification of anemia and its grades using age and gender specific hemoglobin thresholds recommended by WHO (29). (Appendix 4). The overall model configuration is described in Appendix 5.

We anticipated that the dataset would be skewed towards mild-to-moderate anemia cases, even with the attempts made to recruit across all grades of anemia. Using a standard Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss would cause a model to favor this majority group over less common (but clinically important) severe anemia cases. To handle this data imbalance, we applied a two-step optimization strategy. First, a Huber loss function (30) was utilized to balance gradient stability with outlier sensitivity, preventing extreme deviations from disproportionately distorting the model. Second, an inverse bin frequency re-weighting strategy was employed.

The continuous range of Hb values was divided into a fixed number of bins, with bin widths determined by the sample distribution. Bins representing the severe range (Hb 1 to 7 g/dL) capture a much smaller fraction of the dataset due to the inherent rarity of these cases. By mathematically scaling the error penalty inversely to the sample density within each bin, the training became cost-sensitive. This method has been investigated as a cost-sensitive strategy for addressing target-distribution imbalance in deep regression (31), and in our setup forces the model to heavily weigh and accurately predict the more severe (and under-represented) samples.

To address systematic overprediction in the regression outputs, a constant bias-correction term was applied to the predicted hemoglobin values, with its magnitude selected based on validation set performance. The corrected predictions were then classified using the WHO-defined hemoglobin thresholds, improving the sensitivity-specificity trade-off across clinically relevant anemia classification tasks.

The modeling pipeline was designed for low-latency inference, ensuring it could eventually run on mid-range smartphones used by community health workers in India. Ablation experiments evaluated the impact of incorporating various additional (non-conjunctiva) image modalities (tongue, nails) and clinical metadata (including symptoms, past medical history) against "baseline" models which included only conjunctiva, age, sex, pregnancy status.

Additional ablation experiments evaluated the impact of incorporating various combinations of image modalities (ie conjunctivae, tongue, open hand, closed hand), PPG data, clinical metadata (e.g age, pregnancy status, gender) and symptoms. Symptoms for adults and children were only included in age specific models (ie adult symptoms were only applied to a model involving the adult subgroup and a binary indicator variable for child versus adult status).

The outcomes of these ablation studies were used to select the final model for testing in the test set and field validation set, and to describe the tradeoffs between model input complexity and both accuracy of Hb measurement (ie MAE) and classification of anemia.

Sample size and statistical analysis

Sample size calculated was limited by lack of current estimates of MAE or SD from existing image recognition or PPG software. Number of participants was at least 2,322 to have a power of 0.9 to detect a mean error (device -ground truth) in Hb measurement that is significantly < 1.0 g/dL, with multiple postulated mean errors and standard deviations of the error (one-sided P value < 0.05). We aimed to enroll a sample to include approximately 25% of participants with no anemia, 25% mild, 25% moderate, and 25% with severe anemia.

Characteristics of the study population, including demographics, occupation, clinical settings were described using descriptive statistics. The performance of algorithms were examined within each subset of normal Hb, and anemia (mild, moderate, severe) using metrics such as R² , intra class coefficient (ICC) and Bland Altman plots (32). We also measured mean absolute error (MAE) and its standard deviation (SD), as well as 95% confidence interval (95%CI) of estimated Hb. The 95% confidence interval was computed by bootstrapping, resampling 1,000 times with replacement. A one sample t-test (or Wilcoxon signed rank test if data were skewed) was used to determine if the MAE is less than 1.0 g/dL (one-sided P < 0.025).

Sensitivity and specificity (and 95% CIs) were calculated for a priori subgroups, defined by age, sex and pregnancy status using established thresholds for defining mild, moderate and severe anemia. Accuracy of the model was also compared with presence of anemia noted on clinical features, and point of care Hb.

Failure analysis

We conducted a qualitative error analysis to characterize failure modes beyond aggregate metrics. Particular attention was given to clinically significant errors, especially cases of severe anemia misclassified as non-severe and normal cases incorrectly classified as moderate or severe. Among the 106 ground-truth severe cases in the test set, the model achieved 90% sensitivity for severe versus non-severe classification, corresponding to approximately 11 severe cases being misclassified as non-severe. The misclassified severe cases had ground-truth hemoglobin concentrations of 6.5-7.0 g/dL, close to the severe anemia threshold (7.0 g/dL), and involved pregnant women participants aged > 12 years whose conjunctival images exhibited patchy red regions against a white background. These borderline hemoglobin values, potentially overlapping image features, and limited training examples near the decision boundary may have hindered the model's ability to distinguish severe from moderate anemia.

We also examined adjacent-class errors, including normal-mild, mild-moderate, and moderate-severe misclassifications, which may occur when the predicted Hb lies close to the predefined class thresholds. Representative failure cases were further assessed for image-related factors such as poor illumination, image quality and variations in conjunctival appearance. Although nail and tongue modalities were evaluated, their inclusion did not improve performance, suggesting limited complementary information in the current dataset.

Results

Description of participants

A total of 5,455 adults and children were recruited between July 2024 and November 2025, with a further field-representative validation cohort (n=516) collected between December 2025 and January 2026. Of the initial 5,455 captures, 279 were excluded due to incomplete metadata, images, and/or errors in image crops . Hence, a total of 5,176 captures were used for training (3,623), tuning (776) and testing (777) (Figure 1). Participants were distributed across normal Hb (1398, 27.0%), mild anemia (1303, 25.2%), moderate anemia (1764, 34.1%) and severe anemia (711, 13.7%) categories (Table 1). The majority of participants (4334/5176, 83.7%) were female, of whom just less than half, 48.1% were currently pregnant, and almost all were recruited from outpatient settings, mainly from obstetrics and gynecology (56.8%) and general medicine (31.5%) clinics.

Estimation of hemoglobin values using AI model and Hemocue point of care test

The proposed model's prediction of Hb value in the test set (n=777), demonstrated an overall MAE of 1.12 g/dL (Table 2). The MAE was lowest in the mild anemia group (0.74 g/dL) and higher in the normal, moderate, and severe groups (1.19 g/dL, 1.23 g/dL, and 1.43 g/dL, respectively).

The proportion of predictions within +/-1.0 g/dL was 54%. By category, accuracy was highest in the mild anemia group (71%), followed by the normal group (51%), and lowest in both the moderate and severe anemia groups (47%). Figure 2 illustrates the Bland-Altman plot evaluating the agreement between ground truth and predicted Hb values. The analysis reveals a mean bias of -0.02 g/dL, demonstrating that the model possesses negligible systematic error.

The 95% limits of agreement (LoA) ranged from a lower limit of -2.90 g/dL (-1.96 SD) to an upper limit of +2.85 g/dL (+1.96 SD). The vast majority of the data points fell within these boundaries, demonstrating acceptable statistical consistency across the clinical range of Hb.

The MAE for Hemocue point of care device using either venous or capillary samples were broadly similar for overall performance(0.48 g/dL and 0.52 g/dL, respectively) as well as across anemia categories. (Table 2).

Accuracy of classification of anemia using AI model, clinical examination, and Hemocue point of care test

In the test set (n=777), the sensitivity and specificity of the AI model for the presence of anemia were 70% (95%CI 66-74) and 70% (95%CI 64-76) respectively (Table 3). Sensitivity and specificity were higher for severe anemia vs mild, moderate or no anemia, 90% (95%CI 82-94) and 80% (95%CI 77-83) respectively.

Clinical assessment of the presence of anemia (Table 3) showed sensitivity and specificity for differentiating anemia from no anemia of 73% (95%CI 70-78) and 53% (95%CI 46-60) respectively. Sensitivities for detecting severe or moderate vs mild or no anemia was lower (40%, 95%CI 36-45), but specificity was high (94%, 95%CI 91-96). Similarly, sensitivity for clinical assessment of severe vs mild, moderate or no anemia was low (31%, 95%CI 22-40) with high specificity (100%).

The performance of the model on the field-representative validation dataset remained similar to the test dataset (Appendix 6). One important exception was that the performance deteriorated when the images were captured in outdoor conditions where the light intensity was high (data not shown).

The accuracy of the Hemocue point of care test for classification of anemia was higher using capillary samples than venous samples (Table 3). Using venous samples Hemocue demonstrated sensitivity and specificity for anemia vs no anemia of 86% (95%CI 82-88) and 99% (97-100). Accuracy for differentiating severe vs mild, moderate or no anemia showed sensitivity of 82% (74-88) and specificity of 100%. The Hemocue point of care test on capillary samples where this was available (n=516) demonstrated sensitivity and specificity for anemia vs no anemia of 90% (95%CI 87-93) and 83% (77-89). Accuracy for differentiating severe vs mild, moderate or no anemia were higher with sensitivity of 90% ( 80-96) and specificity of 100% (98-100).

Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves, including areas under the curve (AUC) and operating points for the AI model, Hemocue point of care test and clinical assessment are displayed in Figure 3. For classifying anemia vs no anemia, the AI model showed AUC of 0.80, compared to 0.98 for Hemocue. For classifying moderate or severe vs normal Hb/mild anemia, the AUROC for the AI model and Hemocue were 0.81 and 1.0 respectively. Finally, the AUROC of the AI model and Hemocue for classifying severe anemia vs mild/moderate anemia/normal Hb, were 0.93 and 1.0 respectively.

Accuracy of classification of anemia using AI model across subgroups.

Among subgroups, sensitivity of the AI model was higher for women than men, and specificity was higher for men than women across all three classification groups in the test set (n=777) (Table 4). Sensitivity was higher and specificity was lower among non-pregnant women for all anemia grade classifications. Similarly, for the youngest age group (1-12 yrs) sensitivity was higher and specificity lower than the older age groups for all three anemia classifications.

Overall sensitivity and specificity values were similar, with overlapping 95%CI across the three classification groups (Table 4). A total of 106 (106/777, 13.6%) of participants in the test set have Sickle Cell disease, of which 42 (5.4%) had Sickle Cell Trait and 115 (14.8%) were negative, while the majority had unknown status. For those with known Sickle Cell disease present, sensitivity and specificity were somewhat higher than that of the overall sample (Table 4). Hemocue remained highly sensitive and specific across all subgroups (Appendix 7)

The ablation studies which compare the performance of the baseline model (using conjunctiva and patient metadata) with single or combinations of other images like nail bed or tongue, clinical metadata, and symptoms on accuracy of Hb estimation and anemia classification are shown in Appendix 8. No single or combination of image modalities provided lower MAE than the baseline model. Discussion

Main findings

The AI model's estimation of Hb value in the final test set demonstrated an overall MAE of 1.12 g/dL, while the sensitivity and specificity of the AI model for the presence of anemia were 70% (95%CI 66-74) and 70% (95%CI 64-76) respectively. Sensitivity and specificity were higher for classifying severe anemia vs mild, moderate or no anemia, 90% (95%CI 82-94) and 80% (95%CI 77-83) respectively, which is important given that individuals with severe anemia are most likely to benefit from point of care screening, and time sensitive interventions to facilitate prompt treatment (3, 5). We found that a single image of the conjunctiva along with a person's age, sex and pregnancy status provided marginally lower accuracy than a more complex model involving these features as well as the presence or absence of symptoms in adults (9 symptoms) and in children (4 symptoms). The marginal gain of accuracy with the more complex model, requiring in any future use for this additional symptom data to be accurately reported and entered, was not considered a worthwhile tradeoff, and so the main results focused on the simpler model without these symptom inputs. While we explored using different image types in addition to, or instead of the conjunctiva, as well as PPG signals, these yielded comparable or lower accuracy, leading to the conclusion to use conjunctival images alone as the optimal body site images in this study, for anemia detection.

Accuracy results did not vary significantly across a range of skin tones, and smartphone models, which implies that findings may be generalisable to other populations, and not limited to users of high-end smartphones. The AI model performance was sustained on a field-representative validation dataset generally; though high intensity of illumination in outdoor settings lowered the accuracy. The model struggled most with the boundary between normal Hb and mild anemia. This may be due to the subtle chromatic differences in the conjunctiva between mild anemia and no anemia that are smaller than the variance caused by a smartphone's auto-white-balance settings. Even with high-end mobiles like the Samsung S24, the "auto-white balance" of smartphone cameras can shift the perceived redness of the conjunctiva. Our use of a "white paper reference" during capture was essential for normalizing these color shifts. Finally, in children (ages 1-5 years), we noted that data rejection difficulties arose primarily due to motion artifacts in capturing a clear, retracted eyelid image, suggesting potential operational challenges in capture and use of conjunctiva for this age group.

The AI model's performance in anemia classification was compared to that of the clinician examining the patient, who was not blinded to their Hb results. We noted overall lower clinician sensitivity particularly for the severe or moderate vs mild or normal Hb categories, which limits the value of clinical examination for screening. However we noted that specificity of the clinical examination was higher than that of the AI model. Not surprisingly, the Hemocue point of care device provided highly accurate classification, particularly with the capillary samples, with higher sensitivity and specificity than the AI model across all categories. The fact that the AI model using non-invasive smartphone images had substantially higher sensitivity for severe anemia than clinician assessment of physical signs, suggests potential for augmenting screening using this accessible, objective approach of Hb estimation and anemia classification.

Comparison with literature

Many studies have used machine learning with smartphone images of various body sites, in attempts to estimate Hb levels or classify anemia. The challenge with contextualizing within this body of research is that studies are conducted in highly variable populations in terms of size, demographics, distribution of Hb values, as well as varying proportions with ground truth Hb measurement, varying levels of validation, and different technical approaches (33). Comparing findings is further limited by highly variable reporting of many of these studies.

A large study of AI-augmented app using fingernail images, estimated Hb with MAE of +/-0.72 g/dL testing 200,000 U.S. users, and reported a sensitivity of 89% and specificity of 93% at an anemia cutoff of 12.5 g/dL, with MAE improving from 1.36 to 0.74 g/dL after adding in user details, though with relatively few participants with moderate or severe anemia (34). Lip mucosa has been examined in small cohorts, showing variable classification accuracies, for example one study of 138 individuals found overall accuracy ranging from 75 to 96% depending on the model used (35). Lips and the oral mucosa can sometimes be colored by substances such as Betel nut or tobacco, which could limit generalisability to some communities.

Conjunctival imaging, the modality most directly relevant to the present study, has produced a heterogeneous body of evidence. A convenience sample of 435 patients in the emergency department estimated Accuracy of Hb estimation was 75.4% (95% CI 71.3-79.4) for all categories of anemia (36), A radiomics-based approach applied to over 12,000 grayscale conjunctival images from 565 children used machine learning without deep learning (37).

Notably, the NiADA smartphone application, validated across 556 pediatric, pregnant, and adult participants in India, demonstrated inconsistent performance across subgroupssensitivity/specificity of 75.8%/53.8% in adult females versus 23.8%/90% in pregnant womenunderscoring the challenge of generalizing conjunctiva-based models across physiologically distinct populations (38, 39).

Collectively, this literature suggests that while image-based hemoglobin estimation is technically feasible across multiple body sites, model performance remains highly dependent on population characteristics, image standardization, and validation methodology.

Strengths and limitations

The study had several strengths. First, the accuracy of the final models to differentiate severe anemia from moderate or mild was within the target product profile suggested by expert groups consulted before the initiation of the study. Second, the dataset collected for this study provides the largest dataset available that covers a range of values of Hb, surpassing existing alternatives by offering three image modalities (conjunctiva, fingernails, tongue), clinically audited metadata, and a broad demographic range from children to adults. This dataset has a large number of cases at the extremes of Hb values which is unique. Third, we recruited participants from populations in India which provides real world data on various skin tones from a mainly rural population. Fourth, we examined the use of three different smartphones, to provide evidence of the impact of device cost/sophistication on performance of algorithms. This is important given resource constraints for deploying higher end smartphones in healthcare settings in lower resource areas. Lastly, this is to our knowledge the only study that explored the multi-modal approach using images of tongue, conjunctiva and nailbeds.

However, we note several limitations. First, while the accuracy of the final model for classification of anemia was higher than other studies using this technology, they remain lower than the invasive point of care Hb devices which are used in many lower resource settings, including that of the Hemocue test as noted in our study. Specifically, Hb levels were over-predicted for severe class and under-predicted for normal/mild/moderate classes.

Second, the population studied here, while drawn from the rural population of this region who were attending the hospital, may not be representative of individuals with different skin tones, or factors that might affect images of the conjunctiva, tongue or nailbeds (e.g. skin markings, eye irritation). Finally, the research team collecting data on participants were not blinded to Hb results, therefore their assessment of presence of symptoms, signs and classification of anemia is potentially subject to bias. This may have led to higher accuracy for the clinical assessment of anemia presence, although overall the accuracy of clinical assessment was in line with published studies (7, 8). Finally, our sampling method was non-consecutive sampling, which is known to overestimate estimates of accuracy in studies of diagnostics, but allowed us to collect data on a range of Hb levels which is essential for developing and testing models (40).

Implications for researchers, policy makers, others

Our findings have several implications for clinicians and policy makers. Based on the study results, it may be viable to consider deploying a smartphone based severe anemia detection tool as part of routine screening in populations similar to those studied here. Two use cases could be considered: first, screening patients who decline invasive blood testing or invasive blood testing using a noninvasive alternative. Second, a cascaded approach using a smartphone tool as first level screening, followed by use of point of care blood test to confirm suspected anemia cases. We believe this cascaded approach would likely minimize the cost of large scale screening without missing severe anemia cases. Additional modelling and understanding of human behavior is needed to determine the most appropriate step-wise use of this approach. Further research is necessary using larger datasets from diverse populations. Additional technology efforts may be needed to provide user interfaces suitable for frontline healthcare workers to ensure capture of good quality images of conjunctiva as well as developing on-device models for remote territories that lack internet connection. Although the model is performing well for screening of severe anemia, further training of the model is necessary to improve the accuracy of the model for other severity classes of anemia. Clinical validation of the model in real life community settings is required to assess accuracy in deployment settings.

Beyond addressing these limitations, several translational imperatives must be met. Most non-invasive optical platforms remain investigational Software as a Medical Device (SaMD), whereas FDA clearance currently remains restricted to minimally invasive capillary tests (e.g., AnemoCheck Home). Future clinical adoption requires moving beyond binary classification to continuous quantitative Hb regression (g/dL), external validation in pregnant and multi-ethnic cohorts, and hardware-agnostic sensor calibration. Overcoming these translational barriers is essential to accurately evaluating the cost-utility, clinical effectiveness, and practical feasibility of deploying smartphone based models in real world settings. To conclude, we studied the use of noninvasive smartphone based imaging of various body sites for anemia classification and found the conjunctiva alongside simple demographic information provided acceptable accuracy. Further studies and validations will be needed to improve performance and examine how best to integrate such tools into real screening workflows
