How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). Abstract

The lone star tick, Amblyomma americanum, is closely associated with alpha-gal syndrome (AGS), an emerging tick-associated allergic condition. However, heterogeneous tick surveillance and limited clinical AGS data complicate the assessment of geographic exposure. We investigated whether ecological modeling and epidemiological surveillance could identify areas of potentially elevated AGS exposure across Illinois. Using data from 102 Illinois counties, eight statistical and machine-learning models were evaluated through geographic cross-validation to predict the relative abundance of lone star ticks. Random forest and XGBoost predictions were separately combined with reported ehrlichiosis rates to construct county-level AGS exposure-risk indices. Wildland-urban interface (WUI) population was examined in sensitivity analyses. Random forest achieved near-minimum prediction error while XGBoost demonstrated the strongest rank correlation. Both indices showed substantial concordance with CDC suspected-AGS categories across 97 counties (Spearman ρ = 0.710 and 0.721). At the highest category threshold, odds ratios per 0.10-unit index increase were 5.02 and 3.63, respectively. By integrating ecological predictions with epidemiological surveillance, we establish a reproducible, spatially explicit framework for prioritizing AGS surveillance, targeting interventions, and guiding future epidemiological investigations across Illinois counties.

1. Introduction

Alpha-gal syndrome (AGS) is an emerging tick bite-associated allergic condition characterized by delayed reactions to mammalian meat and other products containing galactoseα-1,3-galactose (alpha-gal) [1, 2]. In the United States, the lone star tick (Amblyomma americanum) is the primary tick species associated with alpha-gal sensitization. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) estimated that as many as 450,000 people in the United States may be affected by AGS, although its actual burden remains uncertain [3]. National surveillance of suspected AGS has identified geographic concentrations in the southern, Midwestern, and Mid-Atlantic United States, including Illinois [4]. Identifying areas of potentially elevated exposure is therefore important for improving surveillance, clinical awareness, and public health prevention.

Despite growing recognition of AGS, detailed geographic information on clinically confirmed cases and tick-exposure locations remains limited. The CDC's published county-level distribution is based on positive alpha-gal-specific antibody testing rather than confirmed clinical diagnoses, leaving uncertainty about the geographic burden of AGS [4]. An Illinois survey found that only 32% of physicians knew the cause of AGS, and fewer than 18% were aware of available diagnostic testing [5]. Among surveyed Illinois farmers, 42% reported knowledge of lone star ticks [6], while a subsequent comparison of Illinois stakeholder surveys identified broader gaps in tick and tickborne disease knowledge and prevention practices [7]. In Illinois, the 2025 Tracking Infectious Cases Knowledgeably (TICK) Act emphasized the need for AGS awareness and education for healthcare providers [8]. Meanwhile, differences in tick collection methods and surveillance coverage complicate geographic comparisons of tick relative abundance, highlighting the need for integrated ecological and epidemiological approaches to assess AGS exposure.

Statistical and machine-learning approaches have increasingly been applied to predict tick distributions and habitat suitability by integrating climatic, vegetation, land-use, and host-related variables [9] [10] [11]. Such approaches can estimate ecological patterns across counties where direct surveillance observations are incomplete or inconsistent. However, spatial dependence among neighboring counties may inflate predictive performance when geographic structure is ignored. Geographic cross-validation and spatial modeling provide complementary approaches for evaluating regional predictions and accounting for spatial dependence [12].

Ecological predictions of lone star tick relative abundance alone cannot fully describe opportunities for human exposure. Human ehrlichiosis provides additional epidemiologic information because A. americanum transmits ehrlichiosis-causing pathogens, and the CDC has documented broad geographic overlap between ehrlichiosis and suspected AGS [4, 13]. The wildland-urban interface (WUI), where residential development meets or intermingles with natural vegetation, may also indicate environments where human-tick encounters are possible [14]. Combining these indicators with ecological predictions could provide a more informative estimate of the relative representation of potential AGS exposure, provided their distinct meanings and uncertainties are recognized. Transparent weighting and sensitivity analyses are particularly important when combining indicators that measure related but distinct processes [15].

In this study, we used statistical and machine-learning models, including spatial extensions, to predict geographic variation in the relative abundance of lone star ticks across Illinois based on environmental and host-habitat characteristics. We evaluated model performance across 102 counties using geographically separated validation groups and integrated predictions from selected high-performing models with epidemiological evidence of human-tick exposure to develop county-level AGS exposure-risk scores. We assessed the geographic concordance between these scores and CDC-suspected AGS categories to determine whether integrating ecological and epidemiological evidence could identify geographic patterns of elevated AGS exposure risk in areas where direct disease surveillance remains limited.

2. Materials and Methods

The overall analytical workflow for developing and evaluating county-level AGS exposure-risk indices is illustrated in Figure 1. Ecological predictors and tick surveillance informed five baseline models, of which the three best-performing were also compared with their BYM2 spatial-extension versions. Selected predictions were combined with ehrlichiosis incidence and, in sensitivity analyses, wildland-urban interface (WUI) population. Indices were evaluated against CDC suspected-AGS categories.

2.1. Tick surveillance and ecological covariates

The analysis included all 102 counties in Illinois. County-level tick surveillance data were obtained from the Illinois Department of Public Health (IDPH) for 2022-2024 [16]. The proportion of collected ticks identified as A. americanum was calculated by dividing the number of lone star ticks by the total number of collected ticks across all species. This proportion was used as a measure of relative abundance within surveillance collections, rather than relying on raw tick counts, which may vary with surveillance intensity. This proportion served as the primary outcome for statistical and machine-learning models. All counties had positive total tick counts, including 33 counties with no recorded lone star ticks.

We initially considered nine county-level ecological predictors selected to represent climatic conditions influencing tick survival and activity, vegetation and landscape characteristics associated with suitable tick habitat, and habitat suitability for important wildlife hosts. Five bioclimatic variables were derived from WorldClim historical monthly climate data based on the Climatic Research Unit Time Series (CRU TS) for 2022-2024 [17, 18]: mean diurnal temperature range (BIO2), mean temperature of the wettest quarter (BIO8), mean temperature of the driest quarter (BIO9), precipitation seasonality (BIO15), and precipitation of the warmest quarter (BIO18). These variables capture temperature and moisture conditions relevant to tick development, survival, and seasonal activity. Land-cover predictors comprised forest and wetland coverage derived from the Annual National Land Cover Database (NLCD) for 2022-2024 [19, 20], representing vegetation and moisture-related habitat conditions. White-tailed deer habitat suitability [21] was included because deer are important hosts for adult A. americanum, while forest-edge density was considered to capture habitat transitions that may influence tick and host distributions [22, 23]. All predictors were summarized at the county level, and multicollinearity among the nine candidate predictors was assessed using pairwise Pearson correlation coefficients and variance inflation factors (VIFs) (Figure S1). Forest-edge density was excluded because it overlapped with other forest-related predictors, leaving eight ecological variables for model development (Supplementary material).

2.2. Predictive modeling and spatial validation

We used a two-stage modeling approach to evaluate county-level lone star tick relative abundance and determine whether incorporating explicit spatial dependence improved geographic predictions. First, five baseline statistical and machine-learning models were evaluated: betabinomial regression, elastic-net binomial regression, generalized additive models (GAMs), random forest (RF), and extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost). Beta-binomial regression was implemented using the glmmTMB package to model lone star tick counts relative to other collected ticks while accommodating overdispersion [24]. Elastic-net binomial regression was implemented using glmnet, combining L1 (Lasso) and L2 (Ridge) regularization to account for correlated ecological predictors [25]. Generalized additive models (GAMs) were fitted using mgcv with a quasibinomial logit link and cubic regression splines to capture nonlinear ecological relationships [26]. Random forest regression was implemented using ranger, while extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) used a squared-error regression objective to model complex nonlinear associations and interactions among predictors [27, 28]. County total tick counts were incorporated through binomial count responses in beta-binomial and elastic-net models, observation weights in GAM and XGBoost, and sampling weights in RF.

Geographic predictive performance was evaluated using five-fold spatial cross-validation. Counties were assigned to five geographic groups using k-means clustering of projected county coordinates (EPSG:5070), independently of observed tick proportions (Table S1; Figure S2). In each iteration, one geographic group was withheld for evaluation, while the remaining four groups were used for model fitting and hyperparameter selection. Beta-binomial regression used trainingfold-standardized ecological predictors. Elastic-net tuning evaluated five regularization mixing parameters, with the optimal penalty selected through inner geographic cross-validation. GAMs used cubic regression splines (k = 4), with smoothing parameters estimated by restricted maximum likelihood (REML). RF tuning evaluated 12 combinations of candidate predictors per split (mtry = 2, 4, 6, 8) and minimum terminal-node sizes (3, 5, 10). XGBoost tuning considered maximum tree depths of 1-3, learning rates of 0.03 and 0.10, and minimum child weights of 1 and 5, with early stopping to control model complexity. RF and XGBoost hyperparameters were selected using inner geographically held-out RMSE.

In the second stage, the three best-performing baseline models were extended using Besag-York-Mollié 2 (BYM2) spatial effects to assess whether accounting for spatial dependence improved geographic predictive performance [29]. County adjacency was defined using queen contiguity, in which counties sharing a boundary or vertex were considered neighbors. The spatial extensions incorporated spatially structured and unstructured county effects. For the machine-learning extensions, cross-fitted, logit-transformed predictions were incorporated into spatial regression models alongside BYM2 effects. Withheld county outcomes were excluded from spatial model fitting.

Together, the five baseline models and three spatial extensions yielded eight models for comparison. The same outer geographic partitions were used for all eight models, and predictions from the five withheld groups were combined to generate one out-of-fold (OOF) prediction per county and model. Predictive performance was assessed using root mean squared error (RMSE), mean absolute error (MAE), coefficient of determination (R²), and Spearman rank correlation between observed and OOF-predicted lone star tick proportions. Fold-specific performance was also examined to assess geographic variation in prediction accuracy and whether spatial extensions provided additional predictive benefit. For ecological interpretation, selected high-performing models were subsequently fitted using all 102 counties and examined through variable importance, accumulated local effects (ALE), and SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) [30]. Full-data models were used exclusively for ecological interpretation, while predictive accuracy was evaluated using geographically held-out predictions. Additional implementation details are provided in the Supplementary material.

First, AGS is a tick bite-associated allergic condition rather than a pathogenmediated infection, making lone star tick ecology the primary biological indicator of potential exposure. Second, reported human ehrlichiosis cases provide only indirect evidence of human-tick encounters with Ehrlichia-positive lone star ticks, as they represent diagnosed and reported infections rather than the full extent of tick exposure. Third, ehrlichiosis cases are assigned by county of residence, which may differ from the actual location of tick exposure, limiting their geographic precision as an exposure proxy. Finally, the weights were specified independently of CDC-suspected AGS categories, which were reserved for external evaluation, thereby avoiding optimization against the same data used to assess geographic concordance. Accordingly, the 70% ecological and 30% epidemiological contributions reflect the biological relevance and limitations of the available indicators rather than an empirically established optimal ratio [4, 13]. Additive aggregation allowed both components to contribute without requiring simultaneous high levels, consistent with composite-indicator methodology [15]. Index construction was performed independently for model-specific ecological predictions to preserve their geographic variation.

To evaluate the potential contribution of human contact opportunities, a sensitivity index was constructed to capture the proportion of each county's population residing within the wildlandurban interface (WUI), derived from the Spatial Analysis For Conservation and Sustainability (SILVIS) version 4 dataset [14]. The alternative index was calculated as: Rᵢ,ₘ,WUI = 0.70Vᵢ,ₘ + 0.20Hᵢ + 0.10Wᵢ where Wᵢ represents the county population proportion residing in WUI areas, ranging from 0 to 1.

The ecological component was maintained at 70%, while 10 percentage points were reassigned from the epidemiological component to the WUI indicator to examine the sensitivity of geographic rankings to an additional measure of potential human-tick contact. The WUI-adjusted index was calculated separately for each ecological model selected based on geographic cross-validation performance. Spearman rank correlations among the primary, alternative-weighted, and WUIadjusted indices were calculated across all 102 counties to assess the stability of rankings. Detailed data processing and index implementation are provided in Supplementary material.

2.4. External evaluation using CDC suspected-AGS data

To assess geographic concordance between the developed AGS exposure-risk indices and observed suspected-AGS patterns, county-level categories were reconstructed from the published Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) map based on alpha-gal-specific immunoglobulin E (IgE) laboratory testing during 2017-2022 [4]. The map classified annual suspected-AGS rates per million residents into four ordered categories: zero, <11, 11-87, and >87, with a separate designation for counties where no testing was performed. The digitized categories were matched to Illinois counties using standardized county names. Five counties without testing information were excluded, leaving 97 counties for evaluation. These categories reflect suspected AGS identified through laboratory testing rather than the incidence of clinically confirmed disease. The CDC and surveillance datasets overlapped only in 2022; therefore, the analysis assessed geographic concordance rather than temporal agreement.

Geographic concordance between each AGS exposure-risk index and the ordered CDC categories was evaluated using Spearman rank correlation, with 95% confidence intervals estimated from 5,000 nonparametric bootstrap resamples of counties. Associations were further examined using cumulative logistic regression, with index values scaled to express odds ratios (ORs) per 0.10-unit increase. The proportional-odds assumption was assessed using likelihood-ratio tests of nominal effects. Because the assumption was not satisfied for the primary indices, non-proportional cumulative logit models were fitted, allowing associations to vary across the three cumulative category thresholds. Threshold-specific ORs with Wald 95% confidence intervals were estimated, and predicted probabilities of each CDC category were calculated across the observed index ranges. The same regression specification was applied to the wildland-urban interface (WUI) sensitivity indices for comparability.

2.5. Spatial sensitivity analysis

To examine whether incorporating neighboring-county information influenced geographic concordance with CDC suspected-AGS patterns, an exploratory spatial averaging analysis was performed for the primary and WUI sensitivity indices. Using queen-contiguous county adjacency, one-step spatial averaging assigned equal weight to each county's original index (50%) and the mean index of its adjacent counties (50%). This approach incorporated local geographic context while retaining the original county-specific contribution. Spatial averaging was applied independently to each index using the original scores. Spearman rank correlations with CDC suspected-AGS categories were recalculated for the 97 counties with available testing information. Correlations before and after spatial averaging were compared, and changes in correlation coefficients (Δρ) were calculated to evaluate the sensitivity of geographic concordance to neighboring-county information.

3. Results

>Results

3.1. Lone star tick distribution and ecological patterns

Across 102 Illinois counties, surveillance records included 5,366 Amblyomma americanum ticks among 12,938 collected ticks. The mean county-level lone star tick proportion was 0.394 (SD = 0.373), with a median of 0.324 and a range of 0-1. Overall, 33 counties (32.4%) had no recorded lone star ticks. Geographic variation was pronounced, with lower proportions concentrated in northern Illinois and greater lone star tick relative abundance across much of central and southern Illinois (Figure 2). Mean observed proportions across the five geographic folds ranged from 0.089 to 0.660, reflecting substantial regional heterogeneity (Table S1). Ecological covariates exhibited distinct geographic patterns, with higher temperatures during the driest quarter (BIO9) and generally greater deer habitat suitability in southern Illinois, whereas forest and wetland coverage showed more localized spatial variation (Figure S3).

3.2. Spatial predictive performance

Spatial cross-validation revealed differences in predictive accuracy and geographic generalizability among the eight models (Table S2). Random forest achieved near-minimum prediction error (RMSE = 0.316), comparable to XGBoost-BYM2 (0.313), while XGBoost demonstrated the strongest county-level rank correlation (ρ = 0.556) and lowest worst-fold RMSE. Although incorporating BYM2 spatial effects improved overall RMSE for some models, these extensions did not consistently improve performance across geographically withheld folds (Table S3). Comparisons of observed and predicted lone star tick proportion rank groups revealed some classification discrepancies for random forest and XGBoost (Figure S4).

Given the marginal improvement in overall RMSE from spatial extensions and their inconsistent benefits across geographic folds, random forest and XGBoost were selected for ecological interpretation and development of AGS exposure-risk indices based on their complementary predictive strengths and direct relationships with ecological covariates.

3.3. Ecological predictors and model interpretation

Both random forest (RF) and XGBoost consistently identified five leading ecological predictors: temperature of the driest quarter (BIO9), mean diurnal temperature range (BIO2), temperature of the wettest quarter (BIO8), deer habitat suitability, and forest cover (Figure S5). Temperaturerelated variables dominated both models, with BIO9 ranking highest in RF and BIO2 in XGBoost. Although their relative importance varied, these five predictors consistently contributed most to the model's predictions.

Accumulated local effects (ALE) revealed broadly similar nonlinear ecological relationships across both models (Figure 3). Predicted lone star tick proportions increased with higher mean diurnal temperature ranges and warmer temperatures during the driest quarter. Deer habitat suitability showed a generally positive relationship, whereas the temperature of the wettest quarter exhibited more pronounced increases at the highest values. SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) further supported these patterns, with higher temperature values and greater deer suitability generally contributing positively to county-level predictions, whereas lower values contributed negatively (Figure 3). Although the magnitude and shape of individual predictor effects varied between models, their overall ecological patterns were largely consistent.

3.4. Geographic distribution of AGS exposure-risk indices

The primary AGS exposure-risk indices, combining geographically cross-validated lone star tick predictions from RF or XGBoost (70%) with scaled ehrlichiosis incidence (30%), showed broadly consistent geographic patterns (Figure 4). Higher index scores were concentrated in southern Illinois, with Hamilton, Massac, Pope, Jefferson, and Franklin counties ranking among the five highest in both models. County rankings were strongly correlated between the RF and XGBoost indices (Spearman ρ = 0.952), although XGBoost produced a wider range of index values than RF.

Incorporating wildland-urban interface (WUI) population in the 70:20:10 sensitivity indices preserved similar geographic patterns (Figure S6). Compared with their respective primary indices, county rankings remained highly correlated for RF (ρ = 0.995) and XGBoost (ρ = 0.997), with the same ten highest-ranked counties retained. Alternative ecological-to-ehrlichiosis weightings of 50:50, 60:40, and 80:20 also showed strong agreement with the primary 70:30 formulation (ρ = 0.984-0.996 for RF and 0.990-0.997 for XGBoost; Figure S7), indicating limited sensitivity of county prioritization to index weighting.

3.5. Geographic concordance with CDC suspected-AGS categories

Among 97 Illinois counties with available CDC suspected-AGS categories (Figure S8), both primary exposure-risk indices showed strong positive geographic concordance (Spearman ρ = 0.710 for random forest and 0.721 for XGBoost; both p < 0.001). Index scores generally increased across successive CDC categories (Figure 5). The proportional-odds assumption was not supported for either index, and threshold-specific ordinal regression showed increasing odds with higher index scores (Table S4). Predicted probabilities from the non-proportional ordinal models illustrated the corresponding shifts in CDC category membership as random forest and XGBoost index values increased (Figure 6). The strongest associations occurred at the highest category threshold, with odds ratios of 5.02 for random forest and 3.63 for XGBoost per 0.10-unit increase in the respective index.

Results remained consistent across sensitivity analyses. Incorporating wildland-urban interface (WUI) population yielded comparable correlations with CDC categories (ρ = 0.709 for random forest and 0.725 for XGBoost). Spatial averaging further increased correlations to 0.757 for random forest and 0.773 for XGBoost, indicating improved geographic concordance after incorporating neighboring county scores (Figure S9).

4. Discussion

A central challenge in understanding the geography of AGS is that ecological conditions favorable to its principal implicated tick species do not directly reveal where human sensitization or clinical disease occurs. This study addresses that gap by integrating spatially validated ecological predictions with an epidemiological indicator of human-tick encounters to construct county-level AGS exposure-risk indices for Illinois. The convergence of two machine-learning approaches on similar geographic priorities, together with their concordance with independently reported suspected-AGS patterns, provides evidence that heterogeneous surveillance data can be translated into a structured assessment of relative exposure potential. Previous national investigations established broad geographic overlap among suspected AGS, lone star ticks, and ehrlichiosis [4]. Our contribution extends this observation by developing quantitative county rankings based on geographically validated ecological predictions and epidemiological surveillance, which are evaluated against independent suspected AGS data.

The ecological coherence of the two models strengthens the biological interpretation of the resulting geographic patterns. Mean temperatures of the driest and wettest quarters, mean diurnal temperature range, deer habitat suitability, and forest cover consistently emerged as influential predictors, suggesting that lone star tick relative abundance reflects interconnected climatic and host-habitat conditions. Previous distribution models have emphasized the importance of climatic suitability, including moisture-related constraints, and have documented the complex environmental influences on the geographic range of A. americanum [32, 33]. The nonlinear responses observed in both models indicate that these ecological relationships vary across environmental gradients. The importance of deer habitat suitability is biologically plausible, as white-tailed deer are major hosts for adult lone star ticks, and deer-targeted acaricide treatment reduced host-seeking ticks by more than 90% [34].

The modeling results also illustrate why geographic prediction requires more rigorous evaluation than conventional model-fit assessments. Models can reproduce existing spatial gradients while performing poorly when transferred to counties with different environmental characteristics or surveillance compositions. Spatially structured cross-validation addresses this concern by evaluating predictions in withheld geographic regions, an approach recommended for environmentally dependent datasets [12]. Although the spatially extended XGBoost model achieved the lowest overall prediction error, this advantage did not translate into consistently better performance across geographic folds. Random forest and XGBoost therefore offered complementary strengths in prediction accuracy and county-level ranking, while retaining direct relationships between ecological predictors and model outputs. These findings demonstrate why model selection for spatial risk assessment should consider geographic transferability alongside overall predictive accuracy.

Combining predicted lone star tick relative abundance with ehrlichiosis surveillance links an ecological indicator to an indirect measure of human-tick encounters, while recognizing that AGS and ehrlichiosis have distinct disease mechanisms. Their geographic overlap, previously documented in national surveillance, provides a biological rationale for integrating these information streams without assuming that their underlying disease processes are equivalent [4]. County rankings remained stable across alternative weighting scenarios and after incorporating the proportion of residents living within the WUI. The stability of county rankings following WUI integration further demonstrates the robustness of the proposed framework to alternative estimates of the relative representation of potential human-tick contact.

The correspondence with CDC suspected AGS categories provides an additional, human-healthoriented assessment of the framework. Higher exposure-risk indices were associated with higher suspected-AGS categories, and threshold-specific analyses indicated particularly strong differentiation at the highest category boundary. However, this agreement requires careful interpretation. The CDC categories were derived from laboratory testing rather than clinically confirmed AGS, and alpha-gal-specific immunoglobulin E (IgE) positivity does not necessarily indicate symptomatic disease [1, 4, 35]. But the strong concordance with independently derived CDC suspected-AGS categories provides epidemiological support for the geographic patterns identified by the framework, while improved concordance following spatial averaging further highlights the importance of regional ecological and epidemiological processes. Several methodological choices strengthened the reliability of our findings. Geographic crossvalidation assessed model transferability; comparisons across eight models and alternative index formulations examined sensitivity to analytical choices; and CDC-suspected AGS categories were reserved for external evaluation rather than weight optimization. Nevertheless, the interpretation of these findings remains subject to limitations in the underlying surveillance data. Relative abundance was estimated from the species composition of tick collections rather than standardized measures of tick density or human biting frequency, and geographic variation in collection practices may influence these estimates [36]. Seasonal variation in tick activity and finer-scale exposure information were not available in the aggregated surveillance data, while ehrlichiosis reporting and alpha-gal testing may be influenced by healthcare access and diagnostic practices. County-level aggregation and differences in surveillance periods also limit fine-scale and temporal interpretation. Future integration of standardized longitudinal tick surveillance, human-tick encounter data, and clinically confirmed AGS outcomes could further strengthen the framework and enable more comprehensive evaluation of its geographic predictive performance.

These findings are particularly relevant to the evolving public health response to AGS in Illinois. The state's 2025 Tracking Infectious Cases Knowledgeably (TICK) Act established provisions for AGS education and awareness among communities, healthcare providers, and local health departments [8], while proposed legislation in 2026 sought to establish formal statewide and county-level case reporting [37]. The resulting county-level indices provide an evidence-based foundation for prioritizing targeted tick surveillance, guiding clinician education, and identifying geographic areas requiring further epidemiological investigation. Such applications are especially relevant given documented gaps in healthcare provider awareness and confidence in diagnosing AGS [38]. The framework could also support a staged surveillance strategy in which initial county rankings guide data collection, newly collected observations update ecological predictions, and emerging clinical information is used for independent evaluation. The framework may also be transferable to other regions where ecological surveillance is available, but direct AGS surveillance remains incomplete. Its principal value lies in providing a transparent and testable foundation on which more comprehensive surveillance and prevention efforts can be developed.

5. Conclusion

This study demonstrates that integrating spatially validated ecological predictions with epidemiological surveillance provides a reproducible framework for mapping AGS exposure potential across Illinois. The strong geographic concordance with CDC-suspected AGS categories, the consistency between the random forest and XGBoost indices, and the stability across sensitivity analyses support the framework's utility for identifying geographic priorities. These findings provide an evidence-based foundation for targeted tick surveillance, public health awareness, and future epidemiological investigations, with potential applications in other regions where direct AGS surveillance remains limited.

Figure 1.

Figure1. Overview of the workflow for developing and evaluating alpha-gal syndrome (AGS) exposure-risk indices in Illinois. Ecological predictors and tick surveillance informed five baseline models, of which the three best-performing were also compared with their BYM2 spatial-extension versions. Selected predictions were combined with ehrlichiosis incidence and, in sensitivity analyses, wildland-urban interface (WUI) population. Indices were evaluated against CDC suspected-AGS categories.

Figure 2.

Figure 2. Geographic distribution of the observed proportion of collected ticks identified as Amblyomma americanum across 102 Illinois counties during 2022-2024. White counties indicate a zero proportion of lone star ticks in surveillance collections, rather than an absence of tick sampling.

Figure 3.

Figure 3. Ecological interpretation of random forest and XGBoost models. (a) Accumulated local effects of four ecological predictors (random forest: solid blue; XGBoost: dashed orange). (b) SHAP beeswarm plots showing county-level predictor contributions, colored by feature values.

Figure 4.

Figure 4. Geographic distribution of primary alpha-gal syndrome exposure-risk indices across Illinois. Countylevel indices derived from random forest and XGBoost predictions, combined with scaled ehrlichiosis incidence at a 70:30 weighting.

Figure 5.

Figure 5. Distribution of primary AGS exposure-risk indices across CDC suspected-AGS categories. Violin and box plots show RF and XGBoost indices across four CDC categories (n = 97). Points represent counties, and diamonds indicate category means.

Figure 6. Predicted probabilities of CDC suspected-AGS categories across primary exposure-risk indices. Nonproportional ordinal regression curves illustrate category-specific probabilities across random forest and XGBoost index values.
