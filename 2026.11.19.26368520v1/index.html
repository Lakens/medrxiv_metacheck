Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 1 non-significant p value that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Abstract

Background. We examined transcriptomic (gene expression-based) aging in postmortem central nervous system (CNS) tissues from donors across the amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS)frontotemporal dementia (FTD) spectrum and investigated the molecular and cellular features underlying disease-associated differences in transcriptional aging clocks.

Pasta REG and age-shift) were applied to 2,235 RNA-sequencing samples from 609 donors across 11 CNS regions, with adjustment for age and tissue-specific technical variables. We compared transcriptional age across ALS, ALS-FTD, FTD, and controls, examining regional variation, gene-level contributions, cell composition, disease duration, and C9orf72 status, with independent validation of gene contributions in a cervical spinal cord cohort.

Results. Disease-associated differences in transcriptional age varied by diagnosis, tissue, and clock. ALS and ALS-FTD showed the strongest differences in spinal cord, while FTD differences localized primarily to the frontal and temporal cortex. In ALS spinal cord, RNAAgeCalc DESeq2 estimates were lower than controls, whereas other clocks estimated higher transcriptional age when significant. CHIT1 was identified as the primary contributor to the DESeq2 transcriptional age decrease in ALS. Cell composition adjustment attenuated ALS spinal cord differences, reflecting microglial, lymphocyte, oligodendrocyte, and astrocyte signatures, though the lumbar GenAge increase in transcriptional age and DESeq2 decrease remained significant. The higher transcriptional age in ALS motor cortex also remained significantafter adjustment. Clinical associations were likewise clock dependent. Higher spinal DESeq2 age acceleration (AA) was associated with longer ALS duration, though this association was strongly influenced by the CHIT1 term. Higher GTExAge AA in cervical spinal cord, thoracic spinal cord, and occipital cortex was associated with shorter disease duration; no significant associations were observed for the other clock-tissue combinations or for ALS-FTD.

No clock differed by C9orf72 repeat expansion status.

In this study we found disease-associated differences in transcriptional age that localize to clinically relevant tissues. Differences in transcriptional aging across the ALS-FTD spectrum reflect interactions among clock architecture, disease-associated gene expression, tissue region, and cellular composition. Resolving these components reveals the biological context underlying divergent clock estimates and provides a framework for interpreting changes in transcriptional aging.

1. Introduction

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is characterised by progressive loss of voluntary motor function (Vasta et al. 2025), whereas frontotemporal dementia (FTD) encompasses a group of neurodegenerative diseases characterised by progressive deficits in behaviour, executive function or language (Bang et al. 2015). Despite their distinct clinical presentations, ALS and FTD form an overlapping disease spectrum. Individuals with ALS may develop cognitive and behavioural impairment, including frontotemporal dementia (ALS-FTD), while individuals presenting with FTD may also develop motor neuron dysfunction (Strong et al. 2017). This overlap extends to their genetic architecture, with C9orf72 repeat expansions representing a shared genetic cause of ALS and FTD identified independently in families with FTD, ALS, and combined FTD/ALS phenotypes (DeJesus-Hernandez et al. 2011; Renton et al. 2011). Studying ALS, ALS-FTD and FTD together therefore provides an opportunity to examine shared and disease-specific molecular patterns.

Across the ALS-FTD disease spectrum, age is a major risk factor. Consistent with this observation, population-based studies from European ALS registries have demonstrated an approximately linear relationship between log incidence and log age (Al-Chalabi et al. 2014).

FTD has historically been considered an early-onset dementia, but its incidence also increases with age, with a substantial disease burden occurring after age 65 (Urso et al. 2025).

Nevertheless, substantial heterogeneity in age at onset remains, and currently identified genetic, environmental, and lifestyle risk factors explain only part of this variation (Vasta et al. 2022).

Several pathogenic mechanisms implicated in ALS/FTD overlap with pathways that are also involved in the aging process, including autophagy, inflammation, nucleocytoplasmic transport, and RNA splicing (Niccoli et al. 2017). These observations motivate investigation of molecular measures of aging that may capture biological variation beyond chronological age alone.

Chronological age is the standard reference for aging studies, but it does not fully describe interindividual variation in biological aging (Min et al. 2024). Biological age is an abstract construct that reflects an individual's overall health state and underlying aging processes that cannot be directly observed or quantified (Kriukov et al. 2025). Alterations in gene expression have been associated with aging and age-related outcomes (de Magalhães et al. 2009; Tacutu et al. 2018; Welle et al. 2003; Rodwell et al. 2004; Lu et al. 2004; Glass et al. 2013; Peters et al. 2015; Fleischer et al. 2018). RNAAgeCalc is a multi-tissue transcriptional age calculator developed using RNA-sequencing data from the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) project and provides both across-tissue and tissue-specific age predictors (Ren and Kuan 2020). Pasta (Predicting Age-Shift from Transcriptomic Analysis) is a more recent multi-tissue transcriptomic aging clock, trained on 17,212 healthy samples from 21 studies using an "age-shift" learning framework, that predicts relative age across tissues and data types, including bulk and single-cell RNA-sequencing and microarray data (Salignon et al. 2026). Transcriptional age acceleration (AA) quantifies the deviation of estimated transcriptional age from that expected for chronological age and may therefore capture biologically meaningful variation in transcriptional aging.

Transcriptomic profiling has increasingly been used to characterize molecular heterogeneity in ALS and individual differences in brain aging. Using postmortem motor cortex gene expression data, Marriott et al. identified three molecular phenotypes linked to ALS pathogenesis: synaptic and neuropeptide signaling, oxidative stress and apoptosis, and neuroinflammation.

RNAAgeCalc-derived transcriptional AA was significantly lower in cluster 2 than cluster 1 in TargetALS motor cortex, with a similar but nonsignificant trend in KCL BrainBank, whereas no significant differences across clusters were observed in either blood dataset (Marriott et al. 2023). Using transcriptome-wide cerebral cortex gene expression data from several cohorts totaling 1,904 autopsied human brain samples, Rhinn and Abeliovich quantified individual variability in age-associated phenotypes and identified TMEM106B and GRN loci as determinants of differential aging in the cerebral cortex (Rhinn and Abeliovich 2017).

Transcriptomic clocks have not yet been compared systematically across the ALS-FTD disease

spectrum or across multiple central nervous system (CNS) tissues, including the spinal cord, where lower motor neurons degenerate in ALS. Clock estimates from bulk tissue may also reflect disease-related changes in gene expression and cell composition, as well as aging. In this study, we applied six transcriptomic clocks to 2,235 postmortem RNA-sequencing samples from 609 donors across 11 CNS regions in the New York Genome Center (NYGC) ALS Consortium cohort (Humphrey et al. 2026), including the RNAAgeCalc DESeq2, GTExAge, Pearson and GenAge signatures (Ren and Kuan 2020) and the Pasta regression and age-shift models (Salignon et al. 2026). We compared adjusted transcriptional age in ALS, ALS-FTD and FTD relative to non-neurological controls in each tissue and compared transcriptional age across tissues within each disease group. To identify the contributors to those differences, we decomposed clock estimates into individual gene contributions, evaluated these contributions in an independent cervical spinal cord dataset, and adjusted for cell composition estimated from the bulk RNA data. Finally, we examined associations between transcriptional age acceleration and ALS disease duration and C9orf72 repeat expansion status.

2. Methods

>Methods

2.1 Data and study population

We analysed NYGC ALS Consortium maximum likelihood expected counts and metadata (GEO GSE137810; Zenodo 18989237 and 20090702; Supplementary Table S1). Raw downloaded data included 58,929 genes across 2,574 samples preprocessed using the RAPiD-nf framework (STAR 2.7.2a, RSEM 1.3.1, GRCh38/GENCODE v30) (Di Tommaso et al. 2017; Humphrey et al. 2026). X/Y counts were summed for 45 pseudoautosomal genes. For this study, chronological age was defined as age at death. Donors whose age was reported only as "90 or older" were treated as having missing age and excluded from analyses requiring age at death; this excluded three control donors (15 samples). No ALS, ALS-FTD, or FTD donors were excluded for this reason. For the one donor with conflicting values, age at death was retained because it matched age at onset plus disease duration. C9orf72 repeat expansion status was taken from the consortium's annotation, based on whole-genome sequencing and repeat-primed polymerase chain reaction. Donors with ALS, ALS-FTD, FTD or no known neurological disease (controls) were analysed. For the four donor-tissue pairs sequenced twice, we retained the sample with the higher RNA integrity number (RIN), using read count to break ties. After deduplication and removal of samples with missing age or covariate data, the analysis included 2,235 samples from 609 donors across 11 regions with ≥50 samples each. Analyses were performed using Python and R.

2.2 Transcriptomic clocks

We used six published transcriptomic aging clocks: four brain-specific predictors implemented in RNAAgeCalc v1.22.0, based on the DESeq2 (472-gene), GTExAge (485-gene), Pearson (411gene), and GenAge (302-gene) feature sets (Ren and Kuan 2020), and the 8,113-gene Pasta regression (REG) and age-shift models (Salignon et al. 2026). RNAAgeCalc transcriptional age equalled intercept + Σ w g log(FPKM g + 1), where w g is the gene weight and FPKM is fragments per kilobase per million mapped reads. Only the RNAAgeCalc DESeq2 model contained CHIT1 as a feature. Clock units were years of transcriptional age (RNAAgeCalc), years of predicted age (Pasta REG) and age-score years (Pasta age-shift; one unit per year of age difference).

For each clock and tissue, Pearson correlation between transcriptional age and age at death was calculated separately in two groups: control donors and patients (ALS, ALS-FTD and FTD pooled). P values were corrected across the 11 tissues using Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR), separately for each clock and group (Benjamini and Hochberg 1995).

Unadjusted linear fits were used for visualization.

2.3 Association of disease status with transcriptional age acceleration

Each disease group was compared with controls in each tissue and for each clock by linear regression:

Transcriptional age ~ chronological age + disease status + technical covariates

The disease-status coefficient therefore represented the difference in transcriptional age relative to controls after adjustment for chronological age. Technical covariates were the tissue-specific covariates used in the consortium's differential expression models, selected using an empirical strategy (Methods Table 3; (Humphrey et al. 2026)). The consortium provided no covariates for occipital cortex, which was therefore adjusted for library preparation, the covariate shared by nine of the ten consortium tissue models. Tissues with fewer than eight samples in either group were not tested. FDR was computed across the tested tissues within each disease group and clock.

2.4 Tissue contrasts in transcriptional age within disease groups

Transcriptional age of each clock was fitted separately within ALS, ALS-FTD and FTD using linear mixed models with donor as a random intercept:

Transcriptional age ~ chronological age + tissue + library preparation + sex + (1 | donor)

Each model included samples with complete chronological age, sex and library preparation, from tissues with at least eight such samples in the disease group and no more than one sample per donor-tissue pair. Models were fit with restricted maximum likelihood with cerebellum as the reference tissue. Tissue contrasts were evaluated using Wald tests (z = estimate/SE) and normal approximation 95% confidence intervals (CIs) (estimate ± 1.96 × SE), where SE denotes the standard error. P values were FDR-adjusted across tissue contrasts within each disease group and clock. Because transcriptional age was the outcome, positive tissue coefficients indicate higher adjusted transcriptional age relative to cerebellum, in the clock-specific units defined above (Methods 2.2).

2.5 Gene contributions and independent validation

To quantify the contribution of individual genes to the ALS-control difference in RNAAgeCalc DESeq2 transcriptional age, we multiplied each gene's fixed clock weight by its adjusted ALScontrol difference in log 2 (FPKM + 1), estimated using the model described in Methods 2.3. Gene-level contributions were expressed in years of transcriptional age and summed to the overall ALS-control clock difference. We separately estimated ALS-control differences for the full 472-gene DESeq2 clock, the CHIT1 term, and the remaining 471-gene component using the same model. FDR correction was applied across tissues separately for each outcome.

We assessed reproducibility of these gene-level contributions in an independent cervical spinal cord RNA-seq cohort comprising 7 donors with sporadic ALS and 8 controls (SRA SRP064478, processed through recount3) (Brohawn et al. 2016; Wilks et al. 2021). Given the smaller validation sample numbers, ALS-control expression differences were estimated using parsimonious models adjusted for chronological age only, and the resulting gene contributions were compared with those from the primary cohort.

Finally, to assess whether increased CHIT1 expression in ALS spinal cord could be explained by altered microglial abundance, we modeled log 2 (FPKM + 1) CHIT1 expression in cervical and lumbar spinal cord as a function of ALS status, chronological age and technical covariates, first without and then with estimated microglial fraction. Microglial effects were expressed per 10percentage-point increase in estimated fraction, with FDR correction across the two spinal cord levels separately for each model term.

2.6 Cell composition

Cell type fractions estimated using Multi-subject Single Cell deconvolution (MuSiC) (Wang et al. 2019) were available from the ALS consortium for 11 spinal cord, eight cortical, and 13 cerebellar cell types in ALS and non-neurological control tissues. The consortium supplied no estimates for occipital cortex, hippocampus, thoracic cord or ALS-FTD/FTD samples, so these were not composition-adjusted.

Following the compositional approach of Merbaum et al., modeling included all estimated cell type fractions except one reference cell type to avoid collinearity. Within each tissue, the reference was the cell type with the highest mean and standard deviation across eligible samples. (Merbaum et al. 2025). The reference cell type was oligodendrocytes in all tissues except temporal cortex, where excitatory neurons were used, and cerebellum, where granule cells were used. The reference cell type was chosen once per tissue and used in every model.

Linear models with the Methods 2.3 covariates compared cell type fractions between ALS and controls:

Cell fraction ~ ALS status + chronological age + technical covariates

Clock models in spinal cord and unspecified motor cortex were compared before and after composition adjustment using identical samples, with model fit assessed by adjusted R², Akaike Information Criterion, and partial F-tests. Fractions were expressed as percentages, so differences are in percentage points, with FDR across all tested tissue-cell-type comparisons. To identify the cell types contributing to each ALS-control difference, the Methods 2.3 model was fitted on the composition-available samples without cell fractions and again with each cell type fraction individually, as well as with all fractions except the reference:

Transcriptional age ~ chronological age + ALS status + technical covariates + cell fraction

ALS-control differences with and without each fraction were compared, with FDR across tissues within clock and adjustment. This analysis was restricted to samples with cell composition estimates and used FDR correction across the eight eligible tissues; therefore, statistical significance may differ from that in Methods 2.3.

3.4 CHIT1 term accounts for the RNAAgeCalc DESeq2 decrease in ALS spinal cord

RNAAgeCalc DESeq2 was the only clock to show significantly lower transcriptional age in ALS cervical and lumbar spinal cord than in controls (Figure 1). We therefore examined the contribution of individual genes to this decrease. CHIT1 combined a large negative clock coefficient with the largest ALS-associated expression increase among the clock's genes (Figure 3A). Of all 472 clock genes, CHIT1 was the only gene to contribute an absolute value greater than 5 transcriptional years to the ALS-control difference (-8.37 years in cervical spinal cord; -5.34 years in lumbar spinal cord). Because the clock weight is negative, higher CHIT1 expression in ALS produced a negative contribution to RNAAgeCalc DESeq2 transcriptional age. CHIT1 expression was significantly higher in ALS than in controls after covariate adjustment in both cervical and lumbar spinal cord, resulting in a significant negative CHIT1 term difference between ALS and controls. Its negative contribution exceeded the overall decrease at both spinal cord levels and was therefore partly offset by contributions from other genes in the full DESeq2 model. Removing the CHIT1 term without retraining the model yielded a significantly higher transcriptional age in ALS relative to controls in cervical spinal cord, while the difference in the lumbar spinal cord was no longer significant (Figure 3B).

We next evaluated these findings in an independent cervical spinal cord bulk RNA-seq cohort.

The overall ALS-associated decrease in RNAAgeCalc DESeq2 transcriptional age was not replicated. However, the CHIT1 term was significantly negatively associated with ALS status, whereas DESeq2 transcriptional age without the CHIT1 term was positively associated with ALS status. Thus, although the overall clock result was not replicated, the independent cohort supported the opposing contribution of CHIT1 to the DESeq2 transcriptional age estimate.

In ALS spinal cord, CHIT1 has previously been found to be increased in microglia and macrophages (Steinacker et al. 2018), so we also examined the association between the microglial fraction and CHIT1 expression. The NYGC ALS Consortium provides estimated celltype fractions derived from deconvolution of bulk RNA-seq data for a subset of the CNS tissues, including cervical and lumbar spinal cord. We therefore modeled CHIT1 expression as a function of ALS status with and without adjustment for estimated microglial fraction. Higher microglial fraction was significantly associated with higher CHIT1 expression in both cervical and lumbar spinal cord (Figure 3C). 3.5 ALS-associated transcriptional age differences show tissue-specific sensitivity to cell composition

We next investigated whether ALS-associated changes in cell composition contributed to the transcriptional age differences observed across multiple clocks. To address this, we first characterized differences in estimated cell type fractions between ALS and control tissues. After FDR correction, most significant associations were in the cervical and lumbar spinal cord (Supplementary Figure S2). In ALS spinal cord, oligodendrocyte fractions were significantly lower and astrocyte, microglial and lymphocyte fractions were higher; lumbar Schwann-cell and pericyte fractions were also higher.

Six cortical and cerebellar cell fraction comparisons remained significant after FDR correction.

In contrast to the spinal cord, ALS frontal cortex showed lower astrocyte and higher oligodendrocyte fractions, and astrocyte fractions were also lower in medial motor cortex. No significant cell fraction differences were observed in unspecified motor cortex.

For cell composition to contribute to an ALS-associated clock difference, the relevant cell fractions must differ between ALS and controls and be associated with the clock estimate. We therefore examined the association between transcriptional age and ALS status before and after adjustment for individual cell type fractions and for overall cell composition in the three tissues showing the strongest ALS-associated differences: cervical spinal cord, lumbar spinal cord, and unspecified motor cortex.

In the spinal cord, adjustment for cell composition improved model fit for all clocks and attenuated several ALS-control differences (Supplementary Figure S3; Figure 4). GTExAge, Pearson, Pasta REG, Pasta age-shift, and cervical GenAge no longer passed FDR correction after adjustment, leaving lumbar GenAge as the only significant positive association. The ALSassociated RNAAgeCalc DESeq2 decreases remained significant.

When cell fractions were considered individually, oligodendrocyte adjustment attenuated the spinal ALS-control differences for GTExAge, Pearson, Pasta REG, and Pasta age-shift, but strengthened the GenAge differences in both cord regions. After adjustment for oligodendrocyte fraction, three of six significant cervical and three of five significant lumbar associations no longer passed FDR correction. Astrocyte adjustment similarly attenuated most clock differences, with three cervical and one lumbar association losing FDR significance, but the RNAAgeCalc DESeq2 decreases became stronger after adjustment. Adjustment for lymphocyte or microglial fraction had comparatively little effect on most clocks; however, microglial adjustment attenuated the cervical Pearson increase, and adjustment for either microglia or lymphocytes attenuated the RNAAgeCalc DESeq2 decreases in both spinal cord regions, consistent with the relationship between microglial abundance and CHIT1 expression (Results 3.4). Overall, spinal cord cell composition contributed substantially to the ALS-associated differences in transcriptional age. Among the cell types examined, oligodendrocyte and astrocyte fractions produced the greatest attenuation of ALS-associated transcriptional age differences across most clocks, whereas lymphocyte and microglial fractions had a greater relative effect on RNAAgeCalc DESeq2.

In unspecified motor cortex, inclusion of cell composition also improved the model fit for all clocks, though the partial F-test for the DESeq2 clock was only nominally significant (p = 0.05).

In contrast to the spinal cord, the significant positive associations generally retained their direction and significance after adjustment for overall cell composition. Individual cell type adjustment showed no consistent pattern of attenuation across clocks. In GenAge, adjustment for endothelial or microglial fraction strengthened the positive ALS-control difference, which then passed FDR correction. Conversely, adjustment for excitatory neuron or oligodendrocyte fraction attenuated the Pasta REG difference, which no longer passed FDR correction. Overall, both joint and individual cell type adjustments had less consistent effects on ALS-associated transcriptional age differences in unspecified motor cortex than in the spinal cord.

3.6 RNAAgeCalc DESeq2 and GTExAge transcriptional age acceleration are associated with ALS disease duration

Having examined the contribution of cell composition to transcriptional age estimates, we next investigated whether AA was associated with disease duration in ALS and ALS-FTD. Among the five clocks that generally estimated higher transcriptional age in ALS, only GTExAge showed significant associations between AA and disease duration in ALS. Higher GTExAge AA in the occipital cortex and cervical and thoracic spinal cord was associated with an increased hazard of death, indicating shorter ALS duration (Figure 5A). No associations in ALS-FTD remained significant after FDR correction.

In contrast, higher DESeq2 AA was associated with longer ALS duration in cervical and lumbar spinal cord (Figure 5B). Higher CHIT1 expression was associated with shorter ALS duration at all cord levels and in unspecified motor cortex. After removing CHIT1 from the DESeq2 model, the association between higher AA and shorter ALS duration in the thoracic spinal cord did not meet the FDR significance threshold (FDR = 0.053). No duration associations in ALS-FTD remained significant after FDR correction. 22 Across all tested tissues and clocks, no differences between C9orf72 expansion carriers and noncarriers remained significant after FDR correction. This remained the case after adjustment for estimated cell-type composition in tissues with available deconvolution data (Supplementary Figure S4).

4. Discussion

In this study, we characterized tissue-specific differences in transcriptional age across ALS, ALS-FTD, and FTD using six transcriptional aging clocks. Disease-associated differences varied across tissues and clocks, with the strongest ALS and ALS-FTD differences occurring in the spinal cord. Significant changes were also identified in the unspecified motor cortex in ALS and in the frontal and temporal cortices in FTD. We further examined the contribution of diseaseassociated gene expression and cell composition to these differences and tested whether transcriptional AA was related to disease duration and whether transcriptional age differed by donor C9orf72 repeat expansion status. Together, these analyses show that transcriptional aging estimates in ALS and FTD are strongly dependent on the clock and tissue examined and may reflect disease-associated gene expression and cellular composition in addition to aging-related processes.

Although most clocks correlated significantly with chronological age across tissues, their performance was not uniform. Correlations were higher in controls than in patients for 54 of 66 clock-tissue combinations, despite a greater number of significant correlations in the larger pooled patient group, emphasizing that statistical significance alone does not indicate stronger age prediction. Differences in calibration and predicted age range, including compression of RNAAgeCalc DESeq2 estimates, further support interpreting disease-associated differences in the context of individual clock performance.

4.1 Gene-level contributions to the RNAAgeCalc DESeq2 decrease

The direction of ALS-control differences in the spinal cord depended on the clock. Most of the clocks estimated significantly higher transcriptional age in ALS, whereas RNAAgeCalc DESeq2 estimated lower age. Gene-level decomposition and cell composition adjustment traced these opposite shifts to two related sources. A single gene, CHIT1, was the primary contributor to the DESeq2 decrease in ALS. In our analysis, estimated microglial fraction provided a link between the gene-level and cell composition effects, contributing to the increase in CHIT1 expression. In addition, adjustment for either microglial or lymphocyte fractions attenuated the ALS-associated decrease in DESeq2 transcriptional age. CHIT1 encodes chitotriosidase, which is expressed by cells of the monocyte/macrophage lineage and associated with innate immunity and inflammation (Steinacker et al. 2018). In postmortem ALS spinal cord, chitotriosidase was found mainly in the lateral and anterior corticospinal tracts, where it colocalised with subpopulations of IBA1-positive microglia and CD68-positive macrophages (Steinacker et al. 2018). In the brain, chitotriosidase staining was sparse and much weaker (Steinacker et al. 2018). Chitotriosidase levels were also raised in ALS cerebrospinal fluid (Steinacker et al. 2018) and correlated with the rate of disease progression (Thompson et al. 2019; Steinacker et al. 2018).

Because of its large negative weight in the DESeq2 clock, the marked increase in CHIT1 expression in ALS spinal cord produced a negative contribution larger than the net clock decrease, with other genes partially offsetting the effect of CHIT1 in the full clock. Higher estimated microglial abundance was associated with, and partially contributed to, the CHIT1 expression difference. In the independent cervical dataset, CHIT1 was again the largest negative contributor, but other gene contributions offset it and the full DESeq2 model did not show a significant ALS-associated difference in transcriptional age. These findings show that a single disease-associated gene can strongly influence a multigene clock, underscoring the need for gene-level decomposition before interpreting transcriptional age differences as biological aging.

4.2 Cell-associated contributions to transcriptional aging differences

Cell composition also emerged as a major contributor to the clocks that demonstrated higher transcriptional age in ALS spinal cord. Inclusion of cell composition as a covariate attenuated ALS spinal cord differences in all clocks except the lumbar GenAge, which remained significant.

Cell composition is a plausible contributor to an ALS-control difference in transcriptional age when the estimated cell type fraction differs by disease status and is associated with the clock. In the NYGC data, ALS spinal cord had lower estimated oligodendrocyte fractions and higher astrocyte, microglial, and lymphocyte fractions than non-neurological control spinal cord. These differences were consistent with the glial activation and reduced oligodendrocyte-associated expression reported in an earlier NYGC spinal-cord cohort (Humphrey et al. 2023). Consistent with a compositional contribution, adjustment for oligodendrocyte fraction alone attenuated most of the ALS-control clock differences, whereas astrocyte adjustment preferentially attenuated the GenAge and cervical Pasta REG increases.

Because bulk tissue deconvolution relies on reference expression signatures, inferred cell-type fractions can be influenced by both true compositional differences and alterations in cell typespecific expression. Deconvolution alone cannot reliably distinguish these sources (Wolfram-Schauerte et al. 2025). In the ALS consortium resource, the cell composition estimates were derived from bulk expression using tissue-matched single-cell references, with the single-nucleus reference samples restricted to non-neurological donors. Because reference-based deconvolution assumes that reference expression signatures adequately represent the target tissue, ALSassociated reactive states absent from the reference could influence the inferred fractions. Thus, the estimated differences may reflect true changes in cell abundance, altered cell state, or both. This limitation was also recognized in the NYGC study, which noted that bulk RNA-seq cannot distinguish true composition changes from cell-state shifts (Humphrey et al. 2026).

In the case of glial fractions, both interpretations are biologically plausible. Mature oligodendrocyte degeneration has been demonstrated in SOD1-mutant ALS mice (Kang et al. 2013), whereas human postmortem studies have identified reactive oligodendrocyte, microglial, and astrocyte states (Masrori et al. 2025; Zelic et al. 2025; Georgopoulou et al. 2025).

Consequently, attenuation of ALS-associated clock differences after adjustment for deconvolved cell composition supports the sensitivity of these clocks to cell-associated signals but does not establish whether those signals reflect changes in cell abundance, cellular state, or both.

Importantly, sensitivity to cell composition does not necessarily make these transcriptional aging signals biologically uninformative or distinguish disease-related remodeling from biological aging. In a recent whole blood study of epigenetic aging in ALS, apparent ALS-control differences were eliminated after adjustment for white blood cell proportions, showing that the unadjusted signals were strongly dependent on immune cell composition (Merbaum et al. 2025).

As discussed by Merbaum et al., such composition-dependent signals are not necessarily biologically uninformative, as changes in immune cell populations may themselves reflect aging processes (Merbaum et al. 2025). This distinction is also relevant to transcriptional aging, as immune cell shifts are features of immunosenescence (Castro et al. 2024; von Leden et al. 2017) and age-related CNS changes include alterations in glial abundance and activation state (Labarta-Bajo and Allen 2025; Tylek and Basta-Kaim 2025). Accordingly, attenuation of spinal transcriptional age differences after composition adjustment indicates sensitivity to disease-associated cell-type signals while leaving open how those signals relate to ALS pathobiology, biological aging, or processes shared by both.

Beyond these ALS-specific findings, the regional distribution of transcriptional age differences broadly aligned with the clinical and anatomical features of the ALS-FTD spectrum. In ALS and ALS-FTD, the strongest disease-associated differences occurred in the spinal cord, whereas in FTD, significant differences were observed in frontal and temporal cortices. Within-disease comparisons similarly showed higher transcriptional age in frontal and temporal cortex relative to cerebellum in FTD across most clocks. Although the smaller number of evaluable tissues and samples in ALS-FTD and FTD limits direct comparisons between disease groups, these findings suggest that transcriptional aging signatures vary across anatomically relevant regions of the ALS-FTD spectrum rather than representing a uniform CNS-wide shift.

4.3 Disease duration and C9orf72

Associations with clinical features further illustrate that transcriptional clocks can capture distinct aspects of ALS biology. Higher DESeq2 AA was associated with longer disease duration, but this association was strongly influenced by CHIT1, with higher spinal CHIT1 expression corresponding to shorter duration. This direction is consistent with cerebrospinal fluid chitotriosidase levels that correlate with faster progression (Thompson et al. 2019) and were associated with survival in one cohort (Thompson et al. 2019). By contrast, higher GTExAge AA was associated with shorter disease duration in occipital cortex, cervical and thoracic spinal cord, which is directionally consistent with previous methylation studies linking greater AA to earlier onset and shorter disease duration in C9orf72 expansion carriers (Zhang et al. 2017) and earlier onset and shorter survival in ALS more broadly (Zhao et al. 2024; Zhang et al. 2020). However, this relationship has not been consistent across molecular aging studies, as a subsequent large methylation-based analysis found no association between epigenetic AA and ALS survival (Merbaum et al. 2025).

We found no evidence that tissue transcriptional age differed by C9orf72 repeat expansion status after FDR adjustment. This contrasts with the recent whole blood methylation study by Merbaum and colleagues, in which C9orf72 expansion carriers showed higher DunedinPACE and shorter DNAm-estimated telomere length after adjustment for white blood cell composition (Merbaum et al. 2025). The discrepancy may reflect differences in molecular data type, tissue, clock construction, and statistical power rather than conflicting evidence about aging in C9orf72 ALS.

Indeed, the methylation study itself found no C9orf72-associated difference for the Horvath, PCGrimAge, or pan-mammalian clocks, demonstrating that even within a single molecular layer the apparent aging phenotype depends on the clock used. Together with the divergent tissue and clinical associations observed across transcriptomic clocks in the present study, these findings argue against interpreting transcriptional AA as a single, clock-independent measure of biological aging.

4.4 Limitations

Several limitations constrain the interpretation and generalizability of these findings. MuSiC cell composition estimates were unavailable for ALS-FTD and FTD and for several regions, including thoracic spinal cord, occipital cortex, and hippocampus, preventing compositionadjusted analyses in these groups and tissues. Moreover, cell fractions were estimated from bulk RNA-seq and may reflect both differences in cellular abundance and disease-associated changes in cell state, as discussed above. Independent validation was also limited by the small cervical spinal cord cohort, which included only seven ALS and eight control donors. The crosssectional, postmortem design also limits our ability to distinguish aging-related changes from changes occurring as a consequence of disease, and sample availability differed substantially across tissues and disease groups, particularly for ALS-FTD and FTD. Finally, the RNAAgeCalc brain models were trained on all GTEx CNS tissues, which may limit their calibration and biological specificity. More broadly, differences in performance and direction across clocks indicate that transcriptional age estimates are dependent on clock construction and should not be interpreted as direct measures of biological aging.

5. Conclusions

In this study, we characterized tissue-specific differences in transcriptional age across the ALS-FTD spectrum using six transcriptomic aging clocks and investigated how these signals relate to disease-associated gene expression, cellular composition, clinical duration, and C9orf72 repeat expansion status. Disease-associated differences in transcriptional age localized to clinically relevant tissues but varied in direction and magnitude across clocks. In ALS, the strongest differences were localized to the spinal cord, where RNAAgeCalc DESeq2 estimated lower transcriptional age, whereas the other clocks estimated higher age when significant. Gene-level decomposition identified CHIT1 as the primary contributor to the DESeq2 decrease, emphasizing the importance of gene-level model decomposition when interpreting transcriptional clock estimates. Adjustment for estimated spinal cord cell composition substantially attenuated several of the ALS-associated increases observed with other clocks. Associations with disease duration were likewise clock-dependent, and no differences by C9orf72 repeat expansion status remained significant after FDR correction. Together, these findings suggest that transcriptional aging signatures across the ALS-FTD spectrum reflect interactions among disease-associated gene expression, regional vulnerability, cellular remodeling, and the molecular features captured by individual aging clocks, and should not be interpreted as direct, clock-independent measures of biological aging.
