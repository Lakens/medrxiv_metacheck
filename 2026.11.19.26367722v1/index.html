How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

This module retrieves information from repositories.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Objective: Public electronic health record (EHR) databases differ in schema, identifiers, coding systems, units, and event structure, making disease-specific studies across multiple sources difficult to construct and audit. We developed rendehR, a computational pipeline and R package for cohort-centered extraction and harmonization of multi-source EHR data.

.

1. Introduction

Electronic health records (EHRs) are an important resource for clinical research. Public resources such as the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care III (MIMIC-III) [1], MIMIC-IV [2], and the eICU Collaborative Research Database [3] provide detailed de-identified clinical data and have become widely used in observational studies and machine learning benchmarks [4] [5] [6]. They contain demographics, diagnoses, procedures, medications, laboratory measurements, and physiological observations, but represent this information differently across sources.

EHRs provide an opportunity for multi-source study; however, such studies require source-specific decisions before patients and clinical variables can be represented in a common analysis dataset.

Medical concept extraction, for instance, involves technical and clinical choices: which source tables and item identifiers to use, how to reconcile units and timestamps, and which plausibility rules to apply. These choices shape how a concept appears in the final dataset and affect how downstream analyses can be interpreted. Harmonizing medical concepts, therefore requires balancing between enabling cross-source analysis and preserving clinically meaningful sourcespecific details [7].

A range of software tools in Python and R have been developed to facilitate the extraction, processing and harmonization of EHR data. MIMIC-Extract [8] and FIDDLE [9] provide preprocessing pipelines for structured EHR data, focused on features construction for downstream prediction tasks. The MIMIC-Code and eICU-Code repositories provide reusable SQL implementations of derived clinical variables [10, 11]. In R, rEHR [12] and rcprd [13] provide database-backed workflows for extraction and processing of large primary-care EHR data, while dxpr [14] provides tools for diagnosis and procedure code processing. Ricu provides a unified interface to multiple ICU databases, including MIMIC and eICU-CRD [15] by separating sourceagnostic clinical concepts from the source-specific mappings required to extract them. The Python BlendedICU library harmonizes multiple ICU databases into a common dataset and maps the harmonized representation to the Observational Medical Outcomes Partnership (OMOP) [16] common data format [17].

Existing tools generally harmonize the full source database first and extract the study population afterward. However, a disease-specific study typically starts with identifying the population defined by the research question and the clinical variables extracted afterward. Cohort identification and variable construction may require source-specific diagnosis mappings or a more complex executable phenotype. A disease-specific workflow therefore needs to support cohort extraction while preserving the source-specific definitions and transformations that produced the analytical dataset.

We developed rendehR to support a workflow that integrates with the R ecosystem, scales across data sources and harmonized concepts, and is designed for cohort-first, provenance-aware applications. rendehR treats cohort definition as an explicit executable component of dataset construction. Cohorts can be specified using diagnosis codes, identifiers, or source-specific SQL, allowing definitions that range from code-based phenotypes to algorithms such as sepsis. After cohort membership is determined, source data is restricted to the selected population, and clinical concepts are derived using source-specific SQL. Simple concepts can be generated from configuration files, while more complex definitions can use curated SQL, including adaptations of community implementations where appropriate. The package retains the SQL and provenance associated with cohort and concept construction, and links these definitions to persistent, derived tables and harmonized outputs.

Given the growing use of large language models and agentic approaches in clinical data harmonization, including clinical concept mapping, automated SQL generation, and multi-agent data transformation workflows [18] [19] [20], we extended rendehR to support AI-assisted workflows.

We added three agent skills for cohort construction, concept development, and integration of new data sources, along with an MCP server exposing nine read-only tools for accessing project configurations, concept definitions, source dictionaries, and cohort summaries.

We evaluate rendehR using MIMIC-III, MIMIC-IV, and eICU-CRD. Spinal cord injury is used as a case example to demonstrate use and evaluate cohort construction across different diagnosis and identifier structures, whereas sepsis is used to evaluate SQL-defined cohort selection based on a multivariable clinical phenotype. We also compare selected clinical concepts with established reference implementations, quantify the data retained after cohort selection, and demonstrate construction of an analysis dataset from the harmonized outputs.

2. Materials and Methods

>Materials and Methods rendehR currently supports three public EHR databases available through PhysioNet: MIMIC-III version 1.4 [21], MIMIC-IV version 3.1 [2, 22], and eICU-CRD version 2.0 [23].

2.1 Data sources and software environment

MIMIC-III contains 58,976 hospital admissions and 53,423 ICU stays from Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center between 2001 and 2012. MIMIC-IV expands this resource, containing 546,028 hospital admissions and 94,458 ICU stays from 223,452 unique individuals. eICU-CRD is a multi-center source, with more than 139,000 patients and 200,000 ICU stays from 208 hospitals across the United States. These databases contain de-identified demographics, diagnoses, medications, laboratory measurements, physiological observations, procedures, and clinical outcomes, but differ in their relational structure, identifier hierarchies, diagnosis representations, clinical item dictionaries, and organization of hospital-and ICU-level events.

Access to the full databases is provided through PhysioNet platform [24] and requires completion of the corresponding credentialing and data-use requirements. Once users obtain the required credentials and access permissions from PhysioNet, they can use rendehR to download and process the approved EHR datasets. The package also provides preconfigured workflow for MIMIC-III Demo version 1.4 [25], MIMIC-IV Demo version 2.2 [26], and eICU Demo version 2.0.1 [27]. The demo datasets are publicly available and can be used without credentials. rendehR is implemented in R (>=4.1) [28]. DuckDB [29] is used to query raw source files, execute cohort-selection logic, resolve cohort identifiers, and filter source tables before construction of the cohort-specific dataset. Cohort-restricted datasets are written to Apache Parquet [30] and subsequently loaded into the database backend. PostgreSQL [31] is currently used for the full downstream workflow, including curated concept execution, harmonization, multi-source merging, and the primary data-access interfaces. DuckDB can additionally persist cohort databases and execute supported concepts; full DuckDB backend support is planned for version 2.

2.2 rendehR workflow

rendehR organizes EHR dataset construction around the study cohort (Figure 1). Source configuration defines the raw tables, data types, variable mappings, and patient, hospital-admission, and ICU-stay identifiers for each database. The cohort definition is executed first; source tables are then restricted to the selected patients. Cohort-restricted data can then be stored as Parquet files or loaded into a persistent PostgreSQL database. Next, clinical concepts are constructed from source-specific definitions. rendehR classifies concepts into two general categories, atomic and complex, based on the complexity of their extraction requirements. Extracted concept tables are then harmonized into flat, time-series, and duration representations. Each stage produces persistent intermediate outputs that can be inspected independently and rerun without reconstructing the entire pipeline.

2.3 Cohort definitions and extraction

rendehR supports several cohort-definition methods. A cohort can be specified using diagnosiscode resources, a set of patients, hospital-admission, or ICU-stay identifiers, an R callback, or user-supplied SQL. SQL cohort definitions are executed in DuckDB against typed views of the raw source files. More complex phenotypes can use an ordered sequence of setup SQL statements before the final cohort query runs.

The current version of rendehR includes SCID and Sepsis-3 as packaged reference cohorts. They illustrate two different cohort-definition mechanisms: diagnosis-based selection and an executable clinical phenotype defined in SQL. For diagnosis-based selection, users provide a dictionary of ICD-9-CM and ICD-10-CM codes, which can be supplied as CSV or RDS files. The dictionary must contain the diagnosis codes and their corresponding ICD versions, and codes can be provided with or without decimal points. The SCID reference dictionary additionally includes long descriptions and related diagnostic categories. For SQL-based cohort selection, users provide the path to a SQL file or a series of SQL files containing cohort-selection queries written in DuckDBcompatible SQL. rendehR then executes these queries against the source data to identify the qualifying identifiers of the cohort, as demonstrated by the Sepsis-3 phenotype.

The output of cohort selection is then mapped to the identifier hierarchy defined for each source.

The identifier returned by the cohort definition determines the selection grain, whereas a separate extraction-scope argument determines whether broader linked context is retained (Figure 2). For example, the granularity of sepsis cohort is at ICU-stay level because the cohort is identifies qualifying ICU stays based on Sepsis-3 criteria [32]. The user can keep only those stays or expand the extraction to the corresponding hospital admission (and include non-septic ICU stays) or full patient record available for a patient once was diagnosed with sepsis. A diagnosis-code-based cohort such as spinal cord injury and disease (SCID), on the other hand, is commonly identified at the hospital-admission level. In that case, records from ICU stays linked to the selected admission are kept as part of that admission-level context, while extraction can be expanded further to include the patient's broader record when required.

When the extraction scope is expanded, the linked patient-, admission-, and ICU-level identifiers are recorded in a cohort index. This index identifies which records belong to the selected cohort context and is used to filter downstream source tables during cohort-specific dataset construction.

Once cohort membership and extraction scope have been resolved, clinical tables are filtered to the selected cohort before downstream concept construction. Reference tables that are not patientspecific are kept in their original form. The cohort-restricted tables are converted to Parquet files which the user can choose to keep. This Parquet output is itself a reusable research artifact. It provides a portable, cohort-specific representation of the source EHR that can be used independently or as input to the downstream rendehR concept and harmonization workflow.

2.4 Extraction of clinical concepts

Once the cohort database is built, clinical variables are defined through source-aware concept specifications. rendehR follows the source-agnostic concept principle used by ricu [15] by separating the clinical meaning of a variable from the source-specific instructions needed to extract it. A source-specific recipe specifies the source table, item identifiers or matching expressions, timestamp and value fields, units, transformations, and optional value-range rules. rendehR differs from ricu in how these definitions are executed and retained. In rendehR, source-specific concept definitions are compiled into inspectable SQL, executed against the cohort-specific database, and retained together with the resulting concept tables and provenance information.

rendehR categorizes concepts into two general categories, atomic and complex, based on how they are extracted. Atomic concepts are variables that can be extracted from a source table using source mapping and simple transformation rules, without requiring a multi-stage clinical derivation. For atomic time-series and duration concepts, these specifications are stored in configuration files and translated into SQL before execution. The generated SQL is written to source-specific files, so the implemented definition can be inspected directly. More complex concepts, those requiring joins across multiple tables, temporal logic, or multi-stage derivation, are implemented using curated SQL. Users can override existing concepts by editing the original configuration file or supplying a new one from the provided template. Additional atomic concepts can be added through configuration files; complex concepts can be added with user SQL.

2.5 Provenance and traceability

To make each concept and cohort traceable, rendehR records provenance at each stage of cohort and concept construction. Cohort metadata captures the selection route, identifier level, extraction scope, cohort counts, cohort identifiers, source release, and build time. Table-level reports are also generated during cohort construction. The cohort hierarchy links patient-, hospital-admission-, and ICU-stay identifiers, defining the extraction context. Cohorts trace back from the packaged ICD definition or SQL definitions, through source-specific qualifying identifiers and the cohort hierarchy, to the metadata and table reports.

Concept provenance is stored in four artifacts; a) Concept configuration files record source mappings, units, value ranges, and extraction item identifiers or matching rules; b) SQL files contain the executable definition, whether generated or curated; c) Concept tables hold the raw data alongside source values and identifiers for each source; d) The registry stores attribution and build metadata, with each entry listing concept name, output schema, origin, reference or URL, and build time. A concept can be traced by starting from its harmonized name, finding the source mapping or SQL, then locating the source-specific table and its registry entry.

2.6 Harmonized analytical representations

After source-specific concepts are constructed, rendehR harmonizes them into three output types: flat, time-series, and duration tables. Flat tables contain variables which are constant at the relevant analysis level, such as demographics, comorbidities, outcomes, or summarized clinical characteristics. Time-series tables contain repeated measurements in long format, with the harmonized concept name, value, unit, source identifiers, and measurement time. For events with defined start and end times, duration tables represent interval-valued events, such as treatments or medical ingredient exposures. Time-series and duration observations are aligned relative to hospital admission using elapsed time in minutes. When ICU timing is available, ICU-relative timing and ICU length-of-stay information are also retained. The same concept tables, therefore, support hospital-relative or ICU-relative analyses without redefining the underlying concept.

Harmonized tables can then be combined across supported sources while retaining the source database and native identifiers. Access functions provide long-format data directly or construct wide representations using user-specified time bins and aggregation functions. These outputs are intended to reduce source-specific extraction work. At the current version, tasks such as missingdata handling and feature construction stay with the investigator.

2.7 Verification and validation

rendehR implement both technical verification and concept creation validation. Technical verification checks whether the pipeline produces the expected files, schemas, and tables at each major stage, lowering the risk that a pipeline failure slips silently into the analysis datasets. Five functions cover the main stages: the download process, database generation, and cohort extraction and harmonization.

Concept-level validation compares selected concepts against an established reference, the ricu package. This comparison shows where rendehR and the reference agree, and whether any differences are expected given the study definition. Using the spinal cord injury cohort across all three sources, we compared source-specific mappings, units, timestamps, row counts, and value distributions for each selected concept.

We compared value distributions for all time-series concepts shared by rendehR and ricu. For each concept and data source, we compared values from the same hospital admissions using the twosample Kolmogorov-Smirnov statistic D [33, 34]. This statistic measures the largest vertical distance between the two empirical cumulative distribution functions, ranging from 0 for identical distributions to 1 for complete separation. We first excluded rendehR values outside ricu's plausibility limits to match ricu's built-in filtering, then calculated D using all values for each concept.

Comparisons involved up to one million values, so even very small differences could be statistically significant. We therefore used D as a descriptive effect size and predefined concordance as D ≤ 0.05. Asymptotic p-values are reported for completeness. Concepts for which the packages intentionally use different source items were documented in advance, together with the reasons, and are summarized separately. For each source, we report the number of concepts, the mean and range of D, and the concept with the largest distance. Results for individual concepts, including p-values, are provided in Supplementary Table 6 and 7.

Validation covered sex, age, length of stay, and in-hospital mortality as flat variables, along with all 65 common time series variables and five exposure variables as examples of duration variables.

Clinical plausibility summaries report observed units, value ranges, selected quantiles, missingness, observation counts, unique subjects or visits, and temporal distributions where applicable. Where the concept configuration specifies expected units or value ranges, observed values are checked against them. These summaries are then reviewed to flag unexpected values, units, or distributions. Agreement in mapped items, matched values, and summary measures of numerical differences is reported in Section 3.4.

2-8 Interactive tools and AI agent support

Three components in the current version of rendehR supports users. Assistant wizards are interactive R functions for setting up an assistant, making templates, adding concepts, and adding a new source. Once a flow is complete, the result can be saved as an R script. Moreover, to investigate patient-level data in a cohort, a complementary rendehR Shiny app is provided in the package, allowing users to select the harmonized time-series, flat, and duration variables that exist for a patient and visualize them.

The package also ships three agent skills: rendehR-user (cohort construction, pipeline runs and verification, retrieval, export and merging), rendehR-add-concept (adding or adapting a concept through supplemental configuration or SQL, with provenance and validation steps) and rendehRadd-source (registering a new database family). Skills are instruction sets that an AI coding agent loads on demand; depending on the request, the agent returns reproducible R code or runs it for the user.

The third component is a Model Context Protocol (MCP) server bundled with the package, which exposes nine read-only tools to an AI agent: checking the installation and project configuration, searching the packaged concept definitions and the source dictionaries (with local synonym expansion), previewing a new concept-configuration row from an existing template, and listing the concepts and aggregate cohort counts of a prepared database.

3.1 Case examples of cohort construction across sources

As use-case examples, we used rendehR to construct a spinal cord injury and disease (SCID) and sepsis cohorts across three supported EHR sources (MIMIC-III, MIMIC-IV, and eICU-CRD) and to harmonize concepts. The two cohorts differ in three ways: selection grain, expansion scope, and extraction method.

SCID is identified using source-specific ICD-9-CM and ICD-10-CM code lists developed with expert input. Because diagnostic ICDs are recorded at the hospital level, the selection grain for SCID is hospital admissions. The sepsis cohort, in contrast, is selected at the ICU-stay level using Sepsis-3 criteria [32], where suspected infection is identified from the timing of antibiotic administration and microbiology cultures, and qualifying stays have a Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score [35] of at least 2 within the corresponding infection window.

For SCID, rendehR expands extraction to patient context by default, anchored on the unit where the diagnosis is recorded. This preserves previous and subsequent admissions and ICU stays for the same patient, which may be clinically relevant even when the qualifying diagnosis is recorded during only one encounter.

In MIMIC-III, 4,320 hospital admissions satisfied the SCID definition, corresponding to 3,824 unique patients. Patient-level expansion retained all available records associated with these patients, resulting in 5,725 hospital admissions and 5,983 ICU stays in the cohort-specific dataset.

In MIMIC-IV, 39,850 hospital admissions satisfied the SCID definition, corresponding to 26,177 unique patients. Expansion to patient context resulted in 104,988 hospital admissions and 17,405 ICU stays. In eICU-CRD, the SCID definition identified 2,603 qualifying ICU stays corresponding to 2,343 hospital admissions. After expansion to patient context, the retained patient table contained 2,823 hospital admissions of 2,259 unique patients with 3,441 ICU stay-level records associated with the selected patients. Sepsis-3 is defined at the ICU-stay level in all three sources using the Sepsis-3 criteria, and by default it is not expanded to a higher level of granularity. The resulting cohorts contained 34460, 42296, and 47035 qualifying ICU stays in MIMIC-III, MIMIC-IV, and eICU-CRD, respectively. Sepsis cohort selection also differs from SCID in its method of extraction. SCID uses an R function to select qualifying records, while Sepsis-3 is implemented using source-specific SQL adapted from the MIT-LCP MIMIC-Code definitions [36].

3.2 Cohort restriction and data construction

Restricting source tables to the selected cohort substantially reduced the data retained for downstream concept construction particularly for rare conditions such as SCID. In the MIMIC-III SCID cohort, rendehR retained 34.9 million chartevents rows and 2.44 million labevents rows, compared with 330.71 and 27.85 rows in the corresponding source tables -a reduction of 90 % and 87 %, respectively. These reductions for the same tables are 81% and 88% for MIMIC-IV, respectively. For eICU-CRD, 4.21 million nursecharting rows and 3.98 million vitalperiodic rows, are kept, reductions of 97.2% relative to their source tables.

Across all cohort-filtered clinical tables, 10.79% of MIMIC-III source rows, 31.86% of MIMIC-IV and 2.69% of eICU-CRD were retained for the SCID cohort. For Sepsis-3, these numbers are 81.51%, 50.89%, and 38.15% for MIMIC-III, MIMIC-IV, and eICU-CRD, respectively, which is expected. Detailed per-table row counts and build times are provided in Supplementary Table 1-3.

3.3 Clinical concept inventory

The current rendehR library contains 22 flat concepts, 123 time-series concepts, and 75 duration concepts. The atomic configuration includes 220 distinct concept names with source-specific mappings across MIMIC-III, MIMIC-IV, and eICU-CRD. Flat concepts include variables that are constant within a hospital admission, such as demographics, hospital and ICU length of stay, admission type, and admission location. Time-series concepts include repeated physiological and laboratory measurements such as heart rate, mean arterial pressure, creatinine, lactate, and glucose.

Duration concepts represent interval-valued exposures and interventions.

The current duration library consists primarily of medication-exposure concepts adapted from BlendedICU [17]. These concepts harmonize exposure to a common active ingredient and its timing across sources. Route of administration, dose, formulation, and source-specific units may be retained where available but are not currently harmonized into a common representation.

Additional duration concepts include selected non-medication interventions for which sourcespecific definitions are available.

The complete concept inventory is distributed with rendehR through the packaged concept configuration and source-specific SQL definitions and is also provided in Supplementary Table 4.

Atomic mappings and curated concepts are available within the package, with curated concepts provided under the corresponding source-specific SQL directories. Table 2 summarizes how the three frameworks' concept vocabularies overlap; Supplementary Table 4 gives the concept-by-concept crosswalk. Of 170 distinct harmonized concepts across the three, only 51 (30%) are defined by all three. Ricu supports derived concepts such as the Horowitz (PaO₂/FiO₂) and AVPU (Alert, Verbal, Pain, Unresponsive) [37], which are not currently implemented in rendehR. Conversely, ricu in its current version does not provide concepts of ventilator settings such as positive end-expiratory pressure, plateau pressure, tidal volume and set respiratory rate and that is the reason no respiratory concept is shared by all three frameworks.

BlendedICU is the narrowest, restricting itself to variables it can harmonize across five databases, and it is the only one to keep invasive and non-invasive blood pressure as separate concepts.

rendehR is the broadest, and its surplus is concentrated in three places: analyte concepts absent from the other two such as anion gap, amylase, a specimen-aware design in which cerebrospinal, pleural, ascitic, urinary and stool analytes are modelled as concepts distinct from their blood counterparts where available. An injury-severity layer (e.g. injury severity score ISS, injury mechanism and intent) is also provided by rendehR as specific concepts related to SCID cohort.

The comparison also identifies three concepts that ricu and BlendedICU both expose but rendehR currently derives only through database-specific SQL rather than as harmonized concepts: urine output, total Glasgow Coma Scale, and ventilation windows. Vasopressin exposure is absent from current version of rendehR and will be provided in the next version.

3.4 Validation

We validated rendehR in two ways: an internally written harness of 175 distinct checks that reuses no package code, and an external comparison against ricu v0.6.3. The harness was run first on demo datasets (1,345 checks) and then on the SCID cohort in the full datasets (302 checks), covering 46,513 admissions and 32,260 patients (2,343 eICU, 4,320 MIMIC-III, 39,850 MIMIC-IV) and 62.7 million harmonized time-series and duration rows. Raw counts were read from PhysioNet CSV files as untyped text, and concept selection, numeric parsing, unit conversion, ICD normalization, and cohort derivation were re-implemented from their specifications.

On full data, imported row counts matched the independent counts for all 88 tables, including chartevents at 34,891,373 rows in MIMIC-III and 82,669,177 in MIMIC-IV, with no rows rejected and no values lost in type conversion. Concept extraction reproduced the independent selection exactly for 136/198 concepts in MIMIC-III, 151/209 in MIMIC-IV, and 181/187 in eICU; each of the 126 shortfalls was confirmed row by row to be the documented chart-copy de-duplication.

Sampled events were recomputed from raw offsets and raw value strings: 58,885 timestamps and 59,285 values, all in agreement. Age, sex, and in-hospital mortality matched a from-scratch recomputation across 113,536 admissions with 100% agreement in each source, and cohort membership matched the stored flag for all 46,513 admissions. The cohort artifact was also reproducible: identical whether built in one statement or in chunks, identical through the Parquet cache, and identical after an interrupted build was resumed; at full scale, two independently chunked builds of the complete SCID cohort produced 974 tables with zero differences. Table 3 provides the summary of the ten validation layers.

The external comparison against ricu v0.6.3 used 80 matched concept pairs, covering demographics, static admission variables, laboratory and bedside time series, and vasoactive and insulin exposures, and compared both pipelines on exactly the same admissions. Cohort derivation agreed with the same cohort observations as in Table 1. Age, sex, in-hospital mortality, and hospital and ICU lengths of stay agreed for 100% of admissions. All mapped time-series concepts (64 in MIMIC-III, 65 in MIMIC-IV, 64 in eICU) reproduced ricu row for row on admission, minute, and value (193 comparisons, zero failures). First-value clock alignment, defined as the median per source of the proportion of admissions whose earliest concept measurement falls within ±1 minute, was 99.7-100%; representative values are MIMIC-IV heart rate 100.0% of 7,619 admissions, MIMIC-IV MAP 100%, MIMIC-III lactate 99.85%, and eICU creatinine 99.96%.

Value distributions matched closely: across the 166 concepts compared directly, the mean Kolmogorov-Smirnov distance between rendehR and ricu was 0.0010 (range 0-0.038; 0.0010 in MIMIC-III, 0.0013 in MIMIC-IV and 0.0005 in eICU-CRD), 163 concepts had a distance of 0.01 or less, and none exceeded the 0.05 criterion. The largest distances were for SpO2 in MIMIC-III (0.038; median 98% in both packages), direct bilirubin in MIMIC-IV (0.023; median 1.0 vs 1.1 mg/dL) and magnesium in eICU-CRD (0.014; median 2.0 mg/dL in both). The 20 concepts with a registered definitional difference had a mean distance of 0.054 (range 0.002-0.145; largest for MIMIC-III troponin T, where rendehR also reads charted values), reflecting the different definitions (Supplementary Tables 6 and 7). Weight and height, where rendehR pools admit, daily and echo readings and ricu reads admit-weight items only, still agreed for 84.6-100% of admissions, and exposures matched wherever both packages read the same item sets.

Every remaining difference is registered with its measured magnitude and an item-level explanation. The registry contains 46 entries, including eICU exposures, where rendehR counts medication orders alongside infusions while ricu reads infusions only; ricu's exclusion of venous pO₂ below its plausibility limit; and the MIMIC-IV phenylephrine exposure count (1,581 vs 1,294 admissions), traced to the premixed phenylephrine formulations (items 229630, 229632) that rendehR reads and ricu does not.

3.5 Downstream analysis

The current rendehR pipeline was applied to construct the SCID cohort. Supplementary Figure 1 provides an example of the harmonized physiological measurements, medication exposures, and flat variables obtained from one patient in this cohort, a 29-year-old female with traumatic SCI who was admitted through the emergency department in the MIMIC-IV demo dataset using Shiny app.

We also tested the harmonized output in a prediction task. We constructed an admission-level dataset from the MIMIC-III and MIMIC-IV SCID cohort to predict in-hospital mortality. The setup follows our previous work [38]. Age and sex were obtained from the flat tables. Twenty routine blood biomarker measurements recorded during the first 24 hours of admission were summarized using prespecified minimum, maximum, or mean values and joined to the flat table.

We fit a logistic regression model to predict in-hospital mortality using the R stats package [28].

Using an 70/30 training-test split, the model achieved a test-set area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.70 (95% CI, 0.64-0.76), consistent with the performance observed in our previous analysis using a similar prediction setting [38]. 4. Discussion

rendehR addresses a specific gap in existing multi-source EHR harmonization tools, which implement full database harmonization before any analysis. Many health researchers, however, are interested in the analysis of a defined cohort. Identifying the cohort first, rather than constructing a harmonized representation of all source data, substantially reduces the memory and computational power required. It also makes the workflow more scalable and makes adding sources more straightforward. In packages such as ricu or the BlendedICU pipeline, cohort construction can be implemented by the user, but only after going through harmonization of all data first. rendehR instead derives the study-specific dataset first and gives the user the option to keep intermediate outputs. This matters most for studies where the cohort is a small fraction of the source data, and where the researchers are interested in developing disease specific medical concepts.

In ricu, the user can switch the ID structure while keeping the timing of the time window of events as specified, but rendehR gives the user more flexibility on how to choose the time window of merging the hospital-level events to a particular ICU when the granularity of the data changes from hospital level to ICU level. rendehR also gives researchers the ability to define the specific concepts that are most relevant to one field. The etiology of SCI table provided in the package, for example, is of value in studying an SCI cohort, but may be of less value to a researcher interested in coronary disease, who therefore does not have to build harmonized concepts related to it.

At the current version, rendehR has predefined cohorts of spinal cord injury and sepsis based on

Sepsis-3 criteria. These cohorts could potentially be developed further to a range of conditions such as brain injuries or cardiovascular conditions, which would serve more researchers. Once the cohort is identified, the package filters all available data for each source and provides raw cohort files in Parquet form which are useful on its own, independent of the downstream concept layer.

After a subset of the data is selected, medical concepts are created for a much smaller subset of the data relevant to the researcher. Like ricu, rendehR separates concept definitions from their execution, but executes them on SQL and persists those definitions in the concept tables. Both rendehR and ricu can extend their concepts. When the user is interested in the final output, rendehR, like ricu, can provide that, but the modification such as adding or modifying a concept, changing the refence timeline of measurements, and adding a new data source are relatively easier in rendehR's pipeline with keeping the original sources and auditable SQLs, in comparison to call back functions defined in ricu. Such modifications in BlendedICU are done by changing the source code and pipeline directly.

MCP and agent ready

rendehR can be operated by AI agents as well. Its MCP server is written in R, needs only jsonlite to start, and uses the installed rendehR for its database tools; it runs locally as a standard Adding a new source is possible, but it still requires tasks such as source-specific identifier mappings, cohort logic, and concept definitions. Additionally, the duration concept library is currently dominated by medication exposures from the BlendedICU pipeline, where harmonization happens at the level of active ingredient and exposure timing rather than route, dose, formulation, or unit. While this is important information, further work is needed to harmonize units and doses of such medications.

When a cohort is identified based on ICDs, the cohort selection step uses few relatively small tables to identify qualified patients. However, for conditions such as sepsis, the sepsis criteria itself is a complex concept built from multiple atomic concepts. These concepts are relatively smaller than the whole concept set, but they still have to be harmonized in advance. Although the current structure of DuckDB and the use of SQL facilitate this process, it is still an extra step and involving larger tables makes it slower.

Future work targets three directions. Backend support will expand, including full DuckDB execution for users who prefer a lighter-weight workflow. New source databases and packaged reference cohorts will be added through the existing registration and cohort-definition framework.

OMOP support and an event-log structure are also planned, which will let users adapt rendehR for research on event-log and process-mining analysis.

5. Conclusion

rendehR builds disease-specific datasets from heterogeneous EHR sources by keeping cohort definition and clinical concepts as explicit, executable parts of the pipeline. A study population, the clinical variables derived from it, and the final harmonized dataset stay traceable to one another. The current implementation supports MIMIC-III, MIMIC-IV, and eICU-CRD, with both diagnosis-based and SQL-defined cohort selection, but rendehR is built for scalability, and users can easily expand to new sources. Researchers using rendehR will spend less time repeating source-specific processing, and the datasets they produce are easier to inspect, reuse, and extend.

DuckDB already handles raw-source querying, cohort selection, cohort persistence, and selected concept execution. The full DuckDB backend support is planned for version 2. Source support is limited to MIMIC-III, MIMIC-IV, and eICU-CRD.
