Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. Abstract

Background. The retail food environment is a widely used exposure in behavioural-nutrition and obesity research. Grocery stores have declined across rural America while limited-assortment discount ("dollar") stores have proliferated, yet standard food-environment indices typically exclude dollar stores. We developed a dollar-aware index, assessed how counting dollar stores changes the measured exposure, and derived a trajectory typology.

Methods. Using annual establishment data from Data Axle for all 878 Mississippi census tracts (1997-2024), we classified food retailers with code-based rules and computed the modified Retail Food Environment Index (mRFEI) in standard and dollar-aware forms. We fitted latent class trajectory models suited to the bounded index, tested their stability in seven sensitivity analyses, and related class membership to the Social Vulnerability Index (SVI) and urbanicity with multinomial regression.

Results. Grocery stores fell from 1,616 to 716 while dollar stores rose from 315 to 1,005, crossing in 2018. The indices ranked tracts almost identically (within-year Spearman ≥0.99), but where dollar stores and healthy outlets coexisted, counting dollar stores lowered the index by 4.5 points in 1997 and 9.1 in 2024, and tracts with only dollar-store retail, which the standard index cannot score, rose from 1 to 32. Six trajectory classes emerged: stable adequate (7.8% of tracts), steady decline (15.3%), early collapse (12.9%), late collapse (9.7%), persistently constrained (34.8%) and chronically low (19.6%); only 7.5% of children in classified tracts lived in stable-adequate tracts. Classes were stable across random starts and pandemic-year exclusion; collapse was robust, but its early-late timing split depended on tracts with few retailers. Chronically low and persistentlyconstrained tracts were predominantly urban (relative risk ratios 8.3 and 4.5), consistent with food swamps; persistently-constrained and steady-decline membership rose modestly with vulnerability (1.17 and 1.20 per 0.1 SVI), an association that was weaker with 2010 SVI; collapse classes had no cross-sectional social signature.

Counting dollar stores changes the level of a widely used food-environment index, and whether a growing set of tracts can be scored, rather than how tracts rank. A 27-year typology separates urban food swamps from tracts whose food environments collapsed and provides a timevarying exposure for diet and childhood-obesity research.

1. Background

The retail food environment, the mix of outlets from which a community obtains food, is one of the most widely used place-based exposures in behavioural-nutrition research [1, 2]. Its appeal is mechanistic: the assortment, affordability and quality of nearby food are thought to shape diet quality, food security and, over time, weight and diet-related chronic disease [1, 2]. Evidence linking local food environments to obesity is mixed and comes largely from cross-sectional studies of modest quality, although indices that summarise the overall mix of outlets have been associated with obesity more consistently than counts of single outlet types [3]. Food deserts, characterised by limited access to healthy foods [4], and food swamps, where energy-dense, nutrient-poor outlets saturate an area, are two widely recognised food environments, with food-swamp conditions predicting obesity prevalence at least as strongly as food deserts [5]. Characterising either exposure therefore requires attention to both the availability of food outlets (how many there are and how near) and the composition of local food retail (the mix of healthy and less-healthy outlet types).

The food retail landscape in the United States has shifted substantially, particularly in rural and small-town communities [6, 7]. Over the past two decades, independent grocery stores have closed in large numbers [8, 9], while limited-assortment discount general-merchandise retailers, colloquially "dollar stores", have expanded rapidly [10, 6]. Dollar stores are small-format stores, dominated by a few national chains (Dollar General, Dollar Tree and Family Dollar), that sell low-priced, mostly shelf-stable foods alongside household goods [9]; they compete with other formats in ways that have been compared with the hard discounters found in other countries [11], but they carry a narrower food range with little fresh food [12, 13]. Their growth has been concentrated in low-income and rural communities and in communities of color [10, 6, 7], and dollar-store entry has been followed by the contraction or exit of independent grocers, particularly in rural areas [8, 9], although one dynamic model of entry found it to be followed by the exit of other small-format stores rather than supermarkets [11], and losses of food access after entry appear concentrated in urban neighbourhoods with a single grocery store [14]. Store audits describe shelves dominated by energy-dense, highly processed items with little or no fresh produce [12, 13, 15], even though many shoppers buy food there regularly [16, 6]. Evidence on diet is more mixed: foods bought at dollar stores are generally less healthy than those bought elsewhere, but the overall healthfulness of household food purchases was similar between households that did and did not shop there, consistent with compensating purchases at other outlets [17], and some dollar stores offer fresh produce of comparable quality at lower prices [18]. Dollar stores are also widely authorised to accept Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP) benefits [13], whereas authorisation for the Special Supplemental Nutrition Program for Women, Infants, and Children (WIC) requires vendors to stock minimum varieties and quantities of specified foods [19]; where dollar stores replace other retailers, WIC families may therefore lose places to redeem benefits faster than SNAP families.

How the retail food environment is measured determines the exposures used in diet and obesity research, yet the decisions about which outlets count as healthy are rarely examined, even though the classification of outlets in commercial databases is known to be imperfect [20, 21, 22]. The modified Retail Food Environment Index (mRFEI), one of the most widely used measures and an adaptation of an earlier ratio-based index [23], expresses the proportion of food retailers classified as "healthy" (supermarkets, larger grocery stores and supercentres) among all healthy and "less-healthy" outlets (convenience stores, fast-food restaurants and small grocery stores) [24]. Dollar stores are not among the outlet types the index counts [24], because they are neither full-service grocers nor conventional convenience stores or restaurants. They are also inconsistently represented in the commercial and administrative business databases used to construct food-environment exposures, where classification practices and industry codes have changed repeatedly over time [20, 21]. Whether a proliferating limited-assortment format is counted among less-healthy outlets is not a property of the data but a decision by the analyst, and as the format expands the decision may matter more. This raises a measurement question: does counting dollar stores change how tracts rank, their index values, or whether they can be measured at all, and how does its effect evolve as dollar stores proliferate? Neither version should be regarded as "ground truth". Classifying dollar stores as less healthy is a defensible simplification that reflects their typical inventory, but they are not equivalent to fast-food outlets, and some now stock fresh produce [18].

A second limitation of food-environment exposures is that they are typically measured crosssectionally [3, 2]: in a review of 432 food-environment studies, only 13.9% used a longitudinal design [25]. A single snapshot misses how food retail, diet and health evolve over years, and studies linking time-varying food-retail measures to diet have produced more mixed associations than the cross-sectional literature that shaped food-desert policy [26]. Communities also do not change uniformly: some lose food retail abruptly, others gradually, and others remain persistently underserved. Time-varying summaries of how small areas diverge over decades, suitable for life-course exposure modelling, remain uncommon, although latent class trajectory models offer one established way to construct them [27, 28]. Deriving such a summary is a further goal.

We focus on Mississippi because it is a priority setting for diet-related disease: more than 40% of adults had obesity in 2024, one of only two states at that level [29]; 25.0% of youth aged 6 to 17 years had obesity in 2022-2023, the highest of any state [30]; and nearly one in five residents lives below the poverty line [31]. In this setting we pursue four aims: (1) construct a longitudinal, dollar-aware food-environment exposure and quantify how the measured environment changes when dollar stores are counted; (2) characterise the 27-year trajectory of food-retail composition; (3) derive latent trajectories of tract-level food-retail adequacy as a time-varying exposure typology and test their robustness; and (4) examine how those trajectories relate to social vulnerability, urbanicity and spatial structure. Because food-environment measures are routinely used as exposures in studies of diet, food security and diet-related disease, including childhood obesity, understanding how a changing retail landscape alters those measures is a prerequisite for interpreting the studies that rely on them. Evidence linking dollar stores specifically to children's weight is limited: in neighbouring Arkansas, the presence of dollar stores near children's homes was not associated with higher body mass index [32]. Accurate, time-varying exposure measures are a prerequisite for testing such associations, and the measures developed here will serve as exposures in a planned longitudinal study of childhood obesity in Mississippi.

2. Methods

>Methods

2.1 Data source and study area

Establishment-level records were obtained from Data Axle (formerly Infogroup, whose database was previously known as InfoUSA), a commercial business database compiled from public records, directories and direct verification. Commercial establishment lists are widely used to locate and classify food outlets; against field observation they show moderate to high agreement, with InfoUSA among the more accurate sources, although small and independent outlets are more often missed [20, 21, 33, 22]. We obtained the complete annual Mississippi files for 1997-2024 (28 files). Each record includes the establishment name, address, coordinates, primary and secondary Standard Industrial Classification (SIC) and North American Industry Classification System (NAICS) codes, location employee size, a unique establishment identifier and, where applicable, a corporate-parent identifier. An establishment was counted as operating in a year if it appeared in that year's file. Coordinates were missing for a negligible number of records, which could not be assigned to a tract (for example, one grocery store in 2003). The study area comprised all 878 census tracts in Mississippi; census tracts are small statistical areas defined by the US Census Bureau, generally home to 1,200-8,000 people [34].

2.2 Retail classification and validation

Food retailers were assigned to five categories: grocery stores, supercentres, convenience stores (including gas stations with a store), dollar stores and fast-food outlets. Because industry codes classify dollar stores inconsistently across SIC and NAICS vintages (Additional file 1: Table S1), we identified dollar stores from three signals in each record: establishment name, corporate-parent identifier and NAICS code. Names were matched against the national and regional chains operating in Mississippi (Dollar General, including DG Market and pOpshelf; Family Dollar; Dollar Tree; Fred's discount stores, excluding Fred's pharmacies; Big Lots; Five Below; Ollie's Bargain Outlet; and Super 10) and a screened list of local dollar-store chains with more than two Mississippi locations. Big Lots and Ollie's Bargain Outlet are larger closeout formats but were included because both sell packaged foods: food and beverages accounted for 12-13% of Big Lots' net sales in fiscal 2020-2021 [35], and Ollie's stocks packaged foods including canned goods, cereal and snacks [36]; excluding both reduced dollar-store establishment-years by 2.3%; Dirt Cheap, a regional closeout chain, was excluded; corporate-parent identifiers, which are numeric and therefore unaffected by spelling variation, recovered chain stores whose names were recorded inconsistently (for example, Fred's stores in 2004-2012). Of the 18,582 dollar-store establishment-years, 76.3% matched on both name and parent identifier, 18.5% on name alone and 5.2% on parent identifier alone; 113 records (0.6%) matched more than one chain and were assigned to a single chain by a fixed labelling rule, which does not affect dollar-store counts. Name and parent identifier established chain membership, and the NAICS code served as a confirming signal: 98.2% of identified dollar stores carried a variety-or general-merchandise NAICS code. The NAICS signal was not used on its own, because the same codes also cover supercentres and department and apparel stores (14,284 further records), and because the contemporary dollar-store codes alone would have missed 77-98% of dollar stores before 2006 and about 3-6% by the early 2020s (Additional file 1: Table S1). Annual counts for Dollar General, the largest chain, were within 0.8-7.6% of the Mississippi store totals the company reported in its Form 10-K filings for 2017-2024 (mean absolute difference 4.6%), a slight, consistent undercount with no evidence of overcounting [37] (Additional file 1: Table S2).

Grocery stores, supercentres and convenience stores were classified by name and industry code, with explicit exclusion of non-food establishments carrying food-retail codes (florists, pharmacies, restaurants, used-car dealers, tobacco retailers, frozen-food wholesalers and hardware stores). Supercentres generated multiple records at a single location in later years; only the principal record per location was retained. The SIC-to-NAICS transition around 2008 introduced discontinuities in grocery and convenience counts that reflected re-coding rather than retail change; these were resolved using exact NAICS sub-codes. Fast-food outlets were limited-service restaurants identified by name and industry code, excluding outlets located inside other stores. The categories were mutually exclusive except for one establishment-year counted as both a grocery and a convenience store. The standard mRFEI counts grocery stores with three or fewer employees as less healthy [24]. We counted all grocery stores as healthy because the employee-size field shifted with vendor coding during the study period (the share of grocery records with three or fewer employees rose from 41% in 2012 to 54% in 2013-2015 and fell to 46% in 2016), which would introduce artificial change into a longitudinal measure; the size-based definition is reported as a sensitivity analysis.

Classification was rule-based and implemented in code rather than assigned by hand, so it is fully reproducible. Rather than inter-coder reliability, we therefore assessed the output for systematic misclassification. We identified four sources: electric-vehicle charging stations carrying gas-station codes from 2013 onward (652 records; 1.0% of convenience records), convenience-format stores carrying vendor grocery codes (1,024 records; 3.3% of grocery records), café listings within warehouse clubs classified as supercentres (14 records) and grocery-format Walmart Neighborhood Markets classified as supercentres (103 records; because both categories count as healthy, the index is unaffected) (Additional file 1: Table S3). Their effect on the index and on the trajectory classes was assessed in a sensitivity analysis.

2.3 The dollar-aware food-environment index

We computed the mRFEI for each census tract and year as the percentage of healthy retailers (grocery stores and supercentres) among all healthy and less-healthy retailers, where less-healthy retailers comprise convenience stores and fast-food outlets [24]. The dollar-aware mRFEI additionally counts dollar stores as less-healthy retailers, adding them to the denominator and leaving the numerator unchanged. Both indices range from 0 to 100, with higher values indicating a healthier outlet mix, and are undefined in tract-years with no retailer in their denominator. Counting dollar stores as less healthy is a simplification: assortments vary across stores and over time, and some dollar stores offer fresh produce [18]; we return to this assumption in the Discussion.

Because the two indices differ only in how dollar stores are classified, their difference isolates that single decision. A difference can arise only in tract-years containing both a dollar store and a healthy outlet: it is zero where a tract has no dollar store, and also where it has no healthy outlet, because both indices are then 0. We therefore report (i) the mean difference among all tract-years both indices can score, (ii) the mean difference among tract-years in which a difference is possible, and (iii) the number of tract-years whose only food retailers were dollar stores, which the standard index cannot score and the dollar-aware index scores as 0. We also report the within-year rank correlation (Spearman) between the two indices. Neither index is treated as ground truth (see Background).

2.4 Census tract harmonisation, social and demographic data

Census-tract boundaries changed after the 2000, 2010 and 2020 censuses, so we assigned every establishment in every year to 2020 census tracts (TIGER/Line 2022) [38] by point-in-polygon matching of its coordinates. Tract characteristics were taken from the CDC/ATSDR Social Vulnerability Index (SVI) 2022 (Mississippi, state-ranked), a composite percentile ranking on 16 social factors [39, 40], and from American Community Survey (ACS) 2018-2022 five-year estimates [31] of population, percentage Black, percentage in poverty, median household income and percentage younger than 18 years; the number of children in each tract was the population multiplied by the percentage younger than 18 years. As an earlier-period measure, we mapped SVI 2010 (Mississippi, stateranked, 2010 tracts) [41] to 2020 tracts using the Census Bureau's 2020-2010 tract relationship file [42], weighting by shared land area (872 of 878 tracts matched). A tract was classified as urban if its 2020 population-weighted centroid [43] fell within a 2020 Census urban area [38].

2.5 Statistical analysis

We described the statewide trajectory of each retail category and estimated compound annual growth rates by regressing the natural logarithm of statewide counts on year. Because the coverage and categorisation of commercial business data can change over time [44], we examined year-on-year change in the total number of Mississippi records in each annual file and in each retail category to distinguish retail change from changes in vendor coverage.

To derive a time-varying exposure typology, we fitted group-based trajectory models [45] to the tract-level dollar-aware index for 1997-2024 as latent class mixed models with cubic class-specific trajectories and no within-class random effects (lcmm package [46]); without random effects, classes describe shared mean shapes. Because the index is bounded at 0 and 100 and 30.4% of modelled tract-years were exactly 0, the primary model linked the index to the latent trajectory through a monotone spline transformation with five equidistant knots, which accommodates floor and ceiling effects [46]; a beta-link alternative did not converge. A linear (Gaussian) specification is reported as a sensitivity analysis (Additional file 1). Tract-years with no food retailer, for which the index is undefined, were treated as missing. Tracts with at least one food retailer in at least 15 of the 28 years were modelled (n = 817); the remaining 61 tracts, 17 of which never had a food retailer, could not support a trajectory and are described separately.

Models with one to eight classes were fitted from 24 random starts each and the best start was iterated to convergence. The number of classes was chosen by considering the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), the sample-size-adjusted BIC, relative entropy, the minimum average posterior probability of assignment (APPA; adequate ≥0.7) and occupancy odds ratio (OCC; adequate ≥5) [45], the size of the smallest class, the stability of classes across adjacent solutions and their substantive distinctness [27, 28]. The classes were named by the authors to describe the shape of their trajectories; the names are descriptive and do not by themselves imply a food desert or a food swamp, which we consider in the Discussion. Model fit was assessed by comparing model-predicted class trajectories with observed class means.

We assessed the stability of the selected solution by refitting it (i) from independent random starts; (ii) excluding tracts averaging fewer than three food retailers per year, the level below which a single opening or closure moves the index substantially (year-on-year changes of 50 points or more occurred in 11-12% of transitions from tract-years with one or two retailers but in under 2% from tract-years with three or more); (iii) treating tract-years with fewer than three retailers as missing and retaining tracts with at least 15 such years; (iv) excluding 2020 and 2021; (v) with the sizebased small-grocery definition; (vi) after correcting the misclassifications described above; and (vii) with the linear specification. Agreement with the main solution was summarised by the adjusted Rand index (ARI) [47] and by the percentage of tracts retaining their class after matching classes by majority overlap.

We related class membership to tract characteristics with multinomial logistic regression (reference class: stable adequate), fitting one model with the overall SVI and urbanicity and a second with the SVI components (percentage Black, percentage in poverty and median household income) and urbanicity, because the overall index is constructed partly from these components. Relative risk ratios (RRR) are expressed per 0.1 of SVI (10 percentile points), per 10 percentage points and per US$10,000. Because class membership is spatially clustered, we report 95% confidence intervals from a county cluster bootstrap [48] (1,000 resamples of Mississippi's 82 counties; percentile intervals) as the primary inference, alongside model-based intervals. The SVI and ACS measures describe 2018-2022, the end of the study period, and could partly reflect the food-environment change under study, so these associations are cross-sectional; we repeated the SVI model with SVI 2010 as a sensitivity analysis.

To characterise access, we computed the distance from each tract's 2020 population-weighted centroid to the nearest operating grocery store, and to the nearest grocery store or supercentre, in 2024. We assessed spatial clustering of class membership with join-count statistics [49] under first-order queen contiguity and of index values with the Getis-Ord Gi* statistic [50] (Additional file 1: Supplementary Methods S5).

Analyses were conducted in ArcGIS Pro and R (lcmm, sf, spdep, nnet and ggplot2 packages). This study is reported in accordance with the STROBE guidelines (Additional file 2) and the GRoLTS checklist for latent trajectory studies [51] (Additional file 3).

3. Results

>Results

3.1 Food-retail composition and the grocery-dollar crossover

Statewide, the number of operating grocery stores fell from 1,616 in 1997 to 716 in 2024 (-55.7%; -3.0% per year), while dollar stores rose from 315 to 1,005 (+219%; +4.1% per year); the two crossed in 2018, and the dollar-to-grocery ratio rose from 0.19 to 1.40 (Fig. 1a; Additional file 1: Table S4). Convenience stores, the most numerous category, changed little (-5.8%), fast-food outlets increased by 47.7%, and supercentres grew from 12 to 91. The total number of records in the annual files rose abruptly in 2001 (+17.3%) and in 2012-2013 (+9.0% and +8.2%) (Additional file 1: Table S5; Fig. S1a). In 2012 every retail category rose, including grocery stores (+13.4%), which otherwise rose in only three years and by at most 3.2%; 18.4% of that year's grocery records were new to the database, against a median of 8.0% (range 1.3-14.4%) in other years, and fewer than half of these (41.4%) were still listed as grocery stores in 2016. We therefore interpret the 2012 rise as an expansion of vendor coverage rather than retail change; because the mRFEI is a ratio, coverage changes that affect all categories proportionally largely cancel. The database also contracted between 2015 and 2020, but grocery stores continued to fall as a share of all records, so the grocery decline is not an artefact of coverage. In 2020, convenience stores (-6.8%), grocery stores (-6.6%) and dollar stores (-6.1%) all declined, which may reflect pandemic-related closures, disrupted vendor verification, or both; the trajectory classes were largely unchanged when 2020 and 2021 were excluded (Section 3.4).

3.2 How counting dollar stores changes the measured exposure

The standard and dollar-aware indices share a numerator and differ only in whether dollar stores enter the denominator. They ranked tracts almost identically within each year (Spearman correlation 0.991-0.996; fewer than 3.5% of tracts moved by more than 10 percentile points; Additional file 1: Table S6). The indices differed in level. Across all tract-years both could score, the mean difference rose from 1.1 points in 1997 to 4.1 points in 2024 (Fig. 1b), but this average includes the many tract-years in which a difference is impossible. Where dollar stores and healthy outlets coexisted, counting dollar stores lowered the index by 4.5 points in 1997 and 9.1 points in 2024 (medians 3.1 and 4.3; Fig. 1c). The share of scored tracts containing a dollar store rose from 27.4% to 69.9%, and among these the share with no healthy outlet, in which both indices are 0, rose from 9.2% to 35.7%. Where a difference was possible, it was largest in the least socially vulnerable quartile (16.4 points in 2024) and smallest in the most vulnerable quartile (5.5 points; Additional file 1: Fig. S1b,c). The indices also differed in which tracts could be measured: tracts whose only food retailers were dollar stores, scored 0 by the dollar-aware index but undefined by the standard index, rose from 1 in 1997 to 32 in 2024 (302 tract-years in 72 tracts; Fig. 1d). Of the 72 tracts whose only food retailers were dollar stores in at least one year, 32 were in the least vulnerable SVI quartile and 8 in the most.

3.3 A six-class trajectory typology

Of the 878 tracts, 817 had at least one food retailer in at least 15 of the 28 years and were modelled. In these tracts the median number of food retailers per tract-year was 5 (interquartile range 3-10), 23.0% of tract-years with any retailer had one or two, and 3.7% of tract-years had none and were treated as missing; 636 tracts had a food retailer in all 28 years. The 61 tracts not modelled had a median of 5 years with any food retailer and at most 7 retailers in any year; 17 never had one.

BIC declined with every additional class up to eight in both the spline-link and linear models, with diminishing improvements (0.62%, 0.42%, 0.38% and 0.31% for five to eight classes in the spline-link model; Additional file 1: Table S7; Fig. S2a). We selected six classes because six was the smallest solution that separated early from late collapse (the five-class solution merged them). S4). The seven-class solution split a small class from the persistently-constrained class (44 tracts, with 11 from the chronically low class) while retaining 93-98% of each other class, and the eight-class solution added a further mixed class of low-scoring tracts (Additional file 1: Table S7). The six-class solution had relative entropy 0.964, class-specific APPA of 0.953-0.983 and OCC of at least 80.7, and its smallest class held 7.8% of tracts (Additional file 1: Table S8). The spline-link model fitted far better than the linear specification (BIC 175,077 versus 189,145), and its predicted trajectories tracked observed class means closely (mean absolute difference 0.9-3.4 index points; Fig. 2); the largest single-year deviation was for the early-collapse class in 2002 (observed 44.3, predicted 29.3).

A steady decline class (125 tracts, 15.3%) eroded gradually (47.9 to 29.1). A persistently constrained class (284 tracts, 34.8%), the largest, remained low with slow erosion (23.7 to 14.8), and a chronically low class (160 tracts, 19.6%) stayed near the floor throughout (9.4 to 3.3). 3.4 Robustness of the typology

An independent refit from new random starts reproduced the six classes exactly (ARI 1.00), and the classification changed little when 2020 and 2021 were excluded (ARI 0.84; 91.8% of tracts retained their class) or when the misclassifications identified in the classification review were corrected (ARI 0.88; 94.5%) (Table 2). Correcting those misclassifications changed 5.7% of tract-year index values, lowering them by 0.75 points on average, consistent with the uncorrected index slightly overstating healthy access. Agreement with the linear specification was moderate (ARI 0.50; 74.4%; Additional file 1: Figs. S3-S4): the six trajectory shapes matched one-to-one, and most disagreements involved tracts that the linear model placed in the chronically low or persistently-constrained classes, or the timing of collapse. Excluding tracts with few retailers reduced agreement (ARI 0.53 when tracts averaging fewer than three retailers were excluded; 0.43 when tract-years with fewer than three retailers were set to missing). In both analyses the stable-adequate, steady-decline, persistentlyconstrained and chronically low classes largely persisted (71-100% of tracts retained) and a collapse class remained, but early and late collapse were no longer separated: one merged into the other (Additional file 1: Table S9). The existence of collapse is therefore robust, whereas its division by timing depends on tracts with few retailers, in which a single closure moves the index substantially. Counting grocery stores with three or fewer employees as less healthy lowered the index by 12.8 points on average and changed the classification substantially (ARI 0.20; 53.5%).

Of the 651,864 children living in the 817 classified tracts, 49,109 (7.5%) lived in stable-adequate tracts; the remainder lived in tracts whose index declined, collapsed or remained low throughout (Table 1). Including the 39,212 children in the 61 unclassified tracts lowers the stable-adequate share to 7.1%. We report these figures as a description of the exposure distribution, not a risk estimate.

3.5 Social patterning of the typology

Class membership was patterned more consistently by urbanicity than by social vulnerability (Fig. 3b,c; Table 3). Relative to the stable-adequate class, chronically low and persistently-constrained membership was strongly associated with urban location (RRR 8.33, 95% CI 4.34-26.17; and 4.49, 2.18-14.79). Persistently-constrained and steady-decline membership rose modestly with vulnerability (RRR per 0.1 SVI 1.17, 1.06-1.31; and 1.20, 1.08-1.33). The collapse classes showed no significant association with either SVI or urbanicity. In the components model, percentage Black, percentage in poverty and median household income were not independently associated with class membership, whereas the urban associations persisted (Additional file 1: Table S10). With 2010 SVI, which correlated 0.71 with 2022 SVI, the vulnerability associations for the persistently-constrained and steady-decline classes weakened (RRR 1.05, 0.94-1.16; and 1.10, 0.97-1.24) and chronically low membership was associated with lower vulnerability (RRR 0.87, 0.76-0.98), whereas the urban associations were unchanged (RRR 8.54 and 5.20). In the linear specification, the vulnerability associations were stronger and persisted with 2010 SVI (Additional file 1: Table S10).

4. Discussion

This study examined how the retail food environment of Mississippi changed over 27 years and how a single classification decision, whether dollar stores are counted, shapes the measured exposure. Three findings stand out. First, counting dollar stores barely changed how tracts ranked, but it lowered index values where dollar stores and healthy outlets coexisted, by a margin that doubled over the period, and determined whether a growing set of tracts could be scored at all. Second, a robust typology separated tracts with persistently low scores, mostly urban and close to grocery stores, from tracts whose food environments collapsed, mostly rural and far from them. Third, collapse tracts were indistinguishable from others on present-day social characteristics.

For studies that use the mRFEI as an exposure, the consequences of omitting dollar stores depend on how the index is used. Because within-year ranks were nearly unchanged, analyses that use quantiles or ranks of the index would be little affected. Analyses that use index values or fixed cut-offs would assign higher scores to tracts that contain both dollar stores and healthy outlets, by about 9 points by 2024 in such tracts. Analytic samples would also differ: a study using the standard index would drop tracts whose only retailers are dollar stores, which in Mississippi were concentrated in less vulnerable tracts, so the omission is not random with respect to characteristics that also relate to diet and weight. And because the size of the difference varied with social vulnerability, being largest in the least vulnerable tracts, the measurement difference is correlated with population characteristics rather than random. Such differences could bias associations with diet or obesity in either direction. We do not claim that either index is correct; the choice should be explicit and, where results depend on it, reported both ways.

The trajectory typology converts a long, complex panel into a small set of exposure histories suited to life-course analysis. Its most robust distinction is between tracts that were low throughout and tracts that lost an adequate food environment. The collapse classes showed no association with present-day vulnerability or urbanicity, so a cross-sectional measure would not distinguish a tract whose food environment collapsed from one that never had an adequate one, although their residents may have had very different exposure histories. The split between early and late collapse was less robust: it depended on tracts with few retailers, in which the loss of a single grocery store moves the index substantially, so we treat timing as a hypothesis for future research rather than an established feature. Moderate agreement between model specifications is common in latent class trajectory modelling [28], which is why we report the main classifications under both specifications and base our conclusions on the features that both share. Tract-level exposure histories also assume that residents stayed in place; residential mobility varies across households, so individual exposure histories will differ from those of the tracts in which people currently live.

The typology also distinguishes two forms of poor food environment. The chronically low and persistently-constrained classes were predominantly urban and close to grocery stores but scored low throughout: the configuration of a food swamp, in which less-healthy outlets dominate the retail mix rather than healthy outlets being absent [5]. The collapse classes were rural, far from grocery stores and not spatially clustered: the configuration of a food desert produced by dispersed local losses rather than a contiguous decline front. Measures based solely on distance, or on counts of a single outlet type, would conflate these environments [4, 3]. Social vulnerability was a weaker and less consistent correlate than urbanicity: persistently-constrained and steady-decline membership rose modestly with 2022 vulnerability, but the association weakened with 2010 SVI and, in the components model, no single component was independently associated. Because SVI was measured at the end of the period, these cross-sectional associations could partly reflect the food-environment changes themselves. National studies report that dollar stores are concentrated in low-income and rural communities and in communities of color [10, 6, 7]; in this panel, the trajectory of the overall food environment was more closely tied to urbanicity than to social composition, consistent with arguments that unequal food access reflects broader retail inequality rather than the presence or absence of particular stores [52].

These patterns have implications beyond exposure measurement. Most children in classified tracts lived in food environments that declined, collapsed or stayed low over the study period. From a food-justice perspective, the long-run loss of food retail in these communities can be understood as disinvestment rather than as the outcome of individual choices [52], a view echoed by residents who describe dollar stores as meeting some needs while investing little in the communities they serve [53]. International human-rights instruments define the right to adequate food to include physical and economic access to it [54], so persistent or worsening local food environments are a question of rights as well as of public health. Programme rules shape this landscape: dollar stores are widely authorised to redeem SNAP benefits [13], whereas WIC authorisation requires minimum stocks of specified foods [19], so where dollar stores replace grocery stores, WIC families may lose redemption options faster than SNAP families, and programme rules can shape what authorised stores carry: after the 2009 revision of the WIC food packages, WIC-authorised stores in Texas expanded shelf space for fruit, vegetables and whole grains [55]. Treating all dollar stores as less healthy is also a simplification whose accuracy may vary over time and place: some dollar stores offer fresh produce of comparable quality at lower prices [18], and households that shop at dollar stores may compensate with healthier purchases elsewhere [17]. An intermediate classification, or one informed by in-store produce availability where it can be measured, is a priority for future work.

Mississippi combines high poverty, a large rural population and rapid dollar-store expansion, so the magnitudes reported here should not be assumed to hold elsewhere. Some findings are likely to generalise: that excluding a fast-growing outlet type changes index levels and measurability more than rank, that changes in vendor coverage can create artificial jumps in commercial data, that ratio indices built from few outlets are volatile, and that urban and rural tracts follow different paths. Others are more context-dependent, including the size of the index difference, the specific classes and their prevalence, and their social patterning, which will depend on local retail structure, settlement patterns and data sources. In countries without a comparable dollar-store sector, the same measurement question applies to whichever limited-assortment or discount formats are expanding.

4.1 Implications for food-environment measurement

These results suggest six practices for longitudinal food-environment measurement. First, state how discount and limited-assortment outlets are classified and, where results depend on it, report the index both ways. Second, report how many units each index cannot score, and which units they are. Third, before interpreting year-to-year change, check total records and new-record rates for changes in vendor coverage [44]. Fourth, avoid defining categories with fields that shift with vendor coding, such as employee size, or test their influence. Fifth, identify units with few outlets, in which a single opening or closure moves a ratio index substantially, and test robustness to excluding them. Sixth, model bounded indices with methods suited to floor and ceiling effects [46], and report trajectory models against established guidelines [51].

4.2 Limitations

This study has limitations. The analysis is descriptive and associational; we do not estimate the causal effect of dollar-store entry on other retailers or on any health outcome, and the associations with social vulnerability are cross-sectional. The index measures the mix of outlet types rather than the foods stocked, purchased or eaten, and classifying all dollar stores as less healthy and all grocery stores as healthy are simplifications; the index was highly sensitive to the treatment of small grocery stores, which could not be defined reliably over time. Classification was rule-based: we validated dollar-store counts against company-reported totals and corrected the misclassifications we identified in a sensitivity analysis, but no independent manual double-coding of establishments was performed, and the correctable errors we found, chiefly convenience-format stores carrying grocery codes, would bias the index towards overstating healthy access. Commercial databases also miss some small and independent outlets and date openings and closures imperfectly [21, 33, 22]. The trajectory classes are latent-model constructs whose boundaries are probabilistic, and the timing distinction between the collapse classes depended on tracts with few retailers. Distance to grocery stores was measured as straight-line distance to stores within Mississippi, which understates travel where road networks are sparse and ignores stores across state lines.

Most importantly, we did not measure diet, dietary intake or body weight. This paper characterises the exposure rather than its health consequences; a deteriorating or persistently poor food-retail environment is a reason to study dietary and obesity outcomes, not evidence that such outcomes have occurred. The study was conducted in a single state, so the observed magnitudes are not nationally representative, and comparable long-run establishment data remain scarce for other states.

This paper provides the exposure measures for a planned longitudinal study of childhood obesity in Mississippi, in which tract-level, time-varying food-environment trajectories will be examined in relation to children's weight and related outcomes.

5. Conclusions

Between 1997 and 2024, the food-retail environment of Mississippi reorganised as grocery stores declined and dollar stores proliferated. Whether dollar stores are counted changed the level of a widely used food-environment index and, increasingly, whether a tract could be scored at all, but not how tracts ranked. A 27-year typology, robust to a range of sensitivity analyses, distinguished urban tracts with persistently low scores from rural tracts whose food environments collapsed, differences that cross-sectional measures cannot capture. These tools, and the measurement lessons that accompany them, provide a transferable, time-varying exposure framework for research on diet, food access and diet-related disease, including childhood obesity.
