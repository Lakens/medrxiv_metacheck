Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Full text

Abstract

Polygenic associations with neurodevelopmental traits may reflect both direct and indirect genetic effects. However, evidence separating these effects is limited and mixed, and rarely spans development or reporters, or triangulates across methods. We triangulated twincomparison and trio-based analyses in the Twins Early Development Study, using eight polygenic scores (PGS) and repeated parent, teacher and self-reports of attentiondeficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) and autism spectrum disorder (ASD) traits from ages 4 to 26. Analyses included up to 7,259 offspring with observed or imputed parental genetic data. Both approaches consistently supported direct genetic effects of ADHD and ASD PGS on corresponding traits, whereas evidence for within-trait indirect effects was limited. Crosstrait indirect effects emerged for several PGS, particularly in parent reports, and some were unexpectedly negative. These findings suggest that within-trait polygenic prediction of ADHD and ASD traits mainly reflects direct rather than indirect genetic effects, whereas cross-trait associations reflect some indirect genetic effects.

[div-01]

Neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs) are characterised by atypical brain development which causes impairments in cognitive, emotional, social, or motor functioning 1 . Common NDDs, such as attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) and autism spectrum disorder (ASD), are highly heritable and show strong intergenerational transmission 2, 3 . Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified thousands of common variants contributing to NDDs. When aggregated into polygenic scores (PGS), these variants predict NDD symptoms across development 2, 4.

However, an important consideration of PGS-based prediction is that associations between polygenic scores and outcomes may not solely reflect direct genetic effects (DGE) of inherited alleles 5 . Instead, they can also capture environmentally mediated effects of relatives' genotypes, known as indirect genetic effects (IGEs), as well as confounding by population stratification and assortative mating. Specifically, Kong and colleagues showed that parental genotypes, both transmitted and non-transmitted, can influence offspring indirectly via the rearing environment, or "genetic nurture" 6 . Distinguishing DGEs from IGEs is essential for determining the extent to which polygenic predictions reflect causal effects of inherited alleles, as well as understanding the mechanisms underlying the intergenerational transmission of neurodevelopmental risks.

Several complementary family genomic approaches have been developed to estimate direct and indirect genetic effects by exploiting the random segregation of alleles during meiosis. The sibling-comparison design uses genetic differences among genotyped siblings or dizygotic (DZ) twins to predict corresponding differences in phenotype. Because siblings inherit a random subset of parental alleles, this estimand is unconfounded by IGEs. The trio design uses samples comprising genotyped mother-father-child trios, regressing offspring phenotype on offspring genotype while conditioning on parental genotype. Because parental genotype is held constant, the resulting estimate reflects only the within-family segregation of alleles, meaning both the sibling-comparison and trio design have the same estimand.

It has been noted, however, that the sibling comparison approach is subject to potential bias and limitations. In particular, sibling-based PGS models do not necessarily uncover the estimates of direct genetic effects, even in the absence of indirect genetic effects 7 . Withinfamily estimates can be attenuated or inflated due to the correlation structure of environmental influences between siblings and the extent to which PGS captures a mixture of direct and indirect genetic components.

In comparison, the trio-based PGS model is less biased and more powerful, but relatively few cohorts have genotyped trios. Prior work has demonstrated that missing parental genotypes can partially be reconstructed using Mendelian imputation in parent-offspring and sibling pairs 8, 9 , thereby increasing the power of trio-based PGS analysis.

Despite these methodological advances, few studies have tested for IGEs on neurodevelopmental traits and the evidence is mixed. Sibling-comparison studies have identified IGEs on educational and cognitive outcomes, but few on psychiatric traits 10, 11 . Cross-trait IGEs on parent-reported adolescent ADHD symptoms have been identified using a self-rated general health PGS, although age-specific effects were not examined 10 . Another study examined externalising PGS associations across development and reporters, finding stable IGEs on conduct problems, but not on ADHD traits 12 . Evidence from siblingcomparison studies on other neurodevelopmental traits remains sparse.

Trio-based PGS analyses of parent-reported neurodevelopmental traits at age 3 identified cross-trait IGEs of maternal ASD PGS on hyperactivity and language difficulties, but no within-trait IGEs 13 . Similarly, associations between parental ADHD PGS and mother-reported ADHD traits at age 8 were largely explained by DGEs, although maternal neuroticism PGS showed a cross-trait IGE 14 . Another study found robust DGEs of child ADHD PGS on mother-reported hyperactivity and inattention, with little evidence for IGEs of parental ADHD PGS but modest cross-trait IGEs from other parental psychiatric PGS 15.

Taken together, evidence for IGEs on neurodevelopmental traits remains limited. However, most studies have focused on early childhood, a single cross-sectional assessment and/or a single PGS. Existing studies have also relied on a single analytical approach. No previous study has triangulated across designs or made comparisons across developmental stages, reporters, or neurodevelopmental traits. Finally, current evidence is largely restricted to ADHD-related outcomes, leaving it unclear whether similar patterns extend to other neurodevelopmental outcomes.

In the present study, we estimated DGEs and IGEs on ADHD and ASD traits across ages and reporters using both twin-comparison and trio-based PGS analyses in the Twins Early Development Study (TEDS). We incorporated Mendelian imputation of parental genotypes to maximise power in trio analyses and examined reporter-specific cross-developmental latent factors to determine whether indirect genetic effects differ across developmental stages and informants on stable trait indices. A summary and comparison between the two methods for calculating IGE is shown in Supplementary Table S1.

Specifically, we asked (1) whether child PGS predict ADHD and ASD traits in DZ twin pairs within and between families across development; (2) whether trio-based PGS analyses provide evidence for direct and indirect genetic effects on the same outcomes; and (3) whether these findings differ on reporter-specific and cross-reporter latent factors capturing stable variation over time.

Results

Twin-comparison Analyses

We examined ADHD and ASD traits across development and reporters (Supplementary Table S2). Full estimates of all models, including FDR-adjusted q values, are reported in Supplementary Tables S3-S8.

We first examined DGEs and IGEs using twin-comparison models that contrasted populationlevel and within-family PGS associations (Figures 1 and 2). Twin-comparison analyses provided consistent evidence for within-trait DGEs on both ADHD and ASD across development. For ADHD, within-trait DGEs were observed from ages 4 to 21 across parent-, teacher-and self-reported measures (within-family β = 0.08-0.18), although there was no evidence for a DGE on self-reported symptoms at age 26. Evidence for within-trait IGEs was limited, with only small negative effects observed for parent-reported ADHD symptoms at ages 4 and 7 (β = -0.05), neither surviving FDR correction.

A similar pattern was observed in within-trait analysis on ASD traits (Figure 2). Within-trait DGEs were observed across most measures from ages 8 to 16 (β = 0.05-0.13) but not for selfreported ASD traits at age 26. Evidence for within-trait IGEs was limited to self-reported ASD traits at age 16 (β = -0.07), which survived FDR correction.

Beyond within-trait associations, several cross-trait DGEs were also observed. Depression PGS predicted ADHD traits in childhood and early adulthood, whereas ASD, cognition, educational attainment (EA) and externalising PGS showed direct associations across multiple developmental stages. Cross-trait IGEs were limited to cognition and neuroticism PGS (parent-reported, childhood) and ASD PGS (self-reported, age 21), none surviving FDR correction. For ASD outcomes, cross-trait DGEs were identified for depression, cognition, ADHD, externalising and EA PGS at specific developmental stages. Cross-trait IGEs were evident for ADHD, externalising, cognition and EA PGS, primarily for parent-reported outcomes (strongest for EA PGS, β = -0.09 to -0.17); the cognition and EA effects largely survived FDR correction. Trio-PGS Analyses

We next examined DGEs and IGEs using trio-PGS models including child, maternal and paternal PGS (Figures 3 and 4). Consistent with the twin-comparison analyses, DGEs were considerably more consistent and stronger than IGEs, which were mainly observed in crosstrait associations. Within-trait associations provided evidence for consistent DGEs. Child ADHD PGS predicted ADHD traits across all developmental stages (β = 0.07-0.17), and child ASD PGS predicted ASD traits across most assessments (β = 0.06-0.11), with the exception of age 26. Evidence for within-trait IGEs was limited, and did not survive FDR correction.

Beyond within-trait associations, several cross-trait DGEs were identified. For ADHD outcomes, child depression PGS was predictive at ages 12, 21 and 26. Child ASD, cognitive ability, EA and externalising PGS showed direct associations across multiple developmental stages. For ASD outcomes, child ADHD, cognition and EA PGS also showed positive direct associations. In contrast, externalising PGS was positively associated with parent-report ASD trait at age 12, but negatively associated with parent-report outcome at age 16.

Cross-trait IGEs were more heterogeneous. For ADHD outcomes, maternal depression and neuroticism PGS and paternal ASD, cognitive, EA and externalising PGS showed indirect associations at specific developmental stages, none of which survived FDR correction. For ASD outcomes, IGEs were observed mainly for parent-reported traits: positive for maternal ADHD, depression and neuroticism PGS, and negative for maternal and paternal anxiety, cognitive and EA PGS. After FDR correction, IGEs on ASD outcomes were retained mainly for maternal ADHD, depression, anxiety, neuroticism, cognitive and EA PGS, and for paternal EA PGS (|β| = 0.05-0.11).

Reporter-specific and cross-developmental latent factors

To capture stable trait factors, we calculated three reporter-specific cross-development latent factors (parent-, teacher-and self-report) and cross-reporter, cross-development common factors for ADHD and ASD (Figures 5 and 6). Twin-comparison analyses again provided consistent evidence for within-trait DGEs on both ADHD and ASD latent factors (β = 0.07-0.16), with no evidence for IGEs. Cross-trait DGEs were observed for both ADHD and ASD latent factors across multiple psychiatric and cognition PGS. While no cross-trait IGEs were identified for ADHD latent factors, ASD factors showed some IGEs from ADHD, cognition, EA and externalising PGS, predominantly for the parent-reported and common factors; all except externalising survived FDR correction.

Trio-PGS analyses similarly demonstrated significant within-trait DGEs (β = 0.07-0.16). Evidence for within-trait IGEs remained limited, with only paternal ASD PGS predicting the common ASD factor, which did not survive FDR correction. Several cross-trait DGEs were observed. ADHD latent factors were associated with ASD, cognition, depression, EA and externalising PGS, whereas ASD latent factors were associated with ADHD and depression PGS. Cross-trait IGEs were again limited and different between mothers and fathers. For ADHD, indirect effects were observed for maternal depression and neuroticism PGS, together with paternal EA and externalising PGS; only the maternal effects survived FDR correction. For ASD, cross-trait IGEs were identified across maternal ADHD, depression, cognition, EA, externalising and neuroticism PGS, as well as paternal EA and cognition PGS, although these associations were largely restricted to the parent-reported and common factors. Maternal and paternal anxiety PGS again showed negative IGEs (maternal only after FDR correction).

Together, these findings largely replicated those from individual developmental outcomes across age and reporter, indicating that robust DGEs remained after reducing measurement error, whereas IGEs remained comparatively small and inconsistent. Notably, several DGEs and IGEs were evident only for the parent-reported and common latent factors, with little evidence for teacher-or self-reported factors. Discussion

Across eight waves of data spanning childhood to adulthood, using data from three reporters, and applying two complementary family-genomic designs, we found consistent evidence that polygenic associations with ADHD and ASD traits were driven predominantly by direct genetic effects. These effects were evident across childhood, adolescence and early adulthood, across parent, teacher and self-report, and in latent factors capturing stability across reporters and development. In contrast, within-trait indirect genetic effects were rare and inconsistent. Only one within-trait IGE survived FDR correction. Cross-trait DGEs and IGEs were observed across multiple neurodevelopmental, psychiatric and cognitive PGS.

Our findings provide systematic evidence that within-trait polygenic associations on neurodevelopmental traits are predominantly attributable to DGEs, with little consistent evidence for IGEs. These align with some previous studies on childhood neurodevelopmental traits 13, 14 , while extending this evidence across a substantially wider developmental period and multiple reporters. Our findings also add to a growing but mixed literature on IGEs across psychiatric traits. While IGEs have been relatively consistently observed for educational and cognitive outcomes, evidence for psychiatric traits has been more heterogeneous, with IGEs for some externalising and internalising phenotypes but not consistently replicated across studies 16, 17 . Rather than indicating a categorical distinction between neurodevelopmental and psychiatric conditions, our findings suggest that the relative contribution of DGEs and IGEs varies across traits. ADHD and ASD symptoms may be more closely tied to offspring-intrinsic genetic risk, whereas outcomes such as EA and internalising problems may additionally reflect more environmentally mediated pathways correlated with parental genotype.

In line with previous work 12, 15 , cross-trait findings further provided direct and indirect genetic evidence for the frequent comorbidity of neurodevelopmental, psychiatric, and cognitive conditions. Across both approaches, ADHD outcomes showed direct associations with ASD, depression, cognition, EA and externalising PGS, whereas ASD outcomes were associated with ADHD, cognition and EA PGS, suggesting that genetic liabilities related to psychiatric and cognitive traits also contribute directly to neurodevelopmental symptoms. Cross-trait IGEs of ADHD, cognition and EA PGS on ASD traits were also consistent across both approaches and robust to FDR correction, supporting the existence of environmentally mediated pathways beyond disorder-specific genetic transmission. Interestingly, these indirect effects were largely limited to parent-reported outcomes and the common latent factor, with little evidence in teacher-or self-reported measures. One possibility is that this pattern reflects reporter effects, whereby parental genotype influences parents' perceptions or reporting of their child's behaviour. Alternatively, these indirect effects may reflect environmental pathways that are specific to the home. Parental genotypes may shape aspects of family life, such as household routines, parent-child conflict or how homework and bedtimes are managed, and children's behavioural responses to these may be most evident at home rather than at school. Moreover, robust DGEs were observed across parent-, teacherand self-reported outcomes, suggesting that parent ratings captured meaningful child symptoms rather than simply reflecting parental reporting bias.

We also observed unexpected negative IGEs, whereby higher parental ASD and anxiety PGS were associated with lower offspring ASD traits. These associations were small and inconsistent, and several did not survive FDR correction; they should therefore be interpreted with caution. These patterns were not observed in previous studies examining IGEs of ASD liability on child social communication traits at age 3 13 , raising the possibility that negative IGEs may emerge later in development. Previous studies have reported negative covariance between offspring direct and parental indirect genetic effects for childhood externalising traits 18 and depression 19 . Although different from negative PGS-based indirect associations, these findings also suggest that direct and indirect genetic pathways may act in opposing directions. One possibility is that parental ASD or anxiety-related behavioural tendencies contribute to structured routines, earlier recognition of difficulties or other parental behaviours that support children, although these mechanisms were not directly tested. Selection offers an alternative explanation: van den Berg et al. found that negative correlations between direct genetic effects and nontransmitted coefficients for fluid intelligence in UK Biobank weakened when ascertainment bias was reduced 20 . Recent work in TEDS found that attrition had little impact on genetic and environmental variance components, providing some reassurance, although this does not exclude selection bias in the present PGS analyses 21 . Replication and direct assessment of potential mediating behaviours are therefore needed. Several limitations should be considered. Twin-comparison and trio-PGS approaches rely on different assumptions and capture partially distinct components of IGEs, particularly with respect to non-transmitted alleles. However, the consistency of main findings between approaches increases confidence in the overall conclusions. We used Mendelian imputation for many parental genotypes, which may have influenced the estimation despite previous validation of the imputation methodology. Overall, however, the convergence of results indicates that direct genetic effects dominate polygenic prediction of ADHD and ASD across development, whereas IGEs are rarer, smaller, and less consistent. Replication in independent cohorts with directly genotyped parents and increasingly predictive polygenic scores will be important for extending the robustness of these findings.

Methods

Sample

We analysed data from the Twins Early Development Study (TEDS), a longitudinal cohort of twins born in England and Wales between 1994 and 1996 22 . Standard TEDS exclusion criteria were applied, and twins with available genotype data were included. Recent work has shown that the genotyped TEDS sample is broadly representative of the full cohort, with minimal evidence of selection bias 21 . Twin-comparison analyses were restricted to dizygotic (DZ) twins (n=6614), whereas trio-based analyses included both monozygotic (MZ) and DZ twins (n=7259). Ethical approval for TEDS was obtained from King's College London (HR/DP-20/21-22060).

Phenotypic Measurements

ADHD and ASD related traits were assessed repeatedly from early childhood to young adulthood using age-appropriate, well-validated instruments (age 4, 7, 8, 9, 12, 16, 21, 26). ADHD traits were measured using the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ) 23 and the Conners' Rating Scales 24 . ASD traits were assessed using the Childhood Autism Spectrum Test (CAST) 25 , Autism Spectrum Quotient (AQ) 26 , and Ritvo Autism Asperger Diagnostic Scale (RAADS) 27 . Depending on developmental stage, ratings were obtained from parents, teachers and participants themselves. Supplementary Table S2 provides an overview of the questionnaires and informants across ages.

Covariates

We adjusted all models for child age, sex, genotyping chip and the first ten genetic principal components.

Polygenic scores

Polygenic scores were calculated using LDpred2-auto 28 for children and parents using available TEDS genotype data and imputed parental genotype data. Based on published genome-wide association studies, we included 8 PGS for ADHD 29 , ASD 30 , anxiety 31 , depression 32 , educational attainment 33 , cognition 34 , neuroticism 35 , and externalising problems 36.

Parental genotype imputation

Missing parental genotypes were imputed using SNIPAR v0.0.22, 9 which applies Mendelian inheritance within families to infer the expected genotypes of unobserved parents. Briefly, offspring genotypes contain alleles transmitted by their parents. When multiple siblings are genotyped, identity-by-descent (IBD) sharing between siblings provides additional information about the parental haplotypes from which these alleles were inherited. Similarly, when one parent and their offspring are genotyped, the alleles transmitted by the ungenotyped parent are observed in the offspring, while the remaining untransmitted alleles remain unknown and are inferred using population allele frequencies. In the case where sibling pairs are genotyped, but not their parents, SNIPAR imputes the sum of the maternal and paternal genotypes. In genotyped parent-offspring pairs, half of the ungenotyped parent is observed in the offspring, while the remaining alleles are imputed using population frequency.

Following standard genotype quality-control procedures (autosomal, biallelic SNPs with imputation INFO > 0.99 and Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium mid-P > 1 × 10), one member of each monozygotic twin pair was randomly excluded, in line with SNIPAR recommendations. The remaining sample was then phased using SHAPEIT5 v5.1.1. Sibling IBD sharing was then inferred from the phased haplotypes using SNIPAR's ibd.py function. Lastly, we used SNIPAR's impute.py function to carry out parental genotype imputation, resulting in 7,259 trios. Both parental genotypes were directly measured for 1,240 participants (17.1%), one parental genotype was measured and the other imputed for 2,044 (28.2%), and neither parental genotype was directly measured for 3,975 (54.8%). Polygenic scores for offspring and observed or imputed parents were subsequently calculated from LDpred2-auto weights using SNIPAR's pgs.py.

Statistical analyses

Twin-comparison models

Direct genetic effects were estimated using within-family regression models following previous studies 12, 37 . For each phenotype-PGS combination, we fitted (i) a population model including offspring PGS and (ii) a within-between model including the twin mean PGS (between-family effect) and the child-specific deviation from the mean (within-family effect). The within-family coefficient was interpreted as the DGE, and the IGE was calculated as the difference between the population and within-family estimates. Standard errors and percentile 95% confidence intervals for all estimates were obtained by non-parametric bootstrapping (5,000 resamples), and two-sided p values were derived from z = β/SE.

Trio-PGS models

We fitted trio-based regression models including child, maternal and paternal PGS. Child PGS effects were interpreted as estimates of DGEs conditional on parental genotype, whereas maternal and paternal PGS effects represented IGEs after adjustment for offspring genotype. Trio-PGS models included both members of MZ and DZ twin pairs; standard errors were clustered by family (HC1) to account for non-independence.

Common and reporter-specific latent factors

Frequently, researchers are interested in trait stability over development and/or across informants, as focussing on such stability can remove time-specific variation and error. To investigate IGE and DGE estimates on stability, confirmatory factor analyses were conducted.

For ADHD and ASD separately, we fitted single-factor CFAs to scale scores (i) across all ages and reporters (common factor) and (ii) across ages within each reporter (reporterspecific factors; loadings constrained equal for two-indicator factors), using robust maximum likelihood with FIML for missing data. 38 Factor scores were extracted using lavPredict and used as outcomes. Twin-comparison and trio-based analyses were repeated using these latent factor scores.

Multiple testing

We controlled the false discovery rate (FDR) using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure, separately for each estimate type within each set of analyses (Supplementary Tables S3-S8). We report nominally significant associations (p < .05) and indicate those surviving FDR correction (q < .05).

The TEDS data used in this study are available through an application to the TEDS team, subject to approval and applicable data access conditions.
