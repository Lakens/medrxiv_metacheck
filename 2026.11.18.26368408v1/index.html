Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. Abstract

Medical AI for complex signals such as electroencephalography (EEG) relies on costly expert annotations and predefined diagnostic labels that fail to capture the richness and nuance of clinical interpretation. Whether models trained on routine clinical reports can overcome these limitations while matching expert-supervised performance remains unclear. We introduce EEG Copilot, a biosignal-language foundation model trained on routine EEG-report pairs without dedicated task-specific expert annotations. Across two external datasets (6,078 EEGs), EEG Copilot performed on par with a state-of-the-art expert-supervised model across 11 shared findings (mean AUROC 0.80-0.81), corroborated by direct neurologist review of 200 EEGs. Beyond predefined diagnostic categories in conventional EEG AI, EEG Copilot assessed 23 clinical characteristics through natural-language questions in English and German across 9,689 EEGs from four hospitals (mean AUROC 0.80-0.82), demonstrating that automated EEG assessment need no longer be confined to fixed diagnostic outputs. Crucially, EEG Copilot generated integrated clinical impressions directly from EEG rather than isolated predictions with shuffled-report controls supporting recording-specific content and neurologist review of 800 impressions showing that most were accurate or clinically usable with minor errors.

These findings chart a path beyond label-bound medical AI, transforming routine clinical reports into scalable supervision for flexible, language-driven assessment and integrated interpretation of complex physiological signals.

Introduction

Much of medical artificial intelligence (AI) follows a common paradigm: medical experts annotate datasets for specific diagnostic tasks, and models are trained to predict the resulting labels. Creating these dedicated annotations is labor-intensive, expensive and difficult to scale. More fundamentally, in many clinical settings, predefined labels capture only part of clinical interpretation, whereas physicians must integrate multiple findings, assess their relationships and clinical significance, and communicate nuanced conclusions in medical language. This paradigm thus constrains not only how medical AI is trained, but also the complexity and flexibility of the interpretations it can provide. Electroencephalography (EEG) exemplifies these challenges. A cornerstone of epilepsy diagnosis, the assessment of cerebral dysfunction and neurological monitoring in critically ill patients, EEG interpretation is among the most demanding tasks in routine clinical diagnostics. Recordings comprise high-dimensional, temporally dynamic and artifact-prone signals, in which clinically decisive information may be subtle, transient and distributed across multiple channels and timescales. Accurate interpretation requires integrating waveform morphology, frequency, spatial distribution, temporal evolution and physiological context into a clinical clinical narrative. Yet this complex and labor-intensive process depends on specialized expertise that is unevenly available, with potentially serious consequences of misinterpretation for diagnosis and treatment. AI thus holds considerable promise for making specialized EEG expertise more widely accessible while improving the efficiency and consistency of clinical interpretation.

Automated EEG analysis has made substantial progress, with supervised learning approaches achieving high diagnostic performance across a range of predefined EEG findings 1, 2 . However, these advances have depended on extensive task-specific annotations by EEG experts, with each additional finding or finer diagnostic distinction potentially requiring further costly labeling efforts. More fundamentally, predefined output categories restrict what these systems can explicitly communicate, whereas clinical EEG reports capture gradations, relationships, uncertainty and contextdependent interpretations that extend beyond fixed diagnostic labels. Such rich narrative reports are generated routinely during clinical care yet remain largely untapped as a source of supervision for developing more scalable and flexible EEG AI.

Recent advances in self-supervised and multimodal foundation models suggest a fundamentally different approach to clinical EEG interpretation. Rather than training separate classifiers on manually curated labels, models could learn directly from the vast collections of EEG recordings and corresponding clinical reports generated during routine care. In this paradigm, medical language would serve not merely as an output format, but as a source of supervision through which models learn to represent complex physiological information, its relationships and clinical meaning. Such models could support a broad and extensible repertoire of clinical findings, enabling natural-language questions, contextual interpretation and narrative report generation without requiring dedicated annotations or classifiers for each task. Related approaches have been explored in computational pathology and EEG-language 3, 4 modeling. This raises a fundamental question: can heterogeneous and imperfect clinical reports provide sufficiently rich supervision for a new generation of medical AI-one that learns from routine care and interprets complex physiological signals beyond predefined diagnostic categories?

Here we introduce EEG Copilot, a biosignal-language foundation model that learns from routinely acquired EEG recordings and clinical reports without dedicated task-specific expert annotations. We demonstrate that EEG Copilot achieves diagnostic performance on par with a state-of-the-art expert-supervised EEG foundation model across shared clinical findings, while supporting a substantially broader range of EEG characteristics.

Beyond predefined classifications, the model enables natural-language questioning and generates integrated clinical impressions that describe and contextualize EEG findings.

Together, these findings demonstrate a fundamental shift in how medical AI can be developed and applied -combining scalable learning from routine clinical data with state-of-the-art diagnostic performance and flexible, language-based interpretation of complex physiological signals.

Results

Learning clinical EEG interpretation from routine recordings and reports

We developed EEG Copilot to establish whether learning directly from routine EEG recordings and clinical reports can achieve diagnostic performance comparable to expert-supervised AI, while enabling a broader repertoire of clinical findings and nuanced interpretation through natural language (Fig. 1). EEG Copilot processes multichannel EEG recordings of up to 2 h and supports natural-language questioning, follow-up dialogue and generation of clinical impressions in English and German (Fig. 1a). The architecture combines a self-supervised EEG encoder with a hierarchical EEGlanguage bridge and a large language model, linking physiological signals to clinical language within a unified framework (Fig. 1b). Model development used routinely acquired EEG recordings and accompanying clinical reports from Massachusetts General Hospital (MGH) and Brigham and Women's Hospital (BWH) 5.

We evaluated EEG Copilot in held-out cohorts from both development hospitals and two external Charité campuses in Berlin (Fig. 1c). The evaluation examined diagnostic discrimination across diverse clinical EEG findings, performance against a state-of-theart expert-supervised EEG foundation model, and the quality of generated clinical impressions. In addition to large-scale evaluations against report-derived references, direct neurologist review of 200 EEG recordings provided an independent reference based on the underlying signals.

To learn clinical EEG interpretation without dedicated task-specific annotations, we developed a three-stage training strategy (Fig. 2). First, we developed EEG2Vec, a selfsupervised encoder pretrained on 2.28 million 30-s EEG windows without clinical labels (Fig. 2a-c). Second, a hierarchical EEG-language bridge compressed representations across entire recordings into 64 tokens and learned their alignment with corresponding clinical reports (Fig. 2d). Finally, we adapted a large language model using report-derived impressions, clinical questions and multi-turn dialogues, enabling flexible EEGconditioned interpretation without dedicated task-specific expert annotations.

Self-supervised pretraining yielded representations containing substantial clinically relevant information. Across 3,380 held-out recordings, supervised probes trained on report-derived labels discriminated 24 EEG and ECG findings, achieving mean areas under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROCs) of 0.841 with recurrent readouts and 0.804 with linear readouts (Fig. 2e; Supplementary Fig. 1). Subsequent EEG-language training established cross-modal alignment (Fig. 2f), with matched EEG inputs yielding higher reference-response likelihoods than shuffled recordings (Fig. 2g), indicating that language predictions incorporated recording-specific information.

EEG Copilot achieves diagnostic performance on par with expertsupervised EEG AI

To determine whether learning from routine clinical reports can achieve diagnostic performance comparable to conventional expert-supervised approaches, we directly benchmarked EEG Copilot against MORGOTH, a state-of-the-art EEG foundation model developed using extensive expert-annotated datasets 2 . We evaluated both models on 11 shared clinical findings across 6,078 EEG recordings from two external Charité cohorts (CCM, n = 2,163; CBF, n = 3,915), using the same recordings and report-derived reference labels for each finding (Fig. 3a). Unlike MORGOTH, EEG Copilot had not received dedicated expert annotations for any of these diagnostic tasks.

Despite these fundamentally different supervision strategies, EEG Copilot achieved virtually identical aggregate diagnostic discrimination to MORGOTH, with mean AUROCs of 0.802 versus 0.796 at CCM (p = 0.543) and 0.813 versus 0.811 (p = 0.882) at CBF, respectively (two-sided paired recording-level bootstrap tests; Fig. 3a). Performance varied across individual findings, with neither model consistently outperforming the other. Thus, learning directly from routine EEG-report pairs yielded diagnostic performance on par with an extensively expert-supervised model across the evaluated shared tasks.

Because routine clinical reports are themselves imperfect references, we additionally evaluated both models against an independent assessment based on direct neurologist review of 200 EEG recordings. Against this EEG-based expert reference, mean balanced accuracy across the 11 shared findings was 0.744 for EEG Copilot and 0.746 for MORGOTH (p = 0.940; Fig. 3a). Cohen's κ provided a complementary perspective on agreement: mean κ was identical for both models at CCM (0.282; p = 0.979), lower for EEG Copilot at CBF (0.257 versus 0.297 for MORGOTH; p = 0.0002), but higher for EEG Copilot against the direct expert reference (0.383 versus 0.244; p = 0.0004). All p values were calculated using two-sided, null-centered paired whole recording bootstrap tests.

These results independently corroborate the comparable diagnostic performance observed across the larger external cohorts.

Direct neurologist review also enabled comparison of EEG Copilot with the original clinical reports (Fig. 3b). Across 21 clinical findings, mean balanced accuracy was similar for EEG Copilot and the original reports (0.740 versus 0.751; P = 0.667), although the original reports showed higher agreement with the expert reference as measured by Cohen's κ across 12 eligible findings (0.522 versus 0.335; P = 0.0002). Both comparisons used two-sided, null-centered paired whole-recording bootstrap tests. Together, these findings demonstrate that competitive diagnostic performance can be achieved without extensive task-specific expert annotation, establishing routine clinical documentation as a scalable source of supervision for medical AI.

Natural-language questioning extends diagnostic assessment across diverse EEG findings

Beyond achieving diagnostic performance on par with expert-supervised EEG AI on shared tasks, a central advantage of EEG Copilot is its ability to assess a substantially broader repertoire of clinical findings through natural-language questions in both English and German. Unlike conventional classification-based approaches, EEG Copilot does not require a predefined output head for each clinical finding, allowing diverse EEG characteristics to be assessed through a single language-conditioned model without additional task-specific training. We evaluated 23 EEG characteristics spanning overall interpretation, sleep, slowing and encephalopathy, periodic and rhythmic patterns, and epileptiform activity (Fig. 4), extending its evaluated diagnostic repertoire well beyond the 11 findings available for direct comparison with MORGOTH 2.

We evaluated this capability across 9,689 EEG recordings from four hospital cohorts in the United States and Germany, using English questions for the Boston cohorts and German questions for the Charité cohorts. Each clinical finding was assessed using the difference in likelihood between affirmative and negative answers to a corresponding natural-language question, with discrimination evaluated against findings extracted from routine clinical reports (Fig. 4a). EEG Copilot generates integrated clinical interpretations in natural language

Beyond identifying individual EEG findings, EEG Copilot can synthesize multiple observations into comprehensive clinical impressions expressed in natural language.

We evaluated this capability by assessing its ability to generate EEG reports describing background activity, sleep, pathological abnormalities and their clinical significance directly from the recordings (Fig. 5a). We assessed these generated impressions across four hospital cohorts using automated evaluations at scale and independent neurologist assessments of their clinical content and usability. Across 9,332 EEG recordings from the United States and Germany, a language-model judge evaluated generated impressions against the corresponding clinical reports for correctness, completeness and specificity, following a recently proposed strategy for automatic report judging 10 (Fig. 5b). Overall, impressions received full or partial credit for correctness in 67.6% of cases at MGH (95% CI, 65.7-69.5%), 62.5% at BWH (59.4-65.5%), 71.3% at CBF (69.9-72.7%) and 68.9% at CCM (67.0-70.8%). To determine whether this agreement reflected recording-specific information rather than generic clinical descriptions, we repeated the evaluation with randomly shuffled pairings of generated and reference impressions. Correctness ratings declined substantially to 33.0-39.7%, with similar differences in completeness and specificity across all four cohorts. These results demonstrate that EEG Copilot generates clinically differentiated impressions that show substantially greater agreement with their corresponding reports than with unrelated clinical documentation.

An EEG-specialized neurologist additionally evaluated 800 generated impressions, comprising 200 from each hospital cohort (Fig. 5c). Impressions were judged clinically correct or usable with minor errors in 69.0% of cases at MGH (95% CI, 62.5-75.5%), 67.0% at BWH (60.5-73.5%), 74.5% at CBF (68.0-80.5%) and 75.0% at CCM (69.0-81.0%). Statement-level assessment showed high correctness for overall interpretations (range across sites, 77.4-82.1%), sleep-related findings (77.8-93.8%) and epileptiform abnormalities (81.3-92.6%), whereas performance was lower for slowing and periodic or rhythmic patterns. These findings demonstrate the potential of EEG Copilot to generate clinically meaningful narrative interpretations across diverse clinical settings, while identifying areas for further improvement in reporting accuracy.

Together, these results extend the demonstrated capabilities of EEG Copilot from competitive diagnostic classification to the generation of integrated clinical interpretations, showing how a single model trained from routine clinical documentation can support a richer, language-based approach to complex physiological data. Discussion

In this study, we demonstrate that a single EEG-language foundation model can achieve diagnostic performance on par with state-of-the-art expert-supervised EEG AI while fundamentally expanding how complex physiological signals can be assessed and interpreted. Trained on routinely acquired EEG recordings and clinical reports without dedicated task-specific expert annotations, EEG Copilot enables assessment of a substantially broader repertoire of clinical EEG characteristics through natural-language questions in English and German, without requiring separate classifiers for each finding.

Beyond predefined diagnostic categories, the model generates integrated clinical impressions, bringing automated EEG interpretation closer to the way physicians describe, contextualize and communicate findings in clinical practice. Together, these advances establish a new paradigm for medical AI in which clinical language serves both as a scalable source of supervision and as a flexible interface for interacting with complex physiological data.

A central implication of this work is that clinical reports can provide a fundamentally different form of supervision for medical AI. Conventional EEG models, including SCORE-AI 1 and MORGOTH 2 , have achieved impressive diagnostic performance through extensive expert annotations for predefined tasks: SCORE-AI was developed on recordings annotated by 17 experts, and MORGOTH's training sets for rhythmic and periodic patterns, interictal discharges and sleep staging required 124, 24 and 7 experts, respectively 1, 2 . Although highly effective, this approach is difficult to scale across the full spectrum of clinical findings, as expanding diagnostic capabilities typically requires additional annotations and task-specific model development. Routine clinical reports offer an alternative that is both more scalable and richer in clinical information. Rather than reducing complex recordings to isolated diagnostic labels, they capture multiple findings, their spatial and temporal characteristics, relationships, uncertainty and clinical significance. Because such reports are generated as part of routine care, large collections of EEG-report pairs can be assembled without undertaking dedicated annotation campaigns for each diagnostic task. Related approaches have begun to explore report-based supervision in computational pathology and EEG-language modeling 3,4 . Our findings extend this principle by demonstrating that learning from routine clinical documentation can support competitive diagnostic performance together with broad, language-based clinical interpretation.

Importantly, this increased flexibility did not come at the expense of diagnostic performance on the evaluated shared tasks. EEG Copilot achieved virtually identical aggregate discrimination to MORGOTH across two external cohorts, despite the latter having been developed using extensive dedicated expert annotations. Comparable results against direct neurologist review of the underlying EEG recordings further corroborated this finding. This is particularly consequential because the annotation requirements of conventional approaches have been a major constraint on developing EEG models that encompass the diversity of clinical neurophysiology. Our results demonstrate that competitive diagnostic performance can be achieved using clinical reports as the primary source of task supervision, rather than relying on dedicated expert annotations for each finding. The implications extend beyond reducing annotation effort: they suggest that the information contained in routine clinical documentation can support models that learn more generalizable relationships between complex physiological signals and their clinical interpretation.

A second major advance is the breadth and flexibility of diagnostic assessment enabled by language conditioning. Whereas conventional classification-based systems are largely restricted to predefined output categories, EEG Copilot can be questioned directly about diverse EEG characteristics without requiring a separate prediction head for each finding. We demonstrated this capability across 23 clinical characteristics, substantially extending the repertoire assessed in the direct comparison with MORGOTH. Crucially, this breadth was achieved within a single model and across English-and German-language clinical settings, with consistent aggregate performance across four hospital cohorts. This changes the interaction between clinicians and automated EEG analysis: rather than being limited to the categories anticipated during model development, clinicians can formulate diagnostic questions in the language of clinical practice. Although additional questions require individual validation, the language-based architecture offers an extensible interface through which new clinical concepts can be assessed without developing a dedicated classifier for each one. Such flexibility is particularly relevant to EEG, where clinically meaningful distinctions frequently extend beyond a limited set of diagnostic categories.

Beyond answering questions about individual abnormalities, EEG Copilot demonstrated the ability to synthesize information into integrated clinical impressions. This represents an important step toward aligning automated EEG analysis with clinical practice, where interpretation involves not only identifying patterns but also describing their morphology, distribution, relationships and significance in a coherent narrative. Across four hospital cohorts, most generated impressions were judged correct or clinically usable with minor errors by a neurologist, while substantially greater agreement with matched than shuffled reference reports indicated that the generated text incorporated recordingspecific clinical information. These capabilities distinguish language-based interpretation from conventional systems that communicate their findings primarily through separate classification scores. They also create opportunities for a more interactive form of clinical decision support, in which physicians can request focused assessments, examine different aspects of a recording and use generated impressions as a starting point for clinical reporting. Further improvement in completeness and reliability will be essential, but the ability to express complex EEG information directly in medical language establishes a foundation for AI assistance that fits more naturally into clinical interpretation workflows.

The evaluation strategy is particularly important for interpreting these advances. In contrast to classification models with clearly defined reference labels, generative medical AI presents substantial challenges for objective validation. Clinical reports are imperfect references, reflecting differences in expertise, terminology, documentation practices and the findings considered relevant by individual readers. Indeed, direct expert review revealed meaningful discrepancies between original clinical reports and the underlying EEG findings, illustrating the limitations of treating routine documentation as an unquestioned ground truth. Similarly, agreement between a generated and reference impression does not necessarily establish that the model has interpreted the recording correctly, particularly because standardized clinical descriptions may receive favorable ratings even when unrelated to the underlying EEG. We therefore combined large-scale report-based evaluations with direct neurologist review of EEG recordings, comparison against an established expert-supervised model, assessment of generated impressions and shuffled-report controls. This complementary evaluation provides stronger evidence than any individual metric and highlights the importance of distinguishing linguistic agreement, diagnostic discrimination and clinically meaningful interpretation when assessing medical language models. Several findings also identify opportunities to improve this approach. Diagnostic discrimination was generally stronger for widespread or sustained abnormalities, such as generalized slowing, than for brief or spatially restricted findings, including epileptiform discharges and seizures. One possible explanation is the challenge of preserving temporally localized information when compressing long multichannel recordings into a compact representation, compounded by the limited temporal specificity of routine clinical reports. Moreover, generating an integrated impression requires not only recognizing findings but also selecting and communicating clinically relevant information; consequently, abnormalities that can be identified through targeted questions may still be omitted from narrative reports. These observations suggest several complementary directions for further development, including explicit localization of EEG evidence, tighter alignment between findings and their temporal context, and improved report generation through structured clinical feedback.

Incorporating higher-quality expert-reviewed reports and structured EEG documentation may further improve performance, particularly for uncommon or subtle abnormalities.

Ultimately, combining the scalability of routine report-based learning with targeted expert supervision may offer a particularly effective strategy for further improving clinical reliability.

Several limitations should be considered. The direct EEG-based expert evaluation was restricted to 200 recordings assessed by a single neurologist, while assessments of generated impressions relied on text-based comparisons rather than independent review of the corresponding EEG signals. Large-scale diagnostic evaluations used report-derived reference labels, which are vulnerable to omissions and variation in reporting practices, and the observed similarity between models does not establish formal statistical equivalence. Although performance was demonstrated in English-and German-language settings, language and hospital site were confounded, preventing isolation of language-specific effects. Model development was restricted to adult EEG recordings with a standardized channel configuration and without additional clinical context, and the accuracy of unrestricted responses to arbitrary questions and multiturn clinical interactions remains to be established. Most importantly, the study did not assess prospective use in clinical workflows or its effects on physician performance, diagnostic decisions or patient outcomes. Future studies should therefore investigate human-AI interaction, clinical safety and reliability, and the benefits of integrating EEGgrounded explanations and patient-specific context into physician-supervised interpretation.

Together, these findings demonstrate that the development of medical AI need not be constrained to predefined diagnostic labels and the expert annotation effort required to create them. By learning from the clinical interpretations already generated during routine care, EEG Copilot combines competitive diagnostic performance with broader, multilingual and narrative capabilities that more closely reflect how physicians interrogate and communicate complex physiological information. Beyond EEG, this approach offers a path toward medical AI systems in which clinical language provides both the foundation for scalable learning and the interface through which clinicians interact with their data.

Methods

Study design and datasets

We developed EEG Copilot using clinical EEG recordings and reports from Massachusetts General Hospital and Brigham and Women's Hospital 5 Patients were randomly assigned to training, validation, and test partitions in proportions of 80%, 10%, and 10%, respectively, using a fixed seed of 42. All recordings and windows associated with a patient identifier retained the same assignment. After preprocessing, the partitions contained 29,050 training, 3,630 validation, and 3,709 test recordings.

Self-supervised encoder training used only the Boston training partition, comprising 2,283,348 retained 30-s windows. Consequently, patients assigned to the internal validation and test partitions did not contribute recordings to encoder pretraining.

Subsequent EEG-language training retained the same patient assignments and applied additional requirements concerning recording duration and availability of matched text.

Bridge training included 28,321 recordings. The final instruction-training mixture included 26,565 recordings, of which 26,301 supplied standalone impressions and focused question-answer examples.

External evaluation used recordings from Charité Campus Mitte. Of 2,639 available EDF recordings, 2,591 retained valid windows after preprocessing; five recordings lacked sufficient canonical EEG channels and 43 yielded no valid windows. Following duration filtering and linkage to reports and extracted labels, 2,163 recordings entered the external evaluation. Charité recordings were not used in the inspected encoder, bridge, or language-model training stages. The external archive's storage partitions were pooled for evaluation rather than used as training and test partitions.

Signal preparation

EDF recordings were loaded using MNE python (v1.12.1) 11, 12 . Channel names were standardized to a canonical set of 19 electrodes: Fp1, Fp2, F7, F3, Fz, F4, F8, T3, C3, Cz, C4, T4, T5, P3, Pz, P4, T6, O1, and O2. Modern temporal-channel names T7, T8, P7, and P8 were mapped to T3, T4, T5, and T6, respectively. Recordings were required to contain all 19 canonical EEG channels.

Selected EEG and ECG channels were jointly resampled to 256 Hz when their original sampling frequency differed. EEG signals were referenced to the instantaneous average across the selected EEG electrodes; ECG and other auxiliary channels were excluded from the reference. Signals were converted to microvolts and stored without per-channel amplitude normalization. When separate left and right ECG channels were available, their difference was retained; otherwise, the first identified ECG/EKG channel was used.

Missing ECG was represented by zeros and an explicit availability mask. Signals were divided into non-overlapping 30-s windows containing 7,680 samples. Incomplete terminal windows were discarded. A window was rejected if any selected EEG value was nonfinite, if any EEG channel had a standard deviation below 0.01 µV after preprocessing, or if an available ECG contained nonfinite values. Recordings without any retained windows were excluded. No additional explicit notch or band-pass filtering, independent-component artifact removal, or amplitude-threshold rejection was applied.

For recording-level EEG-language modeling, duration eligibility was calculated as the interval from the first retained window's start to the last retained window's end.

Recordings exceeding two hours under this definition were excluded. For recording-level question answering and impression generation, the model received all retained 30second windows from each eligible recording, without temporal cropping. Within the encoder, each channel was zero-centered within each window; EEG signals were subsequently scaled by their joint root-mean-square amplitude, while ECG was scaled separately.

Self-supervised EEG encoder

EEG2Vec combined a temporal convolutional encoder with a six-layer transformer of hidden dimension 512 and eight attention heads. Convolutional weights were shared across EEG channels; ECG used a separate convolutional encoder. The temporal stride was 128 samples, yielding 60 tokens per channel for each 30-s window. Tokens received sinusoidal temporal embeddings and learned embeddings describing channel identity, montage view, lead type, and source and reference electrodes. A summary token and amplitude tokens were included in the transformer input.

Pretraining used paired referential and bipolar views derived from the same recording.

Batches alternated between a full montage containing 19 referential and 18 longitudinal bipolar leads and a reduced montage containing six referential and four bipolar leads.

An exponential-moving-average teacher, conceptually related to data2vec 13 received unmasked inputs, while the student received inputs with randomly masked channels and temporal token spans. The configured masking procedure hid two to four valid channels and two to three temporal spans of three tokens, aligned across channels.

Temporal reconstruction loss was evaluated at span centers; masked-channel reconstruction was evaluated across the affected channel tokens.

The objective combined latent-token reconstruction, relative channel-amplitude prediction, prediction of the pooled teacher representation from the student summary token, variance and covariance regularization, and cross-view consistency: ℒ encoder = ℒ token + 0.2ℒ amplitude + 0.1ℒ summary + 0.05ℒ var/cov + 0.05ℒ cross-view Cross-view consistency minimized the cosine distance between summary representations obtained from referential and bipolar EEG views. Teacher parameters were updated with momentum 0.999.

Probing frozen EEG representations

We assessed information recoverable from the encoder's 512-dimensional window summary vectors using independent classifiers for each clinical feature. The linear probe applied a single linear output layer to the mean of all retained window vectors. The recurrent probe used a bidirectional, single-layer long short-term memory network with 64 hidden units per direction, followed by concatenated mean and maximum pooling, dropout of 0.1, and a linear output layer. Recurrent state was reset across gaps exceeding 30 s.

Probes were trained with class-balanced binary cross-entropy using AdamW 14 , a learning rate of 10 -3 , weight decay of 10 -4 , and batches of 64 recordings. Training continued for at most 100 epochs, with early stopping after ten epochs without sufficient improvement in balanced validation loss. The checkpoint with the lowest balanced validation loss was restored before test evaluation. Three seeds-17, 29, and 43-were evaluated, and reported AUROCs were arithmetic means across seeds.

Unmentioned pathological findings and ECG abnormalities were treated as negative.

Unmentioned sleep and normality findings, and explicitly uncertain or unassessable answers, remained unknown and were excluded for the corresponding feature. Figure 2e presents 25 EEG/ECG endpoints, excluding clinical-history questions. Confidence intervals were obtained from 1,000 patient-cluster bootstrap resamples, using common patient draws across architectures and seeds within each feature.

Hierarchical EEG-language bridge

A local attention module compressed the frozen encoder's patch tokens into eight learned query tokens per window 7 . These were concatenated with a projected window summary token, producing nine 768-dimensional tokens per window. Learned windowposition embeddings encoded their order. A global query transformer initialized from BiomedBERT 15 used 64 learned queries and cross-attention after every second transformer layer to aggregate the recording into 64 output tokens. Bridge pretraining combined four objectives inspired by language-image pre training 16 : bidirectional reportlevel contrastive alignment, statement-level contrastive ranking, joint EEG-statement matching, and EEG-conditioned text generation. The report-level objective treated texts associated with the same recording as positive matches. Statement objectives ranked report-consistent descriptions above counterfactual alternatives. EEG-text similarity was calculated using the maximum cosine similarity across query tokens. The combined loss was ℒ bridge = ℒ report + 0.25ℒ statement + 0.5ℒ matching + 0.5ℒ generation.

Validation monitored these objectives, statement-selection accuracy, teacher-forced next-token accuracy, and bidirectional retrieval within candidate batches. Retrieval accuracy therefore reflected the evaluated batch composition rather than unrestricted retrieval across the full dataset.

Instruction data and language-model adaptation

Clinical reports were transformed into English-and German-language instruction examples comprising standalone impressions, focused question-answer pairs, and multi-turn dialogues. Qwen3.8-27B 17, 18 generated one impression and five focused question-answer pairs per language and report. Derived examples retained their source recording's dataset assignment. Clinical reports supplied training targets; they were not provided to EEG Copilot during standalone question answering or impression generation.

The bridge's 64 output tokens were projected into the 3,840-dimensional embedding space of Gemma 4 12B 19 using layer normalization and a two-layer projection with an intermediate dimension of 2,048, SiLU activation 20 , and dropout of 0.05. The projected tokens were inserted as a continuous EEG prefix. Training minimized next-token crossentropy over assistant responses, excluding user and system tokens from supervision.

The EEG encoder and base language-model weights remained frozen. Training progressively activated the projection, low-rank language-model adapters, querytransformer cross-attention, query-transformer backbone, and local compressor.

Adapter training began at update 1,500, cross-attention at 9,000, backbone adaptation at 15,000, and local-compressor adaptation at 21,000. Low-rank adapters 6 had rank and scaling factor 128, dropout of 0.1, and targeted the language model's query, key, value, and output projections.

The final continuation, from update 108,000 to 118,000, sampled impressions, focused questions, and multi-turn dialogues in proportions of 50%, 30%, and 20%, respectively.

Recordings were sampled uniformly within each source. Impression and focusedquestion losses received recording weights based on positive pathological findings: each finding's boost was its inverse square-root training prevalence, capped at four; recordings received the maximum applicable boost or a baseline of one. Weights were normalized to a mean of one within each source. Multi-turn losses and evaluation metrics remained unweighted. An auxiliary classifier was trained on detached bridge representations and did not update the generative model.

Final model training used four H200 GPUs, bfloat16 mixed precision, and an effective batch size of 64 examples. AdamW 14 learning rates were 2 × 10 -4 for the projection, 2 × 10 -5 for adapters, 10 -5 for cross-attention, and 5 × 10 -6 for the backbone and local compressor.

Impression generation and assessment

Impressions were generated from the EEG and a fixed request in each site's reporting language (English for MGH and BWH, German for CBF and CCM), using greedy decoding with up to 512 new tokens and without access to the report. References were the impression sections of the original reports, used without translation or rewriting. Qwen3.8-27B 17, 18 rated each pair, without the EEG or model identity, for correctness of the main interpretation, completeness of important reference findings and specificity of generated claims, each on a 0-2 scale using a fixed rubric. Pairs with inadequate references were excluded, leaving 2,378 MGH, 973 BWH, 3,830 CBF and 2,151 CCM pairs. As a chance baseline, the assessment was repeated after five within-site permutations of generated impressions, excluding same-recording and same-patient pairs. A board-certified neurologist specialized in EEG and epilepsy reviewed 200 randomly sampled impressions per site against the reference text, without inspecting the EEG. Individual statements were assigned to seven clinical categories and rated fully correct (2), partly correct (1) or wrong (0); omissions and unsupported additions (fabrications) were annotated separately. Finally, each impression was rated as correct, clinically usable with minor errors or clinically unusable. Statement-level denominators excluded omissions, omission counts were not converted into rates, and 95% confidence intervals were calculated with the Wilson method.

External expert evaluation

External expert evaluation comprised 200 randomly sampled EEG recordings acquired at Charité-Universitätsmedizin Berlin between 2023 and 2025, including examinations from general inpatient wards, intensive care units, and outpatient settings. Each recording was manually reviewed by a board-certified neurologist specialized in EEG and epilepsy. The reviewer assessed 25 SCORE-EEG-based items, recording each finding as present ("yes"), absent ("no"), or unassessable ("N/A"). These recording-based assessments served as the expert reference for evaluation of the original clinical reports.

The reviewer subsequently assessed each original report against the findings established through direct EEG review. Text segments were assigned to the corresponding SCORE-EEG-based items and classified as correct, containing a minor error, or wrong. Findings identified during EEG review but omitted from the original report were documented separately.

Each report also received an overall rating of "correct," "clinically usable with minor errors," or "wrong/clinically not usable." Reports were classified as clinically unusable if their overall interpretation as normal or abnormal disagreed with the expert assessment, or if they omitted or falsely asserted clinically important findings with implications for treatment, such as epileptiform activity or burst suppression. EEG Copilot answers and MORGOTH predictions were matched to the expert assessments by recording and corresponding clinical finding. The findings established through direct EEG review served as the reference for all comparisons; original clinical reports were evaluated as a separate comparator rather than treated as ground truth.

Comparisons with Morgoth were restricted to endpoints shared by the two systems and the expert assessment. Analyses were performed separately for each finding using recordings with an assessable expert reference and an evaluable corresponding output.

For binary findings, performance was summarized by balanced accuracy, calculated as the arithmetic mean of sensitivity and specificity. Agreement with the expert reference was additionally quantified using Cohen's   and Gwet's AC1 25 . Both coefficients were reported to characterize agreement under the different prevalence distributions of the evaluated findings. The numbers of reference-positive and reference-negative recordings were reported for each endpoint. Numeric estimates of posterior dominant rhythm frequency, background frequency, and posterior dominant rhythm amplitude were assessed separately using range overlap.

Summary agreement coefficients were calculated as unweighted means across eligible findings. To limit the contribution of sparsely represented endpoints, these summaries included only findings with at least ten reference-positive and ten reference-negative recordings. The comparison across all shared findings and the comparison restricted to endpoints available from MORGOTH used their respective eligible endpoint sets.

Uncertainty in the mean agreement coefficients was summarized using 95% bootstrap confidence intervals. Comparisons between systems were descriptive; differences in point estimates were not interpreted as evidence of statistically significant differences.

Data availability

The EEG recordings and associated clinical reports from Massachusetts General Hospital (MGH) and Brigham and Women's Hospital (BWH) are available through the Harvard Electroencephalography Database 5 , hosted by the Brain Data Science Platform (BDSP; https://bdsp.io/content/harvard-eeg-db/4.1/). Access is subject to credential verification and a data use agreement. The clinical EEG recordings and associated reports from Charité -Universitätsmedizin Berlin are not publicly available owing to patient privacy, data protection regulations and institutional restrictions on sharing patient-level clinical data.
