Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

The APA manual states: Report exact p values (e.g., p = .031) to two or three decimal places. However, report p values less than .001 as p < .001. However, 2 decimals is too imprecise for many use-cases (e.g., a p value meta-analysis), so report p values with three digits.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 2 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Text Sentence
P=0.175 As the changes in insulin were not correlated with Ra NEFA (rho=0.30, P=0.175), these associations may reflect distinct components of the postprandial response.
P=0.489 In post hoc analyses, liver fat content was also negatively correlated with fasting fractional palmitate esterification (rho=-0.39, P=0.043), but not with fasting net palmitate esterification (rho=-0.14, P=0.489).

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Abstract

After a meal, it is generally assumed that tissue non-esterified fatty acids (NEFA) uptake falls in proportion of the reduction of systemic NEFA appearance from insulin-mediated suppression of adipose tissue lipolysis. Here, we combined dynamic [ 11 C]palmitate PET/CT, stable isotope tracers, and MRI to measure palmitate uptake, oxidation, and esterification in the heart, liver, skeletal muscle, and subcutaneous and visceral adipose tissue, in the fasted state and 60min after a mixed meal in individuals with prediabetes. Net palmitate uptake decreased in all tissues, but most tissues extracted a larger fraction of circulating palmitate after the meal. The intracellular fate of palmitate differed between organs. The heart shifted toward oxidation, the liver toward esterification, maintaining VLDLpalmitate release, and this shift was smaller in individuals with more liver fat. Both adipose depots increased fractional esterification, and visceral fat almost stopped releasing palmitate. Thus, plasma NEFA overestimates the postprandial reduction in lean tissue NEFA exposure.

• After a meal, net palmitate uptake fall in all tissues but extraction rises in most • After a meal, the liver directs palmitate toward esterification and the heart toward oxidation • Plasma NEFA level underestimate lean tissue NEFA exposure after a meal in prediabetes

1 Introduction

Adipose tissue plays a critical role in regulating lipid metabolism and whole-body energy homeostasis. In the fasted state, adipose tissue lipolysis from adipose triglyceride lipase (ATGL) and hormone-sensitive lipase (HSL) maintains elevated circulating concentrations of non-esterified fatty acids (NEFA) 1 , which constitute an important energy substrate for lean tissues. Following the ingestion of a mixed meal, insulin suppresses adipose tissue lipolysis through inhibition of ATGL-and HSLmediated triglyceride hydrolysis 2 , thereby markedly decreasing the systemic appearance of NEFA.

Insulin further enhances fatty acid esterification in adipocytes by increasing glucose uptake to generate the glycerol precursor, glycerol-3-phosphate, required for triglyceride formation and stimulating diacylglycerol acyl-transferase activity [3] [4] [5] . Together, these insulin-driven changes reduce NEFA release while promoting their re-esterification and storage in adipose tissue during the postprandial period.

Although the postprandial fall in circulating NEFA concentrations is well established, less is known about the proportional handling of NEFA across different tissues and their intracellular fate following a meal. Organ-specific NEFA uptake is a function of both plasma concentration and the fractional extraction. If fractional extraction is assumed constant after a meal, tissue NEFA uptake would be expected to fall in proportion to plasma NEFA concentrations. At the whole-body level, however, plasma NEFA clearance is inversely related to the systemic rate of appearance of NEFA in healthy men 6.

Studies using arterial-venous concentration differences and stable isotope labelling have provided important insights into the regulation of NEFA uptake under insulin stimulation in the postprandial state in skeletal muscle and adipose tissue [7] [8] [9] . Whole-body measurements, arterial-venous approaches, and stable isotope tracer methodologies have provided important additional insights into systemic and tissue-level NEFA fluxes, including tissue uptake and storage [10] [11] [12] , but they have largely focused on subcutaneous adipose tissue alone 6, 8, 13, 14 . Furthermore, these methods cannot determine lean tissue NEFA oxidation and esterification rates. Thus, postprandial changes in lean tissue NEFA uptake and intracellular metabolic partitioning remain unclear.

The postprandial handling of NEFA is particularly relevant in prediabetes since lipid metabolism is already altered before overt type 2 diabetes develops. Indeed, in prediabetes, adipose tissue presents reduced catecholamine-stimulated lipolysis, blunted insulin suppression of lipolysis and increased trapping of dietary fatty acids, affecting both lipid release and storage [14] [15] [16] . When adipose tissue fails to buffer the postprandial flux of fatty acids, more NEFA are delivered to lean tissues, where they can accumulate as ectopic lipid and impair insulin action 10, 17 . Whether the fasting-to-postprandial transition induces coordinated or tissue-specific changes in NEFA uptake and downstream metabolism across lean tissues and adipose depots therefore remains unknown. Identifying which tissues take up . NEFA after a meal, their intracellular metabolic fate, could help explain how ectopic lipid accumulates as prediabetes progresses and identify tissue-specific targets for the prevention of type 2 diabetes.

We therefore aimed to compare fasting and early-postprandial NEFA metabolism in skeletal muscle, liver, heart, subcutaneous adipose tissue (SAT), and visceral adipose tissue (VAT) in individuals with prediabetes, by combining [ 11 C]palmitate positron emission tomography with computed tomography (PET/CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and stable isotope tracers. We also examined whether the fasting-to-postprandial changes in tissue-specific palmitate metabolism were associated with changes in circulating insulin levels, glucose and NEFA kinetics, anthropometric markers of adiposity, and organ-specific lipid content. We hypothesized that the early postprandial state, at the peak insulin and at the nadir of NEFA concentrations, would shift NEFA partitioning toward uptake and esterification in adipose tissue. Because adiposity and ectopic lipid deposition are associated with insulin resistance, we further hypothesized that organ-specific palmitate flux would be associated with the magnitude of the insulin response, NEFA and glucose kinetics, adiposity and ectopic fat content.

2.1 Participants and Ethical Approval

Participants were recruited from the local community through posters, social media posts, newspaper advertisements, and clinical records available through the Données Organisées du Réseau Informatique de Santé de l'Estrie (DORISE). 2.4 Image and data Analysis

PET/CT

Blood and tissue time-activity curves (TACs) were extracted from dynamic PET images using PMOD quantification software (version 3.7). PET data were corrected for dead time, radioactive decay, photon attenuation, scatter and random coincidences. Volumes of interest were delineated manually and/or semi-automatically on CT images using 3D Slicer 22 with the TotalSegmentator extension 23 . For manually delineated volumes, a minimum of six contiguous slices was included. For semi-automated segmentations performed with 3D Slicer, the largest possible organ volume was retained. Regions of interest included the left ventricle cavity, liver, myocardium (left ventricle), skeletal muscle (limited to psoas major and paraspinal muscles), visceral abdominal adipose tissue (VAT), and subcutaneous abdominal adipose tissue (SAT). The left ventricular cavity activity was used for the image-derived arterial input function. In addition, Hounsfield units (HU) measured in the left ventricular myocardium on CT images were used as a proxy of myocardial fat content 24 . For ventriculography, the Corridor4DM v5.2 clinical software (Segami; Invia) was used for reorientation and to compute left ventricular function.

Tissue-specific multicompartmental kinetic models of NEFA metabolism were applied to TACs from the heart 25 , liver 26 , skeletal muscle 27 , SAT 27 , and VAT 26 to estimate fractional NEFA uptake, oxidation, and esterification (Supplemental Figure 1). Visual inspection of VAT TACs revealed a rapid early washout phase suggestive of radiorapid tracer tissue clearance (Supplemental Figure 2), a pattern that was not observed in SAT TACs and was not adequately captured by the standard adipose tissue model 27 which does not include an oxidative or metabolic clearance compartment. The multicompartmental kinetic model developed for the liver 26 was therefore applied to VAT TACs, as it includes an oxidation/metabolic clearance compartment and provided a better representation of VAT tracer kinetics. Model performance was assessed by visual inspection of fitted TACs and residual plots.

The selected VAT model adequately fitted the measured TACs, including the rapid early washout phase, with residuals comparable to those obtained using the standard adipose tissue model (Supplemental Figure 1). In the liver, the model also allowed estimation of palmitate release into VLDL-

TG. In VAT, the slow-release compartment of tracer excretion was interpreted as palmitate secretion from VAT. Because [ 11 C]palmitate oxidation releases [ 11 C]CO 2 into the circulation, uncorrected plasma radioactivity may overestimate the [ 11 C]palmitate input function. Metabolite correction of the input function was not applied in the main analysis. As circulating [ 11 C]CO 2 was not measured in our participants, we assessed its potential influence by repeating the kinetic analysis in all tissues using . data reported by Christensen et al. 28 , applying the values obtained in healthy participants under basal conditions to the fasting scans and those obtained in healthy participants under hyperinsulinemic conditions to the postprandial scans. The direction and significance of changes in fractional and net fluxes were unchanged in all tissues. Results were thus presented without correction. Results on fractional constant rates are displayed in Supplemental Table 1. Palmitate uptake represents the sum of palmitate oxidation and esterification. Fractional rates (mL•mL of tissue -1 •min -1 ) were normalized by 100 g of tissue and thus expressed as mL•100g -1 •min -1 with ρ of 1.06 g•mL -1 for the liver, 1.05 g•mL -1 for myocardium, 1.04 g•mL -1 for skeletal muscle and 0.92 g•mL -1 for adipose tissue 29,30 . They were further multiplied by circulating palmitate concentration (assuming that palmitate kinetics are representative of those of the total NEFA pool) to derive tissue-specific net palmitate fluxes (µmol•100g -1 •min -1 ).

MRI

Tissue lipid content was assessed in the liver and skeletal muscle (limited to psoas major and paraspinal muscles). Volumes of interest were segmented on T1-weighted anatomical images using 3D Slicer 22 with the TotalSegmentator extension 23 and subsequently applied to the corresponding PDFF maps to extract mean tissue-specific PDFF values expressed as percentages.

For SAT and VAT compartments, fatty acid composition was further characterized by fitting a 10-peak spectral fat model voxel-wise to the complex multi-echo signal using non-linear least squares optimization (trust-region reflective algorithm). This approach simultaneously estimated PDFF, the number of double bonds (NDB), apparent transverse relaxation rate (R²*), and local frequency offset (ΔB₀). Phase convention calibration was performed automatically by selecting the variant minimizing the root mean square error over an adipose tissue reference region. Segmentation masks were morphologically eroded by one iteration to exclude partial volume voxels at tissue boundaries, and only voxels with a PDFF ≥ 20% were retained to minimize contamination from non-adipose tissue.

Mean NDB was extracted per compartment for each participant and was used as an index of adipose tissue lipid unsaturation.

Total plasma NEFA and triglycerides were measured using specific colorimetric assays (Wako Industrials diagnostics and Thermo DMA, respectively). Insulin was measured by Luminex xMAP-based multiplex immunoassays (Millipore, Billerica, MA). Plasma total glucose and meal [6,6-2 H 2 ]glucose enrichment was measured by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (XEVO TQ-S micro Acquity Class I UPLC) and [6-3 H]glucose activity was determined by liquid scintillation 31 to calculate endogenous, and exogenous glucose rate of appearance (Ra glucose ) and disappearance (Rd glucose ) 32 . 2.6 Calculations

Plasma rates of appearance of palmitate (Ra palmitate ), total NEFA (Ra NEFA ) and glycerol (Ra glycerol ) were calculated using the Steele non-steady-state equations, as previously described 15 . Rates of appearance of endogenous glucose (Ra endo glucose ) and exogenous glucose from the meal (Ra exo glucose ), as well as glucose rate of disappearance Rd glucose were calculated as previously described 32 . Postprandial changes in organ-specific NEFA fluxes, metabolites concentrations, and rates of appearance and disappearance were calculated as the difference between postprandial (60min after meal ingestion) and fasting values.

Hepatic insulin resistance was estimated as the product of fasting endogenous glucose production (Ra glucose ) and fasting insulin concentration, and adipose tissue insulin resistance was estimated as the product of fasting Ra NEFA and fasting insulin concentration. Body mass, FM and LM were divided by height squared to derive body mass index (BMI), fat mass index (FMI) and lean mass index (LMI), respectively.

4 Discussion

In this study, we combined dynamic [¹¹C]palmitate PET/CT with stable isotope tracers and MRI to characterize the fasting-to-early-postprandial changes in tissue-specific NEFA uptake and intracellular fate across lean organs and adipose tissues in individuals with prediabetes. As circulating NEFA fell after the meal, net NEFA uptake decreased in all tissues. It shifted toward oxidation in the heart and toward esterification in the liver and both adipose depots, but did not change in skeletal muscle. As a result, net oxidation was maintained in the heart and net VLDL-palmitate release was maintained in the liver, while NEFA release from VAT was almost completely suppressed. VAT also showed a large palmitate oxidative rate, which has not been described previously. Thus, the postprandial fall in plasma NEFA concentrations does not translate into a proportional reduction in NEFA delivery to tissues.

It has been shown previously that at the whole-body level, lower Ra NEFA is associated with greater NEFA clearance in healthy men 6 , suggesting an inverse relationship between NEFA availability and disposal. Accordingly, the marked suppression of circulating fatty acids after an oral glucose load can increase whole-body palmitate clearance by more than twofold 33 . Our tissue-level findings are consistent with this relationship. However, the change in systemic Ra NEFA was not associated with the changes in fractional uptake in any tissue after correction for multiple testing. In skeletal muscle, a smaller fall in Ra NEFA was nominally associated with a larger increase in fractional uptake, which is opposite to the expected inverse relationship. This suggests that the increase in tissue extraction cannot be fully explained by changes in NEFA availability. Similarly, although insulin is a major regulator of whole-body NEFA clearance through suppression of adipose tissue lipolysis 6 , the postprandial increase in insulin was not associated with the change in fractional NEFA uptake in any tissue and was associated with the intracellular fate of NEFA only in the heart, as discussed below. Whether the absence of association with uptake reflects reduced insulin sensitivity of these tissues in prediabetes or limited statistical power cannot be determined without a metabolically healthy comparison group.

In both adipose depots, net palmitate uptake decreased after the meal. In VAT, net palmitate esterification and oxidation also decreased, and palmitate release was almost completely suppressed.

However, fractional palmitate esterification increased in both depots. In VAT, fractional palmitate uptake did not change, and a larger share of the NEFA taken up was esterified. The increase in fractional palmitate esterification is consistent with the established action of insulin on white adipose tissue, which suppresses intracellular triglyceride lipolysis and promotes fatty acid uptake and esterification 4, 5, 14 . In healthy individuals, fractional re-esterification of fatty acids roughly doubles across the physiological range of insulin concentrations 5 , and direct in vivo measurements in SAT show . a rise from approximately 20% in the fasted state toward near-complete re-esterification during hyperinsulinemia 7 . Our measurements concern palmitate taken up from the circulation rather than palmitate released within the adipocyte from triglyceride lipolysis, but esterification of fatty acids from both sources are promoted by insulin. Yet, in our participants, the increase in fractional palmitate esterification in adipose tissues was not associated with the magnitude of the insulin response.

The reduction in VAT NEFA release reflects lower rate of recycling of newly taken-up fatty acids rather than reduced lipolysis of pre-existing triglyceride stores. [ 11 C]palmitate-PET tracks labelled fatty acids that entered the adipocytes, were esterified, and were then released. Thus, after the meal, VAT retained a larger share of the fatty acids it took up instead of releasing them back into the circulation.

Because VAT drains into the portal vein, this reduces NEFA delivery to the liver. Moreover, individuals with more intra-abdominal fat, but not more subcutaneous fat, show a smaller suppression of plasma NEFA by insulin 34.

Importantly, VAT showed a pattern of NEFA kinetics not previously described. Unlike SAT, VAT time-activity curves showed a rapid early washout of [ 11 C]palmitate (Supplemental Figure 2) that the standard adipose tissue model could not describe. The latter model was developed in SAT 27 , the depot most often studied. Since VAT can only be sampled during surgery, and because it drains into the portal vein, its fatty acid fluxes can only be estimated indirectly in humans. VAT kinetics required a model with an oxidation (or metabolic clearance) compartment, and oxidation accounted for a median of 82 % [Q1-Q3 79-87] of palmitate uptake by VAT in the fasted state, and 77 % [68-85] after the meal. To our knowledge, such a large oxidative component has not been described in human VAT. A higher oxidative component is consistent with the higher mitochondrial content and mass-specific respiration rate in VAT, compared to SAT 35 . VAT is also a highly dynamic adipose depot, rapidly changing in response to energy excess or deficits. Weight loss interventions, including pharmacotherapies and bariatric surgery, reduce VAT proportionally more than SAT 36 , and GLP-1 receptor agonists reduce VAT and SAT areas by similar absolute amounts despite the smaller volume of VAT 37 . However, how VAT handles NEFA in the short term, after an overnight fast or after a meal, was unknown. Our results show that VAT NEFA metabolism also changes rapidly after a meal.

In the heart, fractional palmitate uptake increased after the meal ingestion, together with a shift from esterification toward oxidation. This occurred while glucose and insulin rose and circulating NEFA fell, conditions that classically favor a shift away from fatty acid oxidation 38 . Cardiac output also rose after the meal, and an acute increase in cardiac workload can increase myocardial fatty acid uptake and oxidation 39 , fatty acids being the main source of ATP in the heart 40 . The postprandial rise in cardiac NEFA oxidation could also reflect a reduced capacity of the heart to switch between.

substrates in prediabetes, or the increase in cardiac workload discussed below. Without a metabolically healthy comparison group, however, reduced capacity to switch substrates cannot be established. Insulin also promotes the translocation of CD36 to the sarcolemma, although most evidence to date is limited to ex vivo experiments 39, 41 . In humans, myocardial fatty acid uptake measured by [ 11 C]palmitate is reduced in carriers of a CD36 null mutation, including after an OGTT 33.

A larger postprandial increase in insulin was associated with a smaller decrease in cardiac fractional palmitate esterification and a smaller increase in fractional palmitate oxidation after the meal. Thus, participants with a smaller rise in insulin and glucose relied more on fatty acid oxidation, which is compatible with competition between glucose and fatty acid oxidation. In addition, a smaller fall in systemic Ra NEFA was associated with a smaller decrease in fractional esterification. As the changes in insulin were not correlated with Ra NEFA (rho=0.30, P=0.175), these associations may reflect distinct components of the postprandial response. Whether a greater reliance on NEFA oxidation after a meal contributes to the development of diabetic cardiomyopathy or later cardiac dysfunction in individuals with prediabetes remains unknown and warrants further investigation.

In skeletal muscle, net palmitate uptake, esterification and oxidation decreased after the meal.

Fractional palmitate uptake increased modestly, without a significant change in fractional esterification or oxidation. Participants with a higher postprandial appearance of meal-derived glucose or a greater increase in glucose disposal had a lower fractional palmitate esterification rate after the meal relative to fasting. This is opposite to what substrate competition would predict, as greater glucose and insulin availability normally favors fatty acid esterification over oxidation. Whether this reflects altered NEFA handling by muscle in prediabetes cannot be determined without a metabolically healthy comparison group. Fractional esterification was also lower in participants with a larger waist circumference. These exploratory associations should be confirmed in larger studies.

In the liver, fractional palmitate uptake increased, driven by a higher fractional palmitate esterification, while fractional palmitate oxidation did not change. To our knowledge, postprandial hepatic NEFA fluxes have not been measured with this approach in humans. In pigs under an insulin clamp, the same [ 11 C]palmitate kinetic model showed no change in the fractional uptake rate, so that net uptake fell in proportion to the reduction in circulating fatty acids 26 . This suggests that systemic hyperinsulinemia alone does not explain the increase in hepatic extraction observed after the meal.

An acute increase in hepatocyte transport capacity is also unlikely, as FATP2 and FATP5 are not known to undergo insulin-dependent translocation 42, 43 , and hepatic uptake in response to OGTT is preserved in humans lacking CD36 33 . Unlike a peripheral insulin clamp, a meal delivers insulin and glucose to the liver through the portal vein. A meal also increases hepatic blood flow 44 , and both could contribute to the rise in fractional NEFA uptake.

.

In humans in the fasted state, hepatic fatty acid esterification is mainly determined by systemic and portal fatty acid appearance 26 . After the meal, although net palmitate esterification decreased with lower NEFA availability from circulation, fractional palmitate esterification increased. This suggests that substrate availability alone does not determine postprandial hepatic metabolic partitioning. Participants with more liver fat content showed a smaller postprandial increase in fractional palmitate esterification. In post hoc analyses, liver fat content was also negatively correlated with fasting fractional palmitate esterification (rho=-0.39, P=0.043), but not with fasting net palmitate esterification (rho=-0.14, P=0.489). Hepatic insulin resistance associated with steatosis could contribute to the smaller postprandial increase in hepatic fractional palmitate esterification.

Consistent with this, the change in fractional palmitate esterification was also negatively associated with the liver insulin resistance index (rho=-0.42, P=0.027).

Fractional VLDL-palmitate release doubled after the meal, consistent with the postprandial increase in fractional palmitate esterification in the liver, while net VLDL-palmitate release was unchanged. As fatty acid availability is a major determinant of VLDL-TG production 45 and insulin directly suppresses VLDL particle secretion even when fatty acid availability is maintained, the lower NEFA availability and higher insulin after the meal would be expected to reduce VLDL -TG release 45 . In healthy lean men, a test meal suppresses VLDL-TG secretion by about a half, whereas this suppression is absent in men with obesity and type 2 diabetes 46 . Similarly, insulin acutely suppresses VLDL particle secretion in individuals with less than 5.5% liver fat, but not in those with higher liver fat 34 . Most of our participants had a liver fat content above this threshold. Prediabetes and hepatic steatosis could therefore explain the absence of postprandial suppression of net VLDL-palmitate release in the present study.

Strength and limitations

This This study has also limitations. Measurements were made at a single time point, 60 min after the meal, so the time course of the responses over the full postprandial period remains unknown. All participants had prediabetes, and a metabolically healthy comparison group would be needed to establish which findings are specific to this population. The data come from a secondary analysis of a trial that was not designed for these outcomes, and the cohort included more women than men, so sex differences could not be examined as a primary analysis. [ 11 C]palmitate is a saturated fatty acid tracer, and the intracellular oxidation vs. esterification of oleate (a monounsaturated fatty acid, which make up most of the circulating NEFA pool) and linoleate and other polyunsaturated fatty acids may differ. Net fluxes also assume that palmitate kinetics are representative of the total NEFA pool. Because tissue perfusion was not measured, a contribution of substrate delivery to the intrinsic changes in transport and intracellular metabolism cannot be fully distinguished but unlikely impacts intracellular fate. Finally, the exploratory correlations involved many tests in a modest sample, and the associations that did not survive correction for multiple testing should be regarded as hypothesis-generating.

5 Conclusion

In individuals with prediabetes, fractional palmitate uptake increases in the heart, the liver, SAT, and skeletal muscle postprandially. Thus, exposure of lean tissues to circulating palmitate is higher than what could be predicted from the decline of systemic palmitate level and appearance rate after a meal. Furthermore, the intracellular fate of palmitate differed markedly between tissues. In the heart, palmitate shifted from esterification toward oxidation, so that net postprandial palmitate oxidation was maintained. In the liver, palmitate shifted toward esterification, and net VLDL-palmitate release was maintained. In both adipose depots, fractional palmitate esterification increased, and palmitate release from VAT was almost completely suppressed. Also, a large fraction of palmitate taken up by VAT appeared to be oxidized, in contrast to SAT. Postprandial insulin excursion partly predicted these responses in the heart, whereas glucose disposal and waist circumference partly predicted that in skeletal muscle, and liver fat content partly predicted that in the liver. The present study demonstrates the complex organ-specific responses of postprandial fatty acid metabolism contributing to ectopic fat deposition and lipotoxicity during the development of cardiometabolic diseases.

NEFA uptake and intracellular fate were measured simultaneously in five tissues of the same individuals and combined with wholebody glucose and NEFA kinetics and MRI-based measures of organ fat. Dynamic PET with compartmental modeling allowed net and fractional fluxes to be distinguished, which was essential to interpret the postprandial response. Net fluxes depend on circulating palmitate concentrations and decreased in all tissues after the meal. Fractional palmitate fluxes reflect the capacity of each tissue to extract and metabolize NEFA from plasma, independently of their concentration. Beyond their physiological interest, our findings have clinical implications. Because lean tissues increased their fractional palmitate uptake postprandially, plasma NEFA concentrations may underestimate lean tissue exposure to fatty acids in individuals with prediabetes after meals, a state in which humans.spend most of the day. This could contribute to ectopic lipid accumulation and the progression toward type 2 diabetes47,48 . Identifying tissue-specific responses at an early stage of the disease, such as the smaller hepatic response in individuals with more liver fat and the greater reliance of the heart on fatty acid oxidation in those with a weaker insulin response, may point to interventions aimed at preventing complications associated with type 2 diabetes such as MASLD and cardiac failure.
