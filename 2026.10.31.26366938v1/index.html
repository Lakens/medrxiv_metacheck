Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

P-Value Text
p ≤ 2.8 Across the 66 libraries, both correlated positively with endoscopic grade (Spearman correlation coefficient ρ = 0.66 and 0.65; p ≤ 2.8 × 10⁻⁹; Fig. 4a).
p < 0.05 At adjusted p < 0.05 and |log₂ fold-change| > 1, 522 genes are higher in CRC and 769 in IBD.

The APA manual states: Report exact p values (e.g., p = .031) to two or three decimal places. However, report p values less than .001 as p < .001. However, 2 decimals is too imprecise for many use-cases (e.g., a p value meta-analysis), so report p values with three digits.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 2 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Text Sentence
p ≤ 2.8 Across the 66 libraries, both correlated positively with endoscopic grade (Spearman correlation coefficient ρ = 0.66 and 0.65; p ≤ 2.8 × 10⁻⁹; Fig. 4a).
p = 0.49 CD in cohort 1 is the weakest cell: calprotectin did not track grade there (p = 0.49), although the stool score did (p = 0.044).

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Gastrointestinal diagnosis and monitoring rely on colonoscopy, which requires anesthesia and a long wait, and non-invasive alternatives are lacking. Here we show that whole-transcriptome sequencing of stool host RNA, recovering a median of 6,300 protein-coding genes across 285 libraries spanning colorectal cancer (CRC) and inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), precancerous lesions (APL), and normal samples, reads a census of the cells shed into the lumen. In normal samples, epithelium supplies 81% and immune cells supply 11% of stool host mRNA transcripts; the immune cells' share rises in CRC and IBD, and B-and T-cell receptor clonotypes rise with it. In this work, we show that CRC, IBD, and normal samples carry distinct transcriptome profiles, which a three-class model separates with an area under the curve of 0.94-0.96, and that within inflammatory bowel disease a 15-gene neutrophil score tracks endoscopic activity better than fecal calprotectin in the same stool.

Introduction

Gastrointestinal diagnosis and monitoring rely heavily on colonoscopy, which requires bowel preparation and anesthesia, and carries a small procedural risk. A patient presenting with nonred-flag gastrointestinal symptoms such as abdominal pain, diarrhea or bloating is worked up by exclusion-blood counts, C-reactive protein, stool culture, fecal calprotectin, celiac serologybefore colonoscopy settles whether the cause is neoplastic, inflammatory or functional 1, 2 . The diagnostic pathway can take weeks to months. Once inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is diagnosed, activity is monitored by repeat endoscopy or by fecal calprotectin, a single neutrophil protein that reports inflammation as one number and correlates only moderately with mucosal healing, particularly in Crohn's disease 3, 4 . These two clinical problems share one missing measurement: a molecular readout of the state of the gut tissue and its immune response, obtainable repeatedly from a sample the patient can collect at home. Stool contains that readout. Epithelial cells exfoliate continuously along the entire tract, and immune cells that traffic into the mucosa are shed with them, so the human mRNA in stool integrates active biology across the intestine rather than reporting a single biopsy site or the systemic average that blood provides 5, 6 . Recovering human RNA in stool is hard: most stool RNA is microbial, RNase activity is high, PCR inhibitors are abundant, and the matrix is hard to homogenize reproducibly 7, 8 . The field has taken two routes around the microbial background. The first is to sequence only chosen genes. Huang et al. used amplicon sequencing (Foli-seq) of 543 genes and characterized immune dynamics in the healthy and inflamed gut 9 . Ghannam et al. used hybridization capture of a custom 1,507-transcript panel from stool RNA to separate active from quiescent Crohn's disease 10 . These assays read the genes they were built to read: they cannot report a compartment that was not targeted, cannot assign transcripts to cell types across a whole reference atlas, and cannot be asked a new question without a new design. The second route is poly(A) capture. Using this approach, Halpern et al. recovered a median of 1,745 human genes per stool sample at ~20 million reads, and distinguished CRC patients from controls with a signal that mirrored the matched tumor tissue 11 . These studies show the diagnostic potential of host mRNA in stool, but the genes they detect are a small fraction of the roughly twenty thousand protein-coding genes in the human genome, and their cohorts were either one disease at varying activity or one disease against healthy controls. Whether the stool host transcriptome can tell two gastrointestinal diseases apart has not been explored. Likewise, previous stool-mRNA studies have separated active IBD from remission using a classification model, but none has attempted to track the dynamic changes of different types of immune cells and immune repertoire as the disease severity of IBD increases.

Here, we developed a stool host RNA-seq protocol that detects more protein-coding genes than previously reported methods. Using it, we generated 285 RNA-seq libraries from four clinical groups (normal, IBD, CRC, and APL) to address four questions. First, which cells do the mRNA transcripts in stool come from? Second, can adaptive immune signals, particularly T-cell and Bcell receptor (TCR and BCR) sequences, be detected in stool host RNA? Third, can stool mRNA distinguish between different GI diseases, specifically CRC and IBD? Fourth, can stool mRNA capture changes in immune cell populations across IBD severity, and so serve as a biomarker for disease monitoring? We find that stool host RNA is a census of shed cells, comprising epithelial cells from along the gastrointestinal tract as well as innate and adaptive immune cells, and that it also captures TCR and BCR repertoires. We further found that CRC and IBD leave distinct, separable transcriptional programs in this census, and that within IBD, neutrophil influx, the immune cell fraction, and the adaptive immune response all scale with disease severity and can be read from a single library.

Results

The QC metrics of stool host transcriptome sequencing

We developed an RNA-seq protocol that efficiently isolates stool mRNA and converts it into an Illumina sequencing library (Fig. 1a). In brief, oligo(dT) probes capture the human mRNA specifically and leave the abundant microbial nucleic acids behind. The protocol uses an optimized lysis buffer and conditions that release cellular RNA from human cells efficiently while protecting the mRNA from further degradation. The oligo(dT) probes capture both the relatively intact cellular RNA and the more degraded cell-free RNA, together with optimized mRNA binding and elution conditions and a workflow that can easily scale with stool input. Together, these improvements raised the quality of stool RNA-seq. Compared with existing methods for profiling the stool host transcriptome, ours detects far more protein-coding genes: the median across the 285 libraries was 6,300 (Fig. 1b), almost double the largest number previously reported, even under a stringent definition (genes with ≥ 10 reads, after removing reads with mapping quality (MAPQ) < 30). In RNA-seq libraries from low-biomass samples such as plasma, urine and stool, a high number of detected genes can also be an artifact of incompletely digested residual DNA. As a check, the median intergenic rate of the reads mapped to the human genome was only 0.7%, indicating very little DNA contamination. The mapping rates of these libraries were comparatively high, with a median of 49%, and library complexity was high for a low-input sample type, with a median of 1.7 million unique CDS-exon tags (Fig. 1b).

Stool host RNA is a census of the cells entering the lumen

With this unbiased profiling method, we prepared 285 stool host RNA-seq libraries from four clinical groups: Normal (n = 77), APL (n =63), CRC (n=55), and IBD (n = 90) (Methods, Supplementary Fig. 3). The CRC group has 7 stage I, 17 stage II, 16 stage III, 14 stage IV, and 1 unknown-stage sample. The IBD group has 42 ulcerative colitis (UC) and 46 Crohn's disease (CD) samples, and 2 of unrecorded subtype. We first asked which cell types the stool host mRNA originates from and in what proportions, because this determines which diseases, and at which sites, a stool mRNA test could surveil. We used cell-type deconvolution to estimate the share of transcripts derived from each cell type. A common pitfall of deconvolution is that cell types of the same lineage have very similar expression profiles, so their individual shares cannot be assigned accurately. To avoid this, we first grouped the cell types of the single-cell reference into lineage clusters and deconvolved at the cluster level (Methods; Supplementary Table 1). In people without gastrointestinal disease (the Normal group), on average 81% of stool mRNA transcripts derived from columnar gastrointestinal epithelium, 11% from immune cells and 5.6% from squamous epithelium, most likely shed from the anal canal (Fig. 1c). In the CRC (n = 55) and IBD (n = 76, removed 14 remission samples) groups the share of immune-cell transcripts rose markedly, to a mean of 41% in CRC and 45% in IBD (Fig. 1d).

Deconvolution reports lineages, not cell types. To ask which cell types are detectable in stool, we examined the expression of signature genes for the major cell types of the columnar gastrointestinal-epithelium cluster and of the immune cluster (Fig. 1e, f); how these genes were selected is described in Methods and Supplementary Fig. 6. In the reference single-cell RNAseq dataset, each of these genes is highly specific to its cell type among the major members of both clusters. A marker must also be nearly absent from the cell types that dominate stool (neutrophils, colonocytes and goblet cells), unless it marks one of them, or it could not indicate the presence of a rarer cell type; the reference panels of Fig. 1e, f show that each marker is indeed low in those abundant cell types. Transcripts from both the lower and the upper gastrointestinal tract were detectable, with colonocytes and goblet cells the dominant sources. Tuft-cell signature genes were barely detectable, as expected for a cell type that is rare in the healthy colon 12 , and the abundance of epithelial transcripts tended to fall with the distance of the organ of origin from the anus. An adaptive-immune repertoire comes with the census

We next asked whether adaptive immune cells become more abundant in gastrointestinal disease. The B-cell/plasma-cell transcript JCHAIN, which encodes the small polypeptide that polymerizes immunoglobulins 13 , was higher in CRC and IBD than in Normal stool, and so was the T-cell transcript CD3E which encodes a subunit of the T-cell receptor CD3 complex (Fig. 2b); the curated B-cell and T-cell signature scores show the same ordering (Supplementary Fig. 7). More interestingly, T-cell receptor (TCR) and B-cell receptor (BCR) sequences could be assembled from the stool RNA-seq reads themselves (Fig. 2a). Productive BCR clonotype counts correlated with JCHAIN expression (Spearman ρ = 0.68) and productive TCR clonotype counts with CD3E (ρ = 0.52) across the 208 (Normal: 77, CRC: 55, IBD: 76, the 14 IBD remission samples were removed) libraries of Fig. 2a, b (Supplementary Fig. 2). Of the 5,409 productive clonotypes recovered from the 285 libraries, 20 TCR and 606 BCR light-chain CDR3 sequences matched entries in public databases (VDJdb, IEDB and CoV-AbDab), with the hits concentrated on common human exposures-cytomegalovirus, SARS-CoV-2, influenza and Epstein-Barr virus (Fig. 2c). Such matches are evidence that an assembled sequence has been observed independently, not antigen-specificity calls: immunoglobulin light-chain CDR3s in particular are short and frequently shared, and the databases are heavily weighted toward SARS-CoV-2 because the database holds far more SARS-CoV-2 records than records for any other antigen. In addition, 7 of 88 T Cell Receptor Alpha chain (TRA) clonotypes carried mucosal-associated invariant T (MAIT)-like α chains (TRAJ33 or TRAJ20 junctions, five of them on the canonical TRAV1-2; Fig. 2d). MAIT cells recognize riboflavin (vitamin B2) metabolites synthesized by gut microbes 14 , so their presence supports the conclusion that stool host RNAseq recovers TCR sequences that genuinely belong to the gut immune system.

Stool host mRNA distinguishes gastrointestinal diseases

Can stool mRNA distinguish gastrointestinal diseases from one another, or does it only report how inflamed the gut is? To answer this we performed principal-component analysis on the 2,000 most variable genes, setting aside the IBD libraries in endoscopic remission (n = 14) because a healed mucosa is expected to resemble Normal stool. Plotting component scores by disease group, the dominant axis (PC1, 62% of variance) separated both CRC and IBD from Normal stool (single-component AUC 0.90 and 0.91). The component that best separated CRC from IBD was PC6 (AUC 0.86); no other component reached 0.7. Plotting PC1 against PC6 shows the separation of Normal, active IBD, and CRC libraries (Fig. 3a): this unsupervised decomposition already separates the libraries by diagnosis, which indicates that the signals distinguishing active IBD from CRC are strong. We then ran pathway enrichment on the 100 genes loading most positively and most negatively on each of PC1 and PC6 (Enrichr; GO Biological Process, KEGG, Reactome and MSigDB Hallmark, with the 2,000 genes of the decomposition as background; Fig. 3d). The positive end of PC1 was enriched for neutrophil degranulation and innate immunity, as expected given that neutrophil-derived mRNA dominates the immune signal in the stool host transcriptome. Coloring the PCA by a neutrophil score (the mean z-score of canonical neutrophil-specific genes, Methods) showed that this score separates Normal from IBD and CRC libraries along PC1 (Fig. 3b), with which it correlated almost perfectly (Spearman ρ = 0.99). The positive end of PC6, toward which IBD lies, was enriched for the interferon-γ response, and its negative end, toward CRC, for epithelialmesenchymal transition (EMT) and TNF-α/NF-κB signaling (Fig. 3d). EMT is a hallmark of cancer pathogenesis, and interferon and TNF-α signaling, although strongly up-regulated in IBD, are also involved in CRC. To characterize the host mRNA signals that separate IBD from CRC more fully, we performed differential expression analysis (using DESeq2) between the 76 active IBD and 55 CRC libraries and identified 522 genes higher in CRC and 769 higher in IBD (Fig. 3e). On the volcano plot we labeled the differentially expressed genes belonging to four canonical programs directly linked to IBD and CRC pathogenesis (WNT signaling, EMT/stroma, proliferation/E2F and the IFN-γ response). We also calculated the mean z-score of the genes in these four programs by clinical diagnosis groups (Fig. 3f). Consistent with the biology of the two diseases, the IFN-γ response program was higher in IBD than in CRC and the other three were higher in CRC than in IBD. Notably, CRC libraries also scored higher than Normal on the IFN-γ program, consistent with the inflammatory component of CRC pathogenesis.

Distinguishing IBD, CRC and Normal stool has direct clinical utility: for patients with mild gastrointestinal symptoms, a non-invasive stool test that flags likely IBD or CRC could triage them to colonoscopy and avoid weeks or months of waiting. We therefore built a three-class multinomial logistic-regression model on 208 libraries (CRC 55, IBD 76, Normal 77; Methods). Under five-fold cross-validation repeated ten times, each library was given its consensus out-offold call, the class it was assigned most often; the accuracy of these calls was 0.870 (95% bootstrap confidence interval 0.83-0.91), with one-versus-rest AUCs of 0.964 (0.93-0.99), 0.948 (0.91-0.98) and 0.942 (0.91-0.97) for CRC, IBD and Normal respectively (macro-average 0.951; Fig. 3g, h). The errors were mostly disease libraries called Normal-16% of CRC and 13% of IBD-and these were predominantly early-stage cancers and mild colitis: of the nine CRC libraries called Normal, seven were stage I or II and none was stage IV, and of the ten IBD libraries, seven were mild.

Advanced neoplasia can be called from the stool transcriptome alone

Stool-based screening for CRC and APL is among the most widely used non-invasive stool tests. We therefore asked whether a classifier built directly on the stool host transcriptome can detect CRC and APL. We trained a two-class logistic-regression model on the 20 genes with the largest Welch t-statistic within each training fold, using five-fold cross-validation and CRC (n = 55), APL (n = 63), and Normal (n = 77) samples (Methods). Immune signals in the stool host transcriptome track the severity of IBD

Better ways to monitor disease activity in IBD are an unmet clinical need. The standard is endoscopy, and the only non-invasive alternative in routine use is the fecal calprotectin immunoassay, whose accuracy is limited, especially in Crohn's disease 4 . We therefore asked whether stool host RNA can monitor IBD activity with better accuracy. We took the 66 IBD libraries with an endoscopic grade evaluated within 30 days of stool collection and a recorded subtype-33 ulcerative colitis (UC) and 33 Crohn's disease (CD); remission 12, mild 17, moderate 17, severe 20-together with 24 healthy samples as a reference band. The healthy samples are a subset of the Normal group that, in addition to a negative colonoscopy, showed no evidence of inflammation or other lesions (Methods). We first examined the two genes encoding the calprotectin complex, S100A8 and S100A9, since fecal calprotectin is well established to rise during IBD flares. Across the 66 libraries, both correlated positively with endoscopic grade (Spearman correlation coefficient ρ = 0.66 and 0.65; p ≤ 2.8 × 10⁻⁹; Fig. 4a). Calprotectin is used as an indicator of neutrophil abundance in the gut because of its high expression in neutrophils 18 , but one protein, or two genes, may not represent the neutrophil load accurately. We therefore defined a neutrophil panel of 15 genes that are highly and specifically expressed in neutrophils and took the mean z-score of their expression as a neutrophil score (Methods). The score correlated with grade more closely than S100A8 or S100A9 alone (Spearman correlation coefficient ρ = 0.75) and separated each grade from the next with statistical significance (Fig. 4a; two-sided Mann-Whitney U, p = 0.012 for remission versus mild, 0.013 for mild versus moderate and 0.0011 for moderate versus severe). Beyond neutrophils, the immune-cell share of transcripts estimated by deconvolution (Methods) also rose with severity, reflecting the growing inflammatory infiltrate (Fig. 4a). Host RNA also tracks the lymphocytes: productive BCR and TCR clonotype counts rose with severity (Fig. 4b), as did the B-and T-cell signature scores (Fig. 4c).

To demonstrate the potential clinical utility, we compared the stool-mRNA panel with the conventional method, fecal calprotectin. Because the number of IBD libraries in each endoscopic grade is small, a machine-learning model trained on this dataset would risk wide confidence intervals and over-fitting, so we took a biology-driven approach and used the neutrophil score directly as the stool-mRNA alternative to calprotectin. The IBD libraries were split into two cohorts by the recruiting hospital, whose calprotectin reagents and instruments differ (Methods). The stool-RNA neutrophil score tracked endoscopic grade more closely than calprotectin did on the identical samples, in both recruiting cohorts and in both subtypes (Fig. 4d; Supplementary Fig. 5): in UC, the Spearman correlation with endoscopic grade was ρ = 0.71 for the neutrophil score against 0.48 for calprotectin in cohort 1 (n = 12) and 0.68 against 0.39 in cohort 2 (n = 20); in CD, 0.53 against 0.19 in cohort 1 (n = 15) and 0.76 against 0.62 in cohort 2 (n = 16); and pooled across subtypes, 0.66 against 0.36 in cohort 1 (n = 27) and 0.75 against 0.11 in cohort 2 (n = 36; Fig. 4d). CD in cohort 1 is the weakest cell: calprotectin did not track grade there (p = 0.49), although the stool score did (p = 0.044). Discussion

Everything reported here is enabled by a stool host RNA-seq method that surveys the stool host transcriptome without targeting particular genes and that detects more human genes than any previously reported approach. The first finding is a systematic account of the cells of origin of host RNA in stool, something never done systematically or unbiasedly before. Normal stool is dominated by intestinal epithelium, whose colonocyte, goblet, enterocyte, Paneth and gastric markers are each individually recoverable and specific against a single-cell reference; the immune fraction is small and neutrophil-led; and lymphocyte transcripts sit near the detection floor.

Disease inverts the mixture toward immune cells, and the adaptive compartment becomes readable not only as transcripts but as assembled antigen receptors: these track their lineage genes, match receptors recorded in public databases and include the MAIT-like α chains expected at a mucosal surface. To our knowledge, this is the first recovery of TCR and BCR sequences from stool host RNA.

We demonstrated three potential clinical applications of stool host transcriptome sequencing. The first is differential diagnosis. CRC, IBD and Normal stool carry distinct transcriptional signatures, and the genes that separate cancer from colitis resolve into canonical disease programs: Wnt signaling, stroma and proliferation on the cancer side, and an interferon-γ response on the colitis side. A three-class model read on these features separated CRC, IBD and Normal stool with one-versus-rest AUCs of 0.94 to 0.96, which shows that stool host RNA carries the identity of the disease and not only the presence of inflammation. To our knowledge, this is the first study showing that the stool host transcriptome can discriminate between different gastrointestinal diseases.

The second application is screening for advanced neoplasia. A two-class model distinguished CRC from Normal at an AUC of 0.97; APL from Normal at 0.71, and it did so on hemoglobin, stromal and inflammatory transcripts rather than on the lesion's own epithelial program. The census reports what a lesion sheds, and an adenoma sheds much less than CRC.

The third application is monitoring IBD activity. A classifier on the 15-gene neutrophil score alone separated active disease from the quiescent gut at an AUC of 0.92, and the same libraries reported the infiltrate behind that score: the immune-cell share of transcripts, the neutrophil score and the B-cell and T-cell signature scores all rose with endoscopic grade, as did the number of productive BCR and TCR clonotypes. The stool neutrophil score also tracked endoscopic grade more closely than the conventional fecal calprotectin measured on the same stool, in both recruiting cohorts and in both subtypes. The correlation was weakest in Crohn's disease in cohort 1, as expected for a patchy transmural disease sampled from the lumen, and should be read as a boundary of the approach rather than a failure of the assay. Several limitations apply. The activity cohort is modest: 66 graded IBD libraries, 63 of them with a paired calprotectin. Its grade axis pools UC and CD, which were graded with different instruments-the Mayo endoscopic subscore and SES-CD-that score different lesions and were mapped onto a single four-level band. The subtypes also differ in extent, since UC is confined to the colon whereas CD usually involves the ileum and can involve any segment of the gut, so the endoscopic reference may not capture all the disease a stool sample integrates. We pooled them because the number of libraries within each subtype is small, and the two are unevenly distributed across grades (19 of 33 UC libraries severe, 1 of 33 CD). Detection of a marker is evidence that transcripts of that cell type are present, not a cell count, and the deconvolution reports shares of transcripts, which depend on the mRNA content of each lineage.

In this proof-of-principle study we have shown that stool host transcriptome sequencing recovers mRNA not only from the gut epithelium but also from immune cells, together with their antigen-receptor repertoire, and we have demonstrated its clinical potential in gastrointestinal differential diagnosis, CRC screening and the monitoring of IBD activity. The same readout could serve diagnosis, prediction of drug response and disease monitoring across a wider range of gastrointestinal conditions, such as Clostridioides difficile infection and irritable bowel syndrome.

Study participants

The stool samples in this study were collected from Renji Hospital, School of Medicine, Shanghai Jiao Tong University, Shenzhen Hospital of Southern Medical University, and a contract research organization (ZunTai Medical Technology, Guangzhou, China).

On the basis of colonoscopy, Normal participants were further categorized as healthy (colonoscopy-negative with no inflammation or lesion), NEG (colonoscopy-negative with hyperplastic polyps or a non-neoplastic lesion) or non-advanced precancerous lesion (NAPL: tubular adenoma(s) < 10 mm in diameter, serrated adenomatous polyp(s) < 10 mm, or ≥ 20 hyperplastic polyps).

IBD subtype (UC or CD), Montreal classification, disease location, endoscopic score and date, and fecal calprotectin concentration were obtained from clinical records. Endoscopic activity was recorded as the Mayo endoscopic subscore for UC and as an SES-CD category for CD, and both were mapped onto a common four-level grade (remission < mild < moderate < severe). IBD libraries in endoscopic remission (n = 14) were excluded from every betweendiagnosis comparison-cluster shares (Fig. 1d), repertoire size and lineage transcripts (Fig. 2a, b), principal-component analysis, differential expression, program scores and the diagnostic classifiers (Fig. 3)-because a healed mucosa is expected to shed little disease-specific material. They were retained in the antigen-database and invariant-chain analyses (Fig. 2c, d) and in the IBD disease activity analyses (Fig. 4), in which remission is one of the grades and, with the healthy libraries, forms the negative class of the activity classifier (Fig. 4e). The activitymonitoring set comprised IBD libraries with an endoscopic grade and an endoscopy dated within 30 days of stool collection, in either direction. Of the 90 IBD libraries, 16 had no recorded grade and 6 were graded but outside the 30-day window (no graded library lacked an endoscopy date), leaving 68; the 2 of these without a recorded IBD subtype were also excluded, leaving 66 (UC 33, CD 33; Supplementary Fig. 3).

Stool sample collection, host RNA enrichment, library preparation and sequencing

Fresh stool samples were collected by patients in fecal collection tubes, delivered to the hospital within 4 h, and stored at -80 °C. Human RNA was isolated from 1 g of stool per sample. The method first lyses human cells in lysis buffer and enriches the human mRNA on oligo(dT) beads.

We quantified the extracted RNA by NanoDrop (concentration, A260/A280, and A260/A230), measured host RNA content by GAPDH amount using RT-qPCR (Takara OneStep Prime Script III RT-qPCR 2X Master mix (Takara Bio, part #RR601)), and stored it at -80 °C. The librarypreparation procedure specifies a total RNA concentration ≥100 ng µl⁻¹ (NanoDrop One C, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and a GAPDH RT-qPCR Ct ≤31 as its input requirement for the downstream library preparation.

The isolated fecal RNA was converted into strand-specific, dual-indexed libraries with the Fast RNA-seq Lib Prep Kit V2 (ABclonal, China), using 16 PCR cycles and unique dual-index primers. Libraries were sequenced as 2 × 150 bp paired-end reads on a NovaSeq 6000 to a median of 47 million clean reads (range 25-173 million) after adapter and quality trimming.

Fecal calprotectin measurement

In Cohort 1, the calprotectin concentration was measured using the Fecal Calprotectin Detection Kit (Guangzhou Forreal Biotechnology Co., Ltd.). In Cohort 2, the calprotectin concentration was measured using the BÜHLMANN fCAL ELISA (BUHLMANN, Switzerland).

Read processing and per-library quality metrics

Reads were processed with the RNA-seq pipeline: trimmed and quality-filtered with fastp 1.3.0 19 (default settings) and aligned to GRCh38 with STAR 2.7.11b 20 . Specificity was then imposed by restricting to records with MAPQ ≥ 30, both mates on the same reference and an absolute insert size ≤ 2,000 bp. Duplicates were marked on the filtered BAM files with Picard 3.4.0 21 MarkDuplicates, and insert-size metrics were collected with Picard CollectInsertSizeMetrics on non-duplicate pairs. Gene-level fragment counts were obtained with featureCounts 22 (Subread 2.1.1), and the deduplicated matrix was used for all analyses. Mitochondrially encoded genes (MT-) were removed by the pipeline before its count matrices were written, so the deduplicated matrix contains no mitochondrial genes and every per-million denominator below refers to nuclear genes. The per-library QC metrics were: clean library size (reads after fastp); mapping rate and nuclear mapping rate (the fraction of clean reads aligned to the genome and, at MAPQ ≥ 30, to the nuclear genome); duplication rate (the fraction of MAPQ ≥ 30 mapped reads flagged as duplicates); CDS-exon tags (fragments overlapping coding exons at MAPQ ≥ 30); exonic and intergenic rates (the fractions of assigned MAPQ ≥ 30 tags in UTR and coding exons, and in intergenic regions); and detected protein-coding genes (genes with ≥ 10 deduplicated counts at MAPQ ≥ 30; Fig. 1b). Libraries with an intergenic rate > 10% or < 0.5 × 10⁶ CDS-exon tags were excluded. Both criteria are alignment metrics fixed before any expression analysis, so the filter is independent of the expression patterns analyzed downstream. The 285 libraries (healthy 24, NEG 33, NAPL 20, APL 63, CRC 55 and IBD 90) in this study already passed these filters.

Normalization

One expression matrix serves every analysis. Counts per million (CPM) were recomputed from the deduplicated counts and corrected for composition bias with the trimmed mean of M-values (TMM) 23 method, implemented in Python as a port of edgeR's 24 calcNormFactors with its default settings and fitted once on the 285 QC-passed libraries. This TMM-normalized CPM (TMM-CPM) is the unit of the deconvolution and the marker panels (Fig. 1c-f), the lineage transcripts and signature scores (Fig. 2b, Fig. 3b, Fig. 4a, c; Supplementary Figs. 2 and 7), the principalcomponent analysis, its pole enrichments and the diagnostic classifiers (Fig. 3a-d, g-i; Supplementary Fig. 9), the program scores (Fig. 3f; Supplementary Fig. 8) and the activity analyses (Fig. 4a, d, e; Supplementary Figs. 4 and 5). Differential expression (Fig. 3e) used raw counts. Normalized CPM (nCPM) appears only as the unit of the Human Protein Atlas (HPA) single-cell reference 25 , against which the marker and deconvolution criteria below are stated. All log transforms are log₂(x + 1) unless otherwise stated.

Reference atlas and lineage clusters

The HPA single-cell type atlas 25 (version 25.1, Ensembl 109; 154 cell types; nCPM) was restricted to genes reaching ≥1 nCPM in at least one cell type, and the 5,000 genes with the highest variance of log(nCPM + 1) were retained. Cell types were clustered by average-linkage hierarchical clustering on cosine distance and the tree was cut at a distance of 0.15 (33 clusters). A predefined list of twelve lineage merges, based on prior biological knowledge, was then applied in order, each joining the two clusters that held the named cell types and accepted only if the mean cosine distance between those clusters was ≤0.25 (all twelve were, at 0.16-0.24): tuft cells with colonocytes; microglia with macrophages; platelets with megakaryocytes; erythrocytes with erythrocyte progenitors; late spermatids with undifferentiated spermatogonia, then oocytes with late spermatids, giving one germ-cell cluster; Müller glia with astrocytes, then retinal ganglion cells, astrocytes and retinal horizontal cells each with brain excitatory neurons, giving one neural-glial cluster; and myonuclei, then thymic myoid cells, with cardiomyocytes, giving one striated-muscle cluster. Six squamous cell types (esophageal apical, basal and suprabasal cells, basal and suprabasal keratinocytes, and ocular epithelial cells) were separated into a cluster of their own, because squamous cells reach stool by a different route from intestinal epithelium and pooling them with other epithelia obscures the dominant stool compartment. Hepatocytes were left as the singleton produced by the cut: because a cluster signature is the mean of its members, merging hepatocytes into the columnar gastrointestinal cluster would allow hepatocyte-restricted genes to score as markers of the compartment that dominates stool, and would leave no separate column to absorb apolipoprotein transcripts that both intestine and liver produce (see Cluster-level deconvolution). This procedure yielded 22 clusters (Supplementary Fig. 1; members in Supplementary Table 1).

Cluster-level deconvolution

Cluster signatures were the mean nCPM of member cell types. Marker genes were selected at the cluster level on three criteria: (i) expression ≥10 nCPM in the highest-expressing cluster; (ii) that cluster accounting for ≥85% of the gene's summed cluster means; and (iii) expression ≥10 nCPM in at least 34% of that cluster's member cell types. Criterion (iii) ensures that a marker describes the cluster rather than one of its members: criteria (i) and (ii) score only the contrast between clusters, and because a cluster profile is a member mean, a gene expressed by a single member of a large cluster otherwise appears perfectly specific.

The resulting marker pool comprised 488 gene-cluster assignments, 479 of whose genes were present in the stool matrix. Clusters with fewer than five markers -the five singletons above and C22 (stromal and vascular cell types) -were excluded from the fit, leaving 16 of 22 clusters. Supplementary Table 1 lists every cluster with its signature genes, including the markers of the six clusters that were not fitted.

Each RNA-seq library (TMM-CPM) was deconvolved against the signature matrix, with each cluster's profile scaled to unit Euclidean length so that clusters with very different absolute expression contribute on an equal footing, by dampened weighted least squares (DWLS) 26 : a non-negative least-squares (NNLS) initial estimate followed by four reweighted NNLS iterations with weights 1/ŷ², where ŷ is the fitted expression floored at the library's 25th percentile of nonzero expression so that near-zero genes cannot dominate. This weighting minimizes relative rather than absolute error, which is appropriate when the mismatch between a tissue reference and a stool library is multiplicative. A fixed floor was used in place of the cross-validated dampening constant of the original method, which is selected for stability across gene subsets rather than for accuracy and did not improve any performance measure we examined.

The reported quantity is the transcript share: the fitted coefficients, renormalized to sum to one across clusters. Transcript share is not a cell-fraction estimate, because conversion to cell fractions requires equal mRNA content per cell, an assumption that does not hold across epithelial cells, neutrophils and erythrocytes. Fig. 1c shows the mean share in Normal libraries of each cluster reaching ≥5% mean share in Normal, CRC or IBD stool, with the remaining clusters pooled. Differences in the share of each of these clusters among Normal, CRC and IBD libraries (Fig. 1d) were tested by Kruskal-Wallis test with Benjamini-Hochberg correction across the clusters shown.

Lineage signature scores

The B-cell, T-cell (Fig. 4c) and neutrophil scores (Fig. 3b, Fig. 4a, d) are each the mean, over the genes of the set, of the gene's z-score of log₂(TMM-CPM + 1), so that every gene contributes equally and the score is in standard-deviation units. All three sets are the curated panels specified a priori from canonical lineage biology: the B-cell set is the ten-gene B-cell panel (MS4A1, CD79A, CD79B, CD19, PAX5, BANK1, BLK, CR2, TNFRSF13C and CD22) and the T-cell set the 14-gene T-cell panel (CD3D, CD3E, CD3G, CD2, TRAC, TRBC1, TRBC2, THEMIS, IL7R, LCK, ZAP70, CD28, IL17A and IL22), both from the cross-tissue immune atlas 30 and sorted-blood signatures 31 . Antigen-receptor assembly

Antigen-receptor sequences were assembled with TRUST4 1.1.9 36 from each library's coordinate-sorted, duplicate-marked BAM file, using the TRUST4 hg38 BCR/TCR locus reference and the human IMGT 37 V/D/J/C reference. Each clonotype was assigned to a locus from its V gene, or from its J or C gene where V was unassigned, and classed as productive if its CDR3 began with the conserved cysteine, ended with phenylalanine or tryptophan and contained no stop codon or frameshift. Of the 5,574 clonotypes in the 285 QC-passed libraries, 5,409 (97%) were productive.

Antigen-database matching and invariant T-cell chains

CDR3 amino-acid sequences were reduced to their core (the IMGT junction without the conserved cysteine and phenylalanine or tryptophan) and matched exactly, within locus, against human records of VDJdb 38 (release 2026-06-03), the IEDB 39 TCR and BCR receptor tables (full export of 25 August 2026) and CoV-AbDab 40 (downloaded 27 August 2026), 500,456 records in total. A CDR3 is counted once in each antigen class of its matching records, so class totals exceed the number of matched sequences.

Mucosal-associated invariant T (MAIT) cells carry a semi-invariant α chain joining TRAV1-2 to TRAJ33 or, less often, TRAJ20 or TRAJ12 41, 42 ; in the TRUST4 IMGT reference these J segments encode DSNYQLI, SNDYKLS and MDSSYKLI before the anchor residue, each supplying the conserved Tyr95α that contacts MR1 and the ribityl moiety of the antigen 43 . MAITlike α chains were defined as TRAV1-2 joined to any of these J segments or, irrespective of V assignment, a CDR3α containing DSNYQLI; only TRAJ33 was matched on sequence as well as on J assignment, because exonuclease trimming can shorten SNDYKLS to as little as YKLS. Chains with and without TRAV1-2 are reported separately. Invariant natural killer T-cell α chains (TRAV10-TRAJ18) were sought the same way; none was found.

Principal-component analysis

Log₂(TMM-CPM + 1) values of the 2,000 most variable genes were mean-centered per gene and decomposed by singular-value decomposition across the 271 libraries outside endoscopic remission (Normal 77, APL 63, CRC 55, IBD 76); eight components were retained. The sign of each component is arbitrary and was fixed as follows: PC1 was flipped where necessary so that it correlated positively (Spearman) with the neutrophil score; for every other component, the gene with the largest absolute loading was identified and the component was flipped where necessary so that this gene's loading was positive. Fig. 3a, b place the 271 libraries on PC1 and the further component with the highest area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) for separating CRC from IBD, chosen by that rule (PC6 in this cohort), colored by diagnosis with group means marked by crosses and a 68% contour (1.51 s.d. ellipse) per group (Fig. 3a) or by the 15-gene neutrophil score, which is not an input to the decomposition (Fig. 3b; ρ, Spearman correlation of the score with each component over the 271 libraries). For Fig. 3c the scores of each of the eight components were standardized across all 271 libraries and their distributions shown for Normal, CRC and IBD, with the unadjusted Kruskal-Wallis P across the three groups; APL libraries enter the decomposition and the standardization but are not plotted. For each of the two plotted components, the 100 genes with the most positive and the 100 with the most negative loadings were tested for enrichment with Enrichr 44, 45 (via GSEApy 46 ) against GO Biological Process 2023 47 , KEGG 2021 Human 48 , Reactome 2022 49 and MSigDB Hallmark 2020 50 (hypergeometric test, the 2,000 decomposed genes as background, Benjamini-Hochberg adjustment); the two most significant terms per pole are shown as -log₁₀ adjusted P against the adjusted P = 0.05 line, whether or not they reach it (Fig. 3d). Poles are labeled by component and sign; which diagnoses lie toward each pole is read from Fig. 3c.

Differential expression

Differential expression between CRC and IBD used DESeq2 51 (PyDESeq2 52 ) on raw counts of the 55 CRC and 76 IBD libraries outside remission (131 in total), with the design ~ condition, CRC as reference and Cook's-distance outlier refitting. Before fitting, 448 nuclear-encoded mitochondrial pseudogenes and MT-RNR2-like genes were removed, as they distort size-factor estimation, and genes with fewer than 10 counts across the 131 libraries were discarded, leaving 27,315. Genes with Benjamini-Hochberg-adjusted P < 0.05 (Wald test) and |log₂ fold change| > 1 were called differentially expressed.

Program scores

Four hallmark programs annotate the volcano plot of Fig. 3e and are scored in Fig. 3f, each declared in advance as one named term in the gene-set libraries above, on one side of the CRC-versus-IBD contrast: Wnt signaling (KEGG Wnt signaling pathway), epithelialmesenchymal transition/stroma (Hallmark Epithelial Mesenchymal Transition) and proliferation/E2F (Hallmark E2F Targets) on the CRC side, and the interferon-γ response (Hallmark Interferon Gamma Response) on the IBD side. The genes differentially expressed on each side (522 higher in CRC, 769 higher in IBD) were tested with Enrichr against all tested genes as background, and each program is the overlap of its term with that list: 11, 23, 16 and 57 genes (Enrichr adjusted P = 0.019, 2.2 × 10⁻¹⁰, 1.9 × 10⁻⁵ and 3.0 × 10⁻³⁹). Because every member gene is selected by the contrast it annotates, the scores describe that contrast rather than provide independent evidence for it; they show whether the CRC-IBD difference is one axis or several programs. A program score is the mean z-score of log₂(TMM-CPM + 1) over its genes, standardized jointly across the Normal, IBD and CRC libraries (77, 76 and 55); CRC and IBD were compared by two-sided Mann-Whitney U test, with Normal shown for reference. Supplementary Fig. 8 shows each member gene by group.

Classification

Classifiers were fitted on log₂(TMM-CPM + 1) by selecting the 2,000 most variable genes, standardizing, and fitting L2-penalized logistic regression (multinomial for more than two classes; lbfgs solver, C = 1.0, balanced class weights, up to 4,000 iterations), with gene selection and standardization refitted within each training fold. Cross-validation was five-fold, stratified by class and grouped by stool sample (scikit-learn 53 StratifiedGroupKFold), and was repeated with ten random fold assignments; point estimates are means over the ten. Uncertainties are 95% percentile bootstrap intervals over libraries, resampled with replacement 2,000 times within class, the statistic on each resample being the mean over the ten fold assignments.

The three-class model was trained on CRC (55), IBD (76) and Normal (77) libraries (APL was excluded). Each library's out-of-fold call is its consensus over the ten fold assignments, the class it was assigned most often, with ties broken by its mean out-of-fold probability; Fig. 3g is the confusion matrix of these calls, and its accuracy carries a 95% percentile bootstrap interval over libraries (2,000 resamples, class sizes kept). One-versus-rest AUCs are means over the ten fold assignments with the same bootstrap interval (Fig. 3h). CRC and IBD libraries whose consensus call was Normal were counted by tumor stage and endoscopic grade (Results). The 2,000-gene setting is where out-of-fold accuracy peaks over a sweep from 10 genes to all 58,320 (Supplementary Fig. 9; mean ± s.e. over the ten fold assignments).

A second model asked whether advanced neoplasia can be called from stool alone (Fig. 3i): CRC and APL pooled as the positive class (n = 118) against Normal (n = 77). Within each training fold the 20 genes with the largest absolute Welch t statistic between cases and Normal were selected, standardized and fitted by L2-penalized logistic regression (C = 1.0, unweighted classes); cross-validation was as above, with five fold assignments. The out-of-fold score, averaged over assignments, was read as CRC versus Normal and as APL versus Normal; each AUC carries a 95% bootstrap interval over libraries resampled within study group, and sensitivity at 90% specificity was computed within each assignment at the threshold leaving 90% of Normal libraries below it, then averaged. Genes selected in at least 20 of the 25 training folds are listed in the legend.

IBD activity and fecal calprotectin

Activity analyses used the 66 graded IBD libraries of the activity-monitoring set (UC 33, CD 33), with the 24 healthy libraries as a reference band. Fig. 4a shows S100A8 and S100A9 as log₂(TMM-CPM + 1), the neutrophil score standardized within these 90 libraries, and the immune-cell share from the deconvolution (Fig. 1c, d) as a percentage of transcripts. Associations with grade (remission < mild < moderate < severe) are Spearman correlations across the graded IBD libraries, healthy excluded, with UC and CD pooled in Fig. 4 and within subtype in the Results; for the neutrophil score, adjacent grades were also compared by unadjusted two-sided Mann-Whitney U test. Repertoire size (the number of productive clonotypes; Fig. 4b) and the B-and T-cell scores (Fig. 4c) were related to grade in the same way. Because the pooled grade combines the Mayo endoscopic subscore with SES-CD categories, instruments that score different lesions, each pooled correlation summarizes two diseases measured on different scales.

Fecal calprotectin (µg g⁻¹, routine clinical assay) was available for 32 UC and 31 CD libraries (cohort 1: 12 UC and 15 CD; cohort 2: 20 UC and 16 CD). Calprotectin and the neutrophil score were each correlated (Spearman) with grade on identical libraries, within subtype and cohort and with subtypes pooled (Fig. 4d; Supplementary Fig. 5), and with the raw endoscopic score (Supplementary Fig. 4), so that the two measures differ only in what is measured.

To ask whether activity can be called from the neutrophil score alone, a classifier was fitted on that single feature, standardized across the 90 libraries, to separate active disease (mild, moderate or severe; n = 54) from the quiescent gut, the 24 healthy and 12 remission libraries together (Fig. 4e). It uses the pipeline of Classification reduced to one feature: standardization, then L2-penalized logistic regression with balanced class weights, under five-fold crossvalidation stratified by class and grouped by stool sample, with ten fold assignments. Fig. 4e shows its out-of-fold ROC as in Fig. 3h, with the AUC and its 95% bootstrap interval over libraries resampled within grade and the sensitivity at 90% specificity, as means over the ten fold assignments. With one feature the model is a monotone transform of the score, so the AUC is in effect the score's own; the cross-validation places the call threshold (P ≥ 0.5), and hence its sensitivity and specificity, out of fold.
