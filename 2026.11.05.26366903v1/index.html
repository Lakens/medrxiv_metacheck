To fix Exact P-Values

What we found

We found 1 imprecise p value out of 32 detected p values.

Show details

Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

P-Value Text
p < 0.05 All statistical tests were two-tailed with statistical significance defined nominally as p < 0.05 for the prespecified primary normal approximation analysis.

The APA manual states: Report exact p values (e.g., p = .031) to two or three decimal places. However, report p values less than .001 as p < .001. However, 2 decimals is too imprecise for many use-cases (e.g., a p value meta-analysis), so report p values with three digits.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 17 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) exhibit heterogeneous functional decline, potentially influenced by systemic metabolic reserve. We investigate whether circulating insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1) is associated with subsequent functional change in ALS.

Serum IGF-1 was measured in 37 participants with ALS and 25 healthy controls. Baseline levels were compared between groups, and across disease-duration defined progression categories. Lagged regression assessed whether prior visit IGF-1 level predicted change in Revised ALS Functional Rating Scale (ALSFRS-R) on the next visit, adjusting for intervisit interval, body mass index, age and sex. Mixed-effects and sensitivity analyses assessed robustness.

Baseline IGF-1 did not differ significantly between controls and ALS (mean + SD: 107.3 + 54.3 vs 132.2 + 68.3 ng/mL, p = 0.12), or across progression categories (F(2, 34) = 0.13, p = 0.88). Across 48 intervals from 23 ALS participants, each 1-SD higher IGF-1 from prior visit was associated with 1.40-point less subsequent ΔALSFRS-R decline (95% CI: 0.03 to 2.77, p = 0.045). Generalised estimating equations yielded a similar association (β = 1.50, p = 0.030), whereas small-cluster robust inference showed wider uncertainty (p = 0.084). Repeated IGF-1 showed an intraclass correlation coefficient of 0.69, and the lagged association remained positive in all 23 leave-one-participant-out analyses. The IGF-1-bytime interaction was not significant in a random-slope mixed model (p =0.254).

Higher circulating IGF-1 may reflect systemic trophic-metabolic reserve associated with short-term functional preservation in ALS, but limited precision precludes definitive prognostic or causal conclusions.

Introduction

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is a rapidly progressing neurodegenerative disorder affecting motor neurons. Whilst its etiology is not fully understood, defects in energy metabolism are increasingly recognized as an important contributor to ALS pathophysiology, with a large body of evidence showing that motor neurons, skeletal muscle, and systemic metabolic networks all exhibit bioenergetic defects [1]. Identifying circulating biomarkers that reflect an individual's capacity to withstand these metabolic and neurodegenerative stresses may therefore improve understanding of disease heterogeneity. Because ALS progression varies markedly between individuals, identifying what drives this variation is a critical step toward stratifying patients for prognosis and for precision-medicine trial design.

Insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1) is a pleiotropic hormone involved in growth, energy homeostasis, skeletal muscle maintenance, mitochondrial function and cellular survival [2]. Lack of neurotrophic support is implicated in ALS and increasing evidence indicates that dysregulation of IGF-1 signaling contributes to pathogenesis [3, 4]. IGF-1 is protective in motor neurons [5] involving upregulation of mitophagy and protection of mitochondria from apoptosis [6]. Furthermore, IGF-1 is highly expressed in the relatively resistant oculomotor neurons in ALS [7]. These protective activities have led to therapeutic studies where beneficial effects of IGF-1 in both mouse models [5, [8] [9] [10] and clinical trials [11] have been observed. However, findings have not been consistent across experimental studies and clinical trials of recombinant IGF-1 have not demonstrated clear or reproducible therapeutic benefit in ALS [12] [13] [14]. For example, in a clinical trial for another neurodegenerative condition affecting motor neurons, spinobulbar muscular atrophy, no improvement in muscle strength or function was detected [15].

These conflicting findings suggest that the relationship between IGF-1 and neurodegeneration in ALS may be more complex than a simple linear association with the assumption that higher concentrations are uniformly beneficial. Heterogeneous factors such as age, nutritional status, endocrine regulation, receptor sensitivity and IGF-binding protein can affect IGF-1 signaling. Moreover, its effect can also differ between acute injury, chronic neurodegeneration and physiological aging. Consequently, endogenous circulating IGF-1 may represent an integrated indicator of systemic trophic and metabolic capacity rather than a simply direct measure of CNS IGF-1 activity or therapeutic target. Insufficient IGF-1 may indicate reduced trophic or metabolic reserve, whereas progressively higher concentration may provide insight into a plateau in therapeutic benefit once adequate signaling capacity is reached.

Consistent with this reserve-based framework, alterations in circulating IGF-1 have already been reported in ALS. IGF-1 is present in serum [16], and in ALS patients, lower serum IGF-1 concentrations have been detected compared to healthy controls [17]. In patients with long disease duration, circulating IGF-1 concentrations were also 36% higher than in controls [18]. These findings suggest that circulating IGF-1 may be associated with disease characteristics in ALS, although its clinical significance remains unclear. Moreover, most previous studies have relied on cross-sectional measurements, retrospectively estimated progression rates, or survival outcomes. It remains unclear whether IGF-1 measured at baseline is prospectively associated with functional change occurring before a subsequent visit.

In the present study, we examined circulating IGF-1 concentration in serum of individuals with ALS and healthy controls and evaluated whether IGF-1 was prospectively associated with subsequent functional change, measured using the revised ALS Functional Rating Scale (ALSFRS-R). The primary hypothesis was that higher IGF-1 at the preceding visit would be associated with less subsequent functional decline, after adjustment for age, sex, BMI, and the time interval between assessments. In addition, we explored whether the association between serum IGF-1 and ALS progression was linear, whether IGF-1 differed across clinical progression categories, and whether IGF-1 provided additional explanatory or classification information beyond clinical and trajectory-related measures. Because disease heterogeneity remains a key hurdle to biomarker development and precision medicine trial design in ALS, we tested the overarching hypothesis that serum IGF-1 captures systemic trophic-metabolic reserve contributing to functional resilience, rather than acting as a standalone biomarker of ALS progression.

Methods (A) Study design and cohort

This study used a prospective longitudinal cohort design to examine disease heterogeneity. Participants were selected from the Macquarie University Motor Neuron Disease (MND) Research Centre's Neurodegenerative Disease Biobank. All participants with ALS received a probable or definite diagnosis according to the revised El Escorial criteria [19], as determined by a consultant neurologist; eligible participants were aged 18 years or older and provided written informed consent or where the participant lacked capacity, consent via a legally authorised representative. Unaffected controls (hereafter referred to as healthy controls) without a diagnosis of ALS or another neurodegenerative disease were recruited in parallel through the same biobank; body mass index was not routinely collected for controls. Three highly heterogenous patient cohorts were carefully designed to stratify ALS patients into the desired subgroups while ensuring age and sex matching, regardless of stratification by subtype, disease progression or site of onset. Disease progression was based on the disease duration. Slow progression was defined as five years and over (> 5 years), normal disease progression was between two to five years (> 2 and < 5 years) and fast progression was defined as two years or less (< 2 years).

Analyses used different subsets of this cohort depending on data completeness: crosssectional baseline comparisons required a single IGF-1 measurement per participant, the primary lagged analysis required paired consecutive visits with complete ALSFRS-R and BMI, and the mixed-effects trajectory analyses required complete ALSFRS-R and BMI at each individual visit. The derivation of each analytic sample is reported in the Results and summarised in the STROBE flow diagram (Figure 1).

(B) Laboratory analysis

Blood collection

Samples were collected and processed under standardised biobank procedures. Venous blood was collected by venipuncture (21G or 23G butterfly needle) into a 3.5 mL serumseparating tube (BD Vacutainer SST, cat. no. 367956; Becton Dickinson) drawn first in the order of draw and gently inverted per the manufacturer instructions. Samples were allowed to clot at room temperature for ≥30 min and centrifuged within 2 h of collection at 1,200 x g for 10 min at 20 °C with maximum brake. Serum was aliquoted into 500 µL volumes in barcoded 0.9 mL FluidX tubes and stored at -80 °C. Samples underwent no freeze-thaw cycles before analysis.

(C) Statistical Analysis

Study design and data structure Analyses were conducted using a longitudinal dataset containing repeated Revised ALS Functional Rating Scale (ALSFRS-R) measurements and circulating serum IGF-1 concentrations. To avoid inflation from repeated measures in cross-sectional comparisons, baseline analyses were performed using the first available observation per participant. Longitudinal analyses were restricted to ALS participants and were conducted using consecutive visit-to-visit intervals.

Visit timing was recorded in days and converted to years for longitudinal modelling. For each participant (denoted i), consecutive visit-to-visit intervals were constructed. For each interval, the prior visit is denoted by (t-1) and the subsequent visit is denoted by (t). Lagged IGF-1 and lagged ALSFRS-R were defined as the values measured at visit (t-1) and the inter-visit interval was calculated as the time between visits (t-1) and (t), expressed in years. Short-term functional change (ΔALSFRS-R) was defined as:

ΔALSFRS-R = ALSFRS-Ri,t -ALSFRS-Ri,t-1

Negative values therefore indicate functional decline, whereas higher or less negative values (closer to 0) indicate less decline. Clinically meaningful decline was defined a priori as a decrease of at least 5 ALSFRS-R points between consecutive visits. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated from height and weight as kg/m² and then transformed using z-score normalization for multivariable models.

Progression categories were based on recorded disease duration and classified as slow, normal, or fast progression as described earlier. Because disease duration represented a minimum observed duration for participants who were alive or lost to follow-up, analyses involving progression categories were considered exploratory and descriptive.

Baseline group comparisons

Baseline IGF-1 concentrations were compared between ALS participants and healthy controls using Welch's t-test to account for unequal variances. Effect size was quantified using Cohen's d. Baseline IGF-1 concentrations were also compared across observed ALS progression categories using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA).

Lagged regression analyses of visit-to-visit functional change with IGF-1

Lagged linear regression models were used to evaluate whether serum IGF-1 at prior clinic visit was associated with subsequent visit-to-visit change in ALSFRS-R. The primary outcome was ΔALSFRS-R, and the main predictor was lagged IGF-1 (standardized per SD). Participant-level cluster-robust standard errors with normal-approximation inference were used for the primary lagged analysis to account for repeated visit-to-visit intervals within participants. To test robustness of the primary model, additional adjustment or sensitivity analyses were performed including (i) generalized estimating equations (GEE); (ii) conditional change models adjusted for ALSFRS-R at the prior clinic visit, denoted as ALSFRS-Ri,t-1, to determine whether IGF-1 was associated with subsequent functional change independent of prior functional status; (iii) CR2 bias-reduced cluster-robust covariance estimation with Satterthwaite degrees of freedom as a conservative smallcluster estimate due to the modest sample size (n < 30).

Serum IGF-1 measurement variability and robustness analyses

A null random-intercept model for repeated IGF-1 measurements was used to estimate the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), grouping the observed variance into between-participant and within-participant plus residual components. To examine variation of IGF-1 levels coming from between-participant differences and visit-specific fluctuations, priorvisit IGF-1 measurement was separated into each participant's mean concentration and the deviation of each measurement from that mean; both components were entered simultaneously in an adjusted GEE model. Robustness to individual participants was assessed by repeating the primary lagged analysis after excluding each participant in turn and summarizing the resulting IGF-1 coefficients.

Mixed-effects trajectory analyses

Mixed-effects models were used to examine whether baseline IGF-1 was associated with longitudinal ALSFRS-R trajectories. Fixed effects included time, baseline IGF-1 (standardized per SD), the IGF-1 × time interaction, baseline BMI, BMI x time, age and sex. Four models were fitted to the same complete-case sample: random-intercept models with and without IGF-1 terms, and random-intercept plus participant-specific randomslope models with and without IGF-1 terms. The random-intercept specification allowed participants to differ in baseline ALSFRS-R, whereas the random-slope specification additionally allowed the rate of ALSFRS-R change over time to vary between participants. Models used maximum-likelihood estimation for nested comparisons of fixed effects and random-effects structures; final coefficient estimates were obtained using restricted maximum likelihood.

Model fit was compared using Akaike information criterion (AIC), Bayesian information criterion (BIC), and likelihood-ratio tests. Nakagawa marginal and conditional R² were reported descriptively. Because participant-specific random slopes substantially improved fit, the random-slope model was given greater interpretive weight when assessing the baseline IGF-1 × time association.

Directional specificity

Reverse-direction models were fitted to examine whether prior functional status was associated with subsequent IGF-1 concentrations. This analysis was used to assess temporal (a)symmetry between IGF-1 and functional change as a follow-up to the lagged model. In this model, subsequent IGF-1 at visit t, standardized per SD, was modeled as the outcome and ALSFRS-R at prior visit (t-1) was the predictor of interest. Exploratory analyses

Exploratory analyses of nonlinearity and candidate cutpoints, effect modification by observed progression category, incremental explanatory value, and binary decline classification were conducted as described in the Supplementary Methods. These analyses were considered exploratory for hypothesis-generating and were not considered for the current primary longitudinal conclusion.

Statistical Software

All analyses were conducted in R version 4.4.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Data management was performed using dplyr, tidyr, and readr; mixedeffects models were fitted using lme4; conventional participant-clustered covariance estimates were obtained using sandwich; CR2 small-cluster corrections with Satterthwaite degrees of freedom were calculated using clubSandwich; generalized estimating equations were fitted using geepack; ridge-penalised logistic regression used glmnet; AUC analyses used pROC; and figures were generated using ggplot2. All statistical tests were two-tailed with statistical significance defined nominally as p < 0.05 for the prespecified primary normal approximation analysis. Results near this threshold were interpreted cautiously and further supported with sensitivity analyses given the modest number of participant clusters and the exploratory nature of secondary analyses.

Prospective Association Between IGF-1 and Short-Term Functional Change

We next examined whether serum IGF-1 concentrations at prior clinic visit were associated with subsequent visit-to-visit change in ALSFRS-R (Table 2). The complete case analysis included 48 intervals from 23 ALS participants (Figure 1). In the primary model adjusted for inter-visit interval, baseline BMI, age and sex, every 1 SD higher IGF-1 concentration at the prior visit was associated with a 1.40-point higher subsequent ΔALSFRS-R (95% CI 0.03 to 2.77, p = 0.045), implying less functional decline at the next follow-up visit.

The estimate was similar after additional adjustment for prior ALSFRS-R (β = 1.45, 95% CI 0.10 to 2.80; p = 0.036) in the conditional change model and in the GEE sensitivity analysis (β = 1.50, 95% CI 0.15 to 2.86; p = 0.030). Under conservative CR2/Satterthwaite small-cluster inference, the corresponding effect estimates remained similar in magnitude and direction, but confidence intervals widened and included zero for both the lagged model (β = 1.40, 95% CI -0.22 to 3.02; p = 0.084) and the conditional model (β = 1.45, 95% CI -0.15 to 3.05; p = 0.071). Thus, the estimated association was consistent across specifications, although its precision was sensitive to inference with the modest sample size.

Stability and source of variation in repeated IGF-1 measurement

Repeated IGF-1 measurements showed an ICC of 0.69, indicating that approximately 69% of observed variability occurred between participants, whereas the remaining 31% reflected within-participant variation over time, together with residual measurement variability. In the within-versus between-participant comparison, participants with a higher average IGF-1 concentration showed more preserved subsequent function (between-participant β = 1.67, 95% CI 0.14 to 3.21; p = 0.032). By contrast, visit-specific deviations from a participant's usual IGF-1 level were not associated with subsequent functional change (within-participant β = -0.01, 95% CI -1.00 to 0.98; p = 0.989).

In leave-one-participant-out analyses, the lagged IGF-1 coefficient remained positive after each of the 23 participants was excluded in turn. Estimates ranged from 0.95 to 1.86, with a median of 1.44, close to the full-sample estimate of 1.40. The direction and approximate magnitude of the lagged association were therefore not driven by any single participant.

Baseline IGF-1 and longitudinal ALSFRS-R trajectories

We next examined whether baseline IGF-1 was associated with longitudinal ALSFRS-R trajectories using mixed-effect models (Figure 4). In the random-intercept model, the IGF-1 × time interaction was positive and statistically significant (β = 2.54 ALSFRS-R points/year per SD; p = 0.0006). Adding baseline IGF-1 and the IGF-1 × time interaction improved fit relative to the corresponding model without IGF-1 (model 1 vs model 2: ΔAIC = -7.19; ΔBIC = -2.61; likelihood-ratio χ²(2) = 11.19, p = 0.0037) and increased marginal and conditional R² by 0.051 and 0.062, respectively (Table 3).

Taken together, the direction of the mixed-effects estimates was consistent with the laggedmodel findings, with higher baseline IGF-1 corresponding to slower functional decline in the random-intercept model. When participant-specific random slopes were included, substantially improving model fit, the IGF-1 × time estimate remained positive but was attenuated, estimated less precisely, and was not statistically significant. These findings indicate substantial between-participant heterogeneity in progression rates and limited precision in the estimated association between baseline IGF-1 and long-term ALSFRS-R trajectories.

Directional Specificity

To clarify the relationship between ALSFRS-R and IGF-1 concentration, we further asked whether prior ALSFRS-R predicted subsequent IGF-1 level, testing bidirectionality relative to the primary lagged model. To examine this reverse temporal direction, we modeled subsequent IGF-1 as the outcome and prior ALSFRS-R as the predictor, adjusting for prior IGF-1 and relevant covariates. Prior ALSFRS-R was not significantly associated with later IGF-1 concentrations (p = 0.096), whereas prior IGF-1 strongly predicted subsequent IGF-1 (p < 0.001). Because the reverse-direction estimate was non-significant rather than clearly null and the sample was modest, these findings are consistent with, but do not establish, temporal asymmetry.

Exploratory analyses

Exploratory spline modeling suggested a possible nonlinear pattern, with lower prior-visit IGF-1 concentrations corresponding to greater subsequent functional decline and apparent flattening at higher concentrations (Figure S1). However, the overall spline terms were not statistically significant (p = 0.19). Other analyses, including modeling of clinically meaningful cutpoints, effect modification across observed progression categories, and participant-grouped classification of subsequent decline, were performed to explore the possible clinical utility of IGF-1, but none were conclusive given the modest sample size. These data may inform the design of future, adequately powered studies. Full methods and results are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Discussion

This longitudinal study found that higher serum IGF-1 concentrations at the prior visit were associated with more preserved short-term functional change in Australian ALS patients.

In the lagged model, each 1 SD higher IGF-1 level at the prior visit corresponded to ~1.4 fewer points of functional loss over the subsequent visit while accounting for BMI, age, sex, and inter-visit interval. The magnitude remained similar even after several sensitivity analyses. More conservative analysis to account for the modest sample size produced nearly identical point estimates but less precise confidence intervals, indicating our finding was consistent, but the precision remains uncertain and further replication in a larger cohort is needed. A previous cross-sectional study showed that IGF-1 concentrations correlated with disease severity assessed by ALSFRS-R [16]. However, our findings add new insight that IGF-1 is not merely a synchronous correlate of disease severity but is linked to subsequent trajectory that can only be addressed in a longitudinal study design. Because circulating IGF-1 levels vary between individuals, and analytical variability can further confound the findings, several post-data analyses were performed to clarify the stability and likely source of heterogeneity in IGF-1 levels. Repeated IGF-1 measurements were relatively stable within individuals (ICC = 0.69), and the between-participant component, rather than short-term deviations from an individual's usual IGF-1 concentration, was associated with subsequent functional change. The lagged coefficient also remained positive in every leave-one-participant-out analysis and stayed close to the full-sample estimate. Together, these findings argue against the result being driven by a single influential participant and suggest that IGF-1 may operate primarily as a relatively stable participant-level marker. The reverse-direction analysis did not support evidence of prior ALSFRS-R in predicting subsequent IGF-1 concentrations, suggesting temporal asymmetry between IGF-1 and functional change in ALS progression, although it was not sufficiently precise to establish directionality or causation.

Mixed-effects modelling further clarified this relationship. In the random-intercept model, we examined whether participants with different starting IGF-1 levels also differed in their ALSFRS-R scores at the start of follow-up, whereas the IGF-1 × time interaction examined whether they subsequently declined at different rates. The IGF-1 × time interaction was statistically significant, indicating that participants with higher baseline IGF-1 showed slower longitudinal decline. However, allowing participants to have individual progression rates substantially improved model fit. In this more flexible and better-fitting model, the association between starting IGF-1 levels and subsequent rates of decline remained positive but was attenuated, estimated less precisely, and was not statistically significant (p = 0.254). The central findings therefore rest on the lagged visit-to-visit analyses rather than on the longitudinal trajectory models. Taken together, higher IGF-1 may be associated with slower average functional decline, but baseline IGF-1 did not clearly account for the substantial heterogeneity in longer-term ALS progression rates.

While previous studies have examined associations between circulating IGF-1 and ALS survival or overall prognosis, the present study extends the field by using repeated biomarker measurements and lagged longitudinal analyses to investigate temporal relationships between IGF-1 and functional decline. The higher IGF-1 levels predicted better preservation of ALSFRS-R scores during the subsequent assessment interval, whereas prior functional status did not predict later IGF-1 levels. This temporal asymmetry, together with the consistency of findings across sensitivity analyses, suggests that circulating IGF-1 may reflect a component of systemic trophic-metabolic reserve that precedes and potentially influences short-term functional trajectories in ALS, rather than simply mirroring disease severity.

IGF-1 Signaling and Motor Neuron Survival

These statistical findings raise the question of which biological mechanism may plausibly link higher circulating IGF-1 to preserved short-term function in ALS. IGF-1 is a pleiotropic neurotrophic and metabolic factor that activates the PI3K-Akt and MAPK signaling pathways, promoting neuronal survival, axonal growth, synaptic maintenance and cellular stress responses [2]. In motor neurons, IGF-1 signaling has been linked to antiapoptotic cascades, stabilizes mitochondrial function, and supported axonal transport [6]. IGF-1-related pathways may also contribute to non-cell autonomous mechanisms in ALS. For example, modulation of IGF1R-mTOR pathway in mutant SOD1 astrocytes has been reported to be protective against motor neuron toxicity [20]. Moreover, IGF-1 can delay motor neuron degeneration and prolong survival in some ALS mouse models [5], although findings have not been consistent across all experimental studies [13].

The prospective association observed here is biologically coherent with this framework. Higher circulating IGF-1 may reflect more preserved trophic signaling capacity, greater systemic anabolic reserve, or more intact downstream pathway responsiveness. Even modest preservation of these signaling mechanisms could translate into measurable shortterm differences in functional decline, particularly in a disease characterized by cumulative motor neuron loss.

Exploratory supplementary analyses suggested a possible nonlinear pattern, with visually greater subsequent decline at lower IGF-1 concentrations and a possible flattening of the association at higher concentrations. However, the overall spline test was not statistically significant, and the tertile, threshold, and hinge analyses did not identify a robust cutpoint. These findings therefore do not establish either a specific functional form or a clinically meaningful IGF-1 threshold and should be regarded as hypothesis-generating. Larger studies with adequate power are needed to determine whether the association is linear, nonlinear, or characterized by a biologically relevant threshold.

Compensatory Upregulation and Disease Biology

ALS participants in this Australian cohort exhibited numerically higher serum IGF-1 concentrations compared to healthy controls, although the difference was not statistically significant. This finding is consistent with previous studies reporting no clear difference in serum IGF-1 between ALS participants and controls [16, 18, 21]. One possible interpretation is that peripheral IGF-1 may increase as part of a compensatory response to neurodegeneration, muscle loss, or systemic metabolic stress. However, higher peripheral IGF-1 does not necessarily indicate more effective IGF-1 signaling within the CNS.

Receptor-level resistance, altered post-receptor signaling, changes in IGF-binding proteins, or impaired transport across the blood-brain barrier may all limit the functional aspects of circulating IGF-1.

Within the ALS group, relatively higher serum IGF-1 may therefore reflect either more effective trophic compensation for neuronal damage, or a broader marker of preserved systemic metabolic and anabolic capacity. This prospective association between higher IGF-1 and attenuated short-term functional decline, even after adjusting for age, sex, BMI, and prior ALSFRS-R, is consistent with this interpretation. However, the present observational data cannot delineate whether IGF-1 is directly contributing to neuroprotection or instead acting as a marker of preserved systemic physiological resilience.

IGF-1 and Metabolic Reserve

ALS is increasingly recognized as a multisystem disorder involving metabolic alterations, including hypermetabolism, altered glucose utilization, skeletal muscle dysfunction and weight loss [1]. As a regulator of anabolic signaling and skeletal muscle maintenance, IGF-1 may capture aspects of this systemic metabolic state that are not fully reflected by BMI alone. Although BMI was included as a covariate in the longitudinal models, BMI provides only a broad measure of an individual's metabolic status and does not capture muscle mass, endocrine function, or anabolic capacity.

From a resilience perspective, serum IGF-1 may indicate one component of systemic trophic-metabolic reserve. Individuals with more preserved anabolic and metabolic signaling may be better able to withstand the energetic demands imposed by ongoing neurodegeneration and muscle wasting. This aligns with the prospective association between higher IGF-1 and more preserved short-term function, while also recognizing that IGF-1 provided only limited incremental explanatory information in exploratory linear models and did not improve either apparent or participant-grouped cross-validated classification of clinically meaningful decline. Exploratory supplementary analyses were compatible with a possible flattening of the association at higher concentrations but did not statistically establish a nonlinear or plateauing relationship.

Biomarker Positioning

Taken together, these findings position serum IGF-1 as a biologically plausible and temporally coherent marker associated with short-term functional change in ALS, but not as a standalone prognostic biomarker. Higher IGF-1 was prospectively associated with more preserved short-term ALSFRS-R change. However, baseline IGF-1 concentrations did not differ across the observed slow, normal, and fast progression categories, and there was no evidence that the association between lagged IGF-1 and subsequent functional change varied across these groups. IGF-1 also did not improve internal classification of clinically meaningful decline. These findings suggest that circulating IGF-1 may capture a trophic-metabolic signal associated with short-term functional preservation rather than clearly distinguishing broader patterns of disease progression.

IGF-1 may therefore reflect one component of trophic-metabolic resilience rather than the full complexity of ALS progression. This distinction is important because ALS progression reflects multiple converging pathobiological processes, including excitotoxicity, mitochondrial dysfunction, RNA dysregulation, protein aggregation, neuroinflammation, skeletal muscle dysfunction and systemic metabolic stress [22]. A single circulating trophic marker is therefore unlikely to capture the overall disease trajectory.

Implications for Therapeutic Targeting

The findings have implications for therapeutic strategies targeting the IGF-1 pathway. Previous trials of exogenous IGF-1 administration in ALS did not demonstrate clear or consistent clinical benefit, although these trials were considered to have a high risk of bias [14]. The present results do not contradict those trial findings. Rather, they suggest that endogenous circulating IGF-1 may be associated with short-term functional preservation, potentially as a marker of broader trophic-metabolic reserve.

This distinction is important because an observational association with endogenous IGF-1 does not necessarily imply that pharmacologically increasing IGF-1 will slow ALS progression. Circulating IGF-1 may act as a marker of preserved physiological reserve rather than a direct modifiable therapeutic target or outcome measure in ALS. Future studies should therefore examine not only circulating IGF-1 concentrations, but also downstream pathway activation such as Akt phosphorylation, IGF-1 binding protein dynamics, interaction with other metabolic markers, and longitudinal functional outcomes.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Although the parent study had a prespecified sample size for its original objectives, missing measurements and the requirement for complete data reduced the effective sample available for the present longitudinal analyses. The primary lagged and mixed-effects analyses were both based on modest complete case samples (Figure 1). The modest number of participant clusters limited statistical precision: although the primary lagged association was statistically significant using participant-clustered normal-approximation inference and remained directionally consistent in leaveone-participant-out analyses, its confidence interval widened under the more conservative CR2 small-cluster correction. The study also lacked external validation and did not include direct mechanistic measures such as muscle mass, nutritional biomarkers, IGF-binding proteins, or downstream pathway activation. Replication in larger longitudinal cohorts with more comprehensive metabolic and mechanistic profiling is therefore required.

Conclusion

Higher circulating IGF-1 was prospectively associated with more preserved short-term functional change in ALS after adjustment for age, sex, standardized baseline BMI, and inter-visit interval, and the association remained present in a conditional model additionally accounting for prior functional status. However, uncertainty increased under the smallcluster correction, underscoring the need for replication. Mixed-effects trajectory analyses were directionally consistent with the lagged findings, but baseline IGF-1 did not improve the better-fitting random-slope model or clearly account for the substantial betweenparticipant heterogeneity in longer-term progression rates. Exploratory analyses did not establish a nonlinear threshold, demonstrate variation across observed progression categories, or show improved classification of clinically meaningful decline. These findings position circulating IGF-1 as a biologically coherent candidate marker of systemic trophic-metabolic reserve rather than a dominant standalone prognostic biomarker or direct therapeutic target, supporting its further evaluation within larger longitudinal and multimodal biomarker frameworks.
