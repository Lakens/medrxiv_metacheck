Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 24 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

The RetractionWatch Check module compares the reference list against studies in the RetractionWatch database based on the DOI. If a study in the database is found, a reminder is provided that the study was retracted, has an expression of concern, or a correction.

The module requires that the reference has a DOI: the DOI in the paper’s reference list, or one looked up with add_bib_match() before the module runs (see ref_table()). References without a DOI are not checked.

It is possible the authors are already aware that a study was retracted, but the module can’t evaluate this.

The database can be manually updated with the rw_update function. For more information, see https://gitlab.com/crossref/retraction-watch-data.

How Replication Check works

This module checks references and warns for citations of original studies for which replication or reproduction studies exist in the FLoRA database.

The Replication Check module compares the reference list against studies in the FLoRA (FORRT Library of Replication Attempts) database based on the DOI. If a study in the database is found, a reminder is provided that a replication or reproduction of the original study exists, and should be cited. No reminder is given when the paper already cites that replication or reproduction (matched by its DOI).

The module requires that the reference has a DOI: the DOI in the paper’s reference list, or one looked up with add_bib_match() before the module runs (see ref_table()). References without a DOI are not checked.

It is possible the original study was cited for other reasons than the empirical claim tested, or that the replication/reproduction in the FLoRA database is for only one of the studies in the paper, and not the study the authors discuss.

The database can be manually updated with the FLoRA_update() function. For more information, see https://forrt.org/FLoRA/.

Abstract

Shingles (herpes zoster), caused by reactivation of varicella zoster virus (VZV), affects about one third of people during their lifetime, and host genetics contribute to susceptibility. We performed a genome-wide association study (GWAS) meta-analysis across five cohorts comprising 70,216 cases and 1,646,257 controls of European ancestry. We identified eight genome-wide significant loci, replicating associations in the HLA region and near IFNA21 and identifying novel associations near BACH2, IFNAR2, RHOBTB1, KPNB1 and CPN1 and at the IGH locus. HLA fine-mapping identified eight independent allele signals, six at HLA-B, with the strongest protective association for HLA-B*44:02/B*44:27. Colocalization linked the risk alleles to lower expression of IFNAR2 in T cells, NK cells and skin, BACH2 in CD4⁺ T cells, and IGHG1 and IGHG2 in B cells. These loci implicate MHC class I antigen presentation, type I interferon signaling, T-cell memory and humoral immunity. Shingles was genetically correlated with pain, infectious and immune-mediated diseases. In Mendelian randomization, genetic liability to shingles was associated with multiple sclerosis and systemic lupus erythematosus, but both associations depended on the MHC instrument, suggesting shared HLA effects.

Genetic liability to pain was associated with higher shingles risk. These findings advance understanding of the genetic architecture of VZV reactivation.

Graphical abstract Introduction

Shingles, also known as herpes zoster, is caused by reactivation of varicella zoster virus (VZV). Without vaccination, about one third of people develop shingles during their lifetime 1, 2 . Vaccination reduces the risk by 50-90%, depending on the vaccine 3, 4 , but shingles remains a major public health burden, partly because vaccine uptake among older adults is low 5.

Primary VZV infection causes chickenpox, usually in childhood, after which the virus establishes lifelong latency in sensory ganglia. Upon reactivation, the virus travels along sensory nerves to the skin and causes a painful, one-sided blistering rash, often preceded by local burning pain, fever, headache and fatigue 6, 7 . The rash usually resolves within weeks, but about 18% of adult patients develop postherpetic neuralgia, defined as pain persisting for more than 90 days 1, 8 . Reactivation in the ophthalmic division of the trigeminal nerve can cause herpes zoster ophthalmicus, which can lead to vision loss 9.

The main risk factors for shingles are older age and weakened immunity, including chemotherapy, autoimmune disease, HIV infection and long-term corticosteroid use, although in many patients no cause is found 10 . Latent VZV is controlled mainly by virus-specific T cells, and the decline of this response with age is thought to allow reactivation 11.

Shingles has also been associated with later disease. Epidemiological studies report a nearly 30% higher risk of cardiovascular events, such as stroke and myocardial infarction, after shingles 12 , as well as associations with Parkinson's disease and vascular dementia 13 . For allcause dementia, the evidence is inconsistent 14, 15 . Whether these associations reflect shared susceptibility or effects of the infection itself is unclear.

Family studies suggest that genetic factors contribute to shingles risk 16, 17 , and genetic studies have identified associations in the HLA region [18] [19] [20] . The largest GWAS to date, with 16,711 cases, identified several signals near HLA-A and HLA-B and an association 500 kb upstream of IFNA21 19 . Beyond these loci, the genetic architecture of shingles is largely unknown, including which immune pathways contribute to susceptibility and whether shingles shares genetic risk with the diseases associated with it.

Here, we performed a GWAS meta-analysis of shingles across five cohorts (FinnGen, the Estonian Biobank, UK Biobank, the Million Veteran Program (MVP) and All of Us), totaling 70,216 cases and 1,646,257 controls of European ancestry. We characterized the identified loci using HLA fine-mapping, statistical fine-mapping, colocalization with gene expression and gene set analysis. To study the relationships between shingles and other diseases, we performed a phenotype-wide association study (CodeWAS), genetic correlation analysis, an observational analysis of disease risk after shingles, and bidirectional two-sample Mendelian randomization.

Results

Large-scale GWAS meta-analysis identifies eight genome-wide significant loci for shingles susceptibility

To understand the role of genetics in susceptibility to shingles, we performed a meta-analysis of FinnGen (27,818 cases and 471,937 controls), the Estonian Biobank (13,128 cases and 192,920 controls), UK Biobank (14,173 cases and 384,864 controls), the Million Veteran Program (11,696 cases and 428,273 controls) and All of Us (3,401 cases and 168,263 controls) (Supplementary Figures 1 2 3 4 5). We only included participants of European ancestry. With 70,216 cases and 1,646,257 controls and 10,129,537 variants, we identified eight genome-wide significant loci associated with shingles (Figure 1, Table 1, Supplementary Tables S1 and S2, Supplementary Figure 6).

Conditional and joint analysis with COJO identified one independent signal at each of the seven loci outside the MHC (Supplementary Table S1). The lead variants were located in or near HLA-B (rs1093, OR = 0.84, P = 2.9 × 10 -108 ), TMEM121 in the immunoglobulin heavy chain (IGH) locus (rs4774042, OR = 0.94, P = 3.7 × 10 -22 ), IFNA21 (rs7048266, OR = 0.95, P = 1.2 × 10 -19 ), BACH2 (rs11757155, OR = 1.05, P = 2.4 × 10 -17 ), RHOBTB1 (rs34812402, OR = 1.04, P = 7.9 × 10 -12 ), IFNAR2 (rs9975738, OR = 1.04, P = 4.2 × 10 -10 ), KPNB1 (rs4794048, OR = 0.97, P = 9.7 × 10 -9 ) and CPN1 (rs11599750, OR = 0.97, P = 3.9 × 10 -8 ). The direction of effect was consistent across all cohorts at every locus (Figure 1b). Although the SNP-based heritability of shingles was low (h² = 0.030 on the liability scale, SE = 0.003, assuming a lifetime prevalence of 30%, Supplementary Table S3), consistent with a large contribution of non-genetic factors such as age-related decline in VZV-specific immunity, the identified loci pointed to specific immune mechanisms, which we characterized with fine-mapping, colocalization and enrichment analyses.

Fine-mapping with SuSiE identified six 95% credible sets in six of the eight LD blocks outside the MHC (115 variants, Supplementary Table S4). The two credible sets at RHOBTB1 were in adjacent LD blocks and their top variants were in LD (r² = 0.73), consistent with the single COJO signal. The lead variant had the highest posterior inclusion probability (PIP) in its credible set at IGH (rs4774042, PIP = 0.72), IFNAR2 (rs9975738, PIP = 0.34) and RHOBTB1 (rs34812402, PIP = 0.32), whereas no variant had a PIP above 0.10 in the credible sets at BACH2 and IFNA21. No credible set was identified at the KPNB1 and CPN1 loci.

The strongest signal was located in the HLA-B region, consistent with the well-established role of HLA class I variation in VZV immunity and CD8+ T cell-mediated control of viral reactivation 18, 19, 21 . Signals near IFNA21 and IFNAR2 highlight the importance of type I interferon signaling in shingles susceptibility. The BACH2 signal suggests a role for T-cell regulation and memory, and the signal at the IGH locus points to antibody-mediated immunity.

Shingles risk increases with age (median age at first diagnosis in FinnGen, 66.9 years). Because the controls in our main analysis were population-based and included younger individuals, some of them may still develop shingles later in life, which could dilute the associations. Zoster vaccination similarly prevents shingles in individuals who might otherwise have become cases, and immunocompromise is a strong risk factor for shingles that could contribute to the genetic associations. To assess the influence of these factors, we repeated the GWAS in FinnGen restricting controls to individuals followed up to at least 70 years of age (27,818 cases and 187,102 controls), additionally excluding zoster-vaccinated controls (27,818 cases and 183,909 controls), and excluding cases and controls with severe immunocompromise (21,523 cases and 410,605 controls, Supplementary Table S5). The effects of the eight lead variants were consistent with the main FinnGen analysis in all three analyses. When their effect sizes were plotted against those from the main analysis, the regression slopes were 0.96-1.02, where a slope of 1 indicates identical effects (Supplementary Table S5). All lead variants remained associated (P ≤ 7.4 × 10 -3 ) despite the smaller sample sizes. Given the extensive LD in the region, we performed HLA fine-mapping in FinnGen using imputed HLA allele dosages (27,818 cases and 471,937 controls), where rs1093 had OR = 0.81 (P = 1.9 × 10 -49 ). Of the 98 HLA alleles tested, 33 (17 protective and 16 risk alleles) were associated with shingles at the Bonferroni-corrected threshold (P < 5.1 × 10 -4 , Supplementary Table S6). Stepwise conditional analysis identified nine Bonferroni-significant independent alleles: B*44:02, C*02:02, B*35:01, B*18:01, B*44:27, B*15:01, DRB3*02:02, B*57:01 and B*27:05 (Supplementary Table S6). Discussion

In this meta-analysis of 70,216 shingles cases and 1,646,257 controls, we identified eight genome-wide significant loci. Together with fine-mapping and colocalization, these loci implicate four parts of immunity in shingles susceptibility: antigen presentation by HLA class I molecules, type I interferon signaling, T-cell regulation and memory, and antibody-mediated immunity.

The strongest association was in the MHC, where HLA fine-mapping identified eight independent signals: six at HLA-B, one at HLA-C (C*02:02) and one in class II (DRB3*02:02). Most were protective, led by HLA-B*44:02/B*44:27, whereas B*57:01 and B*27:05 increased risk. Four of the signals replicated in the Estonian Biobank. HLA associations with shingles have been reported before [18] [19] [20] and are consistent with our findings. Of our eight signals, the protective B*44:02 and the risk allele B*57:01 were also reported by Tian et al. 19 , and DRB3*02:02 was protective in UK Biobank 20.

HLA class I molecules present viral peptides to CD8⁺ T cells, a pathway that VZV actively suppresses 22 , and the allele-specific effects at HLA-B likely reflect differences in the presentation of VZV peptides. However, VZV-specific CD8⁺ T cells are rare in latently infected trigeminal ganglia 23 , suggesting a limited role in maintaining latency, whereas CD8⁺ and CD4⁺ T cells infiltrate ganglia during reactivation 21 . CD8⁺ T-cell responses through HLA class I may therefore matter mainly after the virus reactivates, rather than in keeping it latent. The class II signal at DRB3*02:02 points to CD4⁺ T cells. In a large vaccine trial, stronger VZV-specific cellmediated immunity at shingles onset was associated with milder disease and less postherpetic neuralgia 24.

Two loci implicate type I interferon signaling. The IFNA21 signal replicates the association reported by Tian et al. 19 , whose lead variant rs7047299 is in strong LD with our lead variant rs7048266 (r² = 0.98 in 1000 Genomes EUR), with a consistent direction of effect. The mechanism at this locus remains unclear. The signal lies in the type I interferon gene cluster, enhancer-gene predictions linked it to IFNW1, and colocalization suggested a shared variant with KLHL9 expression in only one B cell dataset. In contrast, the IFNAR2 locus has a clear candidate gene. IFNAR2 encodes the high-affinity subunit of the type I interferon receptor, through which type I interferons restrict VZV replication 25 . The shingles risk allele was associated with lower IFNAR2 expression in nine datasets, including T cells, NK cells, neutrophils, whole blood and skin, whereas eQTLs of the neighboring receptor genes IFNAR1, IFNGR2 and IL10RB were driven by different variants. Complete IFNAR2 deficiency causes lifethreatening disease after live-attenuated viral vaccination 26 , and herpes simplex virus 1 (HSV-1), a close relative of VZV, blocks type I interferon signaling 27 . Our findings suggest that common variation in interferon receptor expression also affects control of latent VZV. Together, the IFNA21 and IFNAR2 loci implicate both interferon production and receptor signaling. At BACH2, the risk allele was associated with lower BACH2 expression in CD4⁺ T cells and memory regulatory T cells. BACH2 is a transcription factor that helps T cells become long-lived memory cells rather than short-lived effector cells 28 , and it also stabilizes regulatory T cells 29 . In humans, loss of one functional copy of BACH2 causes an immunodeficiency with impaired lymphocyte maturation and recurrent infections 30 . Lower BACH2 expression could therefore weaken VZV-specific memory T cells, which become less functional with age 31 . Common variants at BACH2 have also been associated with several autoimmune diseases 30 , which is of interest given the epidemiological and genetic links between shingles and SLE observed here.

At the TMEM121 (IGH) locus, the lead variant lies within the immunoglobulin heavy chain constant region, and the signal colocalized with the expression of immunoglobulin G constant genes (IGHG1, IGHG2, IGHG3 and the pseudogene IGHGP) rather than TMEM121, pointing to humoral immunity. The lead variant was in strong LD (r² = 0.89) with the lead variant for serum IgG1 levels in a recent GWAS (rs74093865 32 ). This variant tags G1m(f), the most common IgG1 allotype. The shingles risk allele tagged the allele associated with lower IgG1 levels, consistent with the lower IGHG1 expression observed here. In the same study, this haplotype was associated with higher IgG3, matching the opposite directions of effect we observed for IGHG1 and IGHG3, so the locus may shift the IgG subclass balance rather than total IgG. IgG1 is the dominant VZV-specific IgG subclass during reactivation 33 , and low VZV-specific IgG1 together with absent IgG3 has been proposed to explain shingles susceptibility in immunodeficient patients 34.

To our knowledge, the KPNB1, CPN1 and RHOBTB1 loci have not previously been associated with shingles, and their causal genes remain unresolved. The KPNB1 locus contains three genes with roles in antiviral immunity. KPNB1 encodes importin-β1, which carries the interferonactivated transcription factor STAT1 into the nucleus 35 and is used by herpes simplex virus 1 (HSV-1) to deliver its genome 36 . TBKBP1 encodes an adaptor of the kinase TBK1, which induces type I interferons 37 , and TBX21 encodes T-bet, which is required for Th1 and NK cell responses 38 . Colocalization supported only KPNB1, in a single skin dataset. At the CPN1 locus, the lead variant is intronic to CPN1, which encodes a complement-inactivating enzyme 39 , but the signal colocalized with DNMBP expression in spleen. The RHOBTB1 signal did not colocalize with any gene.

At the pathway level, the analyses consistently pointed to immune cells. In sLDSC, immune annotations covered 23% of variants but explained 95% of the SNP heritability (4.1-fold enrichment), with NK cells and T cells as the top cell types. In MAGMA, spleen and lymphocyte expression ranked highest, and spleen became significant when the MHC was included. Among gene sets, B cell activation was significant, driven by BACH2, IFNAR2 and TBX21.

Beyond the genetic loci, shingles was linked to a wide range of other diseases. In FinnGen, almost 800 diagnosis codes were more common in shingles cases than in matched controls, three quarters of those tested replicated in the Estonian Biobank, and shingles was positively genetically correlated with 290 FinnGen endpoints. The clinical and genetic associations largely agreed, as nearly all genetically correlated endpoints were also more common in shingles cases.

Part of this broad pattern likely reflects factors that affect many diagnoses at once, such as healthcare use, general health and immune function. The negative control outcome, foot or ankle fracture, was 15% more common in shingles cases, which indicates the size of such confounding in the observational analyses.

Pain conditions had the strongest genetic correlations with shingles, and in reverse MR, with the FinnGen pain endpoint as the exposure and shingles as the outcome, genetic liability to pain was associated with a higher risk of shingles. Shingles often begins with pain in the affected dermatome before the rash appears, and pain is also its most common complication.

The shared genetic basis could reflect differences in pain sensitivity or shared biology in sensory neurons, where VZV establishes latency. The associations with genetic liability to seborrhoeic keratosis and, inversely, with thyroid cancer have no clear explanation and need replication.

Among the twelve outcomes selected for Mendelian randomization, the strongest observational associations were with immune-mediated diseases and herpes simplex. In this analysis, which compared shingles cases with matched controls before and after the shingles diagnosis, SLE and multiple sclerosis were more common in cases in both periods. In forward MR, with shingles as the exposure, genetic liability to shingles was associated with a higher risk of both diseases, but these associations were driven by the MHC instrument and were not significant without it. Because HLA alleles strongly affect autoimmune diseases, this likely reflects shared HLA effects rather than a causal effect of shingles. The observational associations, already present before shingles, are more consistent with these diseases or their treatment increasing the risk of shingles, as reported for SLE 40, 41 and for several multiple sclerosis therapies 42 . Herpes simplex had the highest genetic correlation with shingles and a strong observational association, consistent with shared host control of latent alphaherpesviruses. Misclassification between zosteriform herpes simplex and shingles may also contribute, particularly shortly after shingles.

Shingles has been associated with an increased risk of stroke and myocardial infarction, particularly shortly after the infection 12 . We observed the same pattern. New-onset stroke and myocardial infarction were more common within 90 days after shingles, whereas over longer periods the associations were of similar size to that of the negative control. MR did not support a causal effect of genetic liability to shingles on stroke or myocardial infarction. Together, these results are consistent with a transient effect of the acute infection, which MR of lifelong genetic liability cannot capture, rather than a long-term effect of susceptibility to shingles. Bell's palsy was very common shortly after shingles, which likely reflects facial palsy caused by VZV itself (Ramsay Hunt syndrome) being recorded as Bell's palsy. Dementia and Alzheimer's disease were less common in shingles cases, both before and after shingles, and MR did not support a causal effect of shingles on dementia. This inverse association may reflect under-diagnosis of shingles in people with dementia, who may be less able to report symptoms, and illustrates why observational studies of shingles and dementia have given inconsistent results 14, 15.

Our study has limitations. First, all cohorts were of European ancestry, and the findings need to be tested in other populations. Second, shingles was defined from diagnosis codes in health registries and electronic health records. Not all patients seek care, data sources and coding practices differed between cohorts, and misclassification would bias the associations towards the null. Registry-recorded cases made up only 2-6% of each cohort, much less than the assumed lifetime prevalence of 30%, likely because many participants have not yet reached the ages of highest risk and health records cover only part of each participant's life. The liabilityscale heritability estimates (Supplementary Table S3) depend on prevalence assumptions and need to be interpreted with caution. Third, conditional analysis and SuSiE fine-mapping used 1000 Genomes European samples as the LD reference, which does not include Finns, although FinnGen contributed 40% of the cases, and LD differences may affect these results. Fourth, colocalization assumed a single causal variant per signal, so genes at loci with several causal variants may have been missed. Fifth, sequential HLA fine-mapping was performed in FinnGen only, at two-field resolution. In the Estonian Biobank, HLA alleles were imputed with a different method and tested one at a time, without sequential conditioning. Sixth, the CodeWAS compared lifetime diagnoses with matching only on age and sex, so its associations describe cooccurrence and are subject to confounding. Finally, MR had only four instruments for shingles, three outside the MHC, which limits power and the ability to separate causal effects from pleiotropy.

In conclusion, we identified eight loci for shingles, including six not previously reported, and our results implicate HLA class I antigen presentation, type I interferon signaling, T-cell memory and antibody-mediated immunity in the control of VZV reactivation. Shingles shares clinical and genetic links with pain, infectious and immune-mediated diseases, but MR did not support causal effects of genetic liability to shingles on cardiovascular, neurodegenerative or autoimmune diseases beyond shared HLA effects. These findings improve understanding of host control of VZV latency and provide candidate pathways for further functional studies.

Cohorts

The FinnGen study is a large-scale genomics initiative that has analyzed over 500,000 Finnish biobank samples and correlated genetic variation with health data to understand disease mechanisms and predispositions 43 . The project is a collaboration between research organizations and biobanks within Finland and international industry partners. Shingles cases were identified from longitudinal health registry data using ICD-10 code B02 and ICD-8 and ICD-9 code 053 from inpatient, outpatient and primary care registries. The analysis included 27,818 cases and 471,937 controls.

The Estonian Biobank (EstBB) is a large population-based biobank comprising 212,955 participants 44 . Electronic health record data, including ICD-10 diagnostic codes, are obtained through regular linkage with the National Health Insurance Fund and other relevant databases, with the majority of records available from 2004 onwards 45 . Shingles cases were identified using ICD10 code B02, using data freeze 2026v02.

The UK Biobank (UKB) is a large prospective open-access cohort study that recruited over 500,000 individuals aged 40-69 years between 2006 and 2010. At enrollment, participants provided blood and urine samples for genetic and biochemical analysis, alongside detailed health and lifestyle measures. The Million Veteran Program (MVP) is a large longitudinal cohort study examining how genes, lifestyle, military experiences, and exposures influence health among diverse U.S. Veterans. GWAS

GWAS in FinnGen was conducted with REGENIE v3.3 49 using the FinnGen pipeline for binary traits (https://github.com/FINNGEN/regenie-pipelines), adjusting for age, sex, genotyping chip, batch and the first ten genetic principal components.

Association analysis in the Estonian Biobank was carried out for all variants with an INFO score > 0.6 using the additive model as implemented in REGENIE v4.0 with standard binary trait settings. Sensitivity analyses in FinnGen

To assess the influence of control selection and immunosuppression, we ran three additional GWASes in FinnGen with REGENIE using the same settings as in the main analysis. First, controls were restricted to individuals aged at least 70 years at death or end of follow-up. Second, controls aged at least 70 years who had received a zoster vaccination in the FinnGen vaccination register were additionally excluded. Third, cases and controls with severe immunocompromise recorded at any time were excluded: HIV infection (ICD-10 B20-B24, Z21), hematological malignancy (C81-C96), primary immunodeficiency (D80-D84) or organ or bone marrow transplantation (Z94.0-Z94.4, Z94.8, T86) in hospital or drug reimbursement registers, or purchases of immunosuppressants (ATC L04) or antineoplastic agents (L01, excluding topical fluorouracil, L01BC02). We compared the effects of the eight COJO lead variants with the main FinnGen analysis using the slope of a regression through the origin and the Pearson correlation.

Harmonization and meta-analysis Before meta-analysis, the summary statistics of each cohort were harmonized to the GRCh38 reference genome. The genome build of each file was verified to be GRCh38 and alleles were aligned to the reference genome, strand flips were corrected, and ambiguous palindromic variants (A/T and C/G) were removed unless their strand could be resolved from allele frequencies. Effect sizes were expressed as log odds ratios (beta) for the alternative allele, and each variant was identified by chromosome, position, reference and alternative allele (SNPID, chrom:pos:ref:alt). Variants with minor allele frequency < 0.001 or imputation INFO < 0.3 were excluded. The quality of each harmonized file was checked with QQ plots and genomic inflation factors, by comparing reported P-values with P-values computed from beta and standard error, and by comparing allele frequencies with gnomAD (Supplementary Figures 1 2 3 4 5).

We performed a fixed-effects inverse-variance weighted meta-analysis with METAL 53 (SCHEME STDERR), using SNPID as the marker and no genomic control correction. Heterogeneity between cohorts was assessed with Cochran's Q and the I² statistic. Variants with minor allele frequency below 1% and variants present in only one cohort were excluded. The meta-analysis output was harmonized again against the reference genome, and rsIDs were added from dbSNP (GRCh38). Inflation in the meta-analysis was assessed with the genomic inflation factor (λ = 1.10) and the LD score regression intercept (1.03, ratio 0.23), indicating that the inflation was mainly due to polygenicity (Supplementary Figure 6). Genome-wide significance was set at P < 5 × 10 -8 . For Mendelian randomization, we repeated the meta-analysis without FinnGen (four cohorts, 42,398 cases and 1,174,320 controls). Throughout, odds ratios are reported with 95% confidence intervals in square brackets.

Definition of loci and independent signals Lead variants were identified by LD clumping in PLINK v1.9.0-b.7.11 54 of autosomal variants with minor allele frequency (MAF) ≥ 0.01 (index P < 5 × 10 -8 , secondary P < 1 × 10 -5 , r 2 < 0.1, window 1,000 kb). The LD reference was the European samples of the 1000 Genomes Project Phase 3 (n = 503) in GRCh38 55 , to which variants were matched by position and alleles. The same reference was used for COJO and SuSiE. A locus was defined as ±500 kb around each lead variant, overlapping loci (lead variants less than 1 Mb apart) were merged, and the variant with the lowest P-value was reported as the locus lead variant. Because the long-range LD in the major histocompatibility complex (MHC, chr6:25-34 Mb) cannot be resolved with a reference panel of this size, all lead variants in this region were collapsed into the one with the lowest Pvalue, and the region was studied with HLA fine-mapping instead of COJO and SuSiE.

To identify conditionally independent signals, we performed stepwise conditional and joint analysis with GCTA-COJO v1.94.1 56, 57 in each locus outside the MHC. The input was the metaanalysis summary statistics for variants within the locus boundaries with MAF ≥ 0.01 that were present in the LD reference, with the total sample size (N = 1,716,473) for every variant. Signals were selected at P < 5 × 10 -8 with a collinearity threshold of 0.9, and variants whose allele frequency differed from the LD reference by more than 0.2 were excluded.

SuSiE fine-mapping

Loci outside the MHC were fine-mapped with the Sum of Single Effects (SuSiE) model for summary statistics (susieR v0.14.2 58, 59 ) in the European LD blocks of MacDonald et al. (2022) 60 that contained at least one variant with P < 5 × 10⁻⁸. LD between variants with MAF ≥ 0.01 was computed from the European 1000 Genomes reference with PLINK2 v2.0.0-b.1 54 . We allowed up to ten causal variants per block and used the effective sample size summed over cohorts (267,798). For each signal, the credible set was the smallest set of variants with a 95% probability of containing the causal variant. Agreement between the summary statistics and the reference LD was checked in each block with the λ parameter and outlier detection in susieR 59 (Supplementary Table S4).

HLA fine-mapping

The HLA region is characterized by high genetic diversity and extensive linkage disequilibrium (LD), making it challenging to identify causal variants from GWAS signals alone. To identify specific HLA alleles independently associated with shingles susceptibility, we performed HLA fine-mapping in FinnGen (data release 14) using individual-level imputed HLA allele data. Classical HLA alleles were imputed at two-field resolution with HIBAG using a Finnish reference panel 61 . Allele dosages were extracted with PLINK2.

We included HLA alleles with imputation INFO > 0.5 and allele frequency ≥ 0.5% (98 of 135 alleles). The association between each HLA allele and shingles was tested with logistic regression, adjusting for age at death or end of follow-up, sex and the first ten genetic principal components. Alleles with P < 5.1 × 10 -4 (Bonferroni correction for 98 alleles) were considered associated. To identify independently associated alleles, we performed stepwise conditional analysis, adding the most strongly associated allele as a covariate in each round until no further allele reached nominal P < 0.05. Alleles that reached the Bonferroni threshold in their round were considered independent signals.

To test whether B*44:02 and B*44:27 represent the same signal, we fitted two models. First, to test whether the association extended to other B*44 alleles, we added the summed dosage of all B*44 alleles to a model with B*44:02 and B*44:27. With both alleles in the model, this term measures the effect of the remaining B*44 alleles. Second, to test whether the two alleles had different effects, we fitted their combined dosage together with B*44:27 and the other independent signals, so that the B*44:27 term measured the difference between the two alleles. As their effects did not differ and the other B*44 alleles were not associated, the two alleles were combined into one signal. All independent signals were then fitted together in a joint model with the same covariates. LD between the lead alleles was estimated from dosage correlations.

HLA imputation of the EstBB genotype data was performed using the SNP2HLA tool 62 . The imputation was done for genotype data generated on the Global Screening Arrays v1, v2 and v3. We performed additive logistic regression analysis with the imputed HLA alleles using REGENIE v4.0 with standard binary trait settings. Regression was carried out with adjustment for current age, age², year of birth, sex and five PCs as covariates, using ICD-10-based phenotype data from EstBB 2026v02 freeze. Analyses were performed for all HLA alleles with minor allele frequency ≥ 1% and imputation quality info score ≥ 0.9.

Functional annotation

Each lead variant was annotated with the nearest protein-coding gene and with the most severe consequence on a protein-coding transcript using the Ensembl Variant Effect Predictor (VEP) REST API (Ensembl release 116) 63 . To identify protein-altering variants, we also annotated all variants in LD with the COJO signals and clump leads (r2 ≥ 0.8 within ±500 kb in the European LD reference) with VEP, including SIFT and PolyPhen predictions.

To link the loci to target genes, we looked up variants in the GTEx v10 single-tissue eQTL results through the GTEx Portal API (https://gtexportal.org) and aligned the eQTL effects to the metaanalysis effect allele (Supplementary Table S8). We included the COJO signals, the clump lead variants, and SuSiE credible-set variants with P < 1 × 10⁻⁵ that had a posterior inclusion probability ≥ 0.1 or belonged to a credible set of at most ten variants. This lookup was descriptive, and shared causal variants were tested by colocalization (see below). We also used enhancer-to-gene predictions from ENCODE-rE2G 64 available in the Open Targets Platform 65 , queried through its GraphQL API in November 2026, for the COJO signals, the clump lead variants and their LD proxies (r2 ≥ 0.8 within ±500 kb in 1000 Genomes EUR, PLINK v1.9). Open Targets provides ENCODE-rE2G predictions with a score ≥ 0.6 (Supplementary Table S10).

Colocalization analysis

To determine whether GWAS association signals share a causal variant with expression quantitative trait loci (eQTLs), we performed colocalization analysis with the coloc.abf function of the coloc R package (v5.2.3) 66 . This approach calculates posterior probabilities for five hypotheses: no association in either dataset (H0), association only in the GWAS (H1), association only in the eQTL dataset (H2), association in both datasets driven by different causal variants (H3), or association in both datasets driven by a shared causal variant (H4). We used prior probabilities p1 = p2 = 10 -4 and p12 = 5 × 10 -6 , as recommended by Wallace 67 . A posterior probability for H4 (PP.H4) ≥ 0.8 was used as the threshold for concluding colocalization. We tested the seven independent signals outside the MHC region identified with COJO, as the MHC region was studied with HLA fine-mapping. The GWAS input was the European five-cohort meta-analysis (70,216 cases and 1,646,257 controls), treated as a case-control trait, and eQTL data were treated as quantitative traits. For each signal, all genes with a transcription start site within ±250 kb of the signal were tested, without filtering on eQTL significance, using variants shared between the GWAS and eQTL data in the same window. Tests with fewer than 50 shared variants were excluded. eQTL summary statistics were obtained from the eQTL Catalogue 68 (accessed October 2026). We included 194 datasets from immune cells, whole blood, spleen and EBV-transformed lymphocytes (LCL), including stimulated and infected cell states, and from sun-exposed and non-exposed skin and tibial nerve, because VZV reactivates in sensory ganglia and causes skin lesions. GTEx v8 tissues were included through the eQTL Catalogue. For each gene, we report the dataset with the highest PP.H4 among gene-dataset pairs with an eQTL P < 10 -4 in the region, and all tests are reported in Supplementary Table S9.

Gene-based, gene-set and tissue expression analyses

We performed gene-based, gene-set and tissue expression analyses with MAGMA v1.08 69 in FUMA v1.8.2 70 using the European five-cohort meta-analysis (N = 1,716,473). Variants were mapped to protein-coding genes (Ensembl v102) within 10 kb of the gene boundaries, and gene-level P-values were computed with the European samples of the 1000 Genomes Project Phase 3 as the LD reference. The extended MHC region (chr6:25-34 Mb) was excluded, and as a sensitivity analysis we repeated the analyses including it. Gene-based P-values were Bonferronicorrected for the number of genes tested (19,714). Gene-set analysis was performed for 17,009 curated gene sets and Gene Ontology terms from MSigDB v2023.1. Hs, and tissue expression analysis for GTEx v8 expression in 30 general and 54 specific tissues, conditioned on the average expression across tissues. P-values were Bonferroni-corrected for the number of gene sets or tissues tested.

Tissue and cell type-specific analyses

To identify tissues and cell types relevant for shingles susceptibility, we performed stratified LD score regression (sLDSC) with the summary statistics of the European meta-analysis. sLDSC tests whether SNP heritability is enriched in a genomic annotation, conditional on a baseline model of functional annotations 71 . In the tissue-group analysis, each of the ten tissue groups of Finucane et al. 71 , defined from cell-type-specific histone marks, was added separately to the baseline model (Supplementary Table S11). Significance was assessed with the one-sided Pvalue of the tissue-group coefficient, which tests for enrichment beyond the baseline annotations, with Bonferroni correction for ten tests. In the multitissue analysis (LDSC-SEG) 72 , each annotation was added separately to baseline model v1.1. We tested 489 chromatin-based annotations from the Roadmap Epigenomics and ENCODE projects, defined from peaks of six epigenetic marks (H3K27ac, H3K4me1, H3K4me3, H3K9ac, H3K36me3 and DNase hypersensitivity) 73, 74 , and 205 gene expression-based annotations from GTEx and the Franke lab 75 . P-values of the annotation coefficients were corrected with FDR, and annotations with P FDR < 0.05 were considered significant. Variants in the MHC region were not used in the regressions.

Phenotype-wide association study (CodeWAS)

Diseases co-occurring with shingles were studied in FinnGen with CodeWAS in the Cohort Operations tool, using an age-and sex-matched case-control dataset of 27,818 cases and 201,350 controls. ICD-10 diagnosis codes (4,939 codes) and FinnGen endpoints recorded at any time during follow-up were compared between cases and controls with the chi-square or Fisher's exact test. Because codes were counted over the lifetime, the associations describe cooccurrence and do not indicate temporal order. While matching on age and sex provides partial control for confounding, the analysis does not adjust for additional covariates. Codes with P < 0.05/4,939 = 1.0 × 10⁻⁵ and OR ≥ 1.2 or ≤ 1/1.2 were considered associated.

In the Estonian Biobank, a phenome-wide disease association scan was carried out for herpes zoster using ICD-10 code B02 as a binary phenotype. Associations between herpes zoster and other binary disease phenotypes were tested using logistic regression, adjusting for age at last follow-up or death, age², sex, and the first ten genetic principal components. Analyses were performed using EstBB data freeze 2023v01. Codes associated in FinnGen were considered endpoints: drug purchases, procedures, anthropometric measures and smoking), leaving 334 disease endpoints (Supplementary Table S17).

Exposure and outcome alleles were harmonized with TwoSampleMR. The inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method 80 was the primary estimator, with MR-Egger 81 , weighted median 82 and weighted mode 83 as sensitivity analyses. Heterogeneity was assessed with Cochran's Q, and horizontal pleiotropy with the MR-Egger intercept (nominal P < 0.05). We also performed leaveone-out analyses and MR-PRESSO 84 , and in the forward analysis repeated the IVW analysis without the MHC instrument. P-values were corrected with FDR separately in each direction (12 tests in the forward analysis and 334 tests in the reverse analysis). In the reverse analysis, leave-one-out and MR-PRESSO were run only for exposures with P FDR < 0.05.

Observational associations with the Mendelian randomization outcomes

To compare observational and genetic evidence, we studied the twelve MR outcome traits in FinnGen with a matched case-control design. Each shingles case was matched to up to ten controls exactly on sex and birth year and by nearest neighbor on the first ten genetic principal components with the MatchIt R package 85 . The index date was the first shingles diagnosis of the case and was assigned also to its matched controls, and controls whose follow-up ended before the index date were excluded, leaving 27,724 cases and 244,315 controls. Traits were defined from ICD-10 and ICD-9 codes in the hospital inpatient, outpatient and primary care registers (Supplementary Table S15). For each trait, we defined four exposure windows relative to the index date: ever, before the index date, after the index date (new onset, excluding individuals with the trait before the index date), and 1-90 days after the index date (new onset). Diagnoses recorded on the index date were counted only in the ever window, and new onset refers to the first diagnosis recorded in the registers. Associations were tested with conditional logistic regression on the matched sets, adjusted for the first ten principal components, using the survival R package 86 . Trait-window combinations with fewer than five exposed cases or controls were not analyzed, and P-values were corrected with FDR within each window. Foot or ankle fracture was included as a negative control outcome, as in the MR analysis.

Heritability and genetic correlation

We estimated the SNP-based heritability of shingles with LD score regression (LDSC) 76 . Summary statistics were restricted to HapMap3 variants, and European LD scores from the 1000 Genomes Project were used as reference and regression weights. The sample size was the effective sample size summed over cohorts, and heritability was converted to the liability scale 77 with a sample prevalence of 0.5 and a population lifetime prevalence of 30%. Genetic correlations between shingles and all FinnGen endpoints were estimated with bivariate LDSC 78 with unconstrained intercepts in the FinnGen LDSC pipeline, using the European five-cohort meta-analysis. Further information on FinnGen endpoints is available at https://risteys.finregistry.fi/. P-values were corrected with FDR, and for plotting the endpoints were grouped by ICD-10 chapter, as for CodeWAS.

Mendelian randomization

We tested associations between genetic liability to shingles and other diseases with bidirectional two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) using the TwoSampleMR R package 79 . To avoid sample overlap between exposure and outcome, the shingles summary statistics came from the European meta-analysis without FinnGen (42,398 cases and 1,174,320 controls), and the other traits from FinnGen release 13 endpoints.

In the forward analysis, shingles was the exposure. Instruments were genome-wide significant variants (P < 5 × 10 -8 ) clumped for independence (r 2 < 0.001 within 10,000 kb, European 1000 Genomes reference). Because of the extensive LD and pleiotropy in the MHC region (chr6:25-34 Mb), only the strongest instrument in this region was retained. This gave four instruments, one of which was in the MHC region. We tested twelve pre-specified outcomes selected based on earlier epidemiological evidence and the CodeWAS findings: suspected consequences of shingles (stroke, myocardial infarction, dementia, Alzheimer's disease, multiple sclerosis, Guillain-Barré syndrome, giant cell arteritis, Parkinson's disease and Bell's palsy), diseases with shared or previously reported genetic susceptibility (herpes simplex infection and systemic lupus erythematosus), and fracture of the foot or ankle as a negative control outcome.

In the reverse analysis, shingles was the outcome. Instruments for each FinnGen endpoint were the lead variants from the FinnGen autoreporting (P < 5 × 10⁻⁸, one variant per locus), and endpoints with at least ten instruments were tested as exposures. The autoreporting results did not include variants in the MHC region, so no MHC instruments were used.
