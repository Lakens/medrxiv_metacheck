Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Spatial omics defines tumour microenvironments with high biological resolution but remains difficult to scale across large clinical cohorts. We developed HELIOS (H&E Learning Informed by Omics and Spatial Profiling), which transfers spatial-omicsderived cell identities into routine haematoxylin and eosin histology for interpretable single-cell tumour microenvironment profiling. HELIOS integrates 12.9 million cells from 60 specimens across 13 tissue types and four spatial profiling platforms, spanning eight epithelial, immune, stromal, and vascular populations. The resulting multiclass detector generalised across independent histopathology benchmarks. Applied to colorectal, breast, and lung cancers, HELIOS recapitulated molecular subtype-associated microenvironment states and identified prognostic immune and stromal phenotypes. Tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes were consistently associated with favourable outcomes, whereas neutrophil and fibroblast phenotypes showed cancer-specific associations. HELIOS-derived features remained prognostic after adjustment for clinicopathological and molecular variables. These findings demonstrate that spatial omics can provide scalable biological supervision for extracting clinically relevant tumour microenvironment features from routine H&E, supporting scalable risk stratification and biomarker development across clinical cohorts and retrospective pathology archives.

Introduction

Histopathological assessment of haematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained tissue sections remains central to disease diagnosis, grading, staging, and treatment decision-making. Because H&E slides are generated routinely, they represent a clinically embedded resource for studying tumour morphology and the tumour microenvironment. Digital pathology has enabled AI-based analysis of these specimens at scale, supporting tumour detection, tissue classification, molecular prediction, and prognostic stratification [1] [2] [3] . More recently, pathology foundation models have provided a powerful framework for learning transferable visual representations from large histology datasets [4] [5] [6] . Although these models have improved performance across multiple downstream tasks, their ability to resolve biologically defined cell types remains limited by the quality and scale of available supervision. As a result, AI-derived histology biomarkers may achieve predictive performance while remaining difficult to interpret mechanistically, limiting their translational value.

Cell-level analysis of the tumour microenvironment is clinically important because the abundance and spatial organisation of tumour, immune, and stromal cells shape cancer progression and therapeutic response 7, 8 . Precision oncology has transformed cancer management through molecular biomarkers that guide targeted therapies, such as KRAS, BRAF and EGFR alterations and mismatch repair deficiency/microsatellite instability (dMMR/MSI) status in colorectal cancer and HER2 amplification in breast cancer [9] [10] [11] . However, many patients lack actionable molecular alterations or fall within broad, biologically heterogeneous groups, such as microsatellite-stable (MSS) colorectal cancer and HER2-negative breast cancer, for which additional approaches to treatment selection and prognostic stratification are needed 12 . Moreover, substantial variation in clinical outcomes and therapeutic responses persists even among patients within the same molecular subtype 12 . Characterisation of the tumour microenvironment can therefore provide complementary information beyond genomics 13, 14 . Features such as tumourinfiltrating lymphocytes, tumour-associated macrophages and tumour-stromal architecture have been linked to prognosis and treatment sensitivity across cancer types [15] [16] [17].

However, accurate nuclei detection and classification, and quantitative assessment of cellular abundance and spatial organisation from routine H&E remains challenging across diverse organs, tumour types, and histological contexts. Existing datasets often rely on manual labels derived from morphology alone and are often restricted to a limited range of tissues and cell types [18] [19] . Widely used models such as HoVer-Net trained on Lizard 20 , have advanced this field, but the performance are constrained by the quality and scope of manual annotations, which is not only labour-intensive but also limited by inter-observer variability. Co-registration of H&E with immunohistochemistry or multiplex immunofluorescence has produced more reliable ground truth labels and improved immune-cell classification 21, 22 , but these approaches usually assay selected markers, require additional tissue processing, and do not comprehensively capture the cellular complexity of the tumour microenvironment.

The development of high-resolution spatial transcriptomics provides an opportunity to link molecular cell identity with preserved tissue architecture 23, 24 . In cancer, these data offer the opportunity to characterise the actual tumour ecosystem and give insight into tumour biology beyond genomics. Direct prediction of spatial gene expression from H&E, sometimes termed virtual spatial transcriptomics, is promising 25, 26 but challenging because transcriptomic states are not always fully encoded in morphology. Rather than treating gene expression as the prediction target, we instead use single-cell spatial transcriptomics as a high-fidelity supervision: gene profiles define cell identities in the matched H&E for training AI models to recover the corresponding cell classes from routine histology.

Here, we present HELIOS, H&E Learning Informed by Omics and Spatial Profiling, a cancer-focused, biology-grounded atlas for AI-based nuclei detection and classification in routine pathology. HELIOS links high-resolution spatial transcriptomics with matched H&E morphology across 60 samples, 13 human tissue types, and eight cell types spanning the major epithelial, immune, and stromal/vascular compartments, including epithelial cells, lymphocytes, fibroblasts, macrophages, vascular-associated cells (endothelial cells and pericytes), plasma cells, smooth-muscle cells, and neutrophils. This taxonomy was chosen to cover recurrent, biologically meaningful tumour microenvironment cell types while being visually learnable from H&E. We then use HELIOS to train an object detection model that detects and classifies cells directly from H&E images. The model generates whole-slide cellular maps and quantitative measures of tumour-microenvironment composition. Across independent cohorts, HELIOS-derived measures of densities of tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes, tumour-associated macrophages, neutrophils and fibroblast-to-epithelial ratio stratify survival in independent cohorts across lung, breast, and colorectal cancers. These AI-derived biomarkers also provided prognostic information beyond conventional tumour grading and established molecular subgroups. To support translation, HELIOS has also been deployed through a web-based platform that is undergoing evaluation in clinical settings. Since it outputs visually inspectable cell maps and interpretable cellular biomarkers, and requires only routine H&E images, HELIOS has the potential to support scalable tumour-microenvironment profiling and phenotyping, and integration into existing digital-pathology workflows.

Results

HELIOS: A large-scale multi-organ cell atlas for digital pathology

Figure 1 summarises the HELIOS construction pipeline and data composition. We developed HELIOS by integrating spatial transcriptomics with co-registered H&E images, enabling molecularly defined cell identities to be mapped onto histological morphology at single-cell resolution, followed by quality control and benchmarking against pathologist-derived manual annotations (Figure 1A). Annotation accuracy increased progressively with label confidence and exceeded 0.85 among cells with confidence scores greater than 0.5. Notably, 90% of all annotated cells met this highconfidence threshold, supporting the overall reliability of the resulting cell atlas.

HELIOS integrates independent spatial omics datasets generated across four platforms, including Xenium V1, Xenium Prime, Visium HD, and Atera WTA (Figure 1B). The atlas comprises primary cancer specimens across breast, colorectal, cervical, lung, ovarian, kidney, pancreatic, prostate, liver and skin tissues. To capture the broad morphological diversity encountered in routine histopathology and improve the generalisability of the model, we also include non-malignant tissues, including tonsillar hyperplasia, normal colorectal tissue, normal (prelaryngeal) lymph node, normal kidney tissue, normal liver tissue, normal skin tissue, and normal heart tissue (Figure 1C). HELIOS contains 12.9 million annotated cells assigned to eight major epithelial, immune, stromal, and vascular populations: epithelial cells, lymphocytes, fibroblasts, macrophages, vascular-associated cells, plasma cells, smooth-muscle cells, and neutrophils (Figure 1D). Cell-type composition varied substantially across organs, reflecting differences in tissue architecture and the organisation of immune, stromal, and vascular compartments (Figure 1E).

HELIOS supports accurate cell detection and classification across organs

To evaluate whether HELIOS could support accurate cell detection and classification directly from routine H&E images, we trained a multi-class YOLOv10-m model 27 and assessed performance using precision, recall, and F1 score. Across the held-out evaluation set, comprising approximately 1.23 million annotated cells, performance was highest for epithelial cells, which achieved a precision of 0.81, recall of 0.88, and F1 score of 0.85 (Figure 2A). Lymphocytes were also robustly detected (precision, 0.74; recall, 0.80; F1, 0.77). Performance varied across cell types, reflecting differences in abundance, morphology, and histological context. Fibroblasts showed intermediate performance (F1, 0.63), followed by vascular-associated cells (F1, 0.58) and plasma cells (F1, 0.52). Smooth-muscle cells, macrophages, and neutrophils were more challenging to resolve, with F1 scores of 0.47, 0.42, and 0.39, respectively. The confusion matrix also showed strong class-specific performance for epithelial cells and lymphocytes, whereas morphologically overlapping populations exhibited increased misclassification among related cell types (Figure 2B). For example, smooth-muscle cells were commonly assigned to fibroblast or vascular classes, while plasma cells were most frequently misclassified as lymphocytes.

We further assessed whether model performance was consistent across organs. Organ-specific analysis showed that epithelial and lymphocyte classification remained relatively robust across multiple tissue types, whereas stromal and myeloid-lineage populations displayed greater variability (Figure 2C). Cell-type-and organ-resolved F1 scores further revealed that classification performance was influenced by the number of available training instances, with highly represented classes generally showing more stable performance across tissue contexts (Figure 2D).

HELIOS-trained model shows strong cross-dataset benchmarking performance

To externally evaluate the performance of the HELIOS-trained model, we benchmarked it against models trained on existing nuclear detection and classification datasets, including MoNuSAC 18 , NuCLS 19 and Lizard 20 , across each held-out test dataset using harmonised cell classes (Figure 3A). As expected, models generally performed best on their corresponding in-domain test sets. Notably, the HELIOS-trained model showed the strongest cross-dataset performance, achieving F1 scores of 0.66 on Lizard, 0.78 on MoNuSAC, and 0.59 on NuCLS. By comparison, models trained on individual external datasets showed larger performance losses when transferred to other cohorts.

Class-resolved benchmarking further demonstrated that the HELIOS-trained model retained the highest F1 scores across all cell compartments, including epithelial, lymphocyte/plasma, macrophage, neutrophil, and stromal categories (Figure 3B). In contrast, a substantial degradation in F1 score was observed for models trained on other datasets when evaluated out of domain.

We further benchmarked HELIOS against two recent pan-tissue cell-analysis frameworks (Figure 3C). HistoPLUS 28 is a pan-cancer model for nuclear detection and classification trained on a curated dataset of 108,722 nuclei spanning 13 cell types, whereas STHELAR 29 is a spatial transcriptomics-informed resource comprising more than 11 million cells, whose annotations were used to fine-tune CellViT for multiclass nuclear segmentation and classification. Because the HistoPLUS test dataset was unavailable and some source samples overlapped between STHELAR and HELIOS, a fully independent head-to-head evaluation was not possible. We therefore evaluated the native HistoPLUS and STHELAR models on the held-out HELIOS test set and additionally retrained the HistoPLUS architecture using the HELIOS train set. HELIOS achieved higher F1 score across every evaluated class, including epithelial cells (0.78), lymphocytes (0.75), macrophages (0.40), neutrophils (0.37), plasma cells (0.75), vascular-associated cells (0.58), fibroblasts (0.63), and smooth-muscle cells (0.63). The gains were particularly pronounced for plasma, vascular, fibroblast, smooth-muscle, and myeloid populations, which are less consistently represented in existing histopathology datasets.

Representative image overlays from three external cohorts obtained from TCGA confirmed that the HELIOS-trained model could localise and classify diverse cell populations in complex histological regions, including tumour-rich, immune-infiltrated, and stromal compartments (Figure 3D).

HELIOS reveals conserved and cancer-specific prognostic immune states

We next assessed whether HELIOS-derived immune-cell measurements captured clinically relevant tumour microenvironment states across colorectal, breast, and lung cancers. The HELIOS model was applied to diagnostic H&E-stained whole-slide images from TCGA-COAD/READ, TCGA-BRCA, and TCGA-LUAD/LUSC to quantify tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs), tumour-associated macrophages (TAMs), and tumour-associated neutrophils (TANs) within tumour-associated regions. Progression-free survival (PFS) and disease-specific survival (DSS) were then compared between high-and low-density subgroups. Representative images illustrated marked variation between immune-cold tumours, characterised by sparse immune-cell infiltration, and immune-hot tumours containing dense lymphocytic and myeloid infiltrates (Figure 4A), together with the distributions of immune-cell densities across tumour types (Figure 4B). Across all three cancer groups, high TIL density was consistently associated with favourable clinical outcome (Figure 4C). In colorectal cancer, TIL-high patients were associated with prolonged progression-free survival (PFS; HR 0.55, 95% CI 0.35-0.87; p = 0.0098) and disease-specific survival (DSS; HR 0.47, 95% CI 0.23-0.98; p = 0.0384). Similarly, in breast cancer, high TIL density was associated with improved PFS (HR 0.58, 95% CI 0.41-0.82; p = 0.0018) and DSS (HR 0.57, 95% CI 0.36-0.91; p = 0.0167). TIL density also stratified outcome in the combined LUAD/LUSC cohort, with TIL-high patients showing longer PFS (HR 0.80, 95% CI 0.67-0.96; p = 0.0186) and DSS (HR 0.64, 95% CI 0.48-0.85; p = 0.0023).

In contrast, associations between TAM density and outcome were generally adverse (Figure 4D). In colorectal cancer, TAM-high patients showed directionally shorter PFS and DSS, although neither association reached statistical significance (PFS: HR 1.33, 95% CI 0.90-1.95; p = 0.1447; DSS: HR 1.32, 95% CI 0.74-2.33; p = 0.3401). Similar trends were observed in breast cancer, including a stronger association with shorter DSS that approached statistical significance (PFS: HR 1.27, 95% CI 0.87-1.87; p = 0.2137; DSS: HR 1.63, 95% CI 0.95-2.79; p = 0.0742). In LUAD/LUSC, TAM density was not associated with PFS but was significantly associated with shorter DSS (HR 1.47, 95% CI 1.05-2.07; p = 0.0250).

TAN density showed cancer-specific prognostic effects (Figure 4E). In colorectal cancer, high TAN were associated with substantially longer PFS (HR 0.51, 95% CI 0.33-0.79; p = 0.0018), with a similar but non-significant association for DSS (HR 0.58, 95% CI 0.31-1.08; p = 0.0810). High TAN density was also weakly associated with favourable PFS in breast cancer (HR 0.69, 95% CI 0.48-1.00; p = 0.0490), but did not stratify DSS. Conversely, TAN-high lung cancers showed directionally poorer outcomes, including shorter PFS (HR 1.18, 95% CI 0.95-1.47; p = 0.1331) and DSS (HR 1.39, 95% CI 0.96-2.01; p = 0.0844).

HELIOS links histological immune states to molecular tumour subtypes

We further investigated whether HELIOS-derived immune phenotypes reflected molecularly defined tumour states (Figure 5). In colorectal cancer, microsatellite instability-high (MSI) tumours contained significantly higher TAM (p = 0.0003), TAN (p < 0.0001), and overall immune-cell densities (p = 0.0004) than microsatellitestable tumours (MSS), whereas TIL density did not differ significantly (p = 0.340; Figure 5A). Within MSS colorectal tumours, high TIL density was associated with longer PFS (HR 0.56, 95% CI 0.34-0.92; p = 0.0212), as was high TAN density (HR 0.50, 95% CI 0.31-0.80; p = 0.0040; Figure 5B). The corresponding DSS associations were directionally favourable but did not reach statistical significance.

Breast cancers also displayed marked subtype-specific immune landscapes. Among clinically defined receptor groups, HR-negative/HER2-positive and triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) showed substantially higher TIL and overall immune-cell densities than HR-positive tumours (both p < 0.0001; Figure 5C). TAM density also differed between groups (p = 0.002), with higher values in HR-negative/HER2positive and triple-negative disease, while TAN density was highest in HRnegative/HER2-positive tumours (p < 0.0001). Consistent patterns were observed across PAM50 subtypes (Figure 5C). HER2-enriched and basal-like tumours were lymphocyte-rich relative to luminal tumours, whereas basal-like cancers showed the highest TAM and overall immune-cell densities. TAN density was also elevated in basal-like disease. Despite their high overall immune content, TIL density further reveals prognostic value within TNBC. TIL-high tumours in this subgroup were associated with longer PFS (HR 0.44, 95% CI 0.21-0.89; p = 0.0227) and DSS (HR 0.32, 95% CI 0.13-0.80; p = 0.0142; Figure 5D).

Finally, we examined HELIOS-derived immune composition across molecularly defined KRAS groups in LUAD (Figure 5E). Global differences in TIL, TAM, TAN, and overall immune-cell density did not reach statistical significance, although exploratory patterns were apparent. KRAS/TP53-mutant tumours showed numerically higher TIL, TAM, and overall immune-cell densities, whereas KRAS/STK11-mutant tumours showed the highest median TAN density.

HELIOS identifies cancer-associated fibroblast-rich stromal states associated with adverse outcomes

We next evaluated whether HELIOS could quantify fibroblast-to-epithelial ratio (FER) and identify clinically relevant stromal phenotypes from routine H&E images. Wholeslide inference revealed pronounced spatial heterogeneity in fibroblast abundance, ranging from samples with limited fibroblast component to fibroblast-rich samples (Figure 6A).

A high FER was most strongly associated with adverse outcomes in breast cancer (Figure 6B). Patients with FER-high tumours had significantly shorter progressionfree survival (PFS; HR 1.78, 95% CI 1.26-2.52; p = 0.0009) and disease-specific survival (DSS; HR 1.77, 95% CI 1.12-2.80; p = 0.0137). In colorectal cancer, FERhigh tumours showed a trend towards shorter PFS (HR 1.63, 95% CI 0.97-2.75; p = 0.0609), whereas no association was observed for DSS (HR 0.89, 95% CI 0.35-2.23; p = 0.7975). In the combined LUAD/LUSC cohort, high FER was directionally associated with shorter PFS (HR 1.16, 95% CI 0.96-1.40; p = 0.1175) and DSS (HR 1.15, 95% CI 0.85-1.54; p = 0.3722), although neither association reached statistical significance.

HELIOS-derived cellular features provide prognostic information beyond clinicopathological variables

To assess whether HELIOS-derived immune and stromal features provided prognostic information independent of established clinicopathological factors, multivariable Cox models were constructed separately for colorectal, breast, and lung cancers, incorporating age, tumour stage, cohort-specific molecular or histological variables, and HELIOS-derived TIL, TAM, TAN densities and FER (Figure 6C).

In colorectal cancer, TIL density remained independently associated with longer progression-free survival (PFS; HR 0.58, 95% CI 0.35-0.96; p = 0.033) and diseasespecific survival (DSS; HR 0.42, 95% CI 0.19-0.93; p = 0.032) after adjustment for stage, KRAS status, and MSI phenotype. High TAN density was also independently associated with longer PFS (HR 0.59, 95% CI 0.35-0.99; p = 0.044), whereas high TAM density was associated with shorter PFS (HR 1.58, 95% CI 1.02-2.45; p = 0.040). FER was not independently associated with either endpoint.

In breast cancer, high TIL density remained independently associated with longer PFS (HR 0.67, 95% CI 0.50-0.89; p = 0.006), whereas high CAF density was associated with shorter PFS (HR 1.37, 95% CI 1.02-1.83; p = 0.034), after adjustment for age, stage, and clinically defined subgroups. TIL density showed a similar favourable association with DSS that approached statistical significance (HR 0.73, 95% CI 0.53-1.01; p = 0.058), whereas the other HELIOS-derived features were not independently associated with DSS.

In the LUAD/LUSC cohort, TIL density was independently associated with longer PFS (HR 0.79, 95% CI 0.64-0.97; p = 0.025) and DSS (HR 0.61, 95% CI 0.43-0.85; p = 0.003). By contrast, TAN density was associated with shorter PFS (HR 1.35, 95% CI 1.03-1.78; p = 0.032) and DSS (HR 1.82, 95% CI 1.12-2.96; p = 0.015). FER was also independently associated with shorter PFS (HR 1.29, 95% CI 1.05-1.58; p = 0.015), but not DSS.

We further evaluated whether adding HELIOS-derived cellular features improved model likelihood and test C-index beyond clinicopathological variables alone (Figure 5C). In colorectal cancer, inclusion of TIL, TAM, TAN densities and FER significantly improved model fit for both PFS (likelihood-ratio test, χ² = 14.603; p = 0.006) and DSS (χ² = 9.687; p = 0.046). The test C-index increased from 0.686 to 0.691 for PFS and from 0.785 to 0.798 for DSS. In breast cancer, HELIOS-derived features significantly improved the PFS model (χ² = 15.290; p = 0.004), with the test C-index increasing from 0.700 to 0.743. The corresponding improvement for DSS was not statistically significant (χ² = 6.232; p = 0.182), although the test C-index increased from 0.756 to 0.772. In LUAD/LUSC, the addition of HELIOS features improved model fit for both PFS (χ² = 14.692; p = 0.005) and DSS (χ² = 16.971; p = 0.002), with test C-indices increasing from 0.605 to 0.610 and from 0.644 to 0.655, respectively.

Discussion

HELIOS was developed to test the hypothesis that clinically meaningful cellular phenotypes can be captured from routine histology by AI at scale, and spatial omics can provide sufficiently scalable and biologically grounded supervision. In this study, the findings support this premise at three levels. First, molecularly assigned cell identities could be transferred to matched H&E morphology across multiple organs and spatial profiling technologies. Second, models trained using these annotations retained performance across external histopathology datasets with different tissue compositions and annotation schemes. Third, the resulting cellular measurements captured molecularly and clinically relevant tumour microenvironment states in independent patient cohorts. HELIOS therefore extends cell identification using spatial omics beyond the tissues directly profiled: molecular information derived from a comparatively limited set of deeply characterised samples can be propagated to substantially larger collections of routine H&E slides. This strategy is conceptually distinct from predicting visual-omics from H&E-stained images. Although recent visual-omics models demonstrate that selected transcriptional programmes can be inferred from tissue morphology 21, 22 , many molecular states are transient, context-dependent, or only weakly encoded in H&E appearance. HELIOS instead learned from spatial transcriptomics to define cellular identity during model development and restricts the downstream prediction task to phenotypes that are sufficiently represented by morphology. This approach provides a practical balance between biological resolution and clinical scalability: spatial omics supplies the molecular ground truth, whereas routine histology provides the substrate for broad deployment.

Classification performance nevertheless varied across cellular compartments, revealing both the strengths and current limits of morphology-based cell recognition. Epithelial cells and lymphocytes were detected most accurately, likely because they have comparatively distinctive morphological features and were well represented in the training data. By contrast, myeloid and stromal populations showed greater confusion, consistent with their overlapping nuclear and cytoplasmic appearances and the biological continuity between related cell states. Similar limitations were observed in the comparator models, suggesting that this challenge is not specific to HELIOS but reflects a broader constraint of cell classification from H&E morphology alone. Despite this, HELIOS showed the strongest overall performance across the evaluated datasets and cell classes, including comparatively underrepresented immune and stromal populations. These findings indicate that the scale, organ diversity, and spatial omics-informed supervision of HELIOS improve generalisability, while also highlighting the need for hierarchical classification, uncertainty-aware prediction, and more extensive representation of rare and phenotypically overlapping cell types.

The prognostic importance of immune-cell density and spatial localisation has been established across multiple malignancies [30] [31] [32] [33] . The clinical analyses using TCGA cohorts showed that HELIOS recovered a conserved lymphocyte-associated tumour state across colorectal, breast, and lung cancers using a single model applied to routine H&E images. Importantly, TIL density remained associated with outcome after adjustment for stage and tumour-specific molecular variables, indicating that HELIOS-derived immune profiling captures information beyond established clinicopathological risk factors.

The clinical implications of myeloid infiltration were strongly dependent on tumour context. This was particularly evident for TANs, which were associated with a significant favourable outcome in colorectal cancer but an adverse outcome in lung cancer. In colorectal cancer, several studies have associated densities of neutrophils with improved survival [34] [35] [36] [37] . In this study, the enrichment of TANs in MSI-high colorectal cancers and their favourable prognostic association may therefore reflect an organised, immune-active microenvironment. Conversely, in lung cancer, neutrophil-rich microenvironments may arise from chronic inflammatory programmes 38, 39 . Direct mechanistic evidence is particularly strong for STK11/LKB1 loss. In KRAS-driven lung adenocarcinoma models, STK11 deficiency induces neutrophil recruitment and pro-inflammatory cytokine production while reducing intratumoural T-cell activity 40 . KRAS/STK11 tumours in our analysis (Figure 5E) also showed the highest median TAN density but only modest overall immune infiltration. This pattern may represent a neutrophil-dominant, lymphocyteattenuated state that supports immune evasion and tumour progression.

More broadly, studies have demonstrated that tumour-associated neutrophils comprise diverse and plastic states rather than a uniform lineage. These include inflammatory, cytotoxic, immunosuppressive, angiogenic, and antigen-presenting programmes, with their relative abundance shaped by tumour type and local signalling 41, 42 . The opposing prognostic associations observed here may therefore reflect differences in neutrophil state or cellular neighbourhood rather than neutrophil abundance alone.

The molecular subtype analyses recapitulated established immune landscapes across colorectal, breast, and lung cancers (Figure 5), demonstrating that routine H&E contains informative morphological features to recover clinically relevant biological states. This raises the possibility that HELIOS could serve as a rapid, lowcost screening tool to identify tumours with characteristic immune phenotypes or molecular subtypes before comprehensive genomic profiling, helping prioritise patients for downstream molecular testing. The analysis also revealed substantial heterogeneity among tumours within the same molecular group. MSI-high colorectal cancers were broadly immune enriched, consistent with their elevated neoantigen burden and characteristic lymphocytic infiltration 43, 44 . Nevertheless, in our cohort, TIL and TAN densities retained prognostic value within microsatellite-stable disease. HELIOS may therefore help identify immune-active states among the majority of colorectal cancers that lack the MSI-high phenotype. Similarly, triple-negative breast cancers were among the most immune-rich breast tumours. This observation is consistent with pooled clinical evidence showing that TIL abundance is strongly prognostic in early triple-negative breast cancer. Importantly, TIL density still separated patients with markedly different outcomes 45 . Given the aggressive clinical course and limited subtype-defining therapeutic targets of triple-negative disease, reproducible H&E-based quantification could provide a scalable means of resolving prognostic heterogeneity within this high-risk population 46.

Beyond immune-cell infiltration, HELIOS-derived fibroblast abundance was most strongly associated with adverse outcomes in breast cancer and remained independently associated with progression in both breast and lung cancers. This finding is consistent with breast cancer studies showing that enrichment of activated fibroblast populations is associated with recurrence, metastasis, and poorer survival 47, 48 . Spatial single-cell profiling in non-small-cell lung cancer showed that tumour-like and matrix-associated cancer-associated fibroblast (CAF) phenotypes were linked to poor survival, whereas inflammatory and interferon-responsive CAF states were associated with more favourable outcomes 49 . These findings support the clinical relevance of the stromal compartment but also emphasise that total FER is a coarse measure of a heterogeneous cell population. Integrating molecularly defined fibroblast states may distinguish fibroblast populations that promote immune exclusion and tumour progression from those with tumour-restraining functions.

The multivariable analyses provide evidence that HELIOS-derived measurements contain information beyond established clinicopathological variables across cancer types. Adding cellular features consistently improved model likelihood and C-index, and likelihood-ratio results indicate that the tumour microenvironment contributes independent prognostic information. These findings indicate that molecular classification alone does not fully capture the cellular organisation of tumours or the associated variation in clinical risk. HELIOS-derived phenotypes may therefore provide additional stratification, particularly for patients who lack targetable genomic alterations or established predictive biomarkers.

The broader translational value of HELIOS is its ability to extract multiple cellular phenotypes from material already embedded in diagnostic workflows. Once trained, a histology-based model can be applied retrospectively to archived clinical-trial material and population-scale cohorts without further tissue consumption. This creates an opportunity to test cellular hypotheses in datasets that would be infeasible to profile comprehensively using spatial assays. Importantly, HELIOS generates spatially resolved cell maps that can be directly reviewed by pathologists, providing a visually interpretable link between model-derived measurements and the underlying tissue morphology. The same development framework could also be adapted to rare cancers or treatment-specific cohorts by adding targeted spatial omics samples to a shared multi-organ reference. To support clinical translation and reproducibility, we developed a web-based platform (https://octopath.ai) that enables users to upload H&E-stained whole-slide images and run HELIOS inference. The platform is currently undergoing evaluation across multiple clinical centres.

Limitations of the study

First, HELIOS resolves eight major cell populations rather than the full spectrum of molecularly and functionally distinct cell states. Macrophage, neutrophil, fibroblast, and lymphocyte subsets can have divergent biological and clinical effects despite overlapping appearances on H&E. Moreover, the lower classification performance observed for several myeloid and stromal populations indicates that their abundance is estimated less precisely than that of epithelial cells and lymphocytes. Larger and more diverse spatial-omics datasets, together with hierarchical and uncertaintyaware modelling, may enable more refined inference of clinically relevant cellular states.

Second, although spatial-omics-derived labels provide biologically grounded supervision, they are not error-free. Residual uncertainty in cell-type assignment may arise from image co-registration, cell segmentation and differences among spatial profiling platforms. Confidence-based filtering and benchmarking against manual annotations may reduce these sources of noise, while filtering low-confidence cells may result in incompletely annotated training images, which can be suboptimal for object-detection frameworks such as YOLOv10. Future work should therefore evaluate training strategies that account explicitly for partially labelled data, as well as alternative instance-segmentation and cell-classification architectures.

Third, the prognostic analyses were retrospective and conducted primarily in TCGA cohorts assembled across multiple institutions, with heterogeneity in clinical tissue preparation and image quality. Some molecularly defined subgroup analyses also included relatively small numbers of patients and should therefore be regarded as exploratory. Although HELIOS-derived features improved multivariable model fit, these associations do not establish causality or treatment-predictive utility. Independent validation in prospectively collected and uniformly treated cohorts, particularly clinical trials of immunotherapy, will be required before clinical implementation.

Finally, the current clinical analyses primarily quantified global cell abundance and did not fully capture spatial organisation or cell-cell interactions. The clinical effect of a cellular population is likely to depend on its location and neighbourhood. Features such as immune exclusion, tumour-fibroblast niches, vascular proximity, and multicellular neighbourhoods may therefore provide more mechanistically informative biomarkers.

In conclusion, we developed HELIOS, a biology-grounded multi-cancer atlas for nuclei detection and classification from routine H&E-stained whole-slide images. The AI model trained using HELIOS demonstrated robust performance across internal and external benchmarking datasets. The recovery of established immune patterns across colorectal, breast, and lung cancers, together with associations between HELIOS-derived cellular phenotypes and patient survival, indicates that HELIOS can extract clinically relevant information about both tumour biology and prognosis beyond established molecular and genomic markers from routine H&E morphology. By transferring cellular knowledge learned from deeply profiled spatial-omics specimens to large collections of routine H&E slides, HELIOS provides a foundation for interpretable tumour-microenvironment profiling and patient stratification at clinical scale.

Methods

Data Source

Spatial transcriptomics data HELIOS was constructed by integrating 60 publicly available formalin-fixed paraffinembedded (FFPE) spatial transcriptomics samples, comprising 6 normal and 54 tumour tissues from 14 human organs, including breast, colorectum, lung, cervix, lymph node, ovary, pancreas, liver, kidney, prostate, brain, heart, skin, and tonsil. The spatial transcriptomics data were generated using four complementary platforms: 10x Genomics Xenium In Situ, Xenium Prime, Visium HD, and Atera Whole Transcriptome Analysis (WTA). For each sample, spatial transcriptomics outputs, cell segmentation contours, matched H&E-stained whole-slide images, and associated metadata were downloaded from the original data repositories. Repository URLs for all samples included in this study are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Single-cell RNA-sequencing data Single-cell RNA sequencing datasets used as reference atlases for cell type annotation were obtained from the CellXGene Database 50 and the DISCO Single-Cell Omics Database 51 . Healthy tissue reference datasets were collected from CellXGene, whereas tumour reference datasets were obtained from CellXGene and supplemented with tumour-specific datasets from DISCO to improve representation across cancer types. To ensure compatibility across spatial transcriptomics platforms, only genes present in the corresponding spatial transcriptomics gene panels were retained for downstream analyses.

Datasets for benchmarking cell detection and classification

To comprehensively evaluate the generalizability of HELIOS, we benchmarked the proposed models on three publicly available datasets that represent diverse tissue types, annotation strategies, and cell taxonomies (Table 1).

MoNuSAC 18 is a multi-organ nucleus segmentation and classification benchmark comprising H&E image patches from 71 patients across four organs (breast, kidney, lung, and prostate). The dataset contains 46,909 manually annotated nuclei belonging to four major cell categories and provides instance-level segmentation masks together with cell-type labels.

NuCLS 19 is a breast cancer nuclear annotation dataset derived from TCGA wholeslide images. It contains over 220,000 manually annotated nuclei with localisation, segmentation, and fine-grained cell-type labels. The dataset was developed through a collaborative annotation workflow involving pathologists, pathology trainees, and crowdsourcing with expert adjudication, providing high-quality annotations for computational pathology algorithm development.

Lizard 20 is one of the largest publicly available colorectal nucleus datasets containing 495,179 annotated nuclei from H&E image patches. The dataset provides instance segmentation masks and six-class nucleus classification labels generated through an automated annotation pipeline followed by extensive pathologist refinement.

Cell annotation pipeline

We developed a gene-panel-aware cell annotation pipeline to assign cell identities to spatial transcriptomics-derived cells and generate molecularly informed annotations for matched H&E-stained images. The pipeline consisted of three main stages: construction of organ-and panel-matched single-cell references, platform-specific definition of spatial cell objects, and transcriptomic label transfer from the matched reference to each spatial transcriptomics sample. The workflow was applied independently to each sample to preserve sample-specific gene-panel composition, organ context, and tissue morphology.

Construction of organ-and panel-matched single-cell references

Six spatial transcriptomics samples had paired single-cell RNA-sequencing data, which were used directly as matched references for cell annotation. For the remaining samples, we constructed sample-specific single-cell RNA-sequencing references according to the organ type and gene panel used for each spatial transcriptomics assay. For each sample, only genes shared between the spatial transcriptomics dataset and the candidate single-cell reference were retained for downstream analysis. This ensured that label transfer was performed using genes directly measurable in the corresponding spatial transcriptomics platform, reducing bias introduced by differences in gene coverage across assays.

Each constructed reference was initialised using CELLxGENE-derived single-cell data, comprising an organ-specific tissue component and a shared immune-cell component. The tissue component included both normal and tumour-associated cell populations, whereas the shared immune component primarily represented normal immune-cell populations. Reference cells were mapped to a common broad cell-type vocabulary covering T cells, B cells, NK cells, monocytes, macrophages, dendritic cells, plasma cells, mast cells, neutrophils, innate lymphoid cells, epithelial cells, endothelial cells, fibroblasts, stromal cells, mesenchymal cells, pericytes, and smooth muscle cells. The raw labels annotated by the pipeline were then grouped into the final eight classes used for cell detection and classification in H&E images (Table 2). Cells that are not identifiable from H&E-stained morphology were removed from the final dataset.

Platform-specific definition of spatial cell objects For Xenium and Atera samples, the default cell contours provided by the spatial transcriptomics platform were used as the spatial cell objects for label transfer. The corresponding cell-by-gene expression matrices were loaded together with cell-level metadata and spatial coordinates. Platform-derived cell boundaries were retained during label transfer so that each predicted cell identity could be assigned directly to the corresponding segmented cell object on the matched H&E image.

For Visium HD samples, cell-level objects were inferred before label transfer because the platform provides high-resolution binned expression measurements rather than default single-cell contours. For each sample, the 2 μm binned geneexpression matrix was used to define the measured gene set, and a sample-specific panel file was generated from the detected gene-expression features. Organ-specific references were first constructed using the union of genes detected across Visium HD samples from the same organ and then filtered to the sample-specific gene set before label transfer.

To generate cell-level expression profiles for Visium HD, the matched H&E image was used to initialise image-guided cell segmentation with Cellpose-SAM 52 . These image-derived initial contours were then used with ProSeg 53 to infer cell boundaries and assign local gene-expression signal to individual cells. The resulting ProSegderived cell objects, consisting of cell-level expression profiles and corresponding polygonal boundaries, were used as the query cells for downstream label transfer. This approach enabled Visium HD samples to be processed within the same reference-matched label-transfer framework as Xenium and Atera samples, while accounting for the absence of platform-provided single-cell contours.

AI model development and evaluation

Model training

We trained a YOLOv10-m model for cell detection and classification using the HELIOS dataset. Image patches of 640×640 pixels were extracted from annotated H&E-stained whole-slide images, together with the corresponding cell bounding boxes and class labels. The model was trained for 200 epochs using 8 NVIDIA A100 GPUs. The training batch size was 48, and model optimisation was performed using the AdamW optimiser with an initial learning rate of 0.01.

Colour augmentation was applied to the training data to reduce the impact of staining variability and improve model robustness across samples, tissues, and data sources. Stain separation was applied to deconvolved RGB channels into haematoxylin and eosin channels. Stain concentration perturbation strategy was applied to introduce realistic stain variation, in which perturbation factors were uniformly sampled with $ 0.14 and applied to the deconvolved H&E channels before reconstructing the augmented RGB images. Spatial augmentations were also applied during training, with configuration parameters listed in Supplementary Table 3.

Internal evaluation

Internal model performance was evaluated using five-fold cross-validation. In each fold, the dataset was split into training, validation, and held-out test subsets at a ratio of 80:10:10. The model was trained on the training subset, hyperparameters were selected using the validation subset, and final performance was reported on the held-out test subset. This process was repeated across five folds, and performance metrics were summarised across folds. Model performance was assessed using precision, recall, and F1-score at the cell level. Per-class metrics were reported to assess performance across individual cell populations, and weighted-average metrics were used to summarise overall model performance.

External evaluation

To assess generalisability beyond HELIOS, the trained model was externally evaluated on three publicly available nucleus segmentation and classification benchmarks: Lizard, MoNuSAC, and NuCLS, which include key cell groups shared in HELIOS and tissue types across colorectal, breast, lung, kidney, and prostate. Cell labels from each external dataset were harmonised to the closest corresponding HELIOS cell classes. The trained HELIOS model was applied without further finetuning to each external dataset. Performance was assessed using the same classification metrics as in the internal evaluation.

Biomarker Quantification

Tumour segmentation

Tumour-associated regions for colorectal cancers were identified on H&E-stained whole-slide images using an EfficientNet 55 trained on the NCT-CRC-HE-100K 56 dataset. The network was trained for 100 epochs on a single NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090 GPU with a batch size of 256 using the AdamW optimiser and an initial learning rate of 1 × 10. Data augmentation included random horizontal flipping (probability = 0.5) and stain-based colour augmentation to improve robustness to inter-slide staining variability. The same colour augmentation strategy used for YOLOv10 training was applied to the EfficientNet model.

Tumour-associated regions for breast and lung were identified using the ICGI automatic tumour segmentation model 57 . This model generates a tumour probability map for each whole-slide image, which is subsequently thresholded to produce a binary tumour segmentation mask. The model was developed and validated as a pan-cancer tumour segmentation approach for FFPE H&E whole-slide images across multiple carcinoma types.

Statistics

For comparisons of HELIOS-derived cell densities across molecular or clinicopathological subgroups, two-group comparisons were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test, and comparisons across more than two groups were performed using the Kruskal-Wallis test. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. Associations between continuous biomarkers were assessed using Spearman's rank correlation.

For survival analyses, progression-free survival and disease-specific survival were analysed using Kaplan-Meier curves and compared between biomarker-defined groups using the log-rank test. Hazard ratios and 95% confidence intervals were estimated using Cox proportional hazards regression.

Multivariable Cox regression models were used to assess whether HELIOS-derived features provided prognostic information independent of clinicopathological variables. Models were constructed separately for colorectal, breast, and lung cancer cohorts and adjusted for available cohort-relevant variables, including age, tumour stage, and molecular or histological subgroup annotations where available. To evaluate the added value of HELIOS-derived biomarkers, nested Cox models with and without HELIOS features were compared using likelihood-ratio tests. Model discrimination was assessed using the concordance index.

All statistical tests were two-sided, and p<0.05 was considered statistically significant. Subgroup analyses and multi-biomarker comparisons were interpreted as exploratory unless otherwise specified. Complete-case analysis was used for models requiring clinical, molecular, imaging, and survival variables.
