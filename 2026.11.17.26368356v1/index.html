Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. Abstract

Background. Oral pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) is highly effective for preventing HIV acquisition when used consistently, but uptake and persistence remain suboptimal among gay men and other men who have sex with men (MSM) in South Africa. We examined client and provider perspectives on PrEP uptake and persistence.

Methods. Between September and December 2022, we conducted three focus group discussions with 16 HIV-negative MSM eligible for, but not using, PrEP and six key informant interviews with PrEP providers at a specialized key population health clinic in Cape Town, South Africa. We analyzed transcripts thematically, using deductive codes informed by the PrEP cascade and the COM-B (capability, opportunity, motivation) model, plus inductive codes for emerging issues.

Results. Five themes emerged. Limited knowledge, misconceptions about PrEP and antiretroviral therapy, and low perceived risk reduced clients' capability to make informed decisions about PrEP. HIV-and sexuality-related stigma, concerns about disclosure, and social surveillance were socially costly. Distance, transport costs, work commitments, clinic hours, and visit burden limited physical opportunity, whereas free care, short visits, reminders, and multi-month dispensing facilitated access. Daily pill-taking, side effects, forgetfulness, and changing sexual activity complicated persistence; routines, reminders, and acceptable PrEP modalities supported continued use. Non-judgmental, MSM-affirming services, counseling, and trusted providers fostered motivation by strengthening clients' sense of safety, agency, and control.

Conclusion. PrEP delivery for MSM should extend beyond medication provision and address knowledge, stigma, service convenience, and motivation. Low-intensity adaptations, including tailored counseling, affirming care, flexible multi-month dispensing, and reminders, may improve uptake and persistence.

Introduction

South Africa has the largest HIV epidemic in the world. In 2024, 7.8 million people were living with HIV in South Africa, of whom 6.3 million were on treatment. Life expectancy in South Africa has increased from 54.8 years (2004) to 68.3 years (2026) and the success of the antiretroviral therapy (ART) program has been largely credited for this [1, 2]. Despite these successes, South Africa still has 53,000 AIDS-related deaths each year, and HIV incidence in South Africa remains stubbornly high [3]. In 2024, there were 170,000 new HIV infections in South Africa, accounting for 13% of new infections globally [3, 4]. Highly effective biomedical HIV prevention options such as pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) have the potential to significantly reduce new HIV infections [5, 6]. PrEP is one of the UNAIDS pillars of HIV prevention and is recommended for those at substantial risk of HIV infection [7]. Despite the rollout of oral PrEP in South Africa in 2016 and steadily increasing access, uptake and persistence on PrEP among the most vulnerable populations have been suboptimal [8].

Gay men and other men who have sex with men (MSM) are among the populations that are most vulnerable to HIV acquisition in South Africa [3]. The South African national strategic plan recognizes MSM as a key population for HIV prevention and supports HIV care for them through differentiated service delivery models tailored to their needs [9]. Although longacting injectable PrEP (lenacapavir) was introduced in South Africa's public sector in 2026 [10], oral PrEP remains the most widely available and will be a critical modality for the foreseeable future [11]. When taken consistently oral PrEP can reduce HIV acquisition by close to 90%, yet uptake and medium-to long-term persistence on PrEP among MSM and other vulnerable populations remain low globally, including in South Africa [12]. Although some discontinuation reflects genuine changes in an individual's vulnerability, program data suggest that many clients discontinue PrEP because of challenges to continuation rather than a change in risk [8, 13]. Maximizing the impact of MSM-focused PrEP programs thus requires a detailed understanding of the specific, modifiable barriers and facilitators that shape PrEP-related behaviors, as this can allow delivery models to be adjusted to strengthen capability, opportunity, and motivation to start and stay on PrEP.

The evidence base on how best to improve PrEP care in marginalized populations remains limited, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa [14]. Early work (2012) suggested that most (75%) South African MSM were willing to use PrEP, yet this stated willingness has contrasted with low persistence in real-world settings [15, 16]. Studies from South Africa and the region have shown the roles of stigma, lack of confidentiality, perception of efficacy, pill burden, and ease . of use in shaping clients' decisions about starting and continuing PrEP [17] [18] [19]. The studies that have focused on MSM in Africa highlight this population's own unique challenges accessing HIV and other health care services [20]. Despite this growing literature, the barriers identified to date do not fully explain why eligible individuals decline or discontinue PrEP, given its substantial health benefits. As a result, implementers have limited actionable, behaviorally informed evidence on how to adjust PrEP delivery models to encourage more optimal usage amongst those who stand to benefit the most, including MSM in South Africa.

This study was conducted to gain a better understanding of how clients and providers make decisions about using PrEP during periods of heightened vulnerability to HIV acquisition.

Human decision-making is shaped not only by structural and social constraints but also by cognitive biases and heuristics, and emerging work suggests this may also shape PrEP decisions among those most vulnerable [21, 22]. Behavioral science frameworks such as COM-B, which conceptualizes behavior as a product of capability, opportunity, and motivation, provide a structured approach to identifying aspects of PrEP service delivery that might be modifiable to address some of these behavioral barriers [23]. In this study, we used a qualitative approach (focus group discussions with MSM clients and key informant interviews with health care providers) to describe the PrEP decision-making process along the HIV prevention cascade and to classify the barriers and facilitators we identified into COM-B domains [24]. This behavioral mapping aims to generate practical, theory-driven insights into how PrEP service delivery could be adjusted to improve PrEP uptake and persistence among MSM in South Africa.

Methods

Overview.

At a specialized key population health clinic in Cape Town (South Africa), we conducted focus group discussions (FGDs) with MSM who tested HIV negative at the clinic and key informant interviews (KIIs) with health care providers involved in the education, offering, and initiation of PrEP to clients. These data describe the PrEP decision-making process and identify how factors mapped to the COM-B domains of capability, opportunity, and motivation may contribute to poor uptake and/or persistence. We followed the COREQ (Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research) guidelines in reporting our methods and results [25].

Setting.

The study site is a key population clinic based in Cape Town that specializes in services for MSM and offers free services to the public. It is run by a local implementing partner that is a leader in providing specialized HIV prevention and treatment services to key . populations, including MSM and transgender people. The clinic works closely with the government primary health system from which it obtains drugs, but facilities and staff are donor-funded. The clinic is situated in an urban area, is the only public clinic serving this population in the city, and so draws clients from across the city, largely individuals who cannot afford private medical insurance.

Study design.

We used a constructivist thematic analysis approach to qualitatively examine the barriers and facilitators influencing PrEP decision-making from the perspectives of clients and providers. Two frameworks guided the study. First, we organized the data collection around the HIV prevention cascade (hereafter the PrEP cascade) treating each stage, from eligibility and offer through initiation and continuation, as a distinct decision point in the client's journey [26]. The FGD and KII guides were structured around these stages, drawing on recent work emphasizing the importance of the decision-making cascade in identifying opportunities to address behavioral barriers [27]. Second, we used the COM-B model to frame the analysis. COM-B conceptualizes behavior as the product of three interacting components: capability, opportunity and motivation [23]. Capability may be psychological (knowledge and understanding) or physical (skills); opportunity may be social (interpersonal influences and norms) or physical (environmental resources and access); and motivation may be reflective (conscious beliefs and intentions) or automatic (emotions and habits) [28]. Data collection.

For the convenience of the study participants, all study activities (KIIs and FGDs) were held in private rooms at the study site. The study team developed data collection tools based on current literature and then reviewed and refined them based on their experience. All study activities required only a single interaction with each participant.

Consistent with COREQ, we describe the research team and how our backgrounds and assumptions may have shaped data collection and interpretation [25]. LL, a researcher with training in qualitative and quantitative methods and experience conducting HIV research in South Africa, led the KIIs and FGDs. Other team members (BB, JH, JM with experience providing health care to sexual minority populations, in particular MSM) supported the development of study tools. LL had no prior relationship with participants. The team approached this work with the view that PrEP is an important public health tool and that behavioral science can improve its implementation; this framing may have led us to emphasize modifiable behavioral and service delivery factors over broader structural determinants. Throughout the study, we aimed to foreground the participants' own experiences and situate the recommendations within the South African health system and social context.

Data analysis.

All FGDs and KIIs were transcribed from audio recordings and subsequently checked by the primary facilitator. The initial analysis was deductive, based on pre-assigned thematic codes from the study's conceptual framework on behavioral facilitators and barriers to PrEP uptake and persistence, drawing on the COM-B model and the PrEP cascade. Two experienced reviewers (SB, CH) reviewed the transcripts using this preliminary codebook, which was then augmented using inductive code development as new themes emerged.

Next, two reviewers (CP, SG) independently coded an FGD and a KII using the evolving codebook, met to compare their coding and resolve discrepancies through discussion, and LL reviewed and refined the codebook accordingly. CP and SG then each independently coded all remaining transcripts using the final codebook in NVivo™, meeting regularly to reconcile differences by consensus. We then consolidated the final coded datasets, summarized them across reviewers, and developed themes. In the final stage of analysis, we interpreted these themes in relation to the PrEP cascade and classified them into COM-B domains to address the study aims and generate behaviorally informed implications for PrEP service delivery. To limit the influence of our own assumptions, codebooks were codeveloped, coding combined deductive and inductive approaches, and local co-authors were involved in interpreting the findings.

This was particularly salient within formal relationships where clients worried "how to hide the tablets" (KII #1) and often felt like because they were "married … they cannot take PrEP" (KII #4). Even outside of marriage, PrEP use was framed negatively; providers reported that partners might "think they've got multiple partners" (KII #1) and view PrEP as evidence of "intentions of you misbehaving" (KII #4). One client shared that their "mother… checks my medication" (FGD #1), which contributed to a sense that they "don't want to leave it on the counter for everyone to see it and question it" (FGD #2). Others worried that "when they see it in your place… they will mistake it for maybe antiretrovirals" (FGD #3) and therefore assume HIV infection.

These accounts show how actual and perceived stigma, moral judgment, and social surveillance at home, in relationships, and in the wider community increase the potential social costs associated with PrEP use and, as a result, reduce the social opportunity for its use.

Theme 3. Access, convenience, and service delivery (COM-B: Physical Opportunity)

Access to PrEP was shaped by how individuals reached the site including transport time and costs as well as work schedules, waiting times, and arrangement of services at the clinic.

Clients also identified key program features, such as no cost at the point of accessing care, reminders, and multi-month dispensing, that reduced the burden of accessing PrEP, illustrating that the delivery model can either restrict or facilitate physical opportunity.

Providers were aware of the numerous structural barriers related to geography, employment, and the health system that clients faced when trying to access care, including distance to the clinic, transport expenses, and travel for work. Clients echoed these barriers. One provider observed that "in the communities, most of them [clients] they don't have [access] of coming this side" (KII #4), while a client confirmed that they "live far" and "need to take two taxis to travel" (FGD #1) to the clinic. As a result, a major barrier is "… financial-is transport" (KII #1), and because clients "are coming here by public transport" they "have to spend money" to get their PrEP (FGD #3). Extended travel made consistent, timely clinic visits difficult; when providers reached out to clients, they often found people were "out of the country, … at work" (KII #2). For those employed, work added further logistical barriers as they "need to be at work" and "can't come a month later" (KII #2), exacerbated by the fact that "most of them are working during the week" while the clinic was "not working on Saturday" (KII #3).

At the same time, providers and clients described how free care, reduced waiting times, and strong refill systems facilitated PrEP access and use. Providers emphasized that all services at the site are offered for "free" (KII #1, KII #2, KII #4, KII #6), which some clients described as "the most important" (FGD #2) feature. Clients described the process of accessing care as "so easy" (FGD #2) reflecting the providers' efforts to facilitate access by providing "more medication so that they mustn't default" (KII #1) when clients were travelling, making sure "it's quicker to get your stuff done here than going to a public one [clinic]" (KII #2), ensuring that "we never run out of stock" (KII #2), and organizing visits so that "it doesn't take that much of their time" (KII #5). Clients corroborated these efforts, reporting multi-month dispensing if "I won't be back" (FGD #1) and having clear appointment reminders, such as "on my bottle they wrote that I need to come back on the 19 of December" (FGD #3).

Consistently, these accounts show that structural factors such as distance, transport costs, work schedules, and health-system organization limit the physical opportunity for PrEP use, while free care, efficient service delivery, reminders, and multi-month dispensing can partially offset these barriers within the COM-B framework. Preferences for alternative future PrEP modalities (e.g., implants, long-acting injections) reflected attempts to address these obstacles.

Providers and clients flagged that daily pill-taking, forgetting doses, experiencing side effects, and changes in sexual activity all influenced adherence and persistence. One provider shared that "people … are tired of taking PrEP drugs, the pill, each and every day" (KII #2) and that this was amplified by the cognitive dissonance clients experience of "taking a pill every day yet I'm not sick" (KII #6). For others, providers felt the barrier was less about the pill itself than about remembering it "… because they always forget sometimes that I had to take my tablet" (KII #1), a challenge echoed in focus groups where clients acknowledged "forgetting to drink it every day" (FGD #3). Although side effects were generally mild and temporary, providers reported that some clients did not feel comfortable taking the medication "because of the side effects" (KII #4), which included "headaches" or "nausea" and led some to conclude that they "… don't like these tablets, then they leave them" (KII #1). Clients likewise reported that "PrEP makes me feel not good" (FGD #1) and one client described friends on PrEP who had experienced "weight gaining", "feeling tired", and "feel[ing] depressed" (FGD #1). For some, decisions to stop were framed primarily in terms of perceived risk, with providers recounting clients who stopped because they were "not sexually active" (KII #2, KII #5) and others who took PrEP because they were "sexually active" and had "more partners" (KII #1).

In acknowledging challenges, clients also described practical adherence strategies they had adopted. These included phone alarms ("If you are starting … you put an alarm", FGD #3), visual cues tied to existing routines ("I just put it in [a] place that I can see it frequently", FGD #2), and external support from partners ("he will remind me in the morning", FGD #2).

Clients saw these strategies as ways of "getting into a routine" (FGD #3) that could support consistent adherence over time. When asked about future potential PrEP modalities, clients framed their preferences as strategies for improving adherence and persistence, though preferences were not consistent across options, illustrating that different modalities can either support or undermine adherence depending on context and individual. One client expressed a preference for "an implant because I for one I hate pills" (FGD #3), while another stated that they "enjoy drinking pills in the morning" because it gave them the feeling that they were "in control" (FGD #3). Similarly, one client thought a "once-off injection per month it will be better" (FGD #3), but others preferred the pill because they felt that with injections "there's so many more things that could go wrong" (FGD #2).

These narratives highlight how pill burden, side effects, forgetfulness, and changes in sexual activity can reduce both psychological capability and physical opportunity for sustained PrEP use, while habitual routines (automatic motivation), reminder cues, and acceptable PrEP modalities (physical opportunity) helped some clients sustain PrEP use. Providers noted that clear, informative counseling that explained the benefits, risks, and guidelines helped clients to "understand themselves" (KII #5), recognize risk, and make appropriate decisions about PrEP. Providers noted that, "you need to show the person or teach the person" (KII #1); that once you have "educated them well" they "understand the benefits of taking PrEP" (KII #6), which allows them to be "responsible" and "aware of your risk behavior" (KII #5). They actively made supportive space for clients to come to their own decisions by providing those not ready with "small pamphlets for PrEP" and they "write on the pamphlets our cell phone numbers… so if ever you change your mind, you can just give us a call" (KII #3).

Providers emphasized the role that better clinical environments (friendly, men-focused, gayfriendly, and non-judgmental) can play in facilitating open conversation on sensitive topics (sexual orientation, partners, drug use), and clients described their experience of the study clinic as feeling "relieved" (FGD #1) and "included" (FGD #2), often in contrast to their home and community settings which were less welcoming. The fact that clients could "just see it's gay friendly" (KII #3) and that providers were perceived as "easily approachable" (KII #1) resulted in providers being able to get clients to "engage, feel comfortable, and then you'll get all the answers you want" (KII #2). Clients described relief at finding a place where "they specialize in men's health" (FGD #2) and "you feel like no one is judging you" and "have all the information that you're looking for…" (FGD #1). Part of the reason for feeling included and motivated was that clients described having providers who were "experiencing the same lifestyle that I experienced…" (FGD #2) and that looking around the clinic it was "good just to see other men in our community taking care of themselves" (FGD #2). The benefits of such a setting therefore need to be considered together with the knowledge gaps, stigma, and practical barriers described in the other themes.

Clients across all the FGDs framed accessing health care, including PrEP, as part of a broader prevention mindset, linking services like STI screening, counseling, and PrEP with positive mental health and feeling "in control" (FGD #3). Clients described PrEP as a "safety net" to help stay HIV negative when the unexpected happens ("a condom breaks") or when their personal situation (multiple partners, unknown partner status) places them at risk, allowing them to "live your life and enjoy" and giving a "peace of mind that comes with knowing that you are safe" (FGD #1). "HIV testing and counseling" (FGD #3) were given as one of the main reasons for coming to the clinic, giving clients "more control" (FGD #2). The habit of "booking a routine checkup every three months" (FGD #2) encouraged not only PrEP persistence but also regular STI screening ("kept me in line with like getting STI screen", FGD #1).

Together, these accounts suggest the combined role of quality counseling delivered by friendly community providers in a welcoming clinical environment in promoting reflective motivation and social opportunity for PrEP, building clients' agency for risk identification and management, which clients linked to a broader sense of taking care of their health.

Discussion

In interviews and discussions with providers and MSM in South Africa, we found that PrEP use was shaped by multiple decisions along the PrEP decision cascade from initiation to persistence that fall into distinct domains in the COM-B framework for studying behavior change. These include lack of knowledge and pervasive misconceptions (psychological capability), stigma and social surveillance (social opportunity), service delivery constraints (physical opportunity), pill burden and adherence challenges (psychological capability, physical opportunity, and automatic motivation), and the clinical environment and counseling (reflective motivation and social opportunity). Taken together, these findings help explain the continuing gap between high reported willingness to use PrEP and low real-world uptake and persistence amongst MSM and other vulnerable populations in South Africa [31].

Despite counseling and education efforts, there remained gaps in knowledge around PrEP use that allowed myths and misconceptions to play substantive roles in decision-making (e.g., PrEP = ARVs, which is for sick people, but I am not sick; stopping PrEP will turn tests positive). These factors reduce the psychological capability of individuals to evaluate PrEP initiation or persistence, and similar patterns have been reported in adolescent girls and young women in Malawi [32] and elsewhere in sub-Saharan Africa, as well as in other vulnerable populations in South Africa [33].

For those who do start PrEP, there remain barriers outside of physically accessing and taking the medication. PrEP can act as an undesirable social marker, interpreted as living with HIV, being promiscuous, or being gay, each of which can trigger judgment from partners, families, and communities. This dynamic constrains social opportunity, and even specialized MSM clinics can themselves be stigmatizing if attendance is visible or recognizable. These results are consistent with prior work among MSM and other key populations in South Africa, which has identified stigma, privacy concerns, and the reactions of clients' social networks as central barriers to PrEP uptake and persistence [31, 33].

Service design (including clinic location, operating hours, waiting times, and visit frequency) also shaped the physical feasibility of accessing PrEP services; participants highlighted multi-month dispensing, appointment reminders, and free services as particularly valued features. Such service delivery factors largely determine physical opportunity and have been shown to influence PrEP persistence in many South African facilities [34]. Our findings suggest that misunderstandings about PrEP use and safety may act upstream in the decision cascade, preventing clients from self-identifying as suitable PrEP candidates or accepting an initial offer, while stigma and social surveillance are more likely to become salient downstream, when clients must decide whether to store tablets at home or return to the clinic for a refill. Lastly, service design likely acts both upstream and downstream, affecting clients' first encounters with the health system as well as their experience of continued PrEP use.

Participants' accounts suggest that MSM-affirming, non-judgmental clinic settings may improve motivation to access care in the first place and support persistence once PrEP was initiated by creating an environment of trust and perceived control. Similar patterns have been reported in prior work demonstrating that patient-centered, stigma-free services are associated with improved engagement in HIV prevention and care among key populations [14, 19]. Clients in our study who viewed PrEP as a way to proactively protect themselves and take control of their health described greater motivation to use PrEP. This aligns with the literature showing that self-efficacy and positive affect are key determinants of adherence to both antiretroviral and PrEP programs [35] [36] [37] [38]. It integrates both provider and client perspectives from the same key population clinic, allowing us to compare how clients and providers view the same barriers and facilitators, and it applied a COM-B-informed decision cascade to PrEP uptake and persistence, providing a structured, theory-driven lens for unpacking behavioral challenges in this context. However, limitations of this study include the focus on a single, urban, donor-supported key population clinic. Our findings may thus not fully generalize to public sector or rural facilities. In addition, clients were recruited at the clinic and were therefore already engaged with health services; the perspectives of MSM who do not access care may differ. Data were also collected before recent reductions in dedicated services for vulnerable populations, including MSM, in South Africa, which may further exacerbate the barriers described here [40, 41]. Future research should assess the effectiveness of behaviorally informed modifications to PrEP delivery at scale -for example, structured counseling, service design adjustments, and reminder systems -to determine how well they improve key pathways to behavior. It will also be important to extend this foundational work to other vulnerable populations and modalities (e.g., long-acting injectable lenacapavir, now being rolled out in South Africa) to understand whether the mechanisms driving behavior are shared or population-and context-specific, particularly as PrEP delivery moves into more generalized care settings.

Using a prominent framework for studying behavior change, this study highlights that PrEP success among MSM depends not only on access to medication, but on navigating the pathways to PrEP uptake and persistence through the ways information, stigma, clinic environment and service design shape capability, opportunity and motivation across the decision cascade. Harnessing these behavioral insights through light-touch, scalable interventions will be increasingly important if PrEP programs are to remain effective amid shrinking resources and growing reliance on public-sector services.

Our findings point to several behaviorally grounded interventions for improving PrEP service delivery and the health of individuals at risk of HIV acquisition. First, PrEP counseling should go beyond information provision to build skills: correcting common myths and misinformation about PrEP and supporting clients to accurately and independently assess their own risk.
