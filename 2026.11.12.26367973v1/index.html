To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 3 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background: The gut microbiome is an emerging risk factor for cardiovascular disease.

However, there is a lack of large-scale metagenomic studies with replication that identify gut microbial features associated with incident cardiovascular disease (CVD).

Methods: We tested for gut microbiome associations with CVD using metagenomes from stool samples in a discovery-replication framework across four large prospective Nordic cohorts with 635 incident CVD in 11,604 participants in the discovery cohort and 2,309 incident CVD in 20,965 participants combined across three replication cohorts.

Results: Higher Shannon diversity index and species richness were consistently associated with a lower risk of incident CVD. In addition, the relative abundance of nine species and eleven bacterial functional modules showed reproducible associations with incident CVD in the discovery cohort and in a meta-analysis of the three replication cohorts. We identified a femalespecific gut microbiota-related pathway involving the prevalent oral-gut species Rothia mucilaginosa_A, enriched among denture users, thus serving as a proxy for longstanding poor oral health. Mendelian randomization analysis supported a causal effect of denture use on increased relative abundance of Rothia mucilaginosa_A. This pathway was linked to increased CVD risk through altered gut microbial manganese transport, elevated circulating imidazole propionate levels, and increased levels of systemic inflammation biomarkers. The second pathway involved both α-diversity metrics and the remaining eight species, all of which were strongly associated with multiple markers of metabolic syndrome and with bacterial pathways related to purine metabolism, microbial stress responses, altered redox balance, ascorbic acid degradation, and branched-chain amino acid biosynthesis.

Conclusions: These findings demonstrate that gut microbiota diversity, specific taxa, and functional pathways provide new insights into the underlying biology of CVD. Furthermore, these findings suggest that the link between poor oral health and cardiovascular disease may, at least partly, operate through altered gut microbiota.

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is the leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide 1.

Despite substantial advances in prevention and treatment, considerable heterogeneity in individual risk persists, underscoring the need to identify novel biological pathways that contribute to disease development.

Increasing evidence suggests that the gut microbiome, a complex community of microorganisms, may contribute to cardiovascular health and disease 2 . In a cross-sectional analysis in the Framingham Heart study, decreased overall gut microbial α-diversity, determined by 16S rRNA gene sequencing, was associated with an increased calculated 10-year risk of CVD 3 . In a Chinese cohort, a gut bacterial co-abundance group was associated with coronary artery disease severity 4 and in a Dutch cohort, four gut microbial species were associated with cardiovascular risk 5 . Finally, in a cross-sectional analysis, higher gut relative abundances of Streptococcus species were associated with subclinical coronary atherosclerosis in the Swedish SCAPIS cohort, which was included as one of the replication cohorts in the present study 6.

These relationships between the microbiome of the gastrointestinal tract and cardiovascular health are referred to collectively as the gut-heart axis and are thought to be mediated, at least in part, by gut microbiota-derived metabolites 7 . One such metabolite is trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO), for which elevated circulating plasma levels have been associated with an increased risk of incident CVD 8, 9 . Another metabolite, imidazole propionate (ImP), a histidine-derived molecule produced by the gut microbiota, has been linked to atherosclerosis 10, 11 . Furthermore, CVD has been linked to decreased abundance of gut microbial species that produce butyrate 12- 18 . Butyrate and other short-chain fatty acids, which are end products of dietary fiber fermentation by specific gut bacterial species, have been shown to exert beneficial effects on pathways implicated in the regulation of cardiovascular disease 19.

Despite these recent studies, the broader landscape of gut microbial features associated with incident CVD events remains incompletely characterized, and there is a lack of large-scale metagenome sequence-based investigations with successful replication of associations between gut microbial features and incident CVD. In this study, we investigate the associations between gut microbiota α-diversity metrics, relative abundance of metagenomic species, and metagenomic functional pathways and incident CVD across four large, well-characterized, longitudinal population-based Nordic cohorts with stool-derived metagenomic sequencing. We 1 aimed to identify microbiome signatures that may inform biological mechanisms and identify 2 potential pathways for intervention. To our knowledge, this is the first metagenomic study to 3 identify consistent associations between gut microbiome features and incident CVD within a 4 discovery-replication framework. 5 6 Methods

Discovery cohort -the Trøndelag Health Study

We used data from the fourth survey of the population-based Trøndelag Health Study (the HUNT Study) in Norway as the discovery cohort. The HUNT Study was first conducted in 1984-86 (HUNT1) with surveys taking place roughly every ten years until HUNT4 (2017-2019) [20] [21] [22].

Individuals over age 20 living in Trøndelag county were invited to take part in different assessments consisting of questionnaires, interviews, clinical examinations, laboratory measurements and providing biological samples 23 . International Classification of Diseases (ICD) diagnosis codes were obtained from inpatient and outpatient specialist care records at St.

Olav's University, Namsos, and Levanger Hospitals, which together provide specialist and emergency care for the population covered by the HUNT study and biobank. Self-reported dietary intake, alcohol consumption, and smoking were defined as described in the Supplementary Methods. The use of proton pump inhibitors (ATC code A02BC) or antibiotics (ATC code J01 and J04 if available) in the 3 months prior to sampling was collected from the national pharmaceutical registry and used as binary variables.

Replication cohorts

We externally validated our findings in three Nordic population-based cohorts (Figure 1, Table 1). The Swedish CArdioPulmonary bioImage Study (SCAPIS) is a prospective study that invited a random selection from the population register of individuals aged 50-64 living in 6 counties in Sweden 24 . The metagenomics data from SCAPIS comes from the Uppsala and Malmö sites 25.

The Swedish Infrastructure for Medical Population-based Life-course and Environmental

Research (SIMPLER) comprises two longitudinal cohorts (the Swedish Mammography Cohort and the Cohort of Swedish Men), from which a subset with available metagenomic data was included in the present analyses 26, 27 . FINRISK is a population-based health survey and the 2002 cohort is a stratified random sample of adults aged 25-74 from 6 regions in Finland with metagenomics [28] [29] [30] . Detailed descriptions of these cohorts are available in the Supplementary Methods. As dietary covariates differed between cohorts, each cohort handled these covariates in as harmonized manner as was possible, as is described in the Supplementary Methods.

Outcome definition

In HUNT, incident cardiovascular disease (CVD) was defined as the first hospital diagnosis of ischemic heart disease (I20-I25), ischemic stroke (I63), or heart failure (I50), or cardiovascular death (I20-25, I63, I50), occurring after baseline (Supplementary Table 1). Participants with prevalent CVD at baseline, identified using the same ICD codes as those defining the outcome, were excluded. Participants were followed until the incident CVD diagnosis or CVD-related death, non-CVD related death, emigration, or end of follow-up on December 31, 2023. During follow-up, 635 incident CVD events were identified among 11,604 participants free from CVD at baseline. Cardiovascular deaths were identified from the Norwegian Cause of Death Registry (Dødsårsaksregisteret) using ICD codes for CVD listed as the underlying cause of death.

Definitions of incident CVD were harmonized across replication cohorts to the extent possible (Supplementary Table 1). Hospital discharge diagnoses in the HUNT catchment area have been validated and show excellent validity (>80%) for stroke and acute myocardial infarction and moderate positive predictive value for heart failure (67%) 31

Metagenomics

In the HUNT study, stool sample handling, DNA extraction, library construction, and metagenomic sequencing were carried out according to established protocols described previously 32 . Briefly, 56,042 individuals participated in HUNT4 (2017-2019) and 13,268 provided stool samples. 50% of these have participated in all HUNT surveys. After sequencing and quality control, 12,887 individuals were included in this analysis. Samples were processed, and deep metagenomic sequencing was performed by Cmbio (Copenhagen, Denmark; previously named Clinical Microbiomics A/S). Three circular punches (6 mm diameter) were taken from each stool collection card and placed into designated wells of MagMAX™ 96 Deep Well Plates (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Microbial cells were lysed by bead beating, after which DNA was purified using the MagMAX Ultra Microbiome kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions on a KingFisher™ Flex instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific). DNA was enzymatically fragmented and sequencing libraries were generated using the Celero EZ DNA-seq Core Module on a Tecan DreamPrep NGS automated platform.

Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 system with paired-end 150 bp reads, producing an average depth of 22.9 million read pairs per sample with more than 85% of bases achieving a Phred quality score above 30. Metagenomics methodology in SCAPIS, SIMPLER, and FINRISK is described in the Supplementary Methods. Briefly, for SCAPIS, metagenomic sequencing using the Illumina Novaseq 6000 system yielded an average sequence depth of 25.3 million read pairs for the Uppsala samples and 26.3 million read pairs for Malmö samples. For SIMPLER, sequencing on MGI DNBSEQ G400 or T7 platforms generated an average of 51 million reads per sample. For FINRISK, sequencing on the Illumina HiSeq 4000 platform yielded an average of 900,000 reads per sample.

Definition of diversity indices

To estimate α-diversity, abundance tables were first rarefied by randomly subsampling an equal number of signature gene counts from each sample without replacement. Species profiles derived from these rarefied data were then used to calculate α-diversity indices, namely Shannon diversity index and species richness 33 , based on relative species abundances.

Analyses were completed using the diversity function from the vegan R package 34 in all cohorts.

Definition of metagenomic species

In HUNT, SCAPIS, and SIMPLER, taxonomic profiling was performed by Cmbio using the CHAMP profiler with the Human Microbiome Reference (HMR05) catalogue 35 and annotated using GTDB-Tk release R214. In FINRISK, taxonomic profiling was conducted using the GTDB release R214. In the HUNT study, we tested for the association between the relative abundance of 546 species and incident CVD. We required that these metagenomic species had a prevalence of over 30% and the relative abundance was transformed using inverse rank normal transformation. In addition, we used the CLR transformation in sensitivity analyses.

Definition of functional modules

In HUNT, SCAPIS, and SIMPLER, genes were mapped to Kyoto Encyclopedia of Gene and Genomes (KEGG) pathways 36 using KEGG v78.2 by Cmbio. In the HUNT study, we tested for the association between the abundance of 461 KEGG modules with a prevalence over 30% and incident CVD. Associations for KEGG modules were not assessed in FINRISK due to shallow metagenomic sequencing.

Statistical analysis in discovery and replication cohorts

We tested for the association between time to event of CVD and 1) two indices of microbiota αdiversity-Shannon diversity index and species richness; 2) relative abundance of metagenomic species; and 3) abundance of KEGG modules. We performed a Cox proportional hazards model using time to event with right censoring. The age at sampling, sex, and a binary variable recording use of antibiotics in the 3 months before sampling were used as covariates.

Individuals with CVD before baseline were excluded and only individuals without missing covariates were included (e.g., complete cases).

We sought external replication for significant species and KEGG modules with a False Discovery Rate (FDR) adjusted p-value < 0.05 using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure in the HUNT study, as well as both metrics of α-diversity. We performed a fixed-effect meta-analysis to combine effect sizes across SCAPIS, SIMPLER and FINRISK for α-diversity and species associations and across SCAPIS and SIMPLER for KEGG modules. We tested for heterogeneity between cohorts with Cochran's Q-test and α of 5%.

We considered a finding to be replicated for each α-diversity index if the two-sided metaanalysis p-value was significant at an α of 5%. For species and KEGG modules, we considered a finding to be replicated if the meta-analysis estimate had the same direction of effect as the discovery analysis and reached significance in a one-sided test at a Bonferroni-corrected threshold of α =0.05/k where k is the number of features tested. Because the direction of effect was prespecified, this corresponds to a two-sided p-value <0.10/k, specifically p<0.0062 for species and p<0.00323 for KEGG. Proportional hazards assumptions were checked using the cox.zph function in R. After considering corrections for multiple testing, all model assumptions were met (Supplementary Table 2).

Analyses for the HUNT, SCAPIS, and FINRISK cohorts, as well as all meta-analyses and data visualization, were performed in R (versions 4.3.1-4.4.2). Analyses for the SIMPLER cohort were conducted in Stata (version 15.1; StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). The R package pheatmap 37 was used for creating heatmaps. Pearson correlations between the effect sizes for the KEGG module and MGS were estimated, and hierarchical clustering with complete linkage and Euclidean distance was used to group the modules and species based on the correlations.

Sex differences

To explore whether the associations between 1) Rothia mucilaginosa_A and 2) manganese transport system KEGG module and CVD differed by sex, we added a multiplicative interaction term between sex and the exposure of interest to the primary models, with all other covariates unchanged. Interaction was assessed with a Wald test of the interaction coefficient. We then repeated the primary analyses stratified by sex, with sex removed from the covariate set.

Sensitivity analysis

In the HUNT study, we performed several sensitivity analyses for the associations passing the FDR threshold in the discovery analysis and both metrics of α-diversity. We tested the impact of excluding all samples with antibiotic use, adjusting for the Bristol stool chart, excluding follow-up time < 1 year, and using CLR transformation. We also adjusted for self-reported diabetes, current use of antihypertensive medication and cholesterol lowering medication, use of diabetes medication including tablets, non-insulin injectables, or insulin, and combinations of these. In all four cohorts we performed sensitivity analyses to also adjust for current smoking, alcohol consumption, and proton pump inhibitor treatment within 3 months of sampling (Model 2), and for Model 2 covariates plus dietary variables (Model 3). Dietary variables and categories of alcohol consumption were harmonized across cohorts to the extent possible as described in the Supplementary Methods.

Correlations with biomarkers in the HUNT study

To explore the correlations between the relative abundance of species and abundance of KEGG modules with cardiometabolic markers, we analyzed data on body mass index (BMI), waist-hipratio (WHR), systolic blood pressure (SBP), hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), and non-fasting serum levels of triglycerides (TG), non-high density lipoprotein (non-HDL) cholesterol, and C-reactive protein (CRP). Partial Spearman correlations (R package ppcor 38 , v1.1) were estimated for the relative abundance of species or abundance of KEGG modules, adjusting for age, sex, and recent antibiotic use. Sex-stratified analyses adjusting only for age and antibiotic were also performed. For SBP, values were adjusted by adding 15 mmHg for individuals who self-reported antihypertensive medication use. For non-HDL cholesterol, the difference between total cholesterol and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, the total cholesterol levels were divided by 0.8 for individuals who reported lipid-lowering medication use. For HbA1c, individuals who reported a history of diabetes were excluded.

Two-sample Mendelian Randomization

We used denture-wearing as a proxy to assess the causal effect of severe tooth loss and advanced dental disease on the relative abundance of Rothia mucilaginosa_A in the gut. We employed a two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) framework. SNPs serving as instrumental variables (IVs) for the exposure were drawn from the largest available GWAS of self-reported denture use, which combined data from the UK Biobank (UKB) and the Gene-Lifestyle Interactions in Dental Endpoints (GLIDE) consortium 39 . Summary statistics for Rothia mucilaginosa_A came from human genetic associations with relative abundance of gut microbiota species in the HUNT study 32 . Instrumental variants are listed in Supplementary Table 3.

As the primary MR analysis, we used inverse-variance weighted (IVW) estimates with fixed effect, as Cochran's Q test indicated no significant heterogeneity among the genetic instruments. MR-Egger regression was performed as a sensitivity analysis to assess potential directional horizontal pleiotropy 40 . Results

We applied a discovery-replication framework to identify reproducible gut microbiome signatures associated with CVD (Figure 1). Among 11,604 participants in the HUNT discovery cohort, we identified 635 incident CVD cases (5%) during a median follow-up of 5.2 years. The incidence of CVD differed across the three replication cohorts, with the highest incidence observed in SIMPLER (935 cases; 18%) and FINRISK (1,157 cases; 17%), reflecting the higher baseline age in SIMPLER and the longer follow-up time in FINRISK, whereas the lowest incidence was observed in SCAPIS (217 cases; 2%), attributable to its lower baseline age, shorter follow-up time, and the omission of heart failure from the CVD definition (Table 1).

Associations with α-diversity

Higher gut microbiota α-diversity was consistently associated with a lower risk of CVD (Table 2, Supplementary Table 4). In the HUNT study, each standard deviation (SD) increase in the Shannon diversity index was associated with a 9% lower hazard of CVD (HR=0.91; 95% CI 0.84-0.99). Similarly, higher species richness was associated with a lower risk of CVD (HR=0.91; 95% CI 0.84-0.99). In a meta-analysis of the three replication cohorts (SCAPIS, SIMPLER, and FINRISK; Table 2, Supplementary Table 4, Supplementary Figure 1), increases in both the Shannon diversity index (HR=0.90; 95% CI 0.86-0.94) and species richness (HR=0.88; 95% CI 0.85-0.92) were associated with a reduced hazard of incident CVD, with effect estimates closely mirroring those observed in HUNT. There was no significant heterogeneity across the three replication cohorts (Supplementary Table 4).

Associations with metagenomic species

In the HUNT study we identified 16 species associated with incident CVD (5% FDR, Supplementary Table 5, Supplementary Figure 2A). In a meta-analysis of the three replication cohorts, nine of the sixteen species met the predefined statistical significance threshold for replication (Figure 2A, Supplementary Figure 3, Supplementary Table 6), with all nine species exhibiting a consistent direction of effect compared with the discovery cohort. For the replicating species with the lowest p-value, Blautia_A caecimuris, a 1-SD increase in relative abundance was associated with 15% higher hazard of incident CVD (HR=1.15; 95% CI 1.09-1.20). Rothia mucilaginosa_A, Mediterraneibacter torques, and Thomasclavelia ramosa were also significantly associated with a higher hazard of incident CVD, whereas Egerieenecus sp900553315, UBA11524 sp000437595, Blautia_A sp900066205, UMGS882 sp900546385, and Marvinbryantia sp900066075 were associated with a decreased hazard of incident CVD in both the discovery and replication cohorts (Figure 2A, Supplementary Figure 3, Supplementary Table 6). There was no significant heterogeneity across the three replication cohorts (Supplementary Table 6).

Pathways underlying the association between gut microbiota and incident CVD

In the HUNT study, we identified 31 functional KEGG modules associated with incident CVD that remained significant at a 5% FDR (Supplementary Figure 2B, Supplementary Table 7). In a meta-analysis of the two replication cohorts with KEGG data available (SIMPLER, SCAPIS), we replicated 11 of these 31 KEGG modules (Figure 2B, Supplementary Figure 4, Supplementary Table 8). All replicated KEGG pathways were positively associated with incident CVD. A 1-SD increase in the abundance of the most statistically significant KEGG pathway, "Inosine monophosphate biosynthesis, PRPP + glutamine => IMP", was associated with a 17% increased hazard of incident CVD in the HUNT study (HR=1.17; 95% CI 1.08-1.27) and a 15% higher hazard in the replication meta-analysis (HR=1.15; 95% CI 1.09-1.22). For the eleven replicated KEGG modules, the effect estimates were in general comparable between the HUNT discovery cohort and the meta-analyzed replication cohorts, and no significant heterogeneity was observed between the SCAPIS and SIMPLER replication cohorts (Figure 2B, Supplementary Figure 4, Supplementary Table 8).

Correlation analysis between replicated species and replicated functional KEGG modules revealed two general patterns. The first pattern was that the relative abundance of Rothia mucilaginosa_A was positively associated with the abundances of two KEGG modules involved in manganese transport systems, and higher levels of these modules were in turn associated with an increased hazard of incident CVD (Figure 3A, Supplementary Table 9). The second, and more common, pattern was that the relative abundances of all other replicated species associated with increased risk of CVD were positively associated with the remaining replicated KEGG modules, whereas species associated with reduced risk of incident CVD were negatively associated with these modules (Figure 3A, Supplementary Table 9).

We next examined whether the gut microbiome features associated with incident CVD were also related to markers of the metabolic syndrome, a major determinant of cardiovascular risk, as assessed in the HUNT discovery cohort (Supplementary Table 10). Lower gut microbiota αdiversity (Shannon diversity index and species richness), associated with an increased risk of incident CVD, was strongly related to multiple markers of the metabolic syndrome, including elevated waist-to-hip ratio, increased blood pressure, high triglyceride levels, elevated HbA1c, and systemic inflammation as reflected by higher CRP concentrations (Figure 3B, Supplementary Table 11). Eight of the nine replicated species associated with incident CVD were also strongly linked to multiple markers of the metabolic syndrome in the expected direction, consistent with their relationship to incident CVD, with particularly pronounced associations with waist-to-hip ratio, triglyceride levels, blood pressure, and CRP (Figure 3B, Supplementary Table 11). These species were also often strongly associated with circulating metabolites reflecting secondary bile acid metabolism, androgenic steroid metabolism, and benzoate metabolism in the SCAPIS replication cohort (Supplementary Table 12), which could potentially link these gut microbiota-derived pathways to metabolic syndrome and incident CVD risk.

Rothia mucilaginosa_A was not associated with waist-to-hip ratio, triglyceride levels, or blood pressure, but showed an association with markers of systemic inflammation (Figure 3B, Supplementary Table 11). This pattern suggests that a distinct biological pathway, rather than traditional metabolic syndrome-related pathways, may underlie the link between this species and increased risk of CVD. This notion is further supported by the observation that the abundance of the manganese transport system, strongly and directly associated with the relative abundance of Rothia mucilaginosa_A (Figure 3A) and reproducibly linked to increased risk of incident CVD (Figure 2B), was likewise not associated with waist-to-hip ratio, triglyceride levels, or blood pressure, but was associated with systemic inflammation (Figure 3B, Supplementary Table 11).

To further dissect the underlying mechanism, we investigated the association between Rothia mucilaginosa_A and circulating metabolites in the SCAPIS replication cohort. Among the 964 circulating metabolites examined, ImP, a gut microbiota-derived metabolite closely linked to cardiovascular disease, was the metabolite most strongly associated with Rothia mucilaginosa_A (partial Spearman ρ = 0.15; P = 1.1 × 10 -38 ; n = 8351; Supplementary Table 12).

The relative abundance of Rothia mucilaginosa is high in the oral cavity and correlates strongly with its levels in the gut 6 . Furthermore, denture use was associated with a higher relative abundance of Rothia mucilaginosa_A in the gut in the SIMPLER cohort (𝛽 = 0.19; 95% CI 0.12-0.26; adjusted for age, sex, antibiotic use; n = 5,473). The association is almost identical after further adjustment for recent PPI use (𝛽 = 0.25; 95% CI 0.16-0.33) and is only slightly attenuated after additional adjustment for BMI, diabetes, and smoking (𝛽 = 0.21; 95% CI 0.13-0.29). Finally, Mendelian randomization revealed that genetically predicted denture use was causally associated with higher relative abundance of Rothia mucilaginosa_A in the gut (β= 0.25; 95% CI 0.03-0.47, Supplementary Figure 5, Supplementary Table 13). Similar estimates were observed with the weighted median Mendelian randomization approach, and there was no evidence of directional pleiotropy (MR-Egger intercept p=0.43), no heterogeneity (Cochran's Q p=0.23), and no outlier variants detected by MR-PRESSO (Supplementary Table 13).

Thus, for Rothia mucilaginosa A, a prevalent oral-derived gut microbiota species linked to poor oral health, a pathway linking this taxon to increased risk of incident CVD emerges, linked to altered gut microbial manganese transport, elevated circulating levels of ImP, and systemic inflammation (Figure 4). Previous studies have shown that ImP promotes atherosclerosis and is associated with endothelial dysfunction, mechanisms that may contribute to increased risk of CVD 10, 11 (Figure 4).

As a previous report showed that high Rothia mucilaginosa associates with higher coronary artery calcification score with a significant sex interaction 6 , we performed sex-stratified analyses of the association between Rothia mucilaginosa_A and incident CVD (Supplementary Table 14).

Rothia mucilaginosa_A was associated with incident CVD in females (HR=1.33; 95% CI 1.17-1.51) but not males (HR=1.07; 95% CI 0.94-1.21) with a significant interaction with sex (p= 0.0089; Figure 3C, Supplementary Table 14). Similarly, for the functional KEGG module manganese transport system, which was strongly correlated with Rothia mucilaginosa A, we observed a significant association in females (HR=1.31; 95% CI 1.16-1.49) but not in males (HR=1.08; 95% CI 0.97-1.21) with a significant interaction with sex (p=0.012; Figure 3C, Supplementary Table 14). Furthermore, the correlations of both Rothia mucilaginosa_A and the manganese transporter system with CRP appeared slightly more pronounced in females than in males (Figure 3D, Supplementary Table 15). We found no association between circulating estradiol or testosterone and the relative abundance of Rothia mucilaginosa_A in females, indicating that sex steroids do not influence its abundance (Supplementary Table 16).

Sensitivity analyses

In the HUNT discovery study, associations for α-diversity metrics (Supplementary Table 17), species (Supplementary Table 18), and KEGG modules (Supplementary Table 19) with incident CVD remained largely unchanged after adjustment for Bristol stool scale (a proxy for bowel motility), exclusion of participants on antibiotic treatment within 3 months of sampling, removal of participants with an event in the first year of follow-up and when using CLR transformation. In the HUNT study, both diversity indices were no longer significant after adjusting for combinations of diabetes and medications for diabetes, cholesterol, and blood pressure, but the hazard ratios remained similar between 0.92 and 0.94 (Supplementary Table 17). However, all metagenomic species and KEGG module associations that passed the FDR threshold in the discovery analysis remained nominally significant (p-value <0.05) with very similar hazard ratio estimates after adjusting for combinations of diabetes and medications (Supplementary Table 18-19). In general, the effect estimates were robust to further adjustment for alcohol consumption, current smoking status, and use of proton pump inhibitors (Model 2) and diet (Model 3) both in the discovery cohort and in the replication meta-analysis (Supplementary Figures 1, 3, 4, Supplementary Tables 20 21 22 23 24 25).

Discussion

Prior studies have linked gut microbiota to cardiovascular health, but most were cross-sectional, small, used 16S rRNA sequencing, and/or unreplicated 3, 17, [44] [45] [46] [47] [48] . Thus, microbial features consistently associated with incident CVD remain insufficiently defined. In the present study, we examined stool-derived metagenomes within a discovery-replication framework across four large Nordic cohorts (N=32,569; n=2,944 incident CVD events across the four cohorts). We identified the first gut microbiome features reproducibly associated with incident CVD, including two indices of α-diversity, nine species, and eleven functional KEGG modules. We observed two main pathways for these associations between the gut microbiome and incident CVD. The first pathway involved the prevalent oral-gut species Rothia mucilaginosa_A, linking it to increased incident CVD risk through affected gut microbial manganese transport, elevated circulating ImP, and systemic inflammation, and this relationship appeared to be female-specific. The second pathway involved -diversity metrics and the remaining eight species, which were strongly linked to metabolic syndrome. Collectively, these results highlight two principal pathways through which the gut microbiome may be linked to CVD (Figure 4). CVD incidence and severity differ markedly by sex and gender 49, 50 . There is a clear protection against CVD in premenopausal women, linked to the beneficial effects of estrogens in preventing atherosclerosis 51 . As a result, men display CVD at younger ages than women, with an estimation of approximately 7-years earlier development 52 . However, women experience a substantial postmenopausal rise in CVD. Beyond sex steroids, there remains an important gap in female-specific biomarkers and pathways for cardiovascular disease 53, 54 . Notably, we found that Rothia mucilaginosa_A was associated with incident CVD exclusively in females. Moreover, Rothia mucilaginosa_A showed no associations with waist-to-hip ratio, triglyceride levels, or blood pressure, while demonstrating a link with systemic inflammation, suggesting that a distinct biological pathway, rather than traditional metabolic syndrome-related pathways, may underlie its association with increased CVD risk in females.

Furthermore, the abundance of the functional KEGG module for manganese transport system, which was strongly correlated with Rothia mucilaginosa_A, was also linked to increased risk of incident CVD specifically in females, while showing no associations with metabolic-syndromerelated parameters. Manganese is a cofactor that protects against oxidative stress 55 and may be involved in competitive dynamics in the gut microbiome 56 . Manganese homeostasis is an essential component of the host-pathogen interface 57 with dietary manganese protecting bacteria from the immune system in a mouse model of bacterial endocarditis 58 . Circulating manganese has been reported to be associated with cardiovascular disease, with some evidence suggesting that these associations may be more pronounced in women [59] [60] [61] . Further studies are required to clarify the relevance of gut microbiota-mediated manganese transport for CVD in women.

Given the observational and causal evidence that denture use, serving as a proxy for longstanding poor oral health, is associated with the relative abundance of Rothia mucilaginosa_A, these findings raise the possibility that this species may, at least in part, mediate the previously reported association between poor oral health and CVD 62 . Further studies are needed to clarify this potential pathway and its implications for CVD prevention. Interestingly, circulating ImP, a gut microbiota-derived metabolite closely linked to CVD 10, 11 , was the metabolite most strongly associated with relative abundance of Rothia mucilaginosa_A. ImP has been proposed to drive atherosclerosis through the imidazoline-1 receptor (I1R) in myeloid cells, and pharmacological blockade of ImP binding to I1R inhibited atherosclerotic progression and inflammation in mice, identifying the ImP-I1R axis as a potential therapeutic target 63 . Thus, for Rothia mucilaginosa_A, a prevalent oral gut microbiota species, a female-specific pathway linking this species to increased risk of incident CVD emerges that may involve affected manganese handling, elevated circulating ImP, and systemic inflammation (Figure 4). Although we consider a gut-derived pathway the most plausible explanation, we cannot exclude that Rothia mucilaginosa_A may also exert direct adverse cardiovascular effects. This notion is supported by reports describing the species as an opportunistic pathogen capable of hematogenous spread in vulnerable populations 64 and documented presence in endocarditic lesions 65, 66.

The second pattern linked incident CVD to gut microbiome parameters, including α-diversity metrics and eight species which were also strongly linked to multiple metabolic-syndrome markers in the expected direction. Low α-diversity, reflected by reduced Shannon diversity index and species richness, was associated with a higher risk of CVD as well as elevated waist-to-hip ratio, systolic blood pressure, triglycerides, HbA1c, and CRP. These are characteristic features of the metabolic syndrome which is closely linked to increased cardiovascular risk 67, 68 . This metabolic syndrome-associated pathway was also associated with several functional gut microbiota-associated pathways reflecting purine metabolism, microbial stress responses, altered redox balance, ascorbic acid degradation, and branched-chain amino acid biosynthesis.

Our observation that lower Shannon diversity index and species richness were consistently associated with a higher risk of incident CVD aligns with prior cross-sectional findings from the Framingham Heart Study, demonstrating that decreased gut microbiota α-diversity was associated with an increased calculated 10-year risk of CVD 3 . We also observed that low α-diversity was associated with high CRP, reflecting systemic inflammation. Collectively, these findings underscore that reduced gut microbiota α-diversity, a signature of the metabolic syndrome and an unstable and inflammation-prone gut ecosystem, marks increased cardiovascular risk.

Marvinbryantia sp900066075, the metabolic-syndrome-related species most strongly associated with reduced risk of incident CVD in the discovery cohort, belongs to a genus positively associated with dietary fiber intake, favorable metabolic trait profiles, and lower odds of type 2 diabetes 69 . This species remained significantly associated with CVD after adjusting for diet. The only formally characterized species in the genus is Marvinbryantia formatexigens which ferments carbohydrates to the short-chain fatty acid acetate. Two of the species identified here, UBA11524 sp000437595 and Blautia_A sp900066205 were recently associated with glomerular filtration rate 70 , suggesting a potential link between gut microbial composition, renal function, and cardiovascular health.

The most significant metabolic syndrome-related and replicated functional KEGG module was the inosine monophosphate (IMP) biosynthesis pathway which creates a key precursor for building blocks of DNA, RNA, and cellular energy. Microbes with affected purine biosynthetic capacity may contribute to pro-inflammatory and pro-oxidative host environments relevant to CVD through metabolites generated in this pathway, including uric acid, its terminal product 71.

The association between elevated abundance of the microbial osmoprotectant systems and increased CVD risk suggests adaptation to hyperosmotic stress, which may arise during dehydration, high salt intake, or intestinal inflammation. This may contribute to cardiometabolic risk by promoting metabolic dysregulation and systemic inflammation and by reflecting a gut microbial stress phenotype linked to atherogenesis 72 . These associations for functional KEGG modules with incident CVD suggest gut microbial features that may promote low-grade chronic inflammation, providing a biological basis for atherosclerotic plaque progression and thrombosis and thereby contributing to CVD.

We observed that increased relative abundance of Mediterraneibacter torques was associated with increased risk of CVD and was significantly positively correlated with all tested metabolic parameters. This is consistent with previous studies linking Mediterraneibacter torques to high blood pressure and adiposity 73, 74 . In addition, Mediterraneibacter torques is a specialist in mucin degradation and has been associated with intestinal inflammation, potentially driven by increased gut permeability resulting from reduced mucin-protective layers, thereby promoting immune activation and systemic inflammation.

Our study has several strengths. It is by far the largest investigation conducted to date and includes a successful replication phase with complete follow-up enabled through linkage to high-quality Nordic registers. We used metagenomic sequencing rather than 16S rRNA gene sequencing, which enables species-level resolution and functional characterization. Because we were able to perform sensitivity analyses accounting for diet as well as proton pump inhibitor and antibiotic use, we could adjust for notable confounders. Finally, our study was conducted in populations with deep phenotyping, enabling the investigation of potential underlying mechanisms.

Our findings should be interpreted in the context of the study limitations. First, our analysis only indicates microbiome features correlated with, but not necessarily causal for, CVD. We rely on diagnosis codes from hospitals and death registries, rather than validated outcomes, which may be imperfect and result in misclassification. Furthermore, only prescribed medications are captured in the prescription databases, so the binary PPI exposure variable may underestimate true PPI usage. Although metagenomic sequencing allows species-level resolution, some disease-causing metabolic activities of the gut microbiome may exist at the strain level, which we were unable to account for. Residual confounding by unmeasured factors or by imprecise measurement of adjusted covariates such as self-reported diet, remains possible. Finally, our findings may not be generalizable to other global populations outside of the Nordic region.

The use of the CVD outcome has advantages and disadvantages. The composite CVD outcome combines cardiovascular outcomes with partially distinct pathophysiology. While myocardial infarction and stroke are closely related to atherothrombosis and plaque instability, heart failure reflects more heterogeneous mechanisms, from arrhythmias to valvular disease. However, the composite CVD endpoint is frequently used in randomized clinical trials, so our findings are informative for future interventional studies. In addition, combining the cardiovascular outcomes into a composite outcome likely aided in statistical power to identify associations. While the CVD definition was harmonized across cohorts, there was some heterogeneity, for example, data for heart failure was not available for SCAPIS.

In conclusion, we identified gut microbiome features reproducibly associated with incident CVD.

We observed two main pathways for these associations between gut microbiota and incident CVD. The first pathway was female-specific and involved the oral-gut species Rothia mucilaginosa_A, which was enriched among denture users, serving as a proxy for longstanding poor oral health. This pathway linked Rothia mucilaginosa_A to increased incident CVD risk through altered manganese transport, elevated circulating ImP levels, and heightened systemic inflammation. (Figure 4). The second more general pathway involved -diversity metrics and eight species, which were strongly associated with multiple markers of the metabolic syndrome.

These findings indicate that gut microbiota diversity, specific taxa, and functional pathways may provide new insights into the etiology of CVD and may ultimately help guide its prevention and treatment.
