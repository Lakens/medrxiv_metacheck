Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. Abstract

Background: Asthma is a chronic, multifactorial disease that progresses in episodes and requires effective coordination of care between healthcare professionals and patients.

Objective: To describe and evaluate pre-hospital therapeutic management of asthma attacks.

Methods: Cross-sectional, prospective, single-centre, observational and descriptive study of patients consulting for asthma attacks at the Fort de France paediatric emergency department between July and November 2024. All patients included were children between the ages of 1 and 18 who had already been diagnosed with asthma by a doctor and who presented to the emergency department for an asthma attack. Several other parameters were assessed, such as the parents' perception of the emergency situation, the child's asthma control in the month prior to the attack, the child's usual medical follow-up, and their usual treatment.

Results: 175 patients were included in the study. Eight patients were excluded from the study because their questionnaire had a response rate of less than 50%.

Of the 175 patients included, only 6.7% received perfect pre-hospital care, 29.3% received good quality care, 36% received moderate quality care, and 10.3% received poor quality care before arriving at the emergency department. (17.7% did not complete all the items necessary to calculate the score). 11.6% of children did not receive treatment before consulting the emergency department. Among the 88.9% who took short-acting β2 agonists, only 52% received at least 3 series and 66.2% were given doses appropriate for their weight. 21.6% received oral corticosteroids before arriving at the emergency department.

The management of asthma attacks outside of hospital settings is not optimal for a large proportion of patients in the study. Several areas for improvement have been identified and require joint action by healthcare professionals.

INTRODUCTION 1 -Epidemiology

Asthma is a chronic respiratory disease with a high prevalence in the paediatric population. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), there are currently an estimated 262 million patients with asthma, and the estimated number of asthma-related deaths worldwide is 455,000 per year. (1) In France, asthma affects 4 million people and it is estimated that 10 to 12% of the population will have asthma during their lifetime. It currently causes more than 60,000 hospitalisations and 900 deaths per year. (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?UArTcZ) Among these deaths, around twenty are children.

Current epidemiological data show a gradual increase in the prevalence of asthma, but a decrease in mortality in France. The prevalence of asthma in children in the French West Indies is estimated at 15 to 20%, compared with 10 to 12% for children in mainland France, all regions combined. (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?ChfmT3) This difference has not yet been clearly explained, although several hypotheses have been put forward, including higher humidity levels conducive to mould growth and the sandstorm phenomenon, which is well known in the Caribbean. A medical thesis completed in Guadeloupe in 2011 shows a correlation between sandstorm episodes and an increase in consultations for asthma exacerbations. (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?rgPtLR)

2 -Clinical aspects

Asthma is a chronic inflammatory respiratory disease that manifests itself in attacks of varying intensity, which may resolve spontaneously or lead to severe respiratory distress and even death.

Clinically, it is defined by a combination of several clinical signs: wheezing, dyspnoea, coughing, chest tightness and limited expiratory airflow. The clinical presentation varies, particularly in terms of symptom intensity, depending on the attack and the individual.

The diagnosis is based primarily on the combination of symptoms presented by the patient and their progression over time. It will be confirmed by several paraclinical tests carried out outside of attacks (respiratory function testing, peak flow, stress test), in order to make a definitive diagnosis and refine the therapeutic management.

The clinical expression of asthma is highly variable, and asthma is currently classified into different clinical-biological phenotypes: (5) • eosinophilic asthma • neutrophilic asthma • asthma with predominant cough • late-onset asthma (first symptoms in adulthood) • asthma with persistent ventilatory limitation • asthma with obesity

3 -Risk factors

The factors contributing to asthma attacks are numerous, multifactorial and sometimes poorly understood. They make it difficult for health and public health authorities to control and prevent asthma attacks in the general population.

These include allergens, irritants (such as tobacco smoke) (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?7vVeWN air pollutants (such as fine particles), intense physical activity, climates (humid and hot), and viral respiratory infections (RSV/adenovirus, etc.). In certain situations, several interrelated risk factors can lead to an attack, hence the importance of isolating the causative agents in order to adapt the environment and lifestyle habits of patients.

Numerous genes that may contribute to asthma have been isolatedhttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?D1dkcR particularly in certain regions of chromosome 17 (7) , however, this knowledge is limited to the field of research and its incorporation into clinical practice is currently limited. There is no organised screening for asthma to date.

4 -Evolution

Asthma most often manifests itself in childhood. It can persist into adulthood, manifest itself subclinically, or sometimes fade over time. (8) The impact on the quality of life of patients and their loved ones (particularly parents of children with asthma) is significant.

The frequency and intensity of exacerbations can lead to repeated visits to healthcare facilities. (9) Exacerbations manifest as respiratory distress, which can sometimes be severe, with symptoms that are often very noisy and anxiety-inducing for the patient and those accompanying them. This can lead to repeated visits to emergency departments and a deterioration in quality of life in the short to medium term.

4 .

The repercussions can be long-term, as there is a close link between the severity of asthma in childhood and the risk of developing chronic obstructive pulmonary disease in adulthood. (10) To date, asthma-related morbidity remains high, and despite advances in treatment and support, asthma is a public health priority in France and around the world.

5 -Crisis treatments

Short-acting β2 agonists (SABAs) are the cornerstone of emergency management.

Their use is standardised and the dose administered will depend on the clinical severity of the attack and the patient's weight. (11) The recommendations of the SP2A (Pediatric Pulmonology and Allergology Society) define the management of moderate to severe asthma attacks as appropriate if: ❖ Administration of short-acting β2 agonists ❖ Adjusted to the patient's weight (4 to 12 puffs per series) ❖ At least 3 series administered ❖ 20-minute interval between SABA series ❖ Oral corticosteroids administered from the 3rd SABA series onwards ❖ Call the emergency number if symptoms persist and signs of severity are present The administration of SABA requires the use of an inhalation chamber with a mask (for children aged 0 to 3 years) or a mouthpiece (for children aged 4 to 12 years). Scores are used by medical teams to assess the severity of an attack. Several scores have been validated to date, including the Asthma Severity Score, PRAM and Pulmonary Index Score, and their use depends on the centre. The PRAM score is currently used in the Martinique pediatrics emergency department. (appendix 1) It includes several clinical warning signs that parents should look out for.

A moderate asthma attack (PRAM 4) or one with a risk of complications according to the recommendations, the following treatment should be given: ❖ Inhale four to twelve puffs of SABA adjusted to the patient's weight, every twenty minutes three times, then every one to four hours if symptoms improve.

❖ If symptoms persist after the third course, oral corticosteroids should be prescribed at a dosage appropriate for the patient's weight, not exceeding 60 mg/day of prednisolone.

. ❖ If symptoms persist after the third series, or if there are signs of respiratory distress or clinical severity criteria, call the emergency services.

For mild attacks (PRAM 1 to 3), with isolated coughing or mild symptoms, recommendations are not fixed and are often left to the discretion of the practitioner. One possible treatment option is as follows: ❖ Inhale four puffs of SABA, repeating every twenty minutes if necessary.

❖ If symptoms worsen, follow the protocol for moderate or severe asthma attacks.

According to the GINA 2024 guidelines, an alternative treatment is preferred for patients aged 12 years and older: ❖ The combination of inhaled corticosteroids and formoterol would be preferred for initiating maintenance treatment and could be used during attacks in patients receiving this maintenance treatment.

❖ The maximum number of inhalations recommended before seeking emergency medical attention is height in one day.

The goal is to limit the number of treatments patients need to take and to promote patient adherence to treatment.

6 -Chronic treatments

The first-line chronic treatments are inhaled corticosteroids (ICS). They are administered daily and their dosage and use of the inhalation device must be reassessed regularly. Their use reduces asthma-related mortality, the number of hospitalizations, the frequency and severity of asthma attacks. They also improve symptom control and quality of life, and preserve respiratory function. (12) Additional treatments may be prescribed for asthma that is not controlled by ICS alone. ICS may be combined with long-acting bronchodilators (LABA), anticholinergics, or leukotriene antagonists (LA).

For asthma that is resistant to high doses of ICS (step 5), biotherapies may be initiated in specialist centers in accordance with recent recommendations from the French Pediatric Pulmonology and Allergology Society. (11) According to the GINA 2024 guidelines, an alternative treatment is preferred for patients aged 12 years and older, although the ICS +/-SABA option remains among the treatment options. 6.

7 -Healthcare services in Martinique

The management of asthma requires effective coordination between various medical and paramedical professionals.

The general practitioner plays a central role in the overall management of children with asthma, but various other healthcare professionals are needed to ensure proper follow-up. Specialists (pulmonologists, pediatric pulmonologists, allergists) have a role to play, particularly in cases of moderate to severe asthma requiring more intensive treatment.

Pediatric emergency services are necessary when attacks are poorly controlled despite outpatient treatment. Asthma education in hospital through the asthma school, plays a vital role in prevention and teaching children and their parents about asthma. (13) Several obstacles to the proper monitoring of asthma patients currently exist in Martinique and France. Healthcare provision is an integral part of the factors influencing the proper care of children with asthma. There is currently a wide variety of healthcare provision at the national level. This heterogeneity in access to healthcare providers is illustrated by comparing healthcare provision between different departments and, on a smaller scale, between rural areas and cities. Thanks to the government's regional observatory website (14) https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?mHE2yc we were able to compare healthcare provision in Martinique with that in the Pyrénées-Atlantiques department.

In terms of demographics, the Pyrénées-Atlantiques department had a population of 706,360 in 2023. There are 787 general practitioners practicing in this area, 56 private pulmonologists, and 37 private pediatricians. In comparison, Martinique had approximately 7 . 350,000 inhabitants in 2023. There were 330 private general practitioners. There are 6 private pulmonologists and 7 private pediatricians. Although outpatient consultations are organized by specialists at the University of Martinique Hospital to meet demand (in pediatrics and pulmonology), access to specialist consultations is objectively lower in Martinique.

The provision of general medical care, if we compare the number of general practitioners to the number of inhabitants, is fairly similar (1 general practitioner for every 897 inhabitants in the Pyrénées-Atlantiques, compared to 1 for every 1,060 inhabitants in Martinique).

8 -Asthma school

In pediatrics, parents play a fundamental role in the medical care of their children.

Among the skills they need to acquire, we will ask them to learn how to recognize an asthma attack, how to recognize the signs of a severe attack, how to administer the necessary emergency treatment by following the action plan, how to use medical devices at home, and how to alert medical personnel at the right time.

By detailing this knowledge, we can understand the importance of education and prevention in ensuring that the child and those around them are prepared in the event of an exacerbation. In addition, the child must be quickly included in the medical discussion during consultations in order to gradually develop autonomy in their care. All these areas of competence can only be recorded and understood through regular monitoring, consultations focused on prevention, and good coordination between healthcare professionals.

It was precisely in this context that the asthma school was invented in 1992 by the "Asthme et allergie" association in mainland France.

The University of Martinique Hospital has its own asthma school, accredited by the regional health agency since 2015, which provides therapeutic education and a prevention space for families of children with asthma.

It forms an important pillar of care for asthma patients in Martinique through awareness-raising meetings with groups of parents, as well as individualized consultations that allow for a personalized approach to care.

8 .

According to our data, there were approximately 1,600 visits to the pediatric emergency department at the University of Martinique Hospital between January 2023 and December 2023 for asthma attacks. This represents the second most common reason for emergency room visits during this period (after fever), accounting for approximately 7% of visits and requiring hospitalization in 9.6% of cases, representing 154 hospitalizations.

A certain proportion of patients consulting for asthma attacks at the MFME pediatric emergency department did not receive appropriate treatment prior to their arrival, and a significant proportion presented with symptoms classified as mild asthma attacks (according to the PRAM classification). Based on this assumption, we decided to initiate this study.

The main objective of this study is to evaluate the management of asthma attacks prior to consultation at the pediatric emergency department at the University of Martinique Hospital.

Secondly, we will explore data that may explain the poor management of exacerbations in asthmatic children in the pre-hospital setting, by assessing asthma control prior to the attack and describing the medical follow-up of asthmatic patients. The secondary objective is to propose areas for improvement in the medical and paramedical management of asthmatic children in Martinique.

MATERIALS AND METHODS: 1 -Study design

We conducted a prospective, descriptive, single-center, cross-sectional study including children residing in Martinique aged between 1 and 18 years old, previously diagnosed with asthma and attending the pediatric emergency department in the university hospital of Martinique due to an asthma exacerbation. We chose a prospective study in order to increase its power, defining in advance the most relevant clinical data to be collected.

Secondly, we examined asthma control in each patient during the month preceding the crisis using the asthma control criteria set out in the GINA guidelines. Several other data were analyzed, such as the patient's usual medical follow-up, the parents' feelings about the attack, and the current maintenance treatments. Certain data, such as weight, treatments administered in the emergency department, and parameters on admission to the emergency department, were retrieved retrospectively in order to refine our analyses.

2 -Variables

For patient recruitment, questionnaires (Supplementary 1) were distributed in the pediatric emergency department in the university hospital of Martinique to parents consulting for asthma attacks. We took advantage of the long duration of care in the emergency department, due to the frequent need for short-acting bronchodilator inhalation, to distribute the questionnaires to parents accompanying their children, thereby theoretically increasing the response rate among parents. The questionnaire was distributed by the physicians in charge of the asthmatic patient once the clinical evaluation had been completed and treatment had begun. This questionnaire has four different sections: factors motivating the visit to the emergency room, asthma control criteria, asthma history, parents' knowledge, and treatments administered at home for the current attack.

The questionnaire takes approximately 15-20 minutes to complete and contains 34 items to check off.

The questionnaires were distributed over a four-month period from July 1 to November 1, 2024. The study focused on evaluating pre-hospital care for asthma attacks in children aged 1 to 18 years, and we included all asthma attacks in patients with known asthma in this age group.

3 -Eligibility criteria

Patients had to meet the clinical and paraclinical criteria for diagnosing an asthma attack, such as dyspnea, wheezing, coughing, and auscultatory wheezing. These attack criteria were not clearly established or imposed on the practitioner. We therefore implicitly accepted each diagnosis once it had been made.

The practitioners who could administer the questionnaires were as follows: senior physicians, residents specializing in general medicine, pediatrics, and emergency medicine working in the pediatric emergency department.

4 -Exclusion criteria

We excluded any child presenting their first asthma attack (previously undiagnosed asthma). Patients not residing in Martinique. Patients with a differential diagnosis of dyspnea (such as pneumonia). Patients not accompanied by one of their parents.

We also excluded patients under one year of age (to avoid selection bias related to the bronchiolitis epidemic prevalent during the winter) and patients over 18 years of age.

Questionnaires with less than 50% of questions answered were excluded.

5.1 Crisis treatment

For the therapeutic assessment of asthma attacks in the pre-hospital setting, there are numerous recommendations, and these are not entirely consistent across countries and scientific committees. We referred to the tools used in the pediatric emergency department in the university hospital of Martinique to achieve local and regional consistency in the use of emergency treatments for asthma patients.

We therefore relied on the recommendations of the French university hospital Trousseau in Paris, particularly its emergency action plan, to determine the doses of short-acting bronchodilators to be administered based on the child's weight. (15) We also used this action plan to determine the time interval between doses of SABA and the timing of oral corticosteroid administration if signs of respiratory distress persisted. Several criteria had to be taken into account in order to define the quality of therapeutic management of asthma attacks. We therefore developed a score ranging from 0 to 10 in order to assess this as accurately as possible.

5.2 Asthma control criteria

For the asthma control criterion, we referred to the GINA 2024 recommendations.

Asthma control is divided into 3 subgroups based on the symptoms reported by the children during the month preceding the asthma attack.

❖ Controlled (if none of the items are checked).

❖ Partially controlled (if 1 or 2 items are checked).

❖ Uncontrolled (if more than 2 items are checked).

The items in question are as follows: use of SABA more than once a week for children under 6 years of age and more than twice a week for children over 6 years of age; a nighttime cough; asthma-related activity limitations; and daytime symptoms more than twice a week. This assessment of asthma control will help identify children with poor asthma control who may require an adjustment to their maintenance therapy. 5.3 Criteria for optimal medical care

For the criteria for optimal medical care of patients with asthma, we referred to GINA 2024, which defines several criteria for effective disease management: 12 . ○ A follow-up visit for asthma within 6 months or 1 year if there are no changes to the treatment regimen, depending on the severity of the asthma. Note that severity is defined by the intensity of treatment required to control the asthma, which is why it is necessary to continually reassess maintenance therapy to determine the minimum effective doses.

○ If a change is made to the maintenance therapy, a follow-up visit must be scheduled in 3 months. ○ Multidisciplinary care is not recommended given the risk that conflicting messages may lead to a disruption in care. Optimal coordination among the various stakeholders is therefore essential. ○ A child with asthma who is over 6 years old is eligible for a pulmonary function testing, which is necessary to assess asthma control in these patients (and thus to adjust their long-term treatment). (17) ○ A pulmonary function test should be performed at least once a year if maintenance therapy is necessary to control asthma. Specialized follow-up care (by a pulmonologist or pediatric pulmonologist) is necessary depending on the severity of the disease (which corresponds to the intensity of the maintenance therapy required to control asthma).

6 -Statistical method

The population is described in terms of its count and percentage for qualitative and categorical variables, and in terms of the median and the 25th and 75th percentiles for quantitative variables.

The size effect was calculated for continuous quantitative variables using a standardized mean difference and for binary variables using the Mahalanobis distance, with a 95% confidence interval calculated.

These analyses, as well as the creation of the figures, were performed using custom Python code (Python 3.9). The p-value was calculated using the scipy package. DISCUSSION:

This study was conducted as a cross-sectional, prospective study; the response rate to the questionnaire was good, allowing for the enrollment of 175 patients out of the 181 questionnaires distributed during the period from July through the end of October 2024.

The primary objective of the study was to describe and evaluate the management of asthma attacks in children aged 1 to 18 years prior to presentation at the pediatric emergency department. One of the reasons for conducting this study is the high prevalence of asthma in Martinique, as well as the significant challenges in managing this chronic disease, due to the variety of factors required for its optimal clinical control.

1 -Characteristics of the study population

The vast majority of patients included in the study were between 1 and 11 years old, accounting for 86.3% of the study population.

Adolescents aged 12 to 18 accounted for 13.7% of the study population.

Adolescents are a difficult-to-treat population; they rarely seek medical care and tend to present to the emergency department only when their asthma has actually deteriorated significantly, with severe asthma attack criteria, thereby increasing the risk of serious respiratory decompensation and admission to the intensive care unit. (18) Boys are overrepresented compared to girls, accounting for 61.7% of the completed questionnaires. These figures are not surprising, since girls typically develop asthma symptoms primarily during adolescence.(https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?kkvcM5) -Analysis of the results

2.1 Analysis of the primary criterion

Several factors come into play in the proper management of an asthma attack-both from a therapeutic standpoint, involving various treatments, and from a behavioral standpoint, particularly the ability to recognize signs of respiratory distress, the ability to alert others, and the appropriate waiting time before seeking emergency care.

This multifactorial nature has made it difficult to accurately assess the management of asthma attacks in the prehospital setting. To evaluate it comprehensively, we developed a score ranging from 0 to 10 that combines several management criteria we consider important.

To develop this score, we ranked the priority interventions for the proper management of asthma attacks in children.

We felt that the most important intervention was SABA treatment at home (3 points).

The results are detailed in Table 2. Slightly more than 1 in 10 patients (11.1%) did not receive a SABA before their emergency department visit. Among the 88.9% who took SABA, 52.5% administered at least 3 doses. (2 points) Furthermore, 68% of patients had a valid prescription for Ventolin or Ventolin at home. This finding highlights the limitations of attributing poor prehospital therapeutic management solely to parents; a lack of follow-up and foresight can affect asthma attacks.

Management of an asthma attack involves two distinct approaches depending on the severity and tolerance of the attack. If symptoms are mild, it may be recommended to space out SABA doses by 4 hours and repeat as needed until symptoms improve. If the attack worsens or is moderate or severe from the outset, administering SABA every 20 minutes for 1 hour is indicated. The questionnaire did not distinguish between these two situations and could therefore be confusing. We decided to award only one point for this parameter, and 66.2% of patients maintained an interval of <1 hour between Ventolin doses.

The instruction to call the emergency medical services is included in the action plan recommendations and practices vary by approximately 2 puffs depending on the child's weight. Overall, 47.1% of patients received a SABA dose appropriate for their weight. We chose not to include the time elapsed before seeking emergency care in the score, since some patients reported noticing the first signs the day before and then observing an unfavorable progression (thus estimating a delay of >24 hours even though their response to the crisis was appropriate).

2.2 Analysis of secondary objectives

2.2.1 Asthma control

To assess asthma control, we relied-as described in the methods section-on the GINA 2024 recommendations.

We found that 35.4% of the children were well-controlled in the month prior to the crisis. 33.1% were partially controlled, and 24% were uncontrolled.

"Among uncontrolled and partially controlled patients, 53.3% saw their doctor within 6 months" (Figure 2). This estimate is likely an overestimate, given that the study period included the start of the school year. It is possible that many of the reported follow-up visits were solely for the purpose of completing the individualized welcome plan required by elementary and middle schools. Slightly fewer than one in two patients therefore showed signs of poor asthma control in the previous month; these warning signs-such as a loss of control over asthma symptoms-should, in theory, alert the family and lead to a medical consultation for reevaluation.

One of the factors complicating this analysis is the inclusion of patients currently under follow-up for poorly controlled asthma who may be in the therapeutic adjustment phase.

We investigated whether there might be a link between the degree of asthma control and the potential severity of an asthma attack in our population.

We observed that the number of patients treated in the emergency resuscitation unit was higher among well-controlled patients, but that the number of children transferred to the intensive care unit was higher among partially or poorly controlled patients (3 in total). Given the small proportion of patients requiring intensive care (3 out of 174), it is difficult to draw conclusions about a causal relationship.

Some studies have found a causal link between poor clinical control and morbidity and mortality associated with asthma attacks. In particular, by assessing the excessive use of SABAs over a given period-an indirect marker of asthma control-the results show a higher risk of asthma exacerbations and asthma-related mortality in this population.(https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?nhaPKa) (22) Furthermore, anyone with asthma is at risk of a severe attack, which is why monitoring and prevention are so important. Even in patients with few symptoms-who haven't had an asthma attack in years-it's essential to have the tools to deal with one if it occurs.

In 2009, a French study highlighted the low proportion of patients with controlled asthma among those seeking care at the emergency room. (23) In this study, conducted in primary care settings, 3,431 patients were included; of these, 26% had good asthma control, 41.3% had moderate control, and 32.7% had poor asthma control. This is an older but compelling study, with a different research approach (recruitment from primary care settings). Despite this, the percentage of patients with well-controlled asthma in our study is higher. Although 13 years have passed since that study, and setting aside any differences and biases, we can assume that asthma control is slightly better today in Martinique than it was in mainland France in 2009.

2.2.2 Medical management of asthma

According to current GINA recommendations, a child with asthma should be monitored by a primary care physician without the need for a specialist consultation if the asthma is well-controlled and of mild severity. It should be noted that the definition of asthma severity depends on the intensity of the maintenance therapy prescribed to control symptoms. Referral to a specialist should therefore be considered if asthma control proves 27.

difficult and requires an increase in dosage or a change in the type of maintenance therapy, classifying the asthma as moderate to severe.

In our study, 22.3% of parents reported that their child's asthma was not being medically managed. This represents nearly 1 in 5 children who visit the emergency room.

Among children receiving medical care, 54.8% of those under 6 years of age are managed by their primary care physician, and 16.7% of them are seen by a pediatrician. The transition from pediatric care to primary care appears to occur around age 6, since 70.3% of children over 6 years of age receive asthma care from their general practitioner.

Medical management of asthma by an allergist is around 25% among patients over 6 years of age, compared to nearly 12% managed by a pulmonologist. There may be a bias in these data, since one pulmonologist (out of the six private-practice pulmonologists in Martinique) specializes in allergology.

As described in the introduction, the availability of care in Martinique is limited in the private-practice specialties of pulmonology, allergology, and pediatrics. General practitioners therefore play a central role in the care of patients with asthma, regardless of their age.

In terms of follow-up frequency, 51.5% of patients reported having had a follow-up visit for asthma within the previous 6 months, and 70% within the past year.

Medical recommendations for the frequency of follow-up visits for asthma are as follows: 3 months after starting a new treatment or making a recent change in therapy, then every 6 to 12 months depending on the severity and control of the asthma.

Our study does not examine the quality of follow-up visits or the topics discussed during these visits-particularly those focused on prevention and patient education. This could be an area for evaluation and improvement in the future.

One of the most notable figures in this study is the percentage of children with asthma who are on maintenance therapy-only 46.8% (Table 5). The proportions vary little across the different age groups.

The range of treatments used in this study is limited, even though many treatment options are available. This may be due to prescribing practices. One limitation of our assessment is the lack of data on the exact doses administered (which could reveal cases of treatment failure and indications for treatment changes).

2.2.3 Personal experience with an asthma attack

Parents' experiences are an important factor to consider. A qualitative study showed that parents still had significant concerns about the side effects of asthma treatments, and that these concerns could interfere with the administration of treatments at home. (28) If we objectively compare the estimate that all available resources were exhausted with the proportion of patients who received at least three doses of SABA (82 out of 174, or 47.1%) or the number of patients who received oral corticosteroids before arriving at the emergency department (21.6%); a gap becomes evident between parents' perceptions and the actual therapeutic management of asthma attacks.

The morbidity associated with asthma is significant among both children and their caregivers. (9) The repercussions for children are social (school absenteeism, reduced participation in sports) as well as psychological (isolation, stigmatization) and can adversely affect their overall health.

2.2.4 Medical care in the pediatric emergency department

Table 3 shows that 32.3% of patients had a PRAM score between 0 and 3 upon arrival at the emergency department. This means that nearly 1 in 3 children presenting to the emergency department had symptoms of a mild asthma attack. These figures highlight the need for therapeutic education for parents on understanding and recognizing the signs of a severe asthma attack. This aspect of prevention is particularly challenging to address given the emotional component, parents' lack of theoretical knowledge, and their lack of 30.

experience with asthma attacks. These figures do not take the patients' medical history into account. Patients may have exhausted all available treatments and, due to the persistence of their symptoms, sought care in the emergency department.

One of the interesting variables in table 4 is the time spent in the emergency room, which averages 5 hours and 20 minutes across all age groups. Since exacerbations often occur at night, one can imagine the impact an episode can have on the following days, and the resulting decline in quality of life for patients whose condition is not well controlled.

76.7% of patients receive oral or systemic corticosteroid therapy, while 67.7% of patients present with a moderate-to-severe attack upon arrival. Treatment with oral corticosteroids is not indicated for mild attacks (PRAM 0-3). These figures may suggest that treatment in the emergency department is sometimes excessive, but they may also reflect the rapid clinical worsening of attacks in some patients. This interindividual variability can undermine standardized care protocols and requires additional vigilance on the part of medical staff.

2.3 Comparison with the literature

A fairly similar study was conducted in France at the pediatric emergency department in Clermont-Ferrand between November 2010 and February 2011. ( 26) This is a prospective, descriptive study that aims to evaluate prehospital care for children with asthma and to propose ways to improve the management of asthma attacks. In this study, 55.9% of patients used SABA before arriving at the emergency department. 27.3% had been prescribed oral corticosteroids.

They estimated that 17.5% of patients received appropriate prehospital care (based solely on the administration of SABA and oral corticosteroids). Among the asthma patients in this study, 48.8% were on maintenance therapy. 45.2% of the children were not receiving follow-up care for asthma, and 32.2% were being followed by a pediatrician or pulmonologist. A written action plan had already been provided to families in 16.4% of cases. Only 22.7% had an individualized care plan.

In our study, 88.9% of patients used SABA before visiting the emergency department. However, 52% took at least 3 doses of SABA. The use of SABA therefore appears to be fairly similar. The focus for improvement seems to lie in administration methods (number of puffs, dosing intervals). An assessment of inhalation techniques could not be conducted during this study and is a priority, particularly to limit adverse effects. (A simple improvement in techniques could lead to a reduction in prescribed doses.) 31.

In our study, only 21.6% of patients had used oral corticosteroids before arriving at the emergency department. This represents a significant difference. It might be worthwhile to explore the barriers to parents administering oral corticosteroid treatments in an emergency setting (access to treatment, feared side effects, beliefs, etc.).

This is a single-center study. 4 -Areas for improvement

4.1 Primary Care

Asthma is a highly prevalent disease affecting populations worldwide. There are numerous studies and medical articles on the subject, which have enabled the gradual evolution of medical recommendations aimed at improving the management of asthma.

Unfortunately, changes in expert recommendations can sometimes occur more rapidly than changes in medical practice. (30). This phenomenon has been observed in conditions other than asthma, notably bronchiolitis and the treatment of nasopharyngitis. Standardizing practices requires the use of the same guidelines and the same tools.

A collaborative effort could be undertaken with the adult pulmonology department, pediatric pulmonologists, referring general practitioners, and specialized nurses to develop uniform action plans and individualized care plans throughout Martinique. And perhaps a digital platform could be created to make these documents available.

A computer-based tool to assist physicians in decision-making-particularly regarding the initiation or adjustment of treatment-could also be developed.

.

Increasing the recruitment of physicians specializing in pulmonology and pediatric pulmonology could lighten the already very diverse workload of general practitioners and help ensure better patient care. To achieve this, the region must be attractive to these specialists; however, raising awareness of the challenges involved in managing asthma and highlighting current initiatives to develop and improve the healthcare network could potentially attract more than a few of them.

Helping to develop primary care clinics-which serve as a screening mechanism before patients seek emergency care-and establishing common, standardized protocols with emergency services in the event of a crisis could be another area of focus.

The lack of reimbursement for therapeutic education consultations-which are generally lengthy-does not encourage preventive care in private practice. A specific fee schedule tailored to therapeutic education consultations could be one of the tools used to change practices in private practice.

4.2 Emergency medicine

Before visiting the emergency room, 34.3% of patients contacted emergency medical services. A significant proportion of children seen in the emergency department have mild episodes that can be treated at home. It is possible that we underestimated this patient population in our study, since the duration of visits to the emergency department is short and does not allow sufficient time to complete the questionnaire. One area for improvement could be to increase the number of emergency medical services staff and perhaps create a specialized pediatric dispatch unit. (33) In the emergency department, the top priority is treating the child's asthma attack.

But once treatment has been initiated and the attack has subsided, the priority shifts to preventing a relapse in the coming days. To minimize the chances of recurrence as much as possible, trained medical or paramedical staff must conduct a prevention session to address key topics such as the use of inhalers, the importance of ongoing treatment, the avoidance of environmental risk factors such as tobacco, and the need for close medical follow-up after the child returns home (re-evaluation within 3 days). This information must be provided by trained medical or paramedical personnel.

Part of the medical team could be specifically trained in post-crisis preventive measures (for example, an advanced practice nurse or a nurse trained in asthma care within the team, and a referring physician on the medical team). (34) The initiation of maintenance therapy in the emergency department is an important issue, as many clinicians do not consider the setting and timing to be appropriate. However, we found no studies on this topic. The risk of patients losing vision after their visit to the emergency department and the beneficial effect of ICS on the risk of asthma exacerbations suggest that emergency physicians should prescribe maintenance therapy, in conjunction with close follow-up care.

In our study, 14.8% were admitted to a general ward, while 29.7% of patients received more than 6 SABA nebulizer treatments.

According to the SP2A's new guidelines, hospitalization is recommended for children with a PRAM greater than 4 despite 6 rounds of nebulization. It appears that the hospitalization rate is too low compared to what is recommended. If the general ward's capacity allowed it, hospitalization could serve as an anchor point in the initiation or follow-up of asthma care. A direct link with healthcare providers could be established. Access to pulmonary function testing, a connection to asthma education programs, the initiation of specialized care if necessary, and a focus on prevention and education for parents and children. Psychological support could be offered if necessary.

Often, there are few hospital beds relative to the needs arising in the emergency department, and decisions must be made based on the theoretical benefit of hospitalization for the patient. A study on the use of peak expiratory flow in the emergency department could be an interesting avenue to explore, particularly among children for whom the decision to admit or discharge is under discussion.

Given the shortage of inpatient beds and the fact that asthma attacks often improve over time, one option would be to establish a post-emergency care clinic to be visited three days after discharge from the emergency department.

An appointment would then be scheduled in the emergency department, and an automated message could be sent to families to remind them of the appointment date and time, thereby encouraging them to attend the appointment.

4.3 Therapies and tools

Therapies and tools .

4.3.1 Raise awareness about the completion of pulmonary function testing 35

Pulmonary function testing is the cornerstone of paraclinical evaluations in children with asthma. It allows for an accurate assessment of the patient's lung function and is useful for monitoring, adjusting treatment, and evaluating the impact on respiratory function over time.

Given the clinical heterogeneity of asthma, its use can accurately assess a patient's respiratory function and guide the clinician in managing the patient's care.

In our study, 14.3% of children over the age of 6 had access to pulmonary function testing.

A study could be proposed among primary care physicians to assess their attitudes toward the use of pulmonary function testing, as well as their self-assessment of their ability to interpret the results and the barriers to prescribing it (difficulties in access, lack of familiarity, and lack of training).

4.3.2 Using the peak flow meter

In our study, only 4 children had a peak flow meter at home, and 13 caregivers believed they knew how to use it.

The EDELMA study, conducted in 2018 at the CHUM's asthma school, found that 4 of the 108 children included in the study reported that they had mastered the device.

To date, the use of peak flow measurement is recommended primarily for patients with mild asthma symptoms or those who are unable to assess the severity of their attack.

This tool provides a visual assessment of the breathing difficulty associated with the attack and may allow for faster initiation of treatment, thereby potentially increasing the effectiveness of emergency therapy.

A prospective study conducted in 2024 demonstrates the usefulness of peak flow measurement in assessing how children with asthma perceive their respiratory limitations.

Furthermore, after more than 6 months of use, it appears to be beneficial for treatment adherence (consistent use of inhaled corticosteroids) and changes in respiratory function Its short-term use may prove beneficial: (36) • After a change in treatment, to assess the response to therapy.

• When symptoms are pronounced, to confirm the extent of respiratory impairment.

• To help identify aggravating factors at home (e.g., rooms with mold or dust).

Its long-term use may prove beneficial: • For early detection of exacerbations (in patients with few symptoms) and early activation of the crisis action plan.

• In patients with severe exacerbations.

• In patients with severe asthma (difficulty controlling asthma).

There may be a 20% variation in peak flow readings. In this case, three exhalations should be taken each time the device is used, and the highest value should be recorded.

Its use can aid in the diagnosis of asthma (if clinical findings are consistent with asthma and spirometry is not available); normal DEP values do not rule out a diagnosis of asthma.

Improvements in peak flow readings are generally observed within 3 months of starting inhaled corticosteroids. To ensure an adequate response to treatment, patients are asked to take two measurements per day for 2 weeks and record them in a chart. One potential area for improvement could be the use of the peak expiratory flow in pediatric emergency departments to assess severity upon arrival, as well as to guide the decision to discharge patients treated in the emergency department or to help determine whether hospitalization is indicated. To achieve this, standardizing its use and providing training for medical and paramedical staff is crucial.

4.3.3 Using a pulse oximeter at home 37

.

A pulse oximeter is a noninvasive, easy-to-use device that measures blood oxygen saturation. It is widely used in medical and paramedical settings, but its usefulness in the homes of patients with asthma has not yet been proven..

Although oxygen saturation-when it is 94% or lower-is one of the criteria used to assess the severity of an asthma attack, there are several limitations to the optimal use of a pulse oximeter: ➔ poor perfusion at the measurement site.

➔ irregular pulse.

➔ skin color and thickness.

➔ incorrect positioning.

➔ the use of adult pulse oximeters on children.

➔ devices have a margin of error of +/-4% for saturation levels between 70% and 100%.

➔ decreased oxygen saturation in children after taking SABA.

➔ interpreting saturation outside the context of a crisis.

All of these confounding factors, when considered in isolation, can lead to a misinterpretation of the severity of an asthma attack and, consequently, increase anxiety among caregivers and lead to more frequent visits to the emergency room.

It can be assumed that appropriate use of the device at home by patients must be accompanied by a good understanding of the instructions for use and a solid knowledge of asthma.

A study conducted in 2015 examined the use of pulse oximeters at home by patients with asthma, and the results were inconclusive. (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?l8lY3E)

4.4 Communication and prevention

4.4.1 Public health interventions

Asthma awareness and prevention must begin on a small scale with healthcare professionals but must also be part of the public discourse.

To this end, the Ministry of Health implemented an action program for asthma prevention and management from 2002 to 2005 with specific objectives.

The regional health agency must be made aware of the challenges surrounding asthma awareness and prevention, as well as the shortcomings of our current healthcare system. Thus, funds could be allocated to create prevention positions (such as strengthening 38.

asthma education teams in schools), as well as to support the development and reorganization of healthcare structures.

At the local level, outreach campaigns could be organized in schools (through school health programs, for example), on the radio, on TV, or in newspapers.

4.4.2 Develop print and digital patient information materials

The sharing of information upon discharge from the pediatric emergency department varies depending on the healthcare providers involved and the specific work environment.

However, communicating with parents before the child returns home is important to help them understand the care plan that has been put in place. (34) To this end, a comprehensive informational resource could be developed and systematically provided to patients with asthma before they return home. (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?SEqeW9) It could consist of: ❖ a printed action plan.

❖ measures to prevent exposure to and raise awareness of allergens and irritants (tobacco).

❖ criteria for assessing the severity of an asthma attack.

❖ a list of useful phone numbers (pulmonologists, asthma education program, EFR office, emergency medical services).

❖ video links to help with the use of inhalers.

❖ an informational brochure on the benefits of asthma school.

❖ a written notice for a close-monitoring follow-up appointment.

❖ digital tools available.

One area for improvement would be the use and evaluation of available digital tools.

Currently, several apps exist to help manage asthma (and are available on smartphones).

For example, the "findair" app helps track medication use and alerts users to poor asthma control if rescue medication is used too frequently. "Smart Asthma" allows users to monitor lung function using a peak flow meter. Some peak flow meters are equipped with Bluetooth or connect directly to a smartphone, allowing users to enter readings directly and facilitating monitoring. The "effic'asthme" app was developed by the AP-HP in 2019. It is a virtual asthma attack simulation app that allows parents and patients to practice responding appropriately to an asthma attack.

4.4.3 Expand the asthma school 39

.

In our study, a small proportion of parents had heard of the asthma school, and only 15.3% had taken their child for a consultation there.

A study conducted at the CHUM from January through December 2018 (the EDELMA study) documented significant changes in habits as well as positive changes in medication adherence and healthcare utilization-particularly visits to the emergency department-among patients enrolled in the asthma education program. Awareness and prevention sessions were held weekly at the CHUM, and individual consultations were offered to promote treatment adherence. During these sessions, several topics were addressed, such as empowering the child, recognizing the signs of an asthma attack, communication between children and parents, inhalation techniques, and various other prevention strategies. This opportunity for discussion proved to be essential. Among the children attending the asthma clinic in this study, only 2.4% were referred by their primary care physician (66% were referred from the emergency department and 27% from the inpatient unit). This trend appears to be reversing at present, which may point to a lack of communication in the emergency department.

A medical thesis conducted in Martinique in 2015, which described the practices of general practitioners in the region, found that 83% of the physicians surveyed were familiar with the "Asthma School." Rather than a lack of knowledge, a lack of familiarity with its use in daily practice may be suspected. A questionnaire was distributed to each physician (240 practitioners), and the response rate was 37%. The objective of the study was to evaluate the management of asthma by general practitioners in the territory and to compare it to the guidelines that had been in effect for 10 years at that time. In this thesis, 82% of the physicians surveyed wanted to be able to delegate therapeutic education to a facility or a trained professional, while 72% felt they were not providing enough therapeutic education.

However, therapeutic education is key to asthma management and is central to the work carried out by the asthma school.

Currently, the asthma school limits its activities to pediatrics, but the expansion of this program (in terms of staff and resources) could facilitate the extension of its services to adults. A better understanding of the disease in adults could thus have a beneficial effect on the care of children.

4.4.4 Expand home-based prevention interventions

Currently, a project to assess homes for irritants and allergens is being developed in collaboration with the Asthma School. This type of field intervention could facilitate research, concrete action, and awareness-raising among families. With the families' consent, it could 40.
