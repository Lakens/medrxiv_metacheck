Show details

Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 8 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Introduction

Malaria remains a major public health problem in sub-Saharan Africa, which bears most of the global burden of cases and deaths [1]. In Senegal, transmission is linked to the West African monsoon: it is concentrated in the rainy season and follows a south-east to north gradient [2] [3] [4].

This seasonality underlies seasonal malaria chemoprevention (SMC), recommended by the World Health Organization for areas of highly seasonal transmission in the Sahel [5] and shown to be effective in children under ten years of age in Senegal [6].

Weather-driven dynamical models, such as the Liverpool Malaria Model (LMM) [7] [8] [9] and VECTRI [10, 11], simulate vector and parasite development as a function of rainfall and temperature and produce indicators such as the entomological inoculation rate (EIR). They have been used over Senegal and West Africa to reconstruct past transmission [2, 3, 12], to relate malaria variability to climate and ocean conditions [13, 14], to assess seasonal predictability [15] [16] [17] and to project the impact of climate change [4, [18] [19] [20] [21]. Temperature affects transmission non-linearly, with an optimum near 25 °C [22], so that rainfall and temperature jointly control the length and intensity of the season. In Senegal, simulations place the peak of transmission in September-October, one to two months after the rainfall peak, reflecting the time required for mosquito breeding and the sporogonic cycle. [2] [3] [4] 13, 19]. Two limitations recur in these studies. First, their evaluation against observed malaria has relied on short periods, sentinel sites or annual totals [2, 20], whereas the National Malaria Control Programme (PNLP) now reports monthly data for all health districts, including the number of suspected and tested patients, which allows the test positivity rate (TPR) to be computed [23].

Second, climate change projections were often driven by one model or by a multi-model mean forcing, which does not represent the spread of the projections and, because the response of malaria models is non-linear, does not correspond to the mean of single-model simulations. The daily rainfall and temperature from many models of the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 6 (CMIP6) [24], bias-adjusted and downscaled to 0.25° [25], which allows the spread of the climate projections to be described and a representative model to be selected to drive a malaria model.

This study has four objectives: (i) to describe the seasonality, spatial distribution, trends and severity of confirmed malaria in Senegal from the PNLP regional database (2009-2025) and harmonised district reports (2016-2025); (ii) to evaluate the bias-adjusted CMIP6 rainfall against observations and the timing and spatial pattern of transmission simulated by VECTRI against surveillance data, at the national, regional and departmental scales; (iii) to describe the 2025 surveillance data in relation to the LLIN mass distribution campaign; and (iv) to project changes in climatic suitability and simulated transmission under two emission scenarios.

Materials and methods

Study area

Senegal (about 197,000 km 2 ) is divided into 14 regions and 45 departments (GADM version 4.1 boundaries, https://gadm.org) and into 79 health districts. Annual rainfall decreases from more than 1,100 mm in the south (Ziguinchor, Kédougou) to less than 300 mm in the north (Saint-Louis), with a single rainy season from June to October peaking in August. The analysis is conducted on a regular 0.25° grid (331 cells overlapping Senegal), with the share of each cell inside each region and department used as aggregation weight together with the cell area.

Malaria surveillance data

Two PNLP sources were used (Fig 1). The regional database provides monthly confirmed malaria cases (rapid diagnostic test or microscopy) and malaria deaths for the 14 regions from 2008 to 2025, with the regional population of the National Agency of Statistics and Demography (ANSD). The year 2008 was excluded because of the change of regional boundaries, and 2025 is provisional; the analysis period is 2009-2024 (16 years), and 2025 is reported separately.

The district reports were harmonised from the annual Excel reports of 2016-2020 and the District Health Information Software 2 (DHIS2) exports of 2021-2025. They provide, by district and month, the number of suspected, tested and confirmed outpatient cases for children under five years, patients aged five years and over (excluding pregnant women) and pregnant women, the number of hospitalised malaria cases (hospitalised severe malaria in 2016-2019, hospitalised confirmed malaria from 2020) and malaria deaths, and, for 2021-2025, the number of LLINs distributed through routine channels. In the 2016-2019 reports, 36 to 37 hospitals were reported as separate units; they were added to the district in which they are located, as in the 2020 report and the DHIS2 exports, which integrate hospitals in their district. Districts created after 2016 (Sangalkam, Yeumbeul and Diakhao) have no separate report before their creation; analyses over 2016-2025 were therefore made by department (GADM level 2, each district being assigned to the department where it lies) or by region. All reporting-unit names were matched. The district reports also provide the number of LLINs distributed during the 2025 mass distribution campaign (CDM) in 34 districts.

In the DHIS2 exports, 235 of the 3,792 district-months of 2021-2024 (6.2 %) had no value for confirmed cases although the number of tested patients was reported (mean 720 tests); 94.5 % of them fell between January and June, mostly in low-transmission districts of Louga, Fatick and Matam. Because DHIS2 does not store zero values, they were counted as zero. Monthly report completeness, available for 2017-2020, ranged from 95.7 % to 99.6 %.

Annual national totals of the two sources were identical for 2016-2020 and differed by -0.2 % (2021), +0.0 % (2023) and -0.1 % (2024); in 2022, the regional database was 21.8 % lower (358,033 against 458,040 confirmed cases), with lower values in all regions (S1 Fig). The regional database is used for the long period and the district reports for testing, positivity, age, severity and departmental analyses; the sensitivity of the main result to 2022 and to the source was assessed.

Observed rainfall

Monthly rainfall from the Climate Hazards Group InfraRed Precipitation with Station data (CHIRPS v2.0, 0.05°) [26] for 1981-2025 was averaged to the 0.25° grid (mean of the 25 CHIRPS cells of each 0.25° cell) and aggregated to regions and departments.

CMIP6 climate projections

Daily precipitation and near-surface air temperature of ten CMIP6 models from NEX-GDDP-CMIP6 [25] were used: ACCESS-CM2, CNRM-CM6-1, EC-Earth3, GFDL-ESM4, INM-CM5-0, IPSL-CM6A-LR, MIROC6, MPI-ESM1-2-HR, MRI-ESM2-0 and NorESM2-MM, one per modelling centre, for the historical experiment (1995-2014) and the SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5 scenarios [27] (2041-2060 and 2081-2100). CanESM5, a model with very high climate sensitivity [28], and the second members of the ACCESS and CNRM families were not included. NEX-GDDP-CMIP6 is bias-adjusted against the Global Meteorological Forcing Dataset [29] and has no data over the ocean; the 53 Atlantic cells of the box were excluded, and all 331 cells overlapping Senegal had complete data. Missing days of non-standard calendars (360-day and 365-day) were filled by linear interpolation for temperature and with zero for rainfall before computing monthly values. The historical rainfall of each model was compared with CHIRPS over 1995-2014 (annual bias, correlation of the mean seasonal cycle and difference of rainfall centre of gravity).

To drive VECTRI, one model was selected objectively: for each scenario and period, the changes in annual rainfall (%), July-September rainfall (%) and mean temperature (°C) over Senegal were scaled by the spread of the models (median absolute deviation), and the model whose changes were on average closest to the ensemble median was retained. MRI-ESM2-0 [30] ranked first (distance 0.86), followed by MIROC6 (0.99) and GFDL-ESM4 (1.40); EC-Earth3 (5.28) and INM-CM5-0 (4.49), the two models projecting wetter conditions, ranked last.

VECTRI simulations

VECTRI version 1.5.0 [10, 11] is a grid-based dynamical malaria model that represents the effects of rainfall on temporary breeding sites, of temperature on larval development, adult survival and the sporogonic cycle, of population density on biting rates, and a simple representation of immunity; default parameters were used, without calibration and without interventions. VECTRI was run on the 0.25° grid with the daily MRI-ESM2-0 forcing for 1995-2014 and the four future periods, each preceded by a five-year spin-up (the first years of the period replayed) that was removed from the results, and with the population density of AfriPop [31] interpolated bilinearly from 1° to 0.25°. Monthly EIR (infective bites per person per month), new cases per person, detectable parasite ratio, rainfall and temperature were computed from daily outputs and aggregated to the national, regional and departmental units with area weights. The transmission season length was defined as the longest run of consecutive months with EIR ≥ 1 infective bite per person per month in each cell.

Rule-based climatic suitability

As a model-independent complement driven by all ten models, a month was considered climatically suitable when its mean temperature was between 16 and 34 °C [22] and its rainfall reached 60 mm, provided that at least one month of the twelve-month window reached 80 mm [32, 33]; the suitable season is the longest run of suitable months in a twelve-month window starting at the driest month of each cell, and suitability is stable where the mean season exceeds three months [33, 34]. Changes were computed for each model and summarised by the ensemble median, the range and the number of models with a significant change (Mann-Whitney test on annual values, p < 0.05); model agreement on the sign of the change was mapped where at least 8 of the 10 models agreed.

Indicators and statistical analysis

Annual incidence was expressed as confirmed cases per 1,000 inhabitants (‰) and the TPR as confirmed cases per tested patient. Because more than 96 % of suspected cases were tested each year (see Results), the TPR measures the share of febrile patients with a malaria infection and is independent of population estimates; it is less sensitive than incidence to care seeking, although it depends on the background of non-malarial fevers [23]. Seasonal timing was summarised with circular statistics [35]: months were represented as angles (30° per month) weighted by their number of cases, giving the circular mean month, or centre of gravity (9 = mid-September,10 = mid-October), and the concentration R (0 = cases evenly distributed over the year,1 = all cases in one month). The centre of gravity was computed for each year and averaged as a circular mean, with a 95 % CI from 2,000 bootstrap resamples of years [36]. The season length was described by the shortest run of consecutive months holding 75 % of the annual total and by the contiguous months reaching 20 % of the peak of the mean cycle.

Monotonic trends were tested with the Mann-Kendall test [37, 38] and quantified with the Sen slope [39]. The spatial concentration of cases relative to population was summarised by the Gini coefficient across regions. Severity was described by malaria deaths per 1,000 confirmed cases with exact Poisson CIs [40]; this ratio is not a case fatality rate because deaths are not linked to individual outpatient cases.

The comparison between observations and simulations relies on climatological seasonality only: CMIP6 historical simulations are free-running and do not reproduce the observed chronology of years, so simulated and observed years were never paired. The lag of observed cases behind simulated transmission was estimated as the circular difference between the mean centres of gravity of observed cases (2009-2024) and of the simulated EIR (1995-2014), with a CI obtained by resampling observed and simulated years independently. As a shape-based estimate, the Pearson correlation between the normalised mean cycles (monthly share of the annual total) was computed for lags of 0 to 4 months. The same statistics were computed between observed cases and CHIRPS rainfall (2009) (2010) (2011) (2012) (2013) (2014) (2015) (2016) (2017) (2018) (2019) (2020) (2021) (2022) (2023) (2024) and between the simulated EIR and the simulated rainfall, which separates the delay between rainfall and transmission, reproduced by the model, from the delay between transmission and reported cases. Spatial correspondence was assessed with Spearman correlations across the 14 regions and 45 departments, with bootstrap CIs over units. Regional and departmental analyses are exploratory; where several tests are reported, a Bonferroni threshold is indicated.

Projected changes in the simulated EIR were tested with the Mann-Whitney test on annual values (20 years per period), and changes in the EIR centre of gravity with bootstrap CIs. The 2025 data of the districts covered by the LLIN mass campaign were compared with those of the other districts, as the ratio of 2025 to the mean of 2021-2024 for confirmed cases, tested patients and TPR (Mann-Whitney test across districts); this comparison is descriptive, because campaign districts were selected for their high burden and the 2025 data are provisional.

Software, reproducibility and ethics

The chain (download, preparation, VECTRI runs, suitability analysis and dashboard) runs in Python (NumPy, SciPy, netCDF4, pandas, Matplotlib) and is published with an open interactive dashboard that presents all indicators by region and department [41]. Discussion

Four results stand out. First, the timing of 486 confirmed malaria is regular, centred on mid-October, and reported cases lag the transmission 487 simulated by VECTRI by about one month nationally and in 13 of 14 regions and 44 of 45 488 departments; this lag is robust to the observation period and source. Second, the delay between 489 rainfall and reported cases (about two months) is shared equally between the delay between 490 rainfall and transmission, which VECTRI reproduces with a nearly uniform value of one month, 491 and the delay between transmission and recorded cases; the regional differences in observed 492 timing arise in the second part and are not reproduced by the model. Third, the simulated EIR reproduces the spatial gradient of transmission intensity measured by the TPR, whereas the simulated cases do not reproduce the distribution of the burden. Fourth, the projected changes in timing are small, while the intensity of transmission is projected to decrease strongly only under SSP5-8.5 at the end of the century, with increases in the south-east under the other scenarios.

Timing of transmission and reported cases

The one-month lag between simulated transmission and confirmed cases is consistent with previous modelling studies in Senegal, which placed the transmission peak in September-October and reported cases one to two months after the rainfall peak [2, 3, 13], and with entomological and epidemiological observations in the Sahel, where vector abundance precedes clinical malaria by several weeks [42]. It is compatible with an intrinsic incubation period of the order of two weeks for Plasmodium falciparum and with the delays of care seeking, diagnosis and reporting. Hospitalised cases followed outpatient cases by about one week, as expected for the progression to severe disease. With three quarters of annual cases within four consecutive months, malaria in Senegal meets the criterion of marked seasonality proposed by Roca-Feltrer et al. [43], which underlies eligibility for SMC [44].

The separation of the delay into two parts of about one month each is, to our knowledge, new for Senegal. It was made possible because the bias-adjusted forcing reproduces the timing of observed rainfall to within a few days, so that the simulated delay between rainfall and transmission can be compared with the observed delay between rainfall and cases. The uniformity of the simulated delay across regions contrasts with observed delays ranging from 1.3 months in Kédougou to 2.8 months in Sédhiou. Three types of factors not represented in VECTRI may explain this contrast. Hydrology: in Casamance and in the centre-west (Kaolack, Fatick, Diourbel), flooded areas, rice fields, the Saloum and Casamance estuaries and other persistent water bodies can maintain breeding sites after the end of the rains, as shown by pondresolving hydrological modelling [11, 42, 45], which would extend transmission into November-December. Interventions: SMC delivered from July to October [6, 46] reduces cases among children during the rounds and may shift the remaining cases later; the decrease of the share of children under five among confirmed cases over the period is consistent with this effect, but the data do not allow it to be quantified. Reporting and care seeking may also differ between regions. Conversely, the early season in Kédougou, where cases nearly coincide with simulated transmission, coincides with the earliest and most abundant rainfall and the highest burden, and suggests that the early part of the season is underestimated by the model in the south-east, where the season has also become later between 2009-2014 and 2015-2024.

Spatial pattern

The TPR is well suited to evaluate dynamical malaria models with routine data. Because almost all suspected cases were tested, it measures the share of febrile patients with a malaria infection and does not depend on population estimates, which are uncertain at fine scale. Across departments, the simulated EIR was slightly more strongly correlated with the TPR than observed rainfall, although the confidence intervals overlap; the combination of rainfall, temperature and population represented by VECTRI therefore reproduces the gradient at least as well as rainfall alone. The concentration of the burden in Kédougou, Kolda and Tambacounda, where incidence increased over 2009-2024, is consistent with the persistent south-eastern hotspots identified over 2000-2020 [47] and with the gradient of simulated transmission [3, 4, 19, 20]. In contrast, the negative association between simulated new cases and observed malaria reflects the representation of acquired immunity in VECTRI, which reduces the number of clinical cases where transmission is intense [10], and the AfriPop population at 1°, which spreads the population of Dakar and Thiès over neighbouring cells. In VECTRI, high population densities also dilute bites [10], which explains the near-zero EIR of Dakar, whereas the cases reported in Dakar may include infections acquired elsewhere and treated in the capital.

The simulated case counts should therefore not be used to allocate resources between regions without calibration.

The higher share of children under five where the TPR is high, and the lower ratio of deaths to confirmed cases in high-transmission regions, are consistent with the age shift of clinical malaria with transmission intensity [48] and with the relation between transmission intensity and the clinical spectrum of severe malaria [49]. The high ratios in Saint-Louis, Dakar and Thiès may also reflect the referral of severe patients to regional and national hospitals and the lower immunity of populations with little exposure; patient-level data would be needed to distinguish these explanations.

Surveillance indicators and the 2025 data

National incidence showed no trend over 2009-2024, but the TPR decreased significantly over 2016-2024 while testing remained near universal, and incidence increased in the south-east and decreased in the centre and west. A decreasing TPR with a stable number of confirmed cases indicates that more patients with non-malarial fevers were tested (1.56 million suspected cases in 2016, 2.91 million in 2024); national incidence alone would therefore hide both a change in testing practice and a geographical concentration of transmission. In 2025, confirmed cases decreased by more than half in the districts of the LLIN mass campaign, against less than 10 % elsewhere, with a stable number of tests in these districts and a decrease that started in July, after the first half of the year was close to the usual level. This pattern is consistent with an effect of the campaign on transmission, in line with the large contribution of insecticide-treated nets to the decrease of malaria in Africa [50]. It cannot, however, be attributed to the campaign from these data: the campaign districts were selected for their high burden, the 2025 data are provisional, the number of tests also decreased in October-December in the campaign districts, Kolda was flagged as possibly incomplete, and the decrease of confirmed cases in November in the other districts suggests incomplete late-year reporting. A verification of the 2025 data with the PNLP and a comparison with SMC coverage and rainfall will be needed.

Projections

The ten models agree on warming but not on the sign of rainfall change, a known feature of CMIP projections over the Sahel [51]; the median decrease of annual rainfall concerns the core of the rainy season, while October rainfall increases slightly. Driving VECTRI with the model closest to the ensemble median keeps the projected malaria changes representative of the central climate change, but does not describe their spread; the rule-based approach, driven by all ten models, provides this spread for suitability. Both approaches agree that the changes are small by mid-century and under SSP2-4.5, and that transmission decreases under SSP5-8.5 at the end of the century, when temperatures exceed the thermal optimum for transmission [22] during more months and rainfall decreases by about one third. They also agree on a smaller decrease, or an increase, in the south-east, where the burden is concentrated.

For SMC, the main projection result is the stability of the seasonal timing: the centre of gravity of simulated transmission moves by less than 0.1 month and the share of the annual EIR in July-October remains about 90 % in all scenarios. The present calendar would therefore remain aligned with the climatic window of transmission, and the regional differences in observed timing, of more than one month between Kédougou and Casamance or the centre-west, matter more for planning than the projected changes. A regionally differentiated SMC calendar, starting earlier in the south-east and extending to November in the regions with late seasons, would be consistent with these results and with the protection decay observed after each round in Kédougou [46].

Strengths and limitations

The strengths of this study are the use of two independent surveillance sources with nearuniversal testing, the harmonisation of district reports across formats, the evaluation of the climate forcing against observations, the use of ten bias-adjusted models and of an objective criterion to select the model driving VECTRI, the decomposition of the delay between rainfall and cases, and the use of resampling statistics without pairing simulated and observed years.

Several limitations must be considered. CMIP6 historical simulations do not reproduce the observed chronology of years, so the comparison is restricted to climatological seasonality and spatial patterns and cannot assess interannual skill; driving VECTRI with observed or reanalysis forcing over the surveillance period would allow this evaluation. VECTRI was driven by a single model, and its parameters were not calibrated; the absolute EIR, the parasite ratio and the simulated cases are not realistic for current conditions, which include interventions, and the projections assume that interventions remain absent. The 0.25° grid represents Dakar by few cells, and the population is interpolated from 1°. Bias-adjusted rainfall is 10-30 % wetter than CHIRPS in the centre and west, which may lengthen the simulated season there. The two observed sources differ in 2022, although the main result did not depend on this year, and 2025 data are provisional. The comparison of departments relies on the assignment of districts to departments, whose boundaries differ. Finally, SMC coverage, indoor residual spraying and case management were not available by month and district and were not analysed.

Conclusions

Confirmed malaria in Senegal follows a regular seasonal cycle centred on mid-October, with a burden increasingly concentrated in the south-east. VECTRI driven by bias-adjusted CMIP6 climate reproduces the timing of transmission and its spatial gradient: reported cases lag simulated transmission by about one month, and simulated transmission lags rainfall by about one month.
