Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Context: Dementia is associated with significant use of social care, yet we know little about how providers respond to increasing demand and policy ambitions to deliver more care in community settings.

Objective: To examine dementia care provision, workforce training, service integration, and perceived challenges among care homes and home care agencies in England.

We conducted a mixed methods co-designed survey of care home and home care agency managers across five geographically diverse English regions, exploring: training, service delivery, relationships with other services, and future priorities. We analysed data descriptively and free-text responses using qualitative content analysis.

Findings: We received valid responses from 42 home care agencies and 47 care home managers, who estimated around one third of home care clients, and most care home residents, had significant cognitive impairment. Most care homes held regular multidisciplinary meetings (29/40, 72.5%). Home care managers described positive collaborations with health and other social care services, but also difficulties accessing support and concerns about undervaluation of care workforce skills within multidisciplinary care. Across both settings, frequently reported challenges included: increasing care complexity, behaviours that challenge, recruitment, retention and financial constraints. In home care settings, difficulties working in partnership with families were cited as reasons for care breakdown. Most organisations provided dementia-specific training, identifying communication, behavioural support, de-escalation, safeguarding, and working with families as unmet training needs.

Limitations: Findings are probably limited by response and desirability bias; we did not triangulate findings with objective data.

Implications: As policy shifts care to community settings, investment in social care workforce development, specialist support and integrated pathways are essential for sustainable high quality dementia care. Increased funding, improved training, stronger career pathways, and greater recognition of care workers were identified as priorities for improving dementia care.

Introduction

The number of people with dementia is rising worldwide; it is one of the most pressing global health and social care concerns. In England, 498,926 people were diagnosed with dementia in 2025, and it is the leading cause of death (Nuffield Trust, 2026). Dementia profoundly affects individuals, families, and wider society, with UK annual costs expected to reach £90 billion by 2040; most costs are borne by families and social care (Alzheimer's Society, 2024). Almost 400,000 people live in English care homes, and a further half a million receive local authority funded home care (GOV. UK, 2026). Approximately 60% of people receiving inhome social care in England (United Kingdom Homecare Association, 2015), and threequarters of care home residents are estimated to live with dementia (Livingston et al., 2017), supported by 900,000 social care workers (Hopkins, 2025).

Dementia care should be personalised, enabling, inclusive and collaborative (Dalgarno et al., 2021; Lord et al., 2020), but a Care Quality Commission (CQC) commissioned audit, involving 53 social care providers (including home care agencies and care homes), reported that current dementia training in these settings insufficiently equips the workforce to deliver high-quality person-centred care (Smith et al., 2026). The report reflected a key workforce challenge: care workers are low paid, often undervalued and expected to provide skilled care to people with typically just 1-2 hours of dementia specific training (Brown et al., 2022; Leverton et al., 2021a Leverton et al., , 2021b;; Smith et al., 2026). An accompanying survey of 184 social care workers, found, when benchmarking provision against the Dementia Training Standards Framework (Skills for Health, 2018) that most training delivered was at Tier 1 (awareness level), and there were critical gaps at Tier 2, for example training around equality, diversity and inclusion, families as partners in care, and end of life care (Smith et al., 2026).

The Fit for the future:10 Year Health Plan for England (2025) asks the health system to deliver three "big shifts": from sickness to prevention, hospital to community and analogue to digital. It acknowledges the importance of social care and promotes greater health and social care integration. However, broader social care funding and structural reform remain largely outside its scope despite the acknowledgement that social care will be critical to this shift, especially among people with dementia who often have complex needs (Alzheimer's Society, 2024).

Developing an appropriately skilled social care workforce is therefore a policy priority (NHS England, 2023). The UK government's long-term vision is for a National Care Service, to deliver consistency of care (UK Parliament, 2024). The Casey Commission is investigating the adult social care landscape and will make medium-term recommendations (expected 2026) to deliver "funding flows and accountability mechanisms to improve quality and productivity" (Casey Commission, 2025). Plans are likely to focus on appropriate funding and fair pay, and training and support for care workers to improve staff retention, wellbeing and drive-up care quality.

In relation to dementia specifically, a recent review found evidence for the effectiveness of dementia training in care homes in reducing agitation and improving quality of life; with some evidence for training effectiveness in home care. Approaches that engage staff "champions" to integrate practice-based learning were key, highlighting the critical role of the provider network in delivering better dementia social care (Delray et al., 2024). Investment to implement this evidence could deliver savings across health and social care from reductions in acute admissions and GP visits.

National surveys exploring how care home staff manage behaviours that challenge (Mallon, Krska & Gammie, 2019), sensory loss (Andrusjak, Barbosa & Mountain, 2021) and report neglect and abuse (Cooper et al., 2018) among people with dementia, highlight the diverse challenges providers face. To understand the challenges of the contexts in which social care workers support clients with dementia, we developed two co-designed survey instruments for care home and home care service managers and senior staff.

Within a broader national research project exploring dementia provision within differing health and social care organisations, we surveyed domiciliary and residential service managers in diverse areas of England (Kelsey et al., 2025; Muralidhar et al., 2025). We aimed to examine dementia care provision, workforce training, service integration, and perceived challenges. Our objectives were to understand how social care organisations: 1.

Plan and deliver staff training and provide inclusive, high-quality care to service users. 2. Enable, or are unable, to deliver care integrated with local health service provision. 3. Perceive key challenges, as well as factors that generate pride and concern.

Methodology

This cross-sectional national provider survey was conducted by the NIHR Dementia and Neurodegeneration Policy Research Unit at Queen Mary, University of London (DeNPRU-QM), commissioned by the Department of Health and Social Care (DHSC) and approved by the National Research Ethics service and Health Research Authority on 14.05.2024 (24/IEC08/0008).

Survey development and co-design

We developed the surveys (one addressing each context) using a co-design approach with people with lived experience of dementia, and service managers or staff deputising for them from diverse regions of England.

The survey was co-designed around the study objectives outlined above, with input from stakeholder groups. The DeNPRU-QM Patient and Public Involvement and Experience (PPIE) group (seven PPIE members living with or caring for a person with dementia) met with CC and CK in an online focus group, to share ideas about survey development and guide initial survey drafting by the research team. We then held an in-person workshop with four home care agency managers, a senior manager and a home care worker on 30.04.24; and an online workshop with two care home managers, and care home worker and a senior leader in a care home franchise; four nurses, a junior doctor, and psychiatrist, on 29.06.24. Before these meetings, participants were sent an evidence pack to provide context and the drafted survey. During meetings, members proposed and discussed survey items in line with the evidence and aims, providing feedback using a structure, process and outcome framework. After the co-design groups, the drafted surveys (for managers of care homes and home care agencies respectively) were revised using participants comments, who were invited to review revised versions before the final version was produced using Qualtrics (www.qualtrics.com).

The surveys asked about organisational structure (staff and registration) and staff retention; staff training, and processes for managing client care, and ensuring services are inclusive. They comprised descriptive, discrete choice closed and open questions with free text responses for further expansion (surveys are provided in supplementary material). For questions regarding behaviour that challenges, we drew on previous work with care workers (Wong et al., 2025).

Survey sample and data collection

We selected five geographically diverse Integrated Care Boards (ICBs) areas in England within which to conduct the survey, in London, Midlands, South East, and two in North East and Yorkshire regions. Selected urban, rural and coastal regions encompassed diverse ethnic and socioeconomic populations. We used publicly available CQC data to identify eligible home care agencies and care homes.

Contact was through the National Institute of Health (NIHR) ENRICH network and then directly by AD and CD. Providers were sent a cover letter, participant information sheet (PIS) and link to complete the survey independently online via Qualtrics, or if they preferred on the phone with a researcher. Consent was completed electronically prior to survey completion. A £15 voucher was offered as renumeration and only one participant was included per organisation.

We invited one senior member of staff (usually the manager) with a deep knowledge of their service to complete the survey. Participants were currently employed by care homes or home care agencies within the five specified ICBs. Our intention a priori was to purposively select managers from 75 of these organisations to reflect diversity of organisation size, and to include franchises and standalone organisations.

Analysis

We exported survey data from Qualtrics to Microsoft Excel. Survey responses were included if they contained the participant demographics and a minimum of 5 completed survey questions, agreed as a requirement by the team to be inclusive but to exclude surveys that did not include sufficient responses to meaningfully capture the participant's views.

We used standard descriptive statistics to summarise responses to fixed choice items; where items described a range, the midpoint in analyses was used (e.g. 5-10 became 7.5) and if greater or less than signs were written a minimum estimate was assigned (for example >20 became 21. We reported medians and interquartile ranges (IQR) of continuous data, as a skewed distribution was expected due to variances in service size and workforce characteristics using Excel and RStudio (Posit team, 2025).

We processed free text responses in Microsoft Excel, conducting qualitative content analysis. AD and CD coded all home care and care home responses, respectively. CC then independently coded 10% of responses for each question, using the coding list developed, discussing any discrepancies in codes and iteratively modifying the coding frame. We used frequency counts and salient quotes to summarise the prevailing themes amongst those that had written narrative comments.

Of the 236 services that accessed the questionnaire, 89 (38%) provided valid responses and were included in our analyses: those deemed invalid had not completed the required number of survey questions (see above). Almost all excluded surveys were blank and on review did not include usable responses to other questions that would have enhanced or changed our findings. 42/115 (37%) of home care agency responses received, and 47/121 (39%) of care home responses were valid with varying individual response rates for each question resulting in differential denominators.

Valid responses received were from: London (n=8), Midlands (n=9), Southeast (n=7), North East (n=8) and Northwest English regions (n=10). Most survey respondents were the agency manager/ director (n=36, 85.7%); half had been in the role at least five years (n=22, 52.4%), and four (9.5%) for less than a year. Around half of participating agencies were single site, and half were one branch of a multisite provider or franchise (which included a median of two sites, IQR): 1-4).

We received 47 valid survey responses from: Northwest England (n = 10), North East England (n = 12), Midlands (n = 9), the South East (n = 8), and North East London (n =8). A median of 40 (IQR: 30-64.5) staff were employed by care homes who responded. As shown in Table 1, most staff were fulltime carers. As expected, at least two nurses were employed in the 19 homes that provided nursing care. Nine of these homes employed at least one mental health nurse and two employed two; 18 employed a general nurse. In the past 6 months, a median of two staff (IQR: 0-4.75) had left their provider, equating to an approximate annual staff turnover of 10%.

Reason for staff attrition and workforce concerns:

Within home care services, reasons why staff left were grouped during content analysis and included: alternate job opportunities or further education (5/37, 13.5%), "such as upskilling to nursing roles or pursuing opportunities within the NHS", travel and "mileage costs" (4/37, 10.8%), inflexible, unsociable hours (4/37, 10.8%), and low pay and cost of living crisis (3/37, 8.1%), with one manager of an urban, multi-site agency citing the cost of living crisis, clean air zone charges, progression to the NHS sector and general career changes. When asked to identify concerns around how their care home had operated in the past five years, staffing retention and a reliance on agency workers was most frequently raised (8/37, 21.6%) with one manager commenting: "Struggled a little bit for staff. Night staff might bicker, not turn up etc. Since they got to know the agency, they get given the same staff e.g. for 3 years. Relationship [is] a lot better now. The agency [agency] know the residents well. Previously they had a tough time e.g. 7pm ringing to say they can't come for the night shift. More expensive to have agency during the night but more reliable" (registered manager) Client/resident caseload and need:

Home care agencies had a median of 64.5 clients (IQR 26.5 -104.3). Around a third of clients (median 24, IQR 8.3-46.8) were known to have memory problems and a quarter (16; 6-20.5) had a dementia diagnosis. In comparison, most care homes reported that over fourfifths of residents were aged 65 or older and experienced some degree of memory or cognitive impairment that may affect their ability to live independently, and most or almost all had a dementia diagnosis (Table 1). 15/35 (42.9%) home care services indicated they were providing care to clients that were unable to speak English sufficiently to communicate their needs. In the free text comments, strategies to address this included matching clients to staff that spoke the same language (8/16), pictures or non-verbal methods such as common hand signals (6/16, 37.5%) and translations apps (5/16); only three used interpreters and two worked with family members or advocates. Dementia training

Home care:

All home care agencies responding provided some dementia specific training; 22/40 responses (55%) highlighted only training in house, 8/40 (20%) through external suppliers only, and 10/40 (25%) offered both. Twenty-four participants contributing useable data, provided a median of 14.5 hours (IQR 5 -20.8) of dementia specific training. Most agencies delivered at least some in person training (84%, n=33), and e-learning (69.2%, n=27); six (15.4%) were entirely reliant on online/E-learning.

Eight (n=32) respondents were unable to identify any shortcomings in available training. Gaps that were highlighted in free text responses included practical, case-based, interactive training and experiential learning formats; "Virtual reality simulations or the dementia bus which would help staff better understand how disorientating and uncomfortable living with dementia can be." Decision making and communicating, de-escalation/ safeguarding and risk reduction were topics felt to be missing from existing provision: "Scenario-based training on de-escalation, communication in advanced-stage dementia, and supporting families through crisis periods would strengthen staff confidence further. Similarly, more detailed training around capacity assessments, best interest decision-making and safeguarding considerations specific to dementia would support safe practice."

Care homes:

Most care homes reported that 90% or more staff receive dementia specific training (37/41, 90.2%). The median number of hours of in-house training provided to staff was 2 hours (n = 14, 2-18), plus a median of 4.5 (n = 11, 3-12.5) hours by an external supplier. Training was most often supplied with a mixture of in-person and e-learning sessions (23/41, 56.1%) followed by e-learning only (12/41, 29.2%) and in-person only (n = 6, 14.6%), most respondents considered current dementia training sufficient (n = 20/31, 64.5%). Similarly to home care respondents, gaps identified were in the managing and understanding of behaviours that challenge was most frequently highlighted as a gap in dementia training (n = 5/31, 16.1%): "Understanding reasons behind behaviours, triggers, causes and management" Two respondents (6.4%) wanted more interactive training: "I have been to the local hospital where they did training and you could see and act out what it feels like having dementia [by] interacting with the tutors, who were very knowledgeable" Most care homes offered support to staff when residents exhibited behaviours that challenge through various combinations of multidisciplinary discussion groups, additional training specific to behaviours that challenge and employing specific models of care. The most cited combination (n = 12/41, 29.2%) was that of regular multidisciplinary discussions and additional training specific to behaviours that challenge.

Most care homes in our sample reported holding regular multidisciplinary meetings (29/40, 72.5%). Multidisciplinary meeting attendance mostly included combinations of GPs, nurses, and care home staff (12/30, 40%). GP involvement (either alone or in combination) was present in most responses, and nurses were also commonly represented. Regular review of psychiatric medication was commonly reported 39/40 (97.5%,), most frequently by a GP (26/34, 76.4%), sometimes including local mental health services (21/34, 61.7%), family members or next of kin (17/34, 50%), care home staff and management (14/34, 41.1%) and the resident (9/34, 26.4%) "...if they had capacity".

Improvements and concerns in the last five years:

Care homes:

Care homes were asked how their organisations had been working over the past five years. The most frequently reported improvements over the last 5 years related to staffing (n = 12/37, 32.4%) particularly in recruitment and retention of overseas staff: "Overseas recruitment and integrating 35 overseas staff into the home and local community. This ensured we had sufficient staff to provide high-quality care without using agency staff" (registered manager) Care homes were also proud of the improvements in person-centred care (n = 8/37, 21.6% : "…we have moved away from 1 [one-size] fits all to individualise our care plans to be person centred which reflects each residents needs and preferences…"

The adoption of digital systems and technology (n = 8/37, 21.6%) has "...made a massive difference", leaving staff more time "to be with the residents instead of sitting writing notes". Improvements in training and clinical practice (n = 8/37, 21.6%) were equally cited as areas care homes were proud in the last five years. This was followed by changes in culture and staff wellbeing (n = 6/37, 16.7%) that has improved the "...overall wellbeing of the residents and staff", upgrades or maintenance of accreditation and reputation (n = 6/37, 16.7%) from the Care Quality Commission (CQC) and improvements in physical environment (n = 6/37, 16.7%). Some care homes were proud of their relationships and communication (n = 5/37, 13.5%) with "...local professionals" and "...residents and families" with one citing that they particularly valued "community involvement and fundraising events...".

While most respondents reported no concerns around how their care home has operated in the last five years (17/37, 45.9%), the most frequently cited issues related to staffing (8/37, 21.6%) and agency use. The pressure from limited funding (5/37, 13.5%) concerned some care homes: "Mainly financial pressures due to a lack of funding and ever-increasing cost of living. We are determined not to lower standards, but this is proving harder than ever".

External healthcare support issues were highlighted in two responses: "since the pandemic I find it very difficult for a GP to visit when needed and we do struggle with support from other professionals at times and can get passed from pillar to post. It is very frustrating when we are trying to get the support for our residents and feel that each door we approach is closed in our faces." Anticipated changes and priorities for reform:

Funding concerns:

Funding concerns predominated in responses in care home and home care respondents. When asked about proposed changes to how services operate that participants found concerning, funding and budget cuts were most frequently cited by home care agencies (8/29, 27.6%) with one participant stating: "A lack of sustainable funding means local authorities cannot pay a fair hourly rate for care. This has driven a focus on the self-funded market, but cost-of-living pressures are reducing affordability. At the same time, increases in the National Living Wage and National Insurance have significantly raised costs, squeezing margins and impacting sustainability."

Another commented "Council are looking at reverting all AO2 (assistance of 2) visits down to AO1 (assistance of 1) …This is a massive safety concern and we have had several service users be put back down to AO1 and within a week they are deemed not safe with transfers so then back up to AO2 -this has been very disruptive for both staff and service users."

Ten participants (of 29 responding) stressed that anticipated improvements in the home care sector were funding dependent : "Our LA (local authority) is merging with a wider LA which will be more beneficial as they offer a "Dementia" rate fee. This will enable us to provide extra staffing to make the environment more safe." Anticipated changes in the care home sector also mostly related financial pressures (7/32) especially in association of those in receipt of statutory funding: "reduction in funding by local authority for residents" Changes to care provision When asked about anticipated changes services expect within the next five years, most respondents predicted greater collaboration with other services (16/30, 53.3%) "Increase integrated working…participate in joint reviews…expand our role in supporting hospital discharge pathways and preventing avoidable admissions" (Table 3).

Six respondents anticipated more personalised support over the next 5 years, including the adoption of "trauma -informed and culturally competent approaches", and a greater use of "digital technology, remote monitoring…that enable earlier intervention, improved access, and better self-management of long term conditions" . Likewise, care homes foresee a shift towards more innovation and personalisation of services (6/32).

A shift towards more complex needs among clients was also raised by both home care and care home providers, as an anticipated result of the shift from hospital to community care. Six care home providers raised this, with one describing "clearly a growing trend of treat at home [care homes], and as such is placing more strain on the care home market."

Expansion and growth of the care home into larger sites and more varied units, responding to this anticipated increase in need, also featured in six answers: "We are increasing our occupancy from 58 -84 beds. We are developing the [a] site which will enable us to offer our services to the community".

Workforce support

When we asked what would make an important, positive difference to adult social care providers, twelve home care respondents referred to improved training, accreditation or strengthened career pathways and six to the need for carers to be more valued: "more investment into the care sector, more recognition of the value of care staff and national recognition of the training that is involved in becoming a carer."

Care home respondents described development of staff in terms of better training (5/32) as a priority, some noting challenges in staffing particularly around the recruitment and retention so staff (4/32). Additionally, the incorporation of technology and digitalisation (4/32, 12.5%) to improve "...medical recording..." and wider operations is expected to improve digital support for workers.

Discussion

This national survey provides insight into how English care homes and home care agencies support people diagnosed with dementia. It highlights workforce, service delivery, and system-level challenges. Across both settings, respondents described strong commitment to providing person-centred care despite increasing complexity of client needs, workforce pressures, and financial constraints. Findings suggest that the ability of social care services to meet rising demand associated with dementia is influenced by the interaction between workforce capability, funding, service integration, and access to specialist support.

Home care managers estimated that around one-third of their clients had memory problems, fewer than the estimated 60% of home care clients believed to have some form of cognitive impairment nationally (United Kingdom Homecare Association, 2015). Due to our modest sample size this is difficult to interpret and may reflect differences between our respondents and the wider sector. It may also point to a genuine gap in information sharing or recognition: cognitive impairment may be present but not formally diagnosed or a diagnosis may exist yet not be shared with home care providers by health services. In contrast, most care home residents were reported to have dementia or significant cognitive impairment, perhaps indicative of a more impaired population, or of care homes having stronger relationships with healthcare professionals alongside more consistent identification, and more multidisciplinary input and information sharing relative to home care agencies.

Funding pressures emerged as a dominant theme. Respondents frequently described situations in which care packages were constrained by local authority funding arrangements or by the financial circumstances of self-funding individuals and families. These financial pressures influenced the quantity and quality of care that could be delivered, including visit duration, staffing levels, and access to enhanced support. Managers expressed concern that without sustainable investment, services may struggle to meet future demand while maintaining quality and continuity of care.

As policy continues to promote care closer to home and reduce reliance on hospital-based services, the social care workforce will be expected to support people with complex dementia-related needs. Our findings suggest that many providers are already operating at the limits of available resources and capacity, leaving limited capacity to realise the potential of care integration, for example around virtual wards as a way of avoiding distressing admissions for people with dementia (Wong, A. et al., 2025).

Behaviours that challenge were among the most frequently cited reasons why services were unable to support individuals safely at home or why care arrangements broke down. Such findings are consistent with previous research demonstrating that behavioural symptoms, including aggression are major drivers of caregiver burden, service use, and transitions into residential care (Costello, H., et al., 2019). This also points to an unmet training need particularly in home care provision.

Although most organisations reported providing dementia-specific training, the duration and format varied considerably, and managers identified important gaps in content. Respondents, especially from home care settings highlighted a need for more practical and interactive dementia training focused on communication, de-escalation, risk management, and supporting families during periods of crisis, suggesting that existing provision may not fully equip staff to respond to increasingly complex care needs. Previous work has highlighted limited alignment between training delivered in social care settings and the Dementia Training Standards Framework (Smith, S. J., et al., 2026). Experiential and practice-based learning approaches may be more effective than more didactic training programmes in supporting high-quality dementia care (Delray, S. et al., 2024). Peer learning groups are mandated for doctors as a condition of registration, and no health professional would be expected to provide client-facing care without training involving case-based, peer learning. Most home care workers have no such training (Cooper, C. et al., 2024; Kelleher, D. et al., 2024) and the provider model makes it hard to achieve, particularly in the home care workforce. Workforce development will be critical to realising the ambitions of integrated, community-based dementia care.

While many respondents described positive examples of collaborative working between independent provision and statutory social care providers and health services, others reported difficulties accessing health and social care professionals, delays in obtaining support, and limited recognition of the expertise held by care workers. Home care staff felt that their detailed knowledge of clients was sometimes undervalued during multidisciplinary decision-making processes. Effective integration requires not only structural reform but also recognition of the contribution social care providers make to assessment, monitoring, and ongoing support of people living with dementia.

Approximately half of home care agencies and two-thirds of care homes reported supporting people with limited English proficiency, yet access to professional interpreter services was uncommon. Providers relied on multilingual staff, family members, and translation technologies. Pairing clients with a small, familiar pool of staff may support relationship building and care continuity but this could create risk if staff members are unavailable or raise scheduling difficulties. Whilst these approaches offer practical solutions, they may not always offer consistency or the accuracy required for complex conversations about dementia care, decision-making, or safeguarding, potentially compounding disorientation and distress (Cooper, C. et al., 2017).

Managers reported challenges recruiting and retaining staff, frequently attributing departures to low pay, limited career progression opportunities, unsociable or inflexible hours and movement into better-remunerated health sector roles. Travel demands were recognised as negatively influencing retention as unlike NHS employers home care agencies are not required to reimburse workers for mileage and rising fuel prices alongside the introduction of clean air zones appear to have compounded this burden. Respondents also reported high levels of abuse from clients and family members highlighting workplace safety and emotional burden as important but under-recognised aspects of dementia care work (Kadri, A. et al., 2018). Together, these findings suggest that improving workforce retention is likely to require a combination of fair pay, enhanced training, professional recognition, career development opportunities, and improved employment conditions.

Participants anticipated that future dementia care provision would involve greater integration with health services, increased use of digital technologies, and more personalised models of care. These aspirations, which align with theoretical models of good quality dementia home care (Cooper, C et al. 2013), were accompanied by concerns about funding sustainability and workforce capacity. Successful implementation of policies aimed at shifting care from hospitals to community settings will therefore require parallel investment in the social care infrastructure needed to support these ambitions.

Strengths and limitations

This study is among the largest contemporary surveys of dementia care provision across both home care and care home sectors in England and includes organisations from geographically diverse regions. Domiciliary care represents a substantial portion of national dementia support provisions but is less researched. The response rate was modest, raising the possibility of response bias, as well as desirability bias, as respondents may have been more engaged with dementia care than non-participants. Data were reported by managers and senior staff rather than frontline care workers or people with dementia and their families, so reflect organisational rather than direct experiences of care. Many questions relied on self-report and estimation which may be subject to recall or perception bias.

Implications for policy and practice

The findings suggest that achieving national ambitions for community-based dementia care will require greater investment in the social care workforce, enhanced access to specialist dementia support, and stronger integration between health and social care services. Workforce training, especially in the home care sector, should move toward practical, skillsbased approaches focused on behavioural support, communication, and collaborative working with families. Policy initiatives aimed at improving pay, professional recognition, and career progression within social care may contribute to improved workforce retention and care quality.

Conclusion

Social care organisations play a central role in supporting people with dementia across England. Despite examples of innovation, commitment, and collaborative practice, providers face persistent challenges related to funding, workforce capacity, training, and service integration.
