Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background: Distinguishing high-grade (HGSC) from low-grade serous carcinoma (LGSC) is clinically consequential yet difficult, as the subtypes share a serous morphology but differ in prognosis and treatment. Prior deep-learning studies report near-perfect accuracy, but often under patch-level evaluation that leaks slide information between training and testing.

We present a leakage-controlled, slide-level ablation of attention-augmented CNNs for this binary task on the UBC-OCEAN cohort (259 whole-slide images; 217 HGSC, 42 LGSC). Under an identical protocol with five-fold slide-grouped cross-validation, Macenko stain normalisation, and slidelevel pooling, we compared a from-scratch backbone with no attention, Squeeze-and-Excitation, channelonly CBAM and full CBAM, a lightweight CBAM variant, and an ImageNet-pretrained MobileNet with a differential-attention module. A pathology foundation model (Phikon) with attention-based multipleinstance aggregation was evaluated as a modern benchmark. Results are reported across three training seeds, with decision thresholds selected within folds, and compared using DeLong and McNemar tests with Holm correction and bootstrap confidence intervals.

Results: The pretrained model performed best (AUC 0.862 ± 0.005, MCC 0.460) and was the most stable across seeds. It significantly outranked both the Squeeze-and-Excitation model (DeLong, Holm p = 0.002) and the no-attention baseline (p = 0.019). Full CBAM also outranked Squeeze-and-Excitation (p = 0.010), but adding the spatial-attention branch to channel attention produced no detectable change (0.824 to 0.822; p = 0.50), and the lightweight model did not match the deep one once training variance was accounted for. The foundation-model benchmark reached AUC 0.834 ± 0.018, below the pretrained MobileNet. A colour-only classifier reached AUC 0.66, indicating part of the separability is attributable to stain and colour rather than morphology.

Conclusion: Under leakage-free evaluation, performance is more modest than commonly reported. Transfer learning and the avoidance of a weak channel-only configuration produced the clearest gains, while finer attention design, network size and a pathology-specific foundation model produced no detectable improvement. With only 42 LGSC slides the task remains underpowered for finer distinctions, and most architectural differences could not be resolved.

1. INTRODUCTION

Ovarian cancer ranks among the most lethal gynaecological cancers in women, with a median survival below three years in advanced disease and, by GLOBOCAN 2022 estimates, approximately 324,398 new cases and 206,839 deaths worldwide. The majority are epithelial in origin and are stratified into serous, mucinous, clear cell, and endometrioid subtypes. Within the serous group, two grades govern clinical management. High-grade serous carcinoma (HGSC) constitutes approximately three-quarters of cases and accounts for most ovarian cancer mortality through rapid proliferation and early peritoneal dissemination, and low-grade serous carcinoma (LGSC), which is comparatively indolent and associated with longer survival but exhibits marked resistance to platinum-based chemotherapy. As the two grades arise through distinct molecular pathways and mandate different therapeutic strategies, their accurate discrimination is a clinically consequential diagnostic task.(1)(2)(3)(4)(5)(6) (7) This discrimination is challenging even for expert pathologists. HGSC and LGSC share a serous, papillary architecture and are separated principally by nuclear criteria. HGSC exhibits pronounced pleomorphism, prominent nucleoli, and a mitotic rate exceeding twelve mitoses per ten high-power fields, whereas LGSC displays uniform nuclei with only mild atypia. The intrinsic heterogeneity of HGSC, which encompasses a solid, pseudo-endometrioid, and transitional (SET) variant, further attenuates the morphological boundary between the grades. This difficulty carries over to automated systems, making the distinction that matters most for treatment also the one they resolve least reliably.(8)(9) (9) Deep learning has been applied extensively to ovarian subtyping, frequently with reported accuracies approaching ceiling. A closer examination, however, reveals three systematic methodological limitations that qualify these results. First, the task is predominantly formulated as five-class subtyping, so aggregate performance is dominated by the prevalent and readily separable classes and does not isolate the HGSC-LGSC boundary; a high multiclass score is therefore consistent with poor discrimination of the rare serous grades. Second, many reported accuracies derive from image-or patch-level partitioning, in which tiles originating from a single slide are distributed across both training and test sets. Because such tiles share staining, scanner, and patient-specific characteristics, a model may exploit slide identity as a shortcut rather than learn disease morphology, yielding performance estimates that are optimistically biased and do not generalise under case-level evaluation. Third, prevailing pipelines seldom quantify predictive uncertainty, rarely compare attention or backbone configurations under matched conditions, and use explainability for illustration rather than validation. Even where large pretrained models have been carefully tuned, gains in balanced accuracy on this task are reported to be marginal (10), suggesting the problem lies less in model capacity than in how models are evaluated and understood.

What the field therefore needs is not a higher headline number, but evidence that can be trusted. Such evidence requires performance reported with proper statistical uncertainty, comparisons that isolate the effect of each design choice, and confirmation that a model bases its decisions on tumour tissue rather than incidental cues. These requirements matter beyond benchmarking, since a model intended to support diagnosis must be both reliable under honest evaluation and interpretable enough for a pathologist to trust before it can be considered for clinical use. Guided by these principles, the present work makes three contributions.

A slide-level evaluation protocol is implemented in which all tiles from a slide are assigned to the same fold, tile predictions are aggregated to a single slide-level prediction, and results are reported with bootstrap confidence intervals and significance tests.

An ablation of attention mechanisms is performed with the backbone, dataset, and training procedure held constant, varying only the attention block across CBAM, SE, a differentialattention module, and a pretrained backbone.

A shortcut analysis using a colour-only baseline tests whether the separability between grades reflects morphology rather than stain or scanner signatures; direct validation of the attention modules against expert tumour masks is identified as the primary extension of this work.

2. RELATED WORK

Treatment of ovarian carcinoma is increasingly guided by histological subtype and grade, which makes accurate diagnosis a prerequisite for appropriate care. That diagnosis, however, still rests on the visual assessment of haematoxylin and eosin stained tissue, and is known to be difficult to reproduce. Among pathologists without gynaecological subspecialty training, agreement on ovarian carcinoma subtype is only moderate, and the two-tier system used to separate high-from low-grade serous carcinoma has itself shown measurable inter-and intra-observer variability, since it depends on fine nuclear judgements such as atypia and mitotic activity that are inherently subjective. Because of this uncertainty, pathologists frequently rely on ancillary immunohistochemical stains and second opinions to reach a confident diagnosis, which lengthens the diagnostic pathway and adds cost. These pressures, compounded by a global shortage of specialist pathologists, have motivated the search for automated tools that can support and standardise histological assessment. Digital pathology has taken up this challenge. Convolutional networks applied to whole-slide images have moved from tumour detection and tissue segmentation to full histotype classification, and the strongest systems now approach the reproducibility of subspecialist pathologists for the five-class subtyping task, exceeding the agreement typically achieved by general pathologists even when tested on tissue prepared at a different institution. This progress establishes that automated ovarian subtyping is feasible at a clinically meaningful level.(11)(9)(10) (10) That success, however, is largely of coarse subtyping, in which the common and morphologically distinct classes are separated. The finer distinction that lies within the serous group, between high-grade and lowgrade serous carcinoma, has received far less dedicated attention despite being the boundary of greatest therapeutic consequence. It is also intrinsically the hardest, as the two grades share a serous, papillary appearance and are separated mainly by subtle nuclear detail, and as high-grade tumours are themselves heterogeneous through the SET variant. This difficulty is visible even in automated results. In the analysis accompanying the dataset used here, agreement between model prediction and reference diagnosis was lowest for low-grade serous carcinoma of all subtypes. The remainder of this section examines how deep learning has been applied to this specific distinction, and where those efforts fall short.(8)(11) (12) A substantial body of work has applied deep learning to ovarian histopathology and reported strikingly high accuracy, while contributing genuinely useful methodological ideas. Table 1 summarises the representative approaches.

Behera et al. proposed a hybrid two-stage pipeline in which deep features from EfficientNet-B0 are classified by a fine-tuned k-nearest-neighbour model rather than a softmax layer, a design that cleanly separates representation learning from classification, and reported 100 percent accuracy across the five UBC-OCEAN subtypes. Their own per-class test results, however, are considerably weaker than this headline, with sensitivity falling to 42.9 percent for high-grade serous and 64.3 percent for endometrioid carcinoma, with a test AUC for high-grade serous of only 0.69, indicating that the aggregate figure does not reflect performance on the individual classes. The evaluation also used a single 80-to-20 split without cross-validation, on classes containing as few as forty original images before augmentation. The OvCan-FIND model contributed a task-specific architecture built on EfficientNetV2 with squeeze-and-excitation blocks, explicitly aimed at reducing overfitting, and reported 99.74 percent accuracy against a broad panel of standard backbones. Here the evaluation rests on 5942 images from 42 patients under an image-level split, leaving a test partition of only a handful of images, and the authors themselves list dataset bias, class imbalance, overfitting risk, absence of explainability, and lack of uncertainty estimation among the study's limitations. Abd El-Latif et al. combined ResNet-50 features with decision-tree recursive feature elimination and a fuzzy deep-learning classifier, an interesting integration of fuzzy logic with deep representations, and reported 98.99 percent accuracy; this result, however, concerns the comparatively easier cancer-versus-normal detection task rather than subtype classification, and again uses a single 80to-20 split. Together these studies advance hybrid classification, data-efficient architectures, and fuzzylogic fusion, and confirm that deep networks can extract discriminative morphological features from ovarian tissue.(13)(30)(15) (16) The common thread is that these accuracies are obtained on small datasets, under image-level splits without case-level grouping or cross-validation, and without reported uncertainty, conditions under which a model can score highly while learning slide-specific rather than disease-specific cues. The effect becomes apparent when evaluation is tightened. A recent model built on pathology foundation-model features, regularising its attention and validated under ten-fold cross-validation, reported a balanced accuracy of 0.854 and a Cohen's kappa of 0.791, with a lower balanced accuracy of 0.704 when transferred to molecular subtyping on TCGA. In a separate multi-institutional study, an attention-based multiple-instance model aggregated patch predictions to the slide and patient level across four hospitals and was externally validated on high-grade serous cases from TCGA, again reaching robust rather than near-perfect performance. Under these stricter protocols, performance falls below the near-perfect figures reported elsewhere, most clearly on external data, where slide-level accuracy dropped to 0.794, indicating that the difficulty of ovarian subtyping, and of the serous grade distinction in particular, has frequently been understated. Establishing reliable performance therefore calls for leakage-controlled, slide-level evaluation with quantified uncertainty, which the present work adopts.(10) (17) Beyond the choice of classifier, a large part of the methodological effort in this area concerns how attention and instance aggregation are used, since whole-slide images are far too large to classify directly and only a fraction of any slide is diagnostic. Attention modules address the first problem by reweighting features toward informative regions. Channel and spatial attention, exemplified by Squeeze-and-Excitation and CBAM, recalibrate feature responses to emphasise subtle morphology, and this idea has been extended specifically for ovarian subtyping. OVANet couples spatial and channel attention to mitigate class imbalance, while OCCNet introduces an ensemble attention mechanism aimed at imbalanced, multi-centre cohorts. These models confirm that attention improves discrimination, yet OCCNet's own analysis notes that its performance still falls on the subtypes with fewest samples, underlining that attention alone does not resolve the scarcity of rare classes such as low-grade serous carcinoma.(18) (19) The second problem, aggregating many patch-level signals into a single slide decision, is typically handled through multiple-instance learning. Frameworks such as MS-DA-MIL and HAG-MIL pool instance-level attention across multiple resolutions to preserve spatial context, while slide-level representations such as HipoMap weight discriminative regions hierarchically and attention-based survival models such as MHAttnSurv focus on morphologically distinct areas for prognosis. Transformer architectures extend the same principle through self-attention. Vision transformers apply hierarchical attention to gigapixel slides, and kernel-based variants such as KAT capture localised context efficiently. Reliability-oriented work such as MixPatch further shows that calibrated probabilities, not accuracy alone, are essential for clinical use.(20)(21)(22)(23)(24)(25) (26) Two observations follow from this body of work and directly motivate the present study. First, although many attention and aggregation designs are individually effective, they are rarely compared on equal terms; a recent systematic evaluation on this very task found that the choice of feature extractor accounted for far more performance variance than the aggregation strategy, and that dedicating an explicit binary stage to high-grade serous detection outperformed a flat five-class formulation. This indicates that the contribution of any single component can only be established by holding all others fixed, which is the logic of a controlled ablation. Second, the faithfulness of attention is increasingly in question. Work on this dataset has raised the concern that attention may highlight shortcut regions rather than tumour, and comparative studies have begun assessing attention maps against pathologist annotations directly. Together these observations argue for evaluating attention mechanisms under a matched protocol and validating their explanations against expert ground truth, rather than assuming either their relative value or their faithfulness, which is the approach adopted here.(10) (10) In summary, three gaps persist across the literature. The clinically decisive distinction between high-and low-grade serous carcinoma is seldom studied in isolation, and is instead absorbed into a multiclass average dominated by the common high-grade class. Reported performance is frequently obtained under image-or patch-level evaluation without case-level grouping, cross-validation, or quantified uncertainty, so headline accuracies are difficult to trust and often far exceed those of rigorously validated work. And attention mechanisms are typically adopted in isolation rather than compared under matched conditions, with their explanations shown qualitatively rather than validated against ground truth. The present study addresses these gaps together by isolating the binary high-versus low-grade task, evaluating strictly at the slide level under leakage-controlled cross-validation with reported confidence intervals and significance testing, comparing attention mechanisms while holding all other factors fixed, and validating attention against expert tumour masks.

3.1 Dataset and Tile Extraction

The data were obtained from the UBC-OCEAN challenge (AIM Lab, University of British Columbia), a multi-institutional collection of ovarian carcinoma whole-slide images (WSIs) from more than twenty centres. Only the publicly released training set was used, as labels for the challenge test set are withheld. We retained only the two serous subtypes, high-grade (HGSC) and low-grade serous carcinoma (LGSC), framing the task as binary grade discrimination. To hold magnification constant, we used only WSIs (scanned at 20x) and excluded the tissue-microarray cores digitised at 40x. The final cohort comprised 259 slides: 217 HGSC and 42 LGSC. The scarcity of LGSC reflects its clinical rarity and is the principal factor bounding the statistical precision of our results.

Because a WSI spans tens of thousands of pixels per dimension, each slide was decomposed into nonoverlapping 512x512 tiles at native resolution and resized to 224x224 for model input, with slides read in sequential horizontal strips to avoid loading gigapixel images in full. Each tile inherited the grade label of its parent slide. Tile retention then followed a two-branch strategy conditioned on annotation availability. For slides accompanied by the challenge's supplemental tumour masks, a tile was kept only when its mask region was dominated by annotated tumour, yielding confirmed malignant tissue. For slides without masks, a saturation-based tissue detector discarded background, pen marking, and non-stained area, with tumour localisation instead deferred to the model's attention mechanism. A fixed cap of 60 tiles per slide was applied uniformly across both classes to prevent any single slide, particularly the few LGSC cases, from dominating through near-duplicate tiles. This procedure produced 15,532 tiles (13,012 HGSC and 2,520 LGSC), of which 4,260 (27.4 percent) were drawn from mask-confirmed tumour regions and 11,272 from tissue-detected regions. Every tile was tagged with its source-slide identifier, the provenance record underpinning the partitioning described below. The resulting class ratio was inherited directly from the slide-level distribution and deliberately not rebalanced through tile duplication.

3.2 Leakage-Controlled Partitioning

A central priority of this study was the elimination of information leakage between training and evaluation. Because all tiles from one slide share stain, scanner, and patient characteristics, partitioning tiles at random allows a model to recognise slide identity rather than disease morphology, inflating apparent performance. To preclude this, all partitioning was performed at the slide level, so that every tile of a given slide was assigned in full to a single partition. As each WSI in this cohort corresponds to a distinct case, slide-level grouping is functionally equivalent to patient-level separation.

Evaluation used five-fold stratified group cross-validation, with the slide identifier as the grouping key and grade as the stratification target. This guaranteed that no slide contributed tiles to more than one fold while keeping the HGSC:LGSC ratio comparable across folds, with per-fold test composition ranging from six to ten LGSC slides. Within each fold, a further slide-disjoint split reserved a portion of training slides for validation, used for early stopping and learning-rate scheduling. 3.4 Models and Attention Mechanisms

To determine which attention design most benefits the HGSC-LGSC distinction, seven models were evaluated under an identical training and evaluation protocol. Five share one from-scratch four-block backbone and differ only in the attention block, forming a ladder from a no-attention baseline through Squeeze-and-Excitation, channel-only CBAM and full CBAM, with a lightweight CBAM variant isolating network size. The no-attention and channel-only models serve as ablation controls, the first isolating whether attention helps at all and the second whether the spatial branch adds anything beyond the channel descriptor. Two transfer-learning references complete the set, namely an ImageNet-pretrained MobileNet with a differential-attention module, and the Phikon pathology foundation model used as a frozen feature extractor with gated attention-based multiple-instance aggregation. All models were implemented in TensorFlow and Keras, trained with the same class-weighted binary cross-entropy loss, with the attention modules written as interchangeable layers so that a single block could be substituted without any other change.( 30)(31)

The pretrained model addresses a separate question, whether a pretrained backbone offers an advantage over the custom networks. Because MobileNet requires its own input scaling, its preprocessing differed accordingly, and its augmentation was limited to flips and ninety-degree rotations; all other elements of the protocol, including the data splits, loss, and slide-level evaluation, were held constant.

5. DISCUSSION

The seven models were designed to answer, under a common protocol, which attention mechanism and which feature source aid the high-versus low-grade serous distinction. Read together, they give a consistent account in which the source and reliability of the features matter more than the attention design or the network size. The clearest result is that transfer learning helped and was stable. The pretrained MobileNet reached the highest AUC and the smallest seed-to-seed variance, and it significantly outranked both the no-attention baseline and the Squeeze-and-Excitation model. For a cohort of 259 slides, which is too small to train good low-and mid-level filters from scratch, a backbone that already supplies descriptors of edges, textures and nuclear boundaries leaves only the task-specific head to be learned, and this shows in both the point estimate and the stability. The pretrained model did not, however, significantly exceed the best from-scratch network, so the honest statement is that transfer learning provided a reliable rather than a decisively larger improvement.

Within the from-scratch family, the one comparison that survived correction was full CBAM over Squeeze-and-Excitation. Because the two models share a backbone and differ in both the channel descriptor and the spatial branch, this gain cannot be attributed to a single component. The ladder narrows this further, since adding the spatial branch to channel attention produced no detectable change while the channel-only CBAM already matched the full model, which points to the avg-plus-max channel descriptor rather than the spatial branch as the active ingredient. With 42 low-grade slides the study is underpowered to confirm this at the level of the single component, and the intermediate comparisons did not reach significance. The spatial-attention branch, which recent work has treated as the main contribution of CBAM over Squeeze-and-Excitation, did not help here. Network size was not a factor. The lightweight model, at roughly 8000 parameters, fell to the level of the no-attention baseline and below the deep CBAM networks once training variance was included, so the apparent parity between a compact and a deep model seen under a single seed did not survive repeated runs. This is a caution against reading a single-seed result on a small rare class as evidence of a capacity effect. (25) The foundation-model benchmark is the most informative negative result. A pathology foundation model, trained with self-supervision on far larger cohorts than ImageNet, reached 0.834 and did not exceed the pretrained MobileNet. On the five-class subtyping task the literature reports that the choice of feature extractor dominates performance; on this binary serous grade distinction, under leakage-controlled evaluation on a small cohort, that advantage did not appear. The ceiling of roughly 0.83 to 0.86 across the strongest models, rather than the particular backbone, is the recurring feature of the results, and it is consistent with the difficulty of the distinction and the scarcity of the rare class.

A controlled comparison shows which design performs better, but not whether a model reaches its decisions for the right reasons. A colour-only baseline reached a slide-level AUC of 0.66, so part of the separability is carried by stain and colour rather than morphology, even after Macenko normalisation. The trained models exceed this baseline and therefore use more than colour, but the gap between 0.66 and roughly 0.85 bounds the morphological component of their performance and is a reminder that multicentre colour variation is a plausible partial shortcut on this cohort. The per-class recall confirms that the pretrained model's advantage is specific to the rare class. It reached the most balanced recall across grades, 0.86 for high-grade and 0.66 for low-grade carcinoma, while the Squeeze-and-Excitation model had the weakest low-grade recall at 0.51. Against a low-grade prevalence of 0.16, the pretrained model and the foundation model reached the highest average precision, around 0.60, which shows their advantage lies in the rare class rather than the majority. With only 42 low-grade slides even a handful of ambiguous cases can shift a threshold-dependent metric, which accounts for the wide intervals and the fact that most comparisons did not reach significance. These architectural findings carry weight only because they were obtained under a protocol that allows the seven models to be compared on equal terms, and the case for that protocol becomes clear when the existing results on UBC-OCEAN are placed side by side. The dataset was assembled from more than twenty centres with deliberately heterogeneous tissue processing and staining, and its organisers evaluated the challenge on a held-out test set kept separate from algorithm development, explicitly to mitigate the risk of information leakage. The dataset was, in other words, designed with leakage in mind, and the results reported on it vary with how closely later studies have followed that design.( 13) Arranged by the strictness of their evaluation, the published results show a consistent pattern (Table 4). On the organisers' own held-out multi-centre test set, the top-performing solutions to the five-class challenge reached balanced accuracies of only 58 to 66 percent (13). Breen et al., training on 1,864 whole-slide images from an independent centre and validating externally on 513 OCEAN slides, reported a best balanced accuracy of about 0.83 with a self-supervised foundation-model extractor, falling to roughly 0.80 on the external OCEAN cohort, and noted that the dataset's highly heterogeneous preparation contributed to the difficulty (10). The same evaluation found performance to depend strongly on the feature extractor, with the strongest extractors well ahead of ImageNet-pretrained baselines, and summarised earlier cross-validated studies on comparably sized ovarian cohorts reporting balanced accuracies in the region of 0.73 to 0.83 (10). At the other end, results obtained under within-dataset splits drawn at the level of tiles or images are considerably higher. TopOC, trained and tested on 34,000 tiles derived from 975 whole-slide and tissue-microarray images under a predefined tile-level split, reported 66 percent balanced accuracy and compared it with the challenge-winning 66 percent, a comparison between a tile-level internal split and a held-out external evaluation (27). Behera et al. reported complete accuracy under a single image-level 80-to-20 split without cross-validation, while their own per-class results show a high-grade serous sensitivity of 42.9 percent (26). Several of the highest accuracies cited for ovarian subtyping were not obtained on this dataset at all. OvCan-FIND's 99.74 percent, for example, comes from 5,942 images of a single-centre cohort (14). These figures are five-class balanced accuracies and are not directly comparable with the binary AUC reported here; the point is the gradient itself. The more closely an evaluation separates training and test data by case and by centre, the lower the reported performance, which indicates that the difficulty of the task has often been understated.(27)(10)(10)(10)(10) (15) Two further features of the literature prevent these results from being compared at all. Second, some properties of the data remain undocumented, including how ground-truth labels were determined and which tissue types were included, so that differences between studies cannot always be traced to their source. The finding that the choice of feature extractor alone can move balanced accuracy across nearly the entire achievable range makes the point directly, since when a single uncontrolled component produces a swing of that size, a bare accuracy figure says little about a method unless everything else is held fixed. (10) The design of this study was shaped throughout by a single goal, to make each reported difference interpretable rather than merely favourable. Several recurring weaknesses in the existing literature motivated the specific choices. Pooling whole-slide images with tissue microarrays mixes two magnifications and lets scale, rather than morphology, drive part of the separation, so the cohort was restricted to 20x whole-slide images; the trade-off is that resizing tiles to 224 pixels yields an effective resolution of about 1.14 micrometres per pixel, coarser than the acquisition itself. Tile-and image-level partitioning allows patches from one slide to fall on both sides of the split, so a model can recognise slide identity instead of disease, and assigning every tile of a slide to a single partition closes that route. Averaging the rare grade into a five-class score lets a weak class hide behind a strong aggregate, so isolating the binary grade distinction exposes low-grade performance directly, where it is hardest and most clinically consequential. Centre-specific staining is a plausible shortcut on a multi-centre cohort, so normalising every tile against one fixed reference reduces that variation without carrying any label information. Finally, single point estimates on 42 low-grade slides are unstable, so reporting standard deviations across seeds, bootstrap confidence intervals over slides, and Holm-corrected tests replaces them with bounded, corrected quantities. Together these choices are what give the comparison its force, since backbone, loss, data and evaluation are held identical and only the attention block or the backbone varies, so each difference in performance can be attributed to one design choice, which is the defining property of a controlled ablation. The resulting AUCs of 0.74 to 0.86 sit well below the near-perfect figures reported under tile-and image-level protocols, which is the expected consequence of removing those shortcuts rather than a sign of a weaker method. The one gap these controls do not address is external generalisation, since the results come from within-cohort cross-validation rather than an independent test set, and external validation is therefore the natural next step.

These findings should also be read against how the distinction is made in practice. Pathologists separate the two grades using the two-tier system, which rests on nuclear atypia and mitotic activity assessed at high magnification. At the effective resolution used here, individual mitoses and nucleoli are at or below the limit of reliable resolution, so the models most plausibly draw on architectural pattern, nuclear crowding, and gross variation in nuclear size rather than on the criteria a pathologist counts, and they therefore approximate the grading decision rather than reproduce it. Such a system is best suited to an assistive role, flagging slides whose features suggest low-grade morphology for ancillary immunohistochemistry or specialist review where gynaecological pathology expertise is limited. The balanced recall of the pretrained model matters most for this role, since a triage tool must recover the rare class without excessive false alarms on the common one. Temperature scaling improved calibration, but every model continued to overestimate the probability of low-grade carcinoma, a bias that a single temperature cannot correct and that a method with an intercept term, such as Platt scaling, could address. The competitiveness of the lightweight network is relevant here too, since an 8,000-parameter model can run on modest hardware where larger networks are impractical.

Several limitations bound these conclusions, and they also frame how the moderate values reported here should be read. The performance of all models is consistent with other case-level evaluations on this dataset, and the much wider range of figures in the literature appears to reflect differences in evaluation design as much as in method. The cohort is the principal constraint, with only 42 low-grade slides, which widens every confidence interval and leaves most comparisons short of significance after correction. All results come from within-cohort cross-validation on a single dataset, so external validation on independent cohorts with adequate low-grade representation remains necessary, and the scarcity of public low-grade slides currently limits it. The pretrained model also differed from the from-scratch models in both backbone and attention type, so its advantage cannot be cleanly separated between the two, and the coarse effective resolution of the resized tiles further restricts access to the nuclear detail on which grading depends. A further limit concerns interpretability, since the present analysis establishes which design ranks higher but not whether a model attends to tumour tissue, and a direct evaluation of the attention modules against expert masks, using localisation metrics such as the pointing game and perpixel saliency agreement, is the natural next step, with the colour-only control already bounding how much of the separability is non-morphological. Each of these points suggests a direct extension, namely external testing on multi-centre cohorts, higher-magnification inputs that resolve nuclear and mitotic detail, pathology-specific foundation-model features with attention-based aggregation, and finer-grained attribution applied to the attention modules. Taken together, the study shows that under leakage-controlled evaluation the high-versus low-grade distinction remains genuinely difficult, that transfer learning and the avoidance of a weak channel-only configuration contribute measurably while spatial attention and network size do not, and that these conclusions become clear only when every model is measured against the same fixed standard.

6. CONCLUSION

Automated grading of serous ovarian carcinoma matters because the high-versus low-grade distinction directly shapes treatment, yet it is the boundary that both pathologists and algorithms find hardest. This work compared attention and architecture choices one at a time under a common, leakage-controlled standard, across three seeds and with multiple-comparison correction, and showed that the distinction is more difficult than much of the literature suggests. Under these conditions the source and reliability of the features mattered more than the attention design or the network size. Transfer learning and the avoidance of a weak channel-only configuration produced the clearest gains, while the spatial attention branch, a larger network and a pathology-specific foundation model produced no detectable improvement. These conclusions rest on a single cohort of 42 low-grade slides, which leaves most finer comparisons underpowered. Future work should pursue external validation on cohorts with greater low-grade representation, higher-magnification inputs capable of resolving mitotic detail, direct evaluation of the attention modules against expert masks, and a shared evaluation protocol for UBC-OCEAN that allows methods to be compared directly.
