Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

How Effect Sizes in t-tests and F-tests works

The Effect Size module checks if effect sizes are correctly reported in t-tests and F-tests.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Dickkopf-1 (DKK1) is a secreted protein that acts as a Wnt antagonist and is implicated in osteosarcoma progression. Elevated circulating DKK1 levels have been described at OS diagnosis, but its circulating dynamics, prognostic relevance, and cellular distribution remain incompletely characterized.

We analysed longitudinal plasma DKK1 in 34 patients using mixed-effects models, evaluated DKK1 expression and survival in two independent transcriptomic cohorts, and examined cellular and tissue distribution using single-cell transcriptomics and immunohistochemistry. Cultured mesenchymal stem cells were assessed as potential nonmalignant sources.

Circulating DKK1 declined by 12.4% per 30-day period during treatment, without significant changes detected around primary tumour resection or relapse. Metastatic presentation and poor histologic response were associated with higher estimated DKK1 levels. Higher tumour DKK1 expression was associated with worse overall and metastasis-free survival (pooled HRs per SD increase: 1.35 [95% CI 1.02-1.78] and 1.48 [1.06-2.07], respectively). DKK1 transcripts were predominantly detected in malignant cells across multiple subpopulations and protein staining was enriched in osteoid and matrix-rich regions of primary tumours.

Tumour DKK1 expression is associated with adverse outcomes, while circulating concentration decline over treatment time. These findings support further prognostic evaluation and investigation of the associations among DKK1 expression and production, tissue localisation, and circulating levels.

Introduction

One of the central challenges in the treatment of osteosarcoma (OS) is metastatic progression. Although the combination of surgery and multi-agent chemotherapy results in long-term survival in approximately 70% of patients with localized disease, outcomes remain poor for patients with metastatic or recurrent OS [1]. Attempts to intensify conventional chemotherapy have failed to improve outcomes, while many clinical trials investigating systemic therapies for recurrent disease have likewise yielded limited benefits [2, 3]. These limitations highlight the need to identify molecular pathways involved in metastatic progression, particularly those that may be therapeutically targeted or evaluated through circulating biomarkers.

Dickkopf-1 (DKK1) is a secreted protein that acts as an antagonist of canonical Wnt signalling through interaction with the LRP5/6 and Kremen-1/2 coreceptors [4, 5]. In addition, DKK1 can interact with CKAP4, activating the AKT signalling pathway [6]. During normal bone development, DKK1 blocks the differentiation and maturation of osteoprogenitors by suppressing Wnt signalling and can shift lineage commitment in mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) to adipogenesis [7, 8]. DKK1 is often secreted by osteoblasts, resulting in negative regulation of bone formation [9].

In OS, DKK1 has been associated with impaired osteogenic differentiation, increased tumour growth and bone destruction, and activation of ALDH activity [10, 11]. We have previously shown that DKK1-directed interventions reduce metastatic progression in OS models [11] [12] [13]. In addition, a recent analysis combining bulk and single-cell transcriptomic data nominated DKK1 as a candidate prognostic factor [14]. Together, these findings implicate DKK1 in tumour progression and suggest that its effects on osteogenic differentiation and bone homeostasis may contribute to interactions between tumour cells and their surrounding microenvironment.

As a secreted protein, DKK1 is also detectable in circulation. Newly diagnosed OS presents with elevated serum DKK1 compared to healthy controls, suggesting potential utility as a biomarker [15]. However, the interpretation of serum DKK1 is complicated by its potential sources, which can include both normal and malignant cells. Activated platelets secrete DKK1 [16], and experimental work in breast cancer has identified contributions from tumoral and stromal compartments, as well as normal bone [17]. Additionally, in breast cancer, serum DKK1 levels are elevated compared to healthy controls, and even more increased in patients with bone metastases, suggesting the bone microenvironment is especially relevant as a potential source of circulating DKK1 [18]. This provides a rationale for evaluating circulating DKK1 in OS patients longitudinally to understand whether circulating DKK1 concentrations change during treatment, reflect disease burden, or correlate with clinical characteristics.

In this study, we quantified plasma DKK1 concentrations during OS treatment and follow-up and assessed associations with metastatic presentation and histologic response. We then evaluated the relationships between DKK1 expression in biopsy samples with patient outcomes in two public OS cohorts. Finally, we integrated single-cell transcriptomic analyses with immunohistochemical staining of patient tumour specimens to characterize the cellular distribution of DKK1 and identify candidate cell populations involved in its signalling. Together, these analyses aimed to characterize the dynamics of circulating DKK1, evaluate the prognostic relevance of tumour DKK1 expression, and investigate its cellular distribution and potential signalling context within the OS microenvironment.

Results

Plasma DKK1 declines steadily during treatment.

The study cohort comprised 34 patients with OS who were enrolled in a clinical trial, MCC20320, designed to identify and evaluate blood-based biomarkers of sarcoma prognosis and response to therapy. The mean enrolment age of the OS cohort was 15.5 years. Metastatic status at diagnosis was documented for 33 patients, while histologic response was available for 27; seventeen healthy donors provided comparing samples (Table 1). In patients, blood was collected at defined stages of therapy, during post-therapy follow-up, and at the time of relapse, processed to isolate plasma, and DKK1 concentration was measured using a Luminex immunoassay (Fig. 1A).

Individual plasma DKK1 measurements varied widely across patients and over time, showing an initial decline followed by a plateau and potential rebound after end of therapy (Fig. 1B). A generalized additive mixed model (GAMM) fit confirmed the nonlinearity of the trajectory (Supp. Fig. 1C); however, fewer samples were collected after end of therapy, which increased the uncertainty in the trajectory of the latter part of the curve. Therefore, we examined the interval between diagnosis and end-of-therapy separately, during which the plasma DKK1 declined approximately linearly (Fig. 1C).

We modelled the diagnosis-to-end-of-therapy interval using a multivariable linear mixed model (LMM) adjusted for age, metastatic status at diagnosis, and histologic response (138 observations from 26 patients, Fig. 1D). In the primary restricted maximum likelihood (REML) fit, DKK1 declined at a rate of -0.00192 log10 units per day, or around 12.4% per 30-day period. Older age was associated with lower DKK1, at a rate of 5.7% per year (Table 2). The estimated time effect remained negative under alternative approaches to handling measurements below the assay's detection limit (Supp. Fig. 1F). DKK1 levels at diagnosis and end-of-therapy were higher than those observed in healthy controls (Fig. 1E). These comparisons were interpreted descriptively and with caution, as healthy donors were older than the patient cohort, and patient and control samples were collected and processed differently and assayed on different plates, with internal and quality controls varying between plates, with coefficients of variation of 28-89% (Table 1; Supp. Fig. 1D and 1E). The restricted longitudinal analysis from diagnosis to end-of-therapy therefore provides the main evidence for changes in plasma DKK1 during treatment.

Local control does not result in a detectable decrease in DKK1 levels.

To assess whether surgical resection of the tumour was associated with changes in circulating DKK1 levels, we aligned serial measurements to local control. DKK1 concentrations decreased across this interval without a clear abrupt shift in the trajectory (Fig. 2A). Paired comparisons detected no difference between the samples obtained pre-and post-surgical resection of the primary tumour, with the samples obtained prior to local control and two (p = 0.93, 24 pairs) and eight weeks (p = 0.21, 22 pairs) afterward (Fig. 2B).

We also fitted a piecewise LMM, with a knot at the first scheduled post-surgery sample, 14 days after the estimated date of surgery (defined as 14 days before the two week post-surgical sample was obtained). In this analysis, both the earlier and later segments show a decline in DKK1 levels, with no significant difference in their slopes (Fig. 2C); analyses using alternative windows around local control likewise did not identify a clear change in slope (Supp. Fig. 2D).

Together, these analyses did not identify a clear change in circulating DKK1 around local control at the sampled timepoints.

Metastatic status at diagnosis and poor histologic response are associated with higher estimated circulating DKK1 levels.

We next examined the relationship between clinical features and DKK1 concentrations during treatment. Patients with metastatic disease at diagnosis had a higher estimated DKK1 concentration than those with localized disease (ratio 1.95, 95% CI 0.996-3.80, p = 0.051; Fig. 2D). Given the small number of patients presenting with metastatic disease in the primary model (n = 3), we performed a sensitivity analysis using an expanded cohort that included patients with incomplete percent necrosis data (162 observations from 32 patients, 4 with metastatic disease at diagnosis). The expanded cohort model similarly showed increased DKK1 concentrations in patients with metastatic disease (beta: 0.305, ratio: 2.02, 95% CI 1.10-3.71, p = 0.026). In addition, leave-one-out analyses in both the primary and expanded-cohort models showed consistent directionality in the relationship between metastatic status at diagnosis and DKK1 levels, indicating that the estimated direction of this association was not driven by any single patient (Supp. Fig. 2E).

Neither diagnostic DKK1 concentrations nor their change from diagnosis to pre-surgical resection show a clear relationship with percent tumour necrosis (treated as a continuous variable) in exploratory analyses (Supp. Fig. 2F). Comparisons using a 90% necrosis threshold similarly did not identify differences in diagnostic DKK1 concentrations (p = 0.73); however, there appeared to be a difference in preoperative change in DKK1, though this only approached significance (p = 0.10; Supp. Fig. 2G). In the primary model, patients with at least 90% tumour necrosis (deemed as good responders to chemotherapy) had lower estimated DKK1 concentrations compared to patients with less than 90% necrosis (deemed as poor responders), though this association was also more uncertain (ratio 0.653, 95% CI 0.373-1.14, p = 0.128; Fig. 2E). Nevertheless, leave-one-out analyses of the primary model showed consistent directionality in the relationship between histologic response and DKK1 levels (Supp. Fig. 2H). Overall, this suggests that patients with good histologic response have lower adjusted DKK1 concentrations. Although the confidence interval included no difference, the consistency of this estimate across leave-one-out analyses supports further investigation.

Higher tumour DKK1 expression is associated with poor outcomes.

To examine the clinical relevance of DKK1 expression in primary tumours, we analysed diagnostic biopsy tumour expression data and outcomes from TARGET-OS and GSE33382 (Table 3) [19, 20]. DKK1 expression was standardised within each cohort and modelled using Cox regression; cohort-specific estimates were combined by inverse-variance fixed effect pooling (Fig. 3A). For overall survival, the association estimates were similar in both cohorts, with the pooled analysis showing higher DKK1 expression is associated with a 35% increased hazard of death (95% CI, 2-78%, p = 0.034; Fig. 3B). Similarly, relapse-free/metastasis-free survival (RMFS) analyses showed that higher DKK1 expression was associated with increased hazard of metastasis in the TARGET-OS cohort (HR, 1.99, 95% CI 1.16-3.42, p = 0.0120), though the estimate in GSE33382 was smaller and less precise (HR, 1.22, 95% CI 0.79-1.88, p = 0.361). The pooled estimate suggested that higher DKK1 expression is associated with a 48% increased hazard of metastasis (95% CI, 6-107%, p = 0.023; Fig. 3C).

When the overall survival analyses allowed separate baseline hazards according to metastatic status at diagnosis, the DKK1 effect estimates remained above one, with wide confidence intervals in the individual cohorts (Fig. 3D). These results support an association between higher tumour DKK1 expression and adverse outcomes. Kaplan-Meier visualizations and model diagnostics are provided in Supp. Fig. 3.

DKK1 in osteosarcoma is expressed predominantly by malignant cells.

We next decided to investigate the cellular origins of DKK1 expression in primary OS. We leveraged the OsteoCAR atlas, a cross-species collection of scRNA-seq datasets that have been integrated and annotated [21]. We selected primary-site tumours, including local recurrences, from two human datasets (BC and OS). DKK1 expression was highly heterogeneous across different tumours (Supp. Fig. 4A), but the majority of detected DKK1 transcripts originated from malignant cells (Fig. 4A; Supp Fig. 4A-B). Two exceptions were BC21 and OS5, in which nonmalignant cells accounted for a greater proportion of DKK1 transcripts. However, these were the lowest DKK1-expressing tumours in their respective datasets, and contained fewer than 10 raw DKK1 pseudobulk counts, limiting interpretation.

Within the malignant compartment, the fraction of DKK1-expressing cells was significantly different across OsteoCAR-defined subpopulations (p = 0.0003; Fig. 4B). Basal progenitor, proliferative, and COMA (chondroid-osteoid matrix-associated) subpopulations had higher proportions of DKK1-positive cells compared to the fibrogenic, interactive, and MP progenitor subpopulations. Notably, the OsteoCAR atlas describes the basal progenitor and proliferative subpopulations as enriched in regulons that suggest a less differentiated phenotype, while the COMA subpopulation is described as having a more differentiated phenotype and being enriched in hypoxia-and angiogenesis-related genes, as well as having a spatial association with chondroid-osteoid matrix deposition [21]. Overall, this suggests that DKK1 is expressed mainly by malignant cells, but that expression is distributed across multiple malignant cell states.

DKK1 protein is enriched in osteoid and matrix-rich regions of primary tumours.

Given the heterogeneous distribution of DKK1 transcript across malignant cell states, and the possibility that secreted DKK1 protein may accumulate at sites far from its cellular site of production, we decided to explore protein expression in primary tumour samples. We performed immunohistochemical staining of DKK1 on a tissue microarray (TMA) containing 50 cores from primary OS tumours. Using QuPath, we developed a pathologist-guided pixel classifier to distinguish three histologic tumour compartments: matrix-poor/cell dense regions, matrix-rich tumour regions, and osteoid (Fig. 4C). Consistent with these compartment assignments, cell dense regions exhibited higher nuclear density compared to matrix-rich and osteoid regions (Supp. Fig. 4C).

Of the 50 TMA cores, 49 contained all three histologic compartments and were included in the paired compartment-level comparisons. DKK1 staining differed significantly across histologic compartments (Friedman test, p = 4.07 × 10⁻⁷; Fig. 4D). The DKK1 area-intensity score was highest in osteoid, followed by matrix-rich tumour and cell-dense regions, with post hoc Conover comparisons identifying significant differences between compartments. A similar pattern was observed for the fraction of DKK1-positive area within each compartment (Friedman test, p = 5.20 × 10 ⁻7 ; Fig. 4E). Because the relative areas occupied by each histologic compartment varied between TMA cores (Supp. Fig. 4D), we also calculated DKK1 enrichment as the fraction of total DKK1-positive area attributable to a compartment divided by that compartment's fraction of total tissue area. DKK1 enrichment also differed significantly between compartments (p = 5.20 × 10 ⁻7 ), with the greatest enrichment in osteoid, followed by matrix-rich and cell-dense regions (Fig. 4F). Taken together, these findings suggest that, although DKK1 transcripts are detected predominantly in malignant cells, DKK1 protein expression is preferentially distributed within osteoid and matrix-rich regions of primary tumours.

No significant increase in plasma DKK1 was detected at relapse.

Given the association between tumoral DKK1 expression and metastatic hazard, we next investigated whether circulating DKK1 changed upon disease relapse. Plasma DKK1 concentrations were higher at relapse compared with end-of-therapy samples, although this was not statistically significant (Fig. 5A). As relapse occurred at different timepoints during treatment or follow-up across patients, we compared each patient's relapse sample with their most recent available pre-relapse sample (Fig. 5B). This paired analysis revealed no significant change in circulating DKK1 at relapse, consistent with our finding that circulating DKK1 does not behave like a real-time readout of tumour burden.

DKK1 expression in cultured mesenchymal cells identifies a candidate nonmalignant source.

Finally, given the potential contribution of nonmalignant cells to circulating DKK1, particularly at the time of relapse, we explored whether DKK1 expression could be associated with a reactive stromal phenotype. We analysed transcriptomic data from primary osteoblasts and human umbilical cord-derived mesenchymal stem cells (ucMSCs) cultured under basal conditions, induced towards osteogenic differentiation, or cultured in conditioned medium from control or TGF-β-overexpressing LNCaP cells to promote a reactive, myofibroblast-like phenotype. DKK1 expression was elevated in the TGF-β-exposed ucMSCs compared with other conditions (Fig. 5C). This suggests that DKK1 expression may be associated with TGF-β-induced MSC activation, identifying a potential nonmalignant source that could augment the DKK1 levels that derive from the malignant tumour cells.

Discussion

Circulating DKK1 declines over treatment course, with no noticeable abrupt change around the time of surgical resection of the primary tumour. Previous studies have shown elevated DKK1 serum levels at diagnosis in OS patients, as well as protein expression in the tumour [15]. These findings extend earlier cross-sectional observations of elevated serum DKK1 in OS by characterizing its trajectory during treatment. We also show that higher diagnostic tumour DKK1 expression is associated with poorer survival outcomes and with increased metastatic risk. Our analysis of single-cell transcriptomic datasets show that DKK1 transcripts are predominantly attributable to malignant cells within primary tumours, with the protein being enriched in matrix-rich and osteoid compartments of the tumour. Taken together, these observations suggest a unique role of DKK1 at the interface between malignant-cell biology and the surrounding bone and stromal environment, although the separate study cohorts prevent direct assessment of how tumour expression and tissue localization relate to circulating concentrations.

The main finding that DKK1 levels decline monotonically during treatment raises the possibility of multiple potential DKK1 sources. Chemotherapy-induced cell death likely plays a role in this reduction, as the decline in DKK1 precedes surgical resection of the tumour, while host responses to the tumour, treatment, and tissue repair may influence subsequent concentrations. In addition, the resection itself does not seemingly alter the course of the decline, which is compatible with an additional, nonmalignant source of circulating DKK1. Recent work in bone-metastatic breast cancer models has shown that bone-derived DKK1 is the predominant source of systemic DKK1, while cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) contribute most of the tumoral DKK1 in the primary lesion [17]. In OS, reactive changes in the surrounding bone and postoperative repair are therefore plausible contributors. Platelets are another likely nonmalignant source, as it has been shown that DKK1 protein is present in platelet extracts [16, 22]. Interestingly, DKK1 mRNA is not expressed in platelets, suggesting that DKK1 protein presence in platelets is due to uptake by endocytosis [22]. Thus, platelets may provide a releasable pool of DKK1 without identifying the tissue in which the protein was originally synthesized.

We also explored the relationship between tumour DKK1 expression and patient survival, and show that a continuous expression analysis supports a negative association between DKK1 and both overall survival and relapse-free/metastasis-free survival. Notably, there is evidence that DKK1-targeted therapy reduces ALDH activity, enhances osteogenic differentiation in the primary tumour, reduces metastatic burden in OS models [11] [12] [13] 23]. Wnt inhibition through DKK1 expression has also been shown to promote a quiescent, immune-evading state in lung and breast cancer models, suggesting that DKK1 can influence persistence and immune escape, which are critical for the establishment of a metastatic niche [24].

An additional finding of this study was the potential relationship between circulating DKK1 and histologic response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy. DKK1 concentrations at diagnosis were not significantly associated with subsequent tumour necrosis, suggesting that baseline DKK1 alone is not a predictor of histologic response. However, when longitudinal measurements were considered, patients with good histologic response had lower adjusted DKK1 concentrations throughout treatment, albeit with some uncertainty. These findings raise the possibility that changes in circulating DKK1 during neoadjuvant chemotherapy may be more informative about treatment-associated biological responses than its concentration at diagnosis. Experimental work linking DKK1 to ALDH-associated stress-tolerance provides a biological rationale for examining the relationship of DKK1 to chemoresistance [23]. Larger prospective cohorts with serial sampling and detailed histologic response data will be required to determine whether early changes in DKK1 provide information about chemotherapy response beyond baseline measurements.

DKK1 expression across several malignant cell states suggests that it is not restricted to a single differentiation programme. This finding fits with recent studies showing that OS comprises heterogeneous mesenchymal and osteoblast-associated states that can coexist within individual tumours, with subpopulations existing along multiple potential mesenchymal lineages [25, 26]. Our analysis of scRNA-seq datasets show both progenitor-like tumour cells and more differentiated matrix-associated tumour cells express DKK1. Earlier work has also shown that DKK1 is regulated by the osteoblast transcription factor Sp7, while DKK1 immunodepletion attenuates the inhibition of osteogenesis in MSCs cultured in OS-conditioned medium [9, 15]. Osteoid production, thus, need not imply completion of terminal osteogenic differentiation, and DKK1 may participate in paracrine regulation within a heterogeneous tumour. These possibilities require functional testing, particularly as the consequences of Wnt signalling repression differ across OS models [27, 28].

Our IHC analysis shows a clear enrichment of DKK1 protein along matrix-rich and osteoid regions of the tumour, adding a spatial dimension to this interpretation. Importantly, protein localization need not coincide with the site of transcription, as secretion, extracellular association, and turnover can influence local availability. This could point to the osteoid matrix being a DKK1 reservoir, and DKK1-glycosaminoglycan interactions have been identified, showing this extracellular association is plausible [29]. The discordance between cellular transcript expression and extracellular protein distribution also raises the possibility that OS cells produce DKK1 to modulate differentiation within the tumour environment. However, our staining does not establish a functional reservoir or identify the cells that produce or respond to the protein. Combined spatial transcriptomics and histological DKK1 staining could be used to assess these hypotheses, and would allow us to deconvolute candidate DKK1 sources and protein localization. In addition, spatial transcriptomic analysis could reveal receptor localization and downstream pathway readouts, allowing us to distinguish production from accumulation and identify candidate paracrine relationships. Recent spatial transcriptomic studies in OS demonstrate the feasibility of this approach [30].

Our observation of increased DKK1 expression in MSCs treated with cultured media from TGF-βoverexpressing LNCaP cells provides another link between potential host sources of DKK1 and tumour-associated tissue responses. TGF-β signalling is a mediator of wound response stromal remodelling and has been shown to induce a fibrotic response in mesenchymal stem cells, including lung-resident MSCs [31] [32] [33]. Within this framework, the response of MSCs to TGF-β stimulation in the context of the profibrotic microenvironment could be associated with pulmonary metastatic colonization. Notably, recent work has shown that disseminated OS cells induce a persistent wound-healing response in the lung, accompanied by profibrotic epithelial and macrophage states as well as extensive ECM deposition [34]. Within this framework, our results raise the possibility that mesenchymal cells responding to tissue injury or profibrotic signals in the lung might provide an additional source of DKK1. This possibility informs our working model (Fig. 5D), in which DKK1 production and local availability reflect contributions from tumour cells and responsive host compartments. Such contributions could help explain why circulating concentrations do not change directly with removal of the primary tumour. However, whether analogous DKK1-producing mesenchymal states occur within pulmonary metastatic niches and whether their DKK1 contributes to circulating levels remains to be established.

Our findings have different implications for biomarker development and prognostics. The association between DKK1 expression and adverse outcomes supports further evaluation of DKK1 as a prognostic marker. In contrast, plasma DKK1 does not behave like a real-time readout of tumour burden, so its utility for monitoring tumour burden or chemotherapy response, or for detecting disease progression, remains uncertain. Prospective studies should evaluate whether early changes in plasma DKK1 improve assessment of treatment response. The associations presented here also complement existing preclinical evidence that DKK1targeted interventions can reduce OS progression and metastasis [11] [12] [13]. Receptor context may be important to this approach, because DKK1 can activate CKAP4-dependent PI3K signalling as well as antagonize canonical Wnt signalling [35]. These findings provide a rationale for further therapeutic investigation; receptor expression and downstream pathway activity may clarify the biological consequences of DKK1 inhibition.

Several limitations constrain the interpretation of the plasma DKK1 findings. Although repeated sampling allowed assessment of within-patient changes, the total number of patients was modest, particularly those with metastatic disease at diagnosis. In addition, missing histologicresponse data reduced the multivariable analysis cohort. Associations with metastatic disease and tumour necrosis thus require confirmation with larger sample sizes. Surgery dates were not available for analysis, so they were estimated using the post-surgical sample draw dates, so it is possible that immediate abrupt changes in DKK1 occurring post-operatively were missed. Furthermore, the plasma, bulk transcriptomic, single-cell transcriptomic, and histological analyses involved separate cohorts, preventing direct assessment of how tumoral DKK1 expression and protein localization relate to circulating concentrations in individual patients. Bulk expression may reflect cellular composition as well as expression within individual cells, while single-cell estimates depend on cell recovery, transcript detection, and annotation. This is particularly important for DKK1, whose expression may be low and transient. Likewise, expression in cultured mesenchymal cells demonstrates a potential source under the tested conditions but does not quantify its contribution in patients. Paired longitudinal sampling, spatial RNA-protein analysis, and functional perturbation will be needed to test the proposed relationships among DKK1 production, extracellular localization, and tumour-host signalling.

Methods

Study design

Patients in the plasma cohort were enrolled in an ongoing multi-centre clinical trial, MCC20320, a study evaluating blood-based biomarkers of disease response and prognosis in children, adolescents, and young adults with sarcomas. This multi-centre clinical trial was approved by the Advarra central Institutional Review Board, and was conducted in accordance with the Ethical Principles and Guidelines for the Protection of Human Subjects of Research. Newly diagnosed patients less than or equal to 40 years old and weighing over 8 kg were enrolled at participating centres. Plasma from clinical waste of presumed healthy individuals was used for healthy controls. Study accrual opened in 2019 and is ongoing. This report focuses on subjects belonging to Cohort 4 of the trial, which is defined as: confirmed patients with osteosarcoma.

Plasma collection and processing

Blood was collected at enrolment in a Streck Cell-free DNA BCT® tube and shipped to the University of Colorado Sarcoma Research Lab in ATS1 temperature-regulated containers (Akuratemp, Arden, NC, USA). Plasma was isolated using double-centrifugation and was frozen at -80 °C until use, as previously described [36]. Samples were aliquoted and frozen at -80 °C and underwent no more than two freeze/thaw cycles.

Luminex assays

All samples were measured in technical duplicates and averaged for a final concentration. DKK1 protein was measured using HDKK1-MAG obtained from Sigma Millipore (St. Louis, MO, USA). Kit was used according to the manufacturer's instructions. Data were acquired on a Luminex Magpix XMAP Multiplex Reader (Luminex Technologies (Madison, WI, USA), RRID:SCR_023348) and were analyzed using Belysa Immunoassay Curve Fitting Software (Millipore Sigma, Burlington, MA, USA). Interpolated concentrations of analyte were log-transformed before analysis to reduce non-normal skew and inter-assay variability.

Public transcriptomic dataset mining and survival analyses

Publicly available osteosarcoma transcriptomic and clinical data were obtained from GSE33382 and the TARGET-OS cohort. DKK1 expression was standardized within each dataset to a mean of zero and standard deviation of one. Associations between DKK1 expression and overall survival (OS) or metastasis-free survival (MFS) were assessed using Cox proportional hazards regression. MFS analyses were restricted to patients without metastatic disease at diagnosis. Hazard ratios (HRs) were reported per standard-deviation increase in DKK1 expression. Cohortspecific estimates were combined using fixed-effect inverse-variance meta-analysis. Kaplan-Meier curves were generated using cohort-specific median DKK1 expression to define high-and low-expression groups.

scRNA-seq analysis Publicly available single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data were obtained from the OsteoCAR atlas [21], using the annotated Seurat objects deposited in Figshare (doi:10.6084/m9.figshare.31029559, accessed May 15, 2026). Human osteosarcoma specimens were selected from the atlas's primary-site dataset and restricted to two independent studies: BC (GSE152048) and OS (GSE162454). The analysis included 7 specimens from BC and 6 from OS, comprising 85224 and 44153 cells respectively. Samples from other studies and metastatic sites were excluded. Cell classifications were obtained from the original OsteoCAR annotations, using Level 0 to distinguish malignant tumour cells from nonmalignant host cells and Level 2 to identify specific cell populations and malignant transcriptional states.

Raw transcript counts were extracted from the Seurat RNA assay. DKK1 counts were summed across cells belonging to each Level 2 population to generate per-sample cell-state-level pseudobulk counts. The relative contribution of each population was calculated by dividing its summed DKK1 counts by the total DKK1 counts detected across all malignant and nonmalignant cells from the same specimen. Specimens with no detected DKK1 transcripts were assigned undefined rather than zero source fractions. For comparisons of DKK1 expression between specimens, raw counts from malignant cells were summed by gene within each specimen to generate sample-level pseudobulk profiles. DKK1 expression was normalized to counts per million (CPM), using the total number of transcripts detected across all genes in the malignant compartment as the denominator, and transformed as log₂(CPM + 1).

The proportion of DKK1-expressing cells was assessed using log-normalized RNA expression data. Cells with detectable DKK1 expression (DKK1 > 0) were classified as DKK1-positive, corresponding to nonzero raw DKK1 counts. Populations not represented by any recovered cells within a specimen were treated as absent rather than assigned a DKK1-positive fraction of zero. Differences in the percentage of DKK1-positive cells across the six OsteoCAR-defined malignant cell states were assessed using a Friedman test, treating individual specimens as blocks, followed by pairwise Conover comparisons with Holm adjustment for multiple testing. Proteolytic in Silico retrieval for DKK1 Optimal proteolytic retrieval was predicted in-silico using the Expasy Peptide Cutter website plug-in on accession number NP_036374.1 as a reference [37]. Given that the primary antibody is polyclonal, trypsin was selected for retrieval since it has the most conservative cleavage profile (29 sites). Immunohistochemistry IHC was performed according to protocols developed for decalcified bone [38]. Paraffinembedded arrays (TissueArray.com, Osteosarcoma: OS802d, Normal Bone array: BO244g) were pre-warmed in a humidified 100 °C oven for 6 min and de-waxed with xylene. Sections were hydrated through a series of graded ethanol washes: 100% (three times, 3 min each), 95%, 70% (two times, 2 min each). Sections were then washed in GeneTex 1X washing buffer (GeneTex, GTX73344; all wash cycles comprised three washes, 3 min each). After de-waxing and rehydration, sections were subjected to proteolytic antigen retrieval by incubation with Trypsin (Abcam, ab970) for 7 min at 37°C in a humidified oven. After cooling and washing, endogenous enzymatic alkaline phosphatase (AP) activity was blocked by treating with Bloxall (Vector Laboratories, SP-6000-100) for 10 min at RT. Sections were then washed and incubated in Protein Blocker (GeneTex, GT30963) for 10 min at 37° C, followed by incubation in 5% goat serum (GIBCO, 16-2100-72) diluted in 1X automation buffer (GeneTex, GTX30931), for 30 min at RT. Sections were incubated overnight in primary antibody at 4 °C (Bioss Inc., BS2162R 1:200) diluted in primary antibody diluent (GeneTex, GTX28208). After washing, samples were exposed to biotinylated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (Invitrogen 31820, 1:500) diluted in automation buffer for 45 min at 37° C. After washing, sections were then incubated for 30 min in streptavidin-bound AP (Vector Labs, Vectastain ABC kit, AK-5000), followed by washing and 20 min incubation in Vector Red substrate (Vector Labs, SK-5100). Sections were counterstained with Vector hematoxylin QS (Vector Labs, H-3404-100) for 45 seconds at RT, dehydrated through a series of graded ethanol (Two times, two minutes each at 70% and 95%, followed by three times, three minutes at 100%) followed by extensive washing in Histoclear (National Diagnostics, HS-200) and mounting in the non-xylene-based media Omnimount (Electron Microscopy Science, 17997-01).

TMA image analysis

Images were analyzed using QuPath version 0.6. Stain vectors were estimated empirically from representative regions in the image. Tissue regions were classified according to histologic morphology as dense tumor, matrix-rich tumor, or osteoid. DKK1 immunoreactivity was quantified as the percentage of tissue area exceeding predefined low-, intermediate-, and highintensity stain thresholds. An area-weighted staining score (range, 0-300) was calculated by summing the three cumulative positive-area percentages. DKK1 enrichment within each compartment was calculated as its proportion of total DKK1-positive area relative to its proportion of total analysed tissue area.

Cell culture

Umbilical cord-derived Mesenchymal Stem Cells ucMSC9, ucMSC13, ucMSC25 were grown in Alpha MEM + Glutamax media (Gibco, #32561037), containing 10% MSC-grade FBS (Gibco, #12662-029), 0.25X penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco, #15-140-122), and 2 ng/mL Fibroblast Growth Factor (R&D Systems, #233FB025). Cells were passaged at 80% confluency using 0.25% Trypsin (Gibco, #25200-072) at a sub-culture ratio of 1:5.

Human osteoblasts (Promocell) were cultured in Osteoblast Growth medium (Promocell C-27001) supplemented with 0.25X Penicillin-Streptomycin. Human Bone Marrow MSCs (Promocell) were cultured in MSC growth medium 2, according to the manufacturer's instructions (Promocell C-28009). Both cell lines were sub-cultured around 80% confluency using reagents from the Detach Kit (Promocell C-41200), according to the manufacturer's instructions. For these cells, RNA was purified from 5 million-cell pellets, using the Qiagen RNeasy mini kit (Qiagen, #74106), according to the manufacturer's instructions.

LNCaP-PBMN control and LNCaP-TGF-β overexpressing cells were a kind gift from Dr. David Rowley (Baylor College of Medicine, Houston TX). Cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 (Corning, #10040CV) modified to contain 4.5g/L glucose (using Gibco, #A2494001) 2.383 g/L HEPES (Gibco #15630106), and 0.11g/L pyruvate (Gibco #11360070). Basal media was then supplemented with 10% FBS (Corning, #35010CV) and 0.25x Penicillin Streptomycin (Gibco, #15-140-122). Cells were passaged using 0.25% Trypsin (Gibco, #25200-072) at a ratio of 1:5 and allowed for 48hr. Conditioned media were collected from cells around 70% confluency, adding fresh media for a second collection. Conditioned media from both collection time points were pooled and centrifuged to remove cell debris before treatment. Induction of osteogenic differentiation ucMSCs were subcultured into MEMα media (Gibco #12561056) with no nucleosides, in T75 vented flasks and allowed to reach 80% confluency. Media was supplemented with StemXVivo Human Osteogenic Supplement (R&D Systems CCM008), and osteogenesis was induced for 14 days, changing media every two days, according to the manufacturer's instructions. RNA collection was carried out using the RNeasy Fibrous Tissue Mini Kit (Qiagen 74704), according to the manufacturer's instructions. Induction of myofibroblast phenotype Cells were seeded in plastic 6-well plates (Fisher, #FB012927) at 50,000 cells per well and allowed to adhere overnight. Cells were treated with 2 mL LNCaP TGF-β (TGF-β-high) or PBMN (TGF-β-low) conditioned media per well for 48 hours. For collection, media was removed from the wells, and 100 microliters of RLT Buffer (Qiagen) was added per well. Cells were scraped into the buffer, and lysates were pooled per condition. RNA was then purified using the Qiagen RNeasy mini kit (Qiagen, #74106), according to the manufacturer's instructions.

RNA-seq

RNA-seq library construction and sequencing for two technical replicates was carried out by Genewiz/Azenta (South Plainfield, NJ). Sequence reads were trimmed using Trimmomatic v.0.36. trimmed reads were mapped to the Homo Sapiens GRCh37 reference genome using STAR aligner v.2.5.2b. Unique gene counts were calculated using FeatureCounts from the Subread package v1.5.2. Hit counts were summarized and reported using the gene_id feature in the annotation file. Only reads that fell within exon regions were counted. Heatmaps were generated as previously described [39].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed in R 4.4.3. Longitudinal changes in log₁₀-transformed plasma DKK1 were assessed using linear mixed-effects models, incorporating patient-specific random intercepts to account for repeated measurements. The primary model, for the interval between diagnosis and end of therapy, included days from diagnosis, age, metastatic status at diagnosis, histologic response, and Luminex assay plate as fixed effects. Model coefficients were back-transformed to express adjusted concentration ratios. Concentrations below the assay's lower limit of detection (150 pg/mL; 26 of 210 patient samples) were set to half the limit (75 pg/mL) before log transformation; alternative substitutions are shown in Supp. Fig. 1F. Models were fitted by restricted maximum likelihood with Satterthwaite degrees of freedom and tbased 95% confidence intervals. All available samples were used in the mixed models, including repeated pre-local-control draws; paired comparisons used the latest sample at each stage.

Nonlinear changes across the complete follow-up period were evaluated using generalized additive mixed models, incorporating a cubic regression spline for time, assay plate, and a patient-specific random effect. Changes around local control were assessed using mixed-effects models with treatment stage as a categorical predictor.

Within-patient changes between diagnosis and subsequent treatment stages were assessed using paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, with Hodges-Lehmann estimates and 95% confidence intervals. An exact binomial sign test was additionally used to evaluate the direction of change before local control. Pre-versus post-surgical samples were compared with paired t-tests on log10 concentrations. Relationships between plasma DKK1 and percent tumour necrosis were examined using Spearman rank correlations, and comparisons between histologic response groups used Wilcoxon rank-sum tests.

Differences in DKK1 immunoreactivity across tissue compartments were assessed using Friedman tests followed by pairwise Conover tests. P values were adjusted using the Holm procedure for specified pairwise comparisons and the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure for geneset analyses. All tests were two-sided, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.
