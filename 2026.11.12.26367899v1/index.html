How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

The day before the program, salivary cortisol was collected at awakening, 30 minutes post-awakening, and in the evening at home at 4°C; derived indices were the cortisol awakening response, the awakeningto-evening cortisol ratio and the evening cortisol/DHEA ratio (Supplementary Methods). The 10-year cardiovascular risk was estimated with SCORE2, which combines age, sex, smoking status, systolic blood pressure and non-HDL cholesterol, calibrated for low-risk countries including France. SCORE2 is validated for ages 40-69 years; it was recomputed at CU2 with the age and measurements of CU2, and the change is reported for the whole cohort and for men aged 40-69 years at both visits.

c. Preventive recommendations

Following the baseline preventive assessment (CU1), clinical and laboratory findings were reviewed by a physician and integrated into an individualized preventive care plan.

Recommendations were based on an integrated interpretation of clinical, DEXA, laboratory and patientreported findings, and were explained and agreed with the participant in a shared decision-making approach. They included lifestyle advice (nutrition, physical activity, sleep, stress management), pharmacological treatment when clinically indicated (including statin therapy according to ESC/EAS LDL cholesterol targets), dietary supplements tailored to individual profiles, and follow-up monitoring; the indications for each supplement are given in the Supplementary Methods.

Implementation of the recommendations is recalled through the application notifications, and the three months nurse call. They are reviewed by the physician at CU2. However the data is only a subjective, declarative, followed/not followed status, documented for a minority of recommendations; changes in diet, physical activity and medication use between visits were not systematically measured. Adherence was therefore not analyzed.

.

D. Variable Definition

Cardiometabolic conditions and interpretation categories were defined using established international clinical guidelines and standardized biomarker thresholds. Operational definitions, thresholds and calculation formulas are given in the Supplementary Methods.

E. Outcomes

a. Primary Outcome

The primary outcome was the within-individual longitudinal change in continuous cardiometabolic variables between CU1 and CU2. These variables included clinical measurements, anthropometric and functional parameters, body composition measures derived from DEXA, blood and salivary biomarkers, SCORE2, and the STOP-BANG and Epworth scores. b. Secondary Outcomes Secondary outcomes examined complementary dimensions of longitudinal change. First, we described within-individual changes according to baseline categories of BMI, systolic blood pressure, glycemic status and LDL cholesterol. Second, we evaluated transitions between predefined clinical categories from CU1 to CU2.

All analyses relied on the same paired longitudinal framework as the primary analysis.

F. Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as mean (SD) and median [interquartile range, IQR], and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Following current recommendations, results are presented as estimates with 95% confidence intervals (CI) and standardized effect sizes rather than in terms of statistical significance alone [29, 30]; p-values are reported together with false discovery rateadjusted q-values.

Before analysis, values outside pre-specified plausibility ranges (Supplementary Methods, section 5) were set to missing (14 values from 11 participants, e.g., visceral adipose tissue mass above 6,000 g or systolic blood pressure below 70 mmHg). Within-individual change between CU1 and CU2 was assessed in participants with non-missing values at both time points (complete-pair analysis; the number of pairs is given for each variable), for variables with at least 150 pairs. The paired difference was estimated by the Hodges-Lehmann estimator with its 95% CI derived from the Wilcoxon signed-rank distribution and the corresponding two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test; the mean difference (t-based 95% CI) and the paired Cohen's d are also reported, and the relative change is the Hodges-Lehmann estimate divided by the CU1 median. Derived indices (triglyceride/HDL, total/HDL, non-HDL cholesterol, HOMA-IR) were recomputed from their constituents.

No sample size calculation was performed a priori; the sample corresponds to all eligible men in the database at extraction. A posteriori, with a median of 232 pairs per variable, a two-sided paired test at α=0.05 with 80% power detects a standardized difference d≈0.18 (d≈0.27 under a Bonferroni-type α for the 35 tests of Table 2). To assess selection into follow-up, men with a CU2 were compared with men whose CU1 took place at least 15 months before extraction but who had no CU2 (Figure S1, Table S2). Changes according to baseline category (Table S3) are descriptive and presented with confidence intervals only. Sensitivity analyses, detailed in the Supplementary Methods, separated the self-reported and objective STOP-BANG items (Table S4), repeated the Table 2 analyses on pairs with a plausible interval of 6-24 months (Table S5), stratified blood and salivary biomarkers by period of the CU2 (Table S6), and examined the influence of the interval between evaluations (median 13.3 months) with annualized changes and the association between individual change and interval length (Table S7).

Transitions between clinical categories were analyzed with paired 2×2 tables: b denotes the number of participants moving from unfavorable at CU1 to favorable at CU2 and c the reverse; the paired odds ratio c/b (values below 1 indicating net improvement) is reported with a Wald 95% CI, the exact McNemar test and the net proportion improved (b-c)/n. These odds ratios are unadjusted by construction, each participant serving as his own control.

For multiple testing, p-values were adjusted with the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate procedure applied globally over the 36 tests of Table 2 (families A to D together), and separately within Table 3 and within each supplementary table; the resulting q-values are shown next to the raw p-values. Analyses were conducted with R version (2025.05.1+513); the code and the analysis log are available from the corresponding author.

Discussion

This study describes within-individual changes in cardiometabolic, functional, questionnaire-based and neuroendocrine markers between two standardized evaluations, a median of 13 months apart, in 279 men engaged in a structured preventive care program. Handgrip strength and HDL cholesterol increased, the triglyceride/HDL and total/HDL cholesterol ratios, visceral adipose tissue mass, body mass, fat mass and hs-CRP decreased. Blood pressure, LDL cholesterol, fasting glucose (after adjustment for multiple testing), insulin, HOMA-IR, SCORE2 and salivary cortisol and DHEA did not change. An increase in HbA1c was observed but is confined to a specific calendar period and is not interpreted as a biological change. Standardized effect sizes were small for all markers (|d| ≤ 0.29) except grip strength and HDL cholesterol (d 0.43-0.48); the changes described here are therefore modest at the individual level, whatever their direction.These changes, observed in men who completed both evaluations, are in the direction expected from the recommendations delivered; a comparison group will be needed to determine how much of them is attributable to the program. Across the four domains examined, the direction of change was consistently favorable or neutral, with no marker moving in an unfavorable direction.

Body composition and grip strength.

Visceral adipose tissue mass decreased by 49 g (about 5% of the baseline median) while BMI and waist circumference were stable and body mass and fat mass decreased by about half a kilogram. Visceral adiposity is considered a more relevant metabolic correlate of cardiovascular risk than BMI alone [7] ; the changes observed here are small at the individual level (Cohen's d ≤ 0.25) and their clinical relevance over one year is uncertain. Handgrip strength increased bilaterally (right +2.35 kg, left +1.70 kg, about 5-7% of the baseline median), and the proportion of men below the reference value for the right hand decreased from 60% to 48%. Grip strength is an integrative marker of functional reserve and an independent predictor of cardiovascular events and mortality [9-11], and it declines by about 0.5-0.7 kg per year in men over 50 in population-based studies [31]. However, a second measurement with the same device after one year is susceptible to familiarization and motivation effects: test-retest reliability of grip dynamometry is high but not perfect [32], and the increase observed here (about 2 kg) is of the order of the minimal clinically important differences reported for grip strength (about 5-6 kg in most studies) [33]. The increase was also smaller in men with longer intervals between evaluations (Table S7). The observed increase can therefore not be interpreted as a functional improvement attributable to the program..

Lipid profile and estimated cardiovascular risk. HDL cholesterol increased by 3.5 mg/dL (6% of the baseline median), with corresponding decreases in the triglyceride/HDL ratio and the atherogenic index, whereas total, LDL and non-HDL cholesterol and triglycerides did not change. SCORE2 did not change either (∆ -0.04 percentage points, 95% CI -0.11 to 0.02), with a similar estimate (-0.08, -0.16 to 0.00) in the 40-69-year age range for which the algorithm is validated. Because SCORE2 increases mechanically with age, a stable estimate over 13 months indicates that small changes in its components (systolic blood pressure, non-HDL cholesterol, smoking status) offset the effect of ageing over the interval; it does not indicate that risk was reduced. The stability of LDL cholesterol at the cohort level hides opposite trajectories by baseline category (Table S3): LDL increased in men with baseline values below 116 mg/dL, did not change in the 116-189 mg/dL category and decreased in the 10 men with values ≥190 mg/dL.

This pattern is the expected signature of regression to the mean in extreme baseline categories [27]; none of the 10 men had a statin prescription recorded at CU1, and medication use between visits was not systematically recorded, so that a treatment effect can neither be invoked nor excluded.

Glycemic and inflammatory markers. Fasting glucose decreased by 1 mg/dL, a change that did not survive adjustment for multiple testing (q=0.07), and the proportion of men with fasting glucose ≥97 mg/dL decreased from 15% to 10% (q=0.13); insulin and HOMA-IR did not change. HbA1c increased by 0.10 percentage points, but this increase is entirely due to a step of about 0.25 points in men whose CU2 took place from October 2025 onwards, with no change earlier and no corresponding change in fasting glucose (Table S6). A homogeneous shift of this size confined to a calendar period is not compatible with a biological change in glycemic control; HbA1c results are therefore reported but not interpreted. hs-CRP decreased by 0.13 mg/L (about 19% of a low baseline median of 0.65 mg/L); given the skewed distribution of this marker and the absence of a corresponding change in body composition of comparable magnitude, this small change is reported without interpretation.

The STOP-BANG score decreased by a median of one point. The Epworth score did not change. The perceived-stress questionnaire was replaced by a different instrument between the visits and its scores were not compared. Salivary cortisol and DHEA measurements were stable; the increase in the awakening/evening cortisol ratio is driven by very low evening values in the denominator of a highly skewed ratio, no neuroendocrine transition differed from chance (Table 3), and this isolated finding is regarded as hypothesis-generating only.

Categorical transitions.

After adjustment for multiple testing, only two transitions differed from chance:

HDL cholesterol (proportion with values ≤46 mg/dL from 27% to 17%) and right-hand grip strength (proportion below the reference value from 60% to 48%), with left-hand grip strength at the margin. Transitions for systolic blood pressure, visceral adipose tissue, fasting glucose and uric acid were in a favorable direction but compatible with chance, and no transition was observed for LDL cholesterol, HOMA-IR or insulin. The apparent unfavorable transition for HbA1c reflects the period discontinuity discussed above and disappears in men evaluated before October 2025. These transitions are unadjusted paired comparisons and share the limitations of the continuous analyses.

Comparison with other program-based cohorts.

Two recent large observational studies have described cardiometabolic changes in adults exposed to structured prevention. In the digital-health cohort of Xiao et al. [23], 40,216 Chinese adults followed with connected devices showed greater reductions in systolic blood pressure, glucose and body fat percentage with higher engagement; in the Japanese check-up cohort of Kameda et al. [24], 29-57% of adults with blood pressure, glucose or lipid abnormalities had improved one year later, in association with weight loss and medication. Our cohort is smaller and younger, at lower baseline risk, and evaluated with research-grade measurements (DEXA, laboratory panels at both visits, salivary neuroendocrine markers) rather than home devices or routine check-up data. The direction and magnitude of the changes we observe in blood pressure and lipids are compatible with these reports, with the same interpretive limits, since none of the three studies includes a comparison group.

Digital tools in preventive care.

Participants had access to a smartphone application through which they could consult their results and recommendations and report whether recommendations had been followed. Engagement metrics were not available for the present analysis, so that the relation between digital engagement and the observed changes could not be examined. Digital tools (applications, wearables, stepcount monitoring, remote coaching and automated feedback) are increasingly proposed as scalable components of cardiometabolic prevention, and the cohort of Xiao et al. suggests that the level of engagement matters [23]. Whether objectively measured engagement and activity patterns contribute to trajectories such as those observed here is a priority for future analyses of the cohort.

Restriction to male participants. The analysis was restricted to men as the first step of a sex-stratified analysis plan for the cohort, not as a convenience sample. Sex differences in body composition, hormonal regulation and lipid trajectories across the life course are well documented [26, 38], SCORE2 is sexspecific [16] and the analysis of women requires accounting for menopausal status and for cyclic variation of some inflammatory and neuroendocrine markers. Stratifying a mixed cohort would equally have avoided confounding by sex; analyzing the two sexes in separate reports was chosen because the outcome definitions and the interpretation differ. The analysis of women is in preparation.

Several strengths of this study should be noted. First, data were collected within a structured preventive care center using a standardized assessment protocol applied identically at both evaluations.

Second, the study draws on a prospectively maintained clinical database whose design has been reported in a cohort profile [39], combining clinical, biological, body composition (DEXA), functional, questionnaire and neuroendocrine data on the same individuals, a combination that neither of the two recent large program-based cohorts provides [23, 24] . Third, the real-world design captures cardiometabolic dynamics as they occur in routine preventive practice. Fourth, the analysis applies prespecified plausibility rules, a single consistent estimator with its confidence interval and test, a global correction for multiple testing, and several sensitivity analyses, and reports the number of pairs for every variable. Finally, the ongoing nature of the cohort will allow the evaluation of longer-term trajectories.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the absence of a comparison group precludes attributing the observed changes to the program rather than to natural evolution, regression to the mean [36], test-retest effects, secular trends or the behaviour change induced by observation itself (Hawthorne effect) [40] ; participants knew they would be re-evaluated, health check-ups by themselves are associated with subsequent changes in risk factors although not with reduced morbidity or mortality in randomized trials [35], and the magnitude of these effects cannot be quantified in a single-arm design. Second, the study population, men attending a preventive center in Paris, 81% with graduate education, 84% with a household income above €100 000, 8% current smokers compared with about a quarter of French men [41], and a median BMI of 24.5 kg/m², represents neither the general French population nor the high-risk populations usually targeted by prevention programs [20] [21] [22], which limits generalizability. Third, only 32% of eligible men returned for a second evaluation. Their baseline characteristics were close to those of non-returners (all standardized mean differences ≤0.14; Table S2), so that selection on baseline risk appears limited, but selection on motivation or on the trajectory itself cannot be excluded and would bias changes in a favorable direction. Fourth, adherence to recommendations was captured only as a selfreported followed/not followed status, documented for a minority of recommendations, and changes in diet, physical activity and medication use between evaluations were not measured; engagement metrics of the digital platform were not available for this analysis. Associations between recommendations and biomarker changes could therefore not be analyzed, and the behavioural pathways through which the observed changes may have occurred cannot be assessed. Fifth, STOP-BANG and stress questionnaires were revised between the visits, and all questionnaire scores are self-reported and susceptible to reporting bias. Sixth, the interval between evaluations varied (3 to 38 months, median 13.3); changes were unrelated to the interval for almost all markers and the results were unchanged when restricted to intervals of 6-24 months (Tables S5 and S7), but the annualized changes should be read with this heterogeneity in mind. Finally, the follow-up is short and the analysis involves 36 paired comparisons; despite the false discovery rate adjustment, the smaller changes should be regarded as descriptive, and none of the observed changes can be related to clinical events.

Conclusion

In this prospective cohort of 279 men engaged in a structured preventive care program, within-individual changes between two standardized evaluations a median of 13 months apart were in a favorable direction for handgrip strength, HDL cholesterol and its ratios, visceral adipose tissue, body mass and fat mass, whereas blood pressure, LDL cholesterol, glycemic markers, SCORE2 and salivary neuroendocrine markers were stable. These changes are small at the individual level, were observed in a selected, healthengaged population without a comparison group, and cannot be attributed to the program rather than to regression to the mean, test-retest effects, participation effects or secular trends. The study illustrates the multidimensional cardiometabolic trajectories that can be described within a program-based preventive setting and the data-quality requirements of such descriptions in routine care. Establishing whether programs of this type modify cardiometabolic trajectories will require a comparison group, objective measures of adherence and digital engagement, longer follow-up and the inclusion of women.
