Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. Abstract

Measles is a highly infectious, vaccine-preventable disease that remains persistent across much of the world despite continued large-scale control efforts. Local dynamics of measles are shaped by births of susceptible individuals and seasonal drivers of transmission, while broader regional dynamics are shaped by population mobility and metapopulation connectivity. Incidence data, useful for tracking the progress of measles control, are frequently reported only at the aggregate level (e.g., states/provinces), and persistence at this scale may arise because of the independent persistence in all local sub-units or because of regional metapopulation dynamics. Disentangling the contribution of local versus regional persistence is critical in identifying effective strategies for control.

We show, as vaccination reduces local measles prevalence, metapopulation colonization and extinction events dominate regional persistence. This manifests as a change in the seasonal distribution of.

cases throughout the year; incidence becomes more uniformly distributed through the year even with no change in underlying seasonal drivers. We demonstrate this using: 1) a seasonally forced, stochastic metapopulation compartmental model; 2) historical measles incidence in the USA; and 3) recent subnational-level measles incidence in Nigeria.

We create a novel, scale-free measure to quantify the contribution of metapopulation dynamics to persistence, roundness, defined as the geometric mean of the scaled maximum and minimum monthly incidence. A low value of roundness indicates strong local persistence dominated by the seasonal attractor, while a high value indicates strong metapopulation persistence. We illustrate the properties of this measure using simulations and observed data. In the USA, roundness nearly doubled after the introduction of the measles-containing vaccine. In Nigeria, roundness is well correlated with observed vaccine coverage, with a Pearson's r of 0.70 (95% CI: 0.57, 0.80), discriminating between northern states dominated by local persistence of measles and southern states where persistence is maintained by regional dynamics.

Conclusion

Identifying how measles persists is important in its control-vaccination strategies may differ if persistence is largely local, or if persistence is driven by connectivity from surrounding areas, even where yearly incidence is comparable. Introduction

Vaccination has drastically reduced the incidence and burden of communicable disease in humans (Klepac et al., 2013). Since 1974, 154 million deaths have been averted, of which two-thirds were in children less than 1 year of age. Vaccination has accounted for 40% of the observed decline in infant mortality worldwide (Shattock et al., 2024). For example, vaccination has averted 1.6 million deaths due to polio, 279 million deaths due to tetanus, and 94 million deaths due to measles (Shattock et al., 2024). Between 1980 and 2009, coverage for routine childhood vaccines including diphtheria/pertussis/tetanus (DTP3), polio, and measles containing vaccine (MCV) increased by 2 percentage points per year (Baidya et al., 2025). However, since the start of the COVID-19 pandemic, progress has slowed and even reversed in some circumstances, with DTP3 coverage now at its lowest level since 2009, and drops in MCV coverage led to a 30-fold increase in measles cases in Europe compared to 2016 (Gambrell et al., 2022; Mahase, 2025). Even as vaccination has increased, there continue to be large gaps both between and within countries (Bhatia et al., 2025). Local heterogeneity in the density of susceptible individuals, due to in variation in vaccination coverage, can increase the vaccination coverage necessary to maintain elimination (Truelove et al., 2019). Movement between and among groups with varying levels of coverage can lead to outbreaks in areas that may otherwise have a high aggregate level of coverage (Keeling, 2000). Addressing these heterogeneities and gaps in coverage, particularly at the subnational and subgroup level, has been an emphasis of the Immunization Agenda 2030 (IA2030) set out by World Health Organization (WHO) (IA2030, 2020).

Incidence data are frequently recorded and reported at the aggregate, or metapopulation level (i.e., administrative units), and persistence may arise either because subunits below the level of reporting are independently persistent, or because of extinction-colonization processes (Grenfell & Harwood, 1997). As available data become more aggregated, disease dynamics at that aggregated, or metapopulation level may not be informative about transmission dynamics at the patch-level within (Gunning & Wearing, 2013; Metcalf et al., 2013). A key dynamical consequence is that even if some local populations, or patches, have an effective reproductive number (RE) of less than 1, disease may persist at the regional level due to the extinction-colonization dynamics consistent with a metapopulation (Truelove et al., 2019).

These dynamics are complicated by issues of measurement scale. While efforts have been ongoing to emphasize subnational and subgroup-level gaps in disease control (IA2030, 2020), the scale of disease and coverage reporting can often be mismatched with the scale of disease transmission itself. At smaller spatial scales, the reduced size of the population compounded with low reporting rates can make estimates of persistence and risk inaccurate (Gunning & Wearing, 2013; Olsen et al., 1996). Such measurement issues create distinct control challenges. Gunning et al highlighted that underreporting can make the determination of local persistence difficult, where small to medium sized populations may maintain persistent transmission but present as 0 reported cases in administrative time series due to underreporting (Gunning et al., 2017). Another challenge is that spatial units at which data are collected and reported are often political, so they may exclude relevant information about disease dynamics and control from neighboring jurisdictions. Therefore, understanding disease dynamics at smaller spatial scalescloser to the scale at which transmission is occurring-is important in assessing control strategies and progress toward disease elimination.

Measles is a highly infectious viral disease of humans caused by the measles virus, causing over 10 million cases and over 100,000 deaths in 2023 alone (Guerra et al., 2017; Minta, 2024; Tesini, 2023).

The basic reproductive ratio, R0, which quantifies the expected number of secondary infections due to the first infection in a completely susceptible population, of measles is 12-18, higher than many other directly communicable diseases in humans (Guerra et al., 2017). The strong and lasting immunity due to measles infection, coupled with seasonal forcing of transmission due to human behavioral cycles drives cyclic dynamics of outbreaks and frequent local fade-out and metapopulation rescue, even in the prevaccine era (Ferrari et al., 2010; Grenfell & Harwood, 1997). The high R0 of measles necessitates a high level of vaccination coverage to control, which can be difficult to achieve and maintain uniformly. For seasonally forced pathogens, like measles, we see highly synchronized outbreaks in connected spatial regions (Rohani et al., 1999). This synchrony begins to decline after the introduction of the vaccine, even when the seasonality in transmission remains constant (Rohani et al., 1999). As synchrony declines, even when seasonal forcing is unchanged, the aggregate pattern of incidence over many patches should broaden due to extinction and colonization dynamics (Figure 1). At low coverage, outbreaks are likely to be spatially synchronized at the patch level, and with a sufficient supply of susceptible individuals, infection persists through troughs of low seasonal transmission (see Figure 1, A).

If susceptibles are limited, e.g., through vaccination, then the likelihood of local extinction increases during seasonal troughs. As susceptibles build up over time, the start of the following outbreak will depend both on the seasonal transmission rate as well as the rate of re-introduction. This can give rise to later-than-expected outbreaks if introduction occurs later than the seasonal peak in transmission or earlier than expected if susceptible accumulation leads to an increase in RE above 1. Thus, observing this phenomenon at the metapopulation level, we may see, for the same underlying seasonal transmission, an epidemic curve that is much flatter with respect to seasonal variation. We can leverage this expected broadening of the seasonal variation in incidence to indicate the transition from endemic circulation to metapopulation persistence in the overall dynamics. We first establish this through a stochastic metapopulation compartmental model. We then illustrate this phenomenon using historical measles surveillance data from the United States spanning the pre-vaccine era through disease elimination, and contemporary measles surveillance data in Nigeria, where there is substantial spatial variation in vaccination coverage, and subsequently, in measles transmission dynamics.

Roundness

We created vectors of scaled incidence (the maximum incidence for a given period was scaled to 1) for each year on a polar plane, where the angle represented the month or biweekly period, depending on data aggregation and availability, and the magnitude represented the relative number of cases compared to the maximum number of cases in that year. For example, the first biweekly period of the calendar year was represented as the cardinal direction North to 13.8° clockwise. The 14 th biweekly period, or the first biweekly period of the latter half of the calendar year, was represented as the cardinal direction South to 13.8° clockwise. In data available at a monthly level, the first monthly period was represented as the cardinal direction North to 30° clockwise, while the 7 th monthly period was represented as the cardinal direction South to 30° clockwise.

Using moving 3-year periods of scaled incidence vectors, we calculated the geometric mean of the minimum and maximum radii created by those vectors for each year. A larger geometric mean, or a higher value of roundness indicates that the maximum and minimum incidence values are similar, and that there is a reduced seasonal pattern of incidence. A smaller geometric mean, or a lower value of roundness, indicates that the maximum and minimum incidence values in a year are less similar, and that there is a more prominent seasonal patterns of incidence.

to be equal and all 𝑠 ! to be equal. In our simulations we used 𝑛 ! = 1 and 𝑠 ! = 0.07. A time-varying construction of 𝑄 ! was developed to better reflect the realities of disease reporting and human mobility. If we consider our metapopulation as a jurisdiction (e.g., a country), population mobility from outside of the metapopulation may lead to introduced cases that may not be detected or reported by the jurisdiction of the metapopulation until further transmission has occurred. This can be especially true in areas with substantial cross-border mobility and reduced reporting capacity (WHO Ebola Response Team, 2014).

The number of susceptible individuals at time 𝑡 + 1 is affected by births of susceptible individuals 𝐵 ! , the proportion of new births vaccinated 𝜌, and the infection process moving susceptible individuals to the infected class at time 𝑡.

We varied 𝜌 between 0 and 1 (corresponding to vaccination coverage at birth of 0% to 100%).

Historic measles time series

Monthly, state-wise counts of measles cases in the United States of America (US) were extracted from Project Tycho, ranging from years 1883 through 2001, covering the range of years including prevaccine endemic circulation, the introduction and adoption of the measles-containing vaccine, and measles elimination in the US (Orenstein et al., 2004; Van Panhuis et al., 2018). While we do not have comprehensive national or state-level estimates of vaccination coverage spanning this time period, coverage at the national level among 1-4 year old children increased from 40-60% in 1970 to above 90% by 1998 (Hutchins et al., 2004). Our data spans several major changes in the epidemiology of measles and its control. The MCV was introduced in 1963, and by 1966, the first elimination effort was introduced (Orenstein et al., 2004). While this and subsequent attempts to eliminate measles (1978 and 1993) were unsuccessful, these attempts dramatically reduced the incidence of measles, from 313 cases per 100,000 in 1956-1960, immediately prior to the introduction of the MCV, to 1.3 cases per 100,000. Later that decade, outbreaks in school-aged children led to a second dose being recommended for all children, which led to a further decline in incidence, before elimination was declared in 2000 (CDC, 2024).

Contemporary measles time series

Monthly, state-wise counts of suspected measles cases in Nigeria were extracted from aggregated data on healthcare-seeking visits, routinely submitted to the World Health Organization (WHO), encompassing the years 2007 through 2020. Suspected measles cases are defined as those with maculopapular rash (non-vesicular) and fever, and any of conjunctivitis, coryza, or cough (World Health Organization, 2018). Data were obtained from WHO measles surveillance database through a request to the Immunizations, Vaccines, and Biologicals (IVB) Department. These surveillance data are also publicly reported through routine measles situation reports published by the Nigeria Centre for Disease Control. However, the WHO database was used for this analysis because it allowed standardized extraction of historical data across the study period (Nigeria Centre for Disease Control and Prevention, 2025). Data on ADM1-level vaccination coverage in Nigeria were obtained from the Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS). Specifically, we used the percentage of children aged 12-23 months of age who received at least 1 dose of the measles containing vaccine (MCV) at any time in the past, according to a documented vaccination card or the child's caregiver (Croft et al., 2018).

As the DHS have historically been conducted approximately every 5 years, data for Nigeria were available for years 2013 and 2018.

Calculation of metapopulation indicators

In addition to roundness, we calculated the following measures for the simulated and US timeseries: incidence (also for Nigeria), proportion of time each patch was extinct (i.e., had 0 reported cases), proportion of patches 0 at any given time, maximum duration of extinction in any given year, and number of rescue events (i.e., a patch going from extinct at time 𝑡 to extant at time 𝑡 + 1).

Results

Simulation

Results from our simulation show that extinction and colonization dynamics tend to become more prevalent at higher levels of vaccination coverage upon running the model to equilibrium. Namely, the proportion of patches at 0 increases sharply as vaccination coverage increases, with values close to or at zero up to 92%. A sharp increase is observed above 95% coverage, where a majority of patches are extinct at any given time. The point at which this increase occurs varies at different levels of connectivity-at a higher degree of connectivity, we observe non-zero proportions of extinct patches at a higher level of coverage. Finally, the number of rescue events, or biweekly periods where a case was observed after a patch went extinct, increases dramatically as vaccination coverage increases. This phenomenon, too, varies by connectivity. at a higher degree of connectivity, we see a much steeper slope and a higher overall frequency of rescue events. We also observe a smaller maximum slope and higher overall value of roundness in settings with lower transmission. Notably, roundness starts to increase rapidly at a level of vaccination coverage below a theoretical herd immunity threshold.

USA Time series

Measles incidence declined dramatically after the introduction of the vaccine, from multiple peaks of above 100,000 monthly cases, and an average of over 40,000 monthly cases in the 10 years prior to the introduction of the measles-containing vaccine in 1963, to under 10,000 monthly cases on average in the 10 years following the introduction of the vaccine. Considering each state in the US as a patch in the metapopulation, the proportion of patches at 0 at any given month increased dramatically after the introduction of the vaccine, starting at 5% prior to the introduction of the vaccine and peaking at 100% in the 1990s (see Figure 3, A). Accordingly, the proportion of states experiencing metapopulation rescue (defined as monthly incidence increasing from 0 to above 0 in a given calendar year) also increased from ~5% prior to the introduction of the vaccine to over 80% in the 1990s (Figure 3, B). Notably, roundness increased after the introduction of the vaccine, going from a value of ~0.2 prior to the introduction of the vaccine to a value of ~0.6 in the 1990s (Figure 3, C). We can also consider each state as a metapopulation itself. When we do so, we find that state-level roundness tracks roughly with national-level roundness in terms of magnitude, while leading the nationallevel roundness values by 1-2 years (Figure 3, C). Notably, we also see a continued increase in state-level roundness in the late 1980s onwards despite a drop in national-level roundness. This may be indicative of the resurgence of measles in the US in 1989 to 1991 in a handful of states, driving roundness down (as measured at the national level), while keeping roundness across most states high (Atkinson et al., 1992; Orenstein et al., 2004). Discussion

We demonstrate that the changing seasonal distribution of disease incidence without an underlying change in the seasonality of transmission, captured by a novel measure called roundness, is indicative of the dominance of metapopulation extinction-colonization dynamics as vaccination coverage increases and incidence declines. Results from our simulations show that as vaccination coverage increases, patch-level indicators of metapopulation dynamics such as the proportion of patches extinct, and the number of rescue events per year, also increase. Similarly, roundness increases with increasing vaccination coverage, with a more gradual increase evident in lower transmission settings. We also note that roundness follows a trajectory that reflects between-patch dynamics. As coverage increases, roundness stays flat. At higher levels of coverage, metapopulation extinction-colonization dynamics start appearing, and it is at this point that we observe an "inflection point" in the value of roundness at roughly 94%, occurring at a level of coverage slightly below a theoretical herd immunity threshold for a similar mean value of the transmission parameter, 𝛽 of 96.7%foot_0 . Roundness, once past an inflection point, appears to indicate the presence of metapopulation extinction-colonization dynamics. At very low levels of transmission (achieved by scaling all 𝛽 values by 0.2), we see a more linear increase in roundness, indicating the presence of extinction-colonization dynamics even at low levels of vaccination coverage.

Using historical data from the United States, we see similar signatures of changing seasonality at the national level as observed in our simulations. This is further supported by state-level observations (e.g., increasing number of months with no cases, and increasing number of consecutive months with no cases) signaling a change in the dominant regime from endemic circulation toward metapopulation persistence after the introduction of the measles-containing vaccine. Our observations correspond with milestones in measles control in the USA, with sharp increases in roundness seen in 1966 and 1980, where elimination efforts were particularly strong (Orenstein et al., 2004). Notably, national-level roundness decreases in the late 1980s while state-level roundness continues to increase. This can be explained by severe measles outbreaks in largely-unvaccinated children of pre-school age in a few cities in the United States (Los Angeles, Houston, Milwaukee, Chicago, Dallas, and New York City) between 1989-1990, where there were over 40,000 cases reported in the two years, the highest since 1977 (Atkinson et al., 1992). These outbreaks would have disproportionately contributed to the burden of disease, and calculation of the roundness metric, skewing it downward at a national level. As most states were not affected by these outbreaks, state-level roundness remained high.

Within states in Nigeria, where metapopulation dynamics cannot be directly observed, we see a strong correlation between roundness and coverage. While we cannot rule out the effect of changing seasonality over time in our observed time series data, it is striking that the patterns that we do see are consistent with known gradient and vaccination control over time and space and with each other.

This study is situated in work that aims to understand the level of control required where there is no longer any persistence of the pathogen in the metapopulation (i.e., elimination of the pathogen), akin to a herd immunity threshold in a single population. This threshold can depend on the size of the metapopulation and its constituent patches, vaccination coverage, connectivity between patches, and spatial and temporal heterogeneity between patches (Kortessis et al., 2025). Other approaches that have aimed to study this phenomenon include critical slowing down theory, canonical path, and the early warning system (EWS) indicators such as autocorrelation (Brett et al., 2020; Graham et al., 2019; O'Regan & Drake, 2013). Somewhat similarly to roundness, these indicators also rely on changing properties of incidence values over time to detect changes in the dynamics of the system from steady state to either emergence or elimination. In addition to signaling shifts in the regime, our measure of roundness also indicates how the regime is changing-a system with endemic transmission has much lower, and much more stable values of roundness compared to a system propped up by metapopulation persistence and extinction/colonization events.

An advantage of roundness is that it can be measured even when case reporting is imperfect. We tested under-reporting by artificially reducing case counts from our simulations by 60% and found no qualitative difference in roundness values created from the artificially under-reported data (Appendix B).

Even if a low proportion of cases are seen and subsequently reported, roundness can be used to assess whether a transition away from endemic transmission is occurring. The use of roundness can also be supplemented by the use of other measures that are demonstrably linked to immunity, such as the mean age of cases, the proportion of cases testing positive for ongoing infection, and the vaccination history of cases when case counts are low, to assess whether incidence is truly low and a transition to metapopulation persistence and elimination is occurring (Bhatia et al., 2025).

One limitation of this study is the roundness may not be interpretable on an absolute scale, as the shape and strength of seasonal drivers affecting transmission can vary between places. For example, term time forcing and agricultural cycles, factors that affect the seasonality of transmission, vary between places (Bharti et al., 2011; Ferrari et al., 2010). While we cannot exclude the probability that differences are due to changes in underlying seasonality over time, the relationship between roundness and control efforts holds in a range of different seasonal transmission regimes (USA through the 20 th century vs.

individual states in Nigeria in the 21 st century). If there is data collection on incidence prior to, during, and after the introduction of large-scale vaccination programs, then the roundness value can be well characterized for a given place, such as was done in the US. The key utility of this measure is to allow comparison in a metapopulation over time, or to allow comparison of different metapopulations in a larger system.

Conclusion

We show that a novel measure, roundness, can be used to effectively track regional transitions from endemic circulation to metapopulation persistence without using sub-regional data on immunity, which may not be available or reliable. We first show how roundness changes in a connected metapopulation with varying levels of vaccination coverage using simulations. We then consider the entirety of the United States as one metapopulation and show that roundness, as well as indicators suggesting increases in metapopulation-based dynamics both increased over time after the introduction of the measles-containing vaccine until elimination was achieved. Finally, we consider each state in Nigeria as a metapopulation and show that roundness at the state level is highly correlated with vaccination coverage, further suggesting the difference in dynamics between high-coverage, high-roundness states and lower-coverage low-roundness states.

Understanding how disease systems change over time is important in managing those systems toward elimination and extinction. However, relying only on reported cases can be misleading in situations where the ability to report cases consistently changes, such as resource limitations or conflict. It is in situations like these where populations can be more vulnerable due to a reduced capacity to vaccinate against, report, and manage vaccine-preventable diseases such as measles. Roundness is a novel method to assess how systems are changing, and the use of roundness can offer insights not only on how disease systems are changing, but where they are changing, and where efforts may need to be directed.
