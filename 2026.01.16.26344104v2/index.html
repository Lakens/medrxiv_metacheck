Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

How Replication Check works

The Replication Check module compares the reference list against studies in the FLoRA (FORRT Library of Replication Attempts) database based on the DOI. If a study in the database is found, a reminder is provided that a replication or reproduction of the original study exists, and should be cited. No reminder is given when the paper already cites that replication or reproduction (matched by its DOI).

Abstract

Neurodevelopmental conditions (NDCs) arise from complex genetic-environmental interactions, yet their molecular underpinnings remain incompletely defined. We applied an integrative multi-omics approach within a deeply phenotyped twin cohort to identify systemic molecular signatures and pathways associated with NDCs. Our study included 237 twins aged 8-28 years from the Roots of Autism and ADHD Twin Study in Sweden (RATSS), with one or more omics layers available per individual, including serum and cerebrospinal fluid proteomics, urine and fecal metabolomics, blood metallomics, and whole-genome sequencing in a subset of 14 monozygotic (MZ) twin pairs. Using the DIABLO framework, we identified cross-omics features that distinguish individuals with and without NDCs. The identified features converged on enriched metabolic and immune-related pathways, including purine metabolism, lysine degradation, PI3K-Akt, and MAPK signaling. Several key molecules also showed differential abundance at the single-molecule level in generalized estimating equation (GEE) models.

ADA protein, pyrimidine-related metabolites (thymidine, glutamine, and 2'-deoxyuridine), and copper showed suggestive associations within NDC-discordant MZ twin pairs, pointing to potential non-shared condition-related effects, while flavin mononucleotide and manganese associations with NDCs were influenced by shared genetic or familial environmental factors. Our findings indicate that NDC-related molecular alterations manifest across multiple biological layers and tissues, and are detectable through integrative systems-level analysis. Our scalable framework provides critical insights into altered metabolic and immune processes in NDCs and highlights candidate features that may inform future biomarker development and mechanistic research in precision psychiatry.

1 Introduction

Neurodevelopmental conditions (NDCs), such as autism, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), motor and language disorders, are defined by cognitive and behavioral difficulties causing functional challenges in social, academic, and occupational arenas [1]. In high-income countries, approximately 15% of children aged 3 to 17 years are diagnosed with NDCs [2], which exhibit pronounced heterogeneity while sharing clinical features across diagnoses [3] [4] [5]. Sex and gender are also important contributors to the biological and behavioral variability [6]. NDC diagnoses frequently co-occur, and individuals with NDCs are also at increased risk for somatic and psychiatric disorders [1, 7]. Although the origins, physiological mechanisms, and consequences of NDCs remain incompletely understood, both genetic and environmental factors contribute substantially to NDC liability, with heritability estimates from 0.66 across conditions to approximately 0.83 for autism specifically in children and adolescents [8, 9]. Metabolic, immune, and inflammatory factors also contribute to NDC likelihood and may interact with genetic susceptibility, further complicating their etiology [10] [11] [12] [13].

Single-omics studies have contributed to elucidating the biological underpinnings of NDCs. Genomic research has identified hundreds of NDC-associated genes enriched for rare and de novo variants [14] [15] [16], many of which disrupt transcriptional regulation, synaptic function, and brain development [17] [18] [19].

Proteomic analyses of peripheral blood and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) have revealed altered expression profiles linked to mitochondrial dysfunction, neuroinflammation, and metabolic processes in NDCs, with some proteins correlating with clinical symptoms [20, 21]. Because metabolic alterations are sensitive to gene-environment interactions, they offer particular promise for understanding NDC physiology [22, 23]. Metabolomic analyses of non-invasive biospecimens, such as fecal and urine samples, have demonstrated altered microbial-host co-metabolism and amino acid signatures in children with NDCs [24, 25]. Despite the breadth of reported molecular associations, no highly reliable NDC biomarker has been established [26].

Growing omics datasets increasingly enable integration of multiple molecular layers to investigate the complex interplay between biological systems. However, applications in NDC research have only recently emerged [27]. Early efforts typically combined two or three omics layers, most commonly archived genome-wide data with proteomic or transcriptomic data, to assess how genetic variants shape NDC physiology through altered gene or protein expression [28]. Metabolomics has frequently complemented these layers by linking molecular changes to functional pathways. In autism, integrated urine metabolomic and proteomic analyses implicated altered neuroimmune pathways [29], while crosstissue meta-analyses identified shared molecular signatures across the central nervous system, circulatory system, and non-invasive biofluids [30]. Despite limited sample sizes, integration of placental transcriptomics and metabolomics has shown diagnostic promise, with a machine-learning model classifying NDCs at >95% accuracy using only a few components [31]. Recently, urine metabolomics combined with genomics and epigenomics revealed differential association patterns in ADHD, including strong correlations between specific CpG loci and ADHD polygenic scores [32].

Beyond traditional omics layers, incorporating nutritional status and toxic metal exposure may more directly connect molecular findings to modifiable dietary and environmental factors in the etiology of NDC [33]. Both essential metals (e.g., copper, manganese, and zinc) and toxic metals (e.g., lead, mercury, and arsenic) can influence brain development and function, as altered homeostasis or disrupted metabolic pathways may affect neuronal function and contribute to neurodevelopmental phenotypes [34] [35] [36] [37]. However, comprehensive frameworks that integrate multi-tissue, multi-omics data collected within the same cohort, including less-explored domains such as metallomics, remain rare.

Twin study designs provide a uniquely powerful framework for isolating condition-associated biological variation from shared genetic and environmental confounding. By controlling for age and most of the early-life environment and, in monozygotic (MZ) pairs, for essentially the entire inherited genome, within-pair comparisons substantially reduce background sources of heterogeneity. Consequently, differences observed in condition-discordant twin pairs can be more confidently attributed to conditionrelated processes rather than shared genetic or environmental influences [38, 39].

In this exploratory study, we applied an integrative multi-omics approach with a twin design to identify molecular signatures and convergent biological pathways associated with NDCs across multiple layers of biological organization. We leveraged data from the Roots of Autism and ADHD Twin Study in Sweden (RATSS) cohort [40, 41], which includes twin pairs concordant and discordant for NDCs, as well as typically developing twin pairs. By integrating complementary single-omics datasets generated from the RATSS cohort, including serum and CSF proteomics [21], fecal and urine metabolomics [24], blood metallomics, and whole-genome sequencing (WGS) data from a subset of 14 MZ twin pairs, we systematically examined cross-omics relationships and biological convergence across molecular layers.

The most important molecules were examined using across-and within-twin-pair analyses to highlight genetic and environmental contributions.

2 Methods and materials

2.1 Study participants

The sample was drawn from RATSS [40, 41], a deeply phenotyped cohort of twin pairs with various NDC diagnoses and neurotypically developing twins (Supplementary Fig. S1, Table S1). Detailed recruitment, clinical assessment, and behavioral measures are described in Supplementary Methods S1 and in previous publications [40, 41]. Briefly, twins were recruited from a population-based twin cohort and other sources, targeting autism and ADHD discordant pairs. All twins underwent a comprehensive behavioral assessment at a clinical research center (Supplementary Methods S2), including ageappropriate parent-rated Child/Adult Behavior Checklist (CBCL/ABCL), self-rated Youth/Adult Behavior

Checklist (YSR/ASR), Adaptive Behavior Assessment System-Second Edition (ABAS-II), Social

Responsiveness Scale 2nd Edition (SRS-2), Wechsler Intelligence Scales (WISC-IV/WAIS-IV) or the Leiter scales. NDCs and psychiatric disorders were assessed by expert-clinician consensus according to DSM-5 and ICD-10 criteria [1]. Zygosity was determined by genotyping or a 48-single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) panel [42, 43]. A subset of 14 MZ twin pairs provided WGS data as part of the EU-AIMS initiative [44]. NDC discordance was defined by one twin meeting DSM-5/ICD-10 criteria for any NDC (e.g., autism, ADHD, Intellectual Disability [ID], Tic Disorder, and Specific Learning Disorder) while the co-twin did not. Individuals with one or more NDC diagnoses were assigned to the anyNDC group, and those without any to the noNDC group.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Swedish Ethical Review Authority (2016/145-231). Informed consent was obtained from all participants and/or their caregivers.

2.2 Biological sample processing and measurement

Serum and CSF proteomic data were available for 126 and 86 individuals, respectively. Proteins were measured using multiplex immunoassay panels (Olink Bioscience, Sweden; RRID:SCR_003899), including Inflammation I, CVD (cardiovascular), and Oncology I, covering 203 proteins. More details are provided in Supplementary Methods S3.1 and Smedler et al. [21]. Urine and fecal metabolomic data were available for 112 and 189 individuals, respectively. Urine was profiled by untargeted 1 H nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy, and fecal samples by untargeted liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (Metabolon). More details are provided in Supplementary Methods S3.2, S3.3 and Swann et al. [24]. Metallomic profiling was performed on blood samples from 220 individuals, quantifying essential [calcium (Ca), copper (Cu), iron (Fe), magnesium (Mg), manganese (Mn), molybdenum (Mo), selenium (Se), and zinc (Zn)] and toxic metals [arsenic (As), cadmium (Cd), cobalt (Co), mercury (Hg), and lead (Pb)] by inductively coupled plasma-mass spectrometry (ICP-MS). More details are provided in Supplementary Methods S3.4.

2.3 Integrative omics analysis

We applied an integrative multi-omics strategy using the DIABLO (Data Integration Analysis for Biomarker Discovery using Latent Variable Approaches for Omics Studies) framework implemented in the R package mixOmics (version 6.22.0) [45]. DIABLO applied multiblock sparse Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (sPLS-DA) to identify molecules correlated across omics layers while jointly contributing to discriminating anyNDC from noNDC. Through LASSO penalization, a sparse subset of molecules was selected, producing latent components (variates) for each omics block. Each individual received a variate score for each component per block, reflecting their position along the axis of maximal cross-omics covariation associated with NDC status. The average variate 1 and variate 2 scores across omics blocks indicate cross-omics NDC-associated separation at the individual level. We first applied single-omics sPLS-DA models to optimize variable selection parameters for each dataset. To maximize sample coverage across diverse omics combinations, we designed three complementary multi-omics models: MultiOmic-SCFB (serum-proteomics, CSF-proteomics, fecal-metabolomics, and bloodmetallomics datasets) with 72 samples (37 anyNDC, 35 noNDC); MultiOmic-SFB (serum-proteomics, fecal-metabolomics, and blood-metallomics datasets) with 109 samples (56 anyNDC, 53 noNDC); MultiOmic-UFB (urine-metabolomics, fecal-metabolomics, and blood-metallomics datasets) with 89 samples (29 anyNDC, 60 noNDC) (Fig. 1, Supplementary Fig. S2). Detailed data processing and model construction are described in Supplementary Methods S4 and Fig. S3.

Associations between average variate scores and potential confounders and clinical measures were assessed using Generalized Estimating Equation (GEE) models with the R package drgee (version 1.1.10) [46] to account for the family linkage.

2.4 Pathway enrichment analysis for proteins and metabolites

We analyzed the enrichment of proteins and metabolites selected from the multi-omics models across Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways [47] using MetaboAnalyst 6.0 web tool (accessed 2025-08-12) [48], via the Joint Pathway Analysis module. Using the default background, we evaluated both metabolic-pathway and all-pathway libraries. Enrichment significance was determined by a hypergeometric test, with degree centrality as a topology measure to weight individual molecular importance. We applied different integration methods for the background: for metabolic pathway analysis, the "Overall Combine p values" method, which weights the proportion of each omics; for all pathway analysis, the tighter "Combine Query" method, which pools the genes/proteins and metabolites into a single query. The false discovery rate (FDR) method was used to correct for multiple comparisons [49]. Furthermore, we applied Gene-Metabolite Interaction Network analysis in the Network Analysis module (default parameters) to examine interactions between selected proteins and metabolites.

2.5 Metallome and NDC-related pathways

We selected seven metals that showed correlation coefficients > 0.6 with features from other omics layers in multi-omics models, including the essential metals Ca, Mg, Fe, Zn, and Mn, as well as the toxic metals Co and Cd. In the Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (CTD, accessed 2025-01-09) [50], we searched the condition keyword "neurodevelopmental disorder" and retrieved curated gene sets for each metal with a documented association with NDCs. Ca was excluded because its association with NDCs in CTD lacked curated evidence. We then conducted KEGG pathway enrichment analysis on the combined gene lists using the R package clusterProfiler (version 4.6.2) [51], with p-values adjusted by the FDR method.

2.6 Differential analysis of molecular levels

To assess associations between molecular levels and NDC diagnosis, we focused on molecules mapped to significantly enriched metabolic pathways and all 13 metals. The lysine degradation pathway was additionally included as it reached nominal significance in the protein-metabolite pathway analysis and FDR-significance in the metal-related pathway analysis. We first assessed across-individual effects using standard GEE models adjusted for age, sex, BMI, medication (yes/no), and other psychiatric diagnosis (yes/no), with standard errors clustered by twin pair. To control for unmeasured family-constant confounders (e.g., parental psychiatric history, growth environment, and shared genetic factors of twin pairs), we then applied conditional GEE (CGEE) models to estimate within-pair molecular effects. CGEE models were adjusted for cluster-varying covariates (BMI, medication, and other psychiatric diagnoses).

Analyses were performed across all twin pairs, MZ twin pairs, and NDC-discordant MZ pairs to isolate the effects of non-shared exposures.

2.7 WGS variant analysis and pathway enrichment

We analyzed WGS data from 14 MZ twin pairs (3 NDC-discordant, 6 NDC-concordant, and 5 noNDCconcordant pairs). Zygosity was confirmed by kinship coefficient analysis (Kinship >0.345). We prioritized two variant types to identify potential genetic drivers of discordance and shared liability. First, we identified 5,531 unique rare variants (allele frequency [AF] <0.001 in gnomAD/SweFreq) present in only one twin of a pair and then absent from all other individuals. Second, we identified 1,197 shared rare variants, defined as exonic variants (AF <0.01) shared within a twin pair. Shared variants were restricted to NDC-related genes, including SFARI genes (score 1, 2, or syndromic) and the Intellectual Disability gene panel [52, 53]. Functional impact of genes harboring NDC-specific unique or shared rare variants was assessed by KEGG pathway enrichment analysis using the R package clusterProfiler (version 4.6.2), with FDR-adjusted p-values. Detailed QC and filtering parameters are provided in Supplementary Methods S5.

3 Results

Our study included 237 twins from the RATSS cohort with available omics data, including 116 complete twin pairs and five unpaired individuals whose co-twins lacked omics data. Among them, 122 twins (51.5%) were diagnosed with at least one NDC (Fig. 1), and the study population ranged in age from 8 to 28 years (mean 14.7, SD 3.7). When stratified by NDC diagnoses, two groups differed in age, BMI, medication, and psychiatric diagnosis status (Table 1). Within each omics dataset, the proportion of anyNDC and noNDC groups was balanced (anyNDC 49.2-52.4%), except for the urine dataset (anyNDC 33.0%). Detailed demographic characteristics for each omics dataset and individual are presented in Supplementary Fig. S1 and Tables S1-S2. The overall analytical workflow is illustrated in Fig. 1.

3.1 Identification of multi-omics profile

To incorporate a broader range of omics layers while maximizing the number of samples with complete data, we first integrated the serum-proteomics, CSF-proteomics, fecal-metabolomics, and bloodmetallomics datasets (72 individuals: 37 anyNDC, 35 noNDC; Fig. 1, Supplementary Fig. S2). Details of the single-omics investigations are described in Supplementary Results S1. The resulting model, MultiOmic-SCFB, identified 106 key molecular features across two latent components. Detailed results for parameter tuning and model optimization are provided in Supplementary Table S3. The model revealed the highest correlation between the first latent components of the serum and CSF datasets (r = 0.54; Supplementary Figs. S4A-B), likely reflecting the shared Olink panel across both tissues, while the modest correlation indicates partially distinct signals highlighting the complementary value of multitissue sampling. Projecting twins onto each omics block's latent components (variate 1 and variate 2 scores) revealed that serum-proteomics provided the strongest single-layer separation of NDC status (Fig. 2A). The average variate scores across all omics blocks, representing the integrated cross-omics signal, showed clearer group separation than any single-omics projection (Fig. 2B). The intermediate region between two groups was enriched for NDC-discordant twin pairs, reflecting molecular similarity potentially shaped by shared environmental or genetic factors within pairs. However, no associations between averaged variate 1 or variate 2 scores and potential confounders (age, sex, BMI, medication use, and other psychiatric diagnoses) survived FDR correction (Supplementary Table S4), indicating that the multi-omics signatures are not driven by these covariates. A global map of the key features and their inter-omics correlations is visualized in a circos plot (Fig. 2C, Supplementary Figs. S4C-D), providing a holistic view of the interconnected molecular architecture of NDCs. More details about multi-omics model results are described in Supplementary Results S2.

We further tested whether the omics profile captured in the MultiOmic-SCFB model reflected variation in dimensional NDC traits and more general psychiatric symptoms. In GEE models adjusting for sex, age, BMI, medication status and psychiatric diagnosis status, significant but modest correlations (GEE-p<0.001; Fig. 2D-G) were observed between the averaged variate 1 scores and autistic traits (SRS), ADHD traits (CBCL/ABCL attention subscale), adaptive functioning (ABAS), and parent-reported general psychiatric problems (total CBCL/ABCL scores). No association was found with self-reported behavior problems (total YSR/ASR score; GEE-p=0.482; Fig. 2H). Stratified analyses for anyNDC and noNDC groups showed no significant associations for CBCL/ABCL, ABAS, and SRS scores, while a significant negative association between IQ scores and averaged variate 1 scores was observed in the anyNDC group (GEE-p=0.012; Fig. 2I).

To maximize sample coverage given incomplete omics overlap and improve statistical power, we then integrated serum-proteomics, fecal-metabolomics, and blood-metallomics datasets to build the MultiOmic-SFB model (n=109; Supplementary Fig. S5). For urine-metabolomics, we maximized sample retention by integrating it with fecal-metabolomics and blood-metallomics, producing the MultiOmic-UFB model (n=89; Supplementary Fig. S6). More details about MultiOmic-SFB and MultiOmic-UFB models are described in Supplementary Results S2.2 and S2.3. Overall, integrating multiple omics layers tended to improve discrimination between anyNDC and noNDC beyond that achieved by fewer-omics or single-omics datasets (Fig. 2B). We therefore selected molecules identified as key features across the three models to identify enriched biological pathways contributing to the anyNDC and noNDC separation.

3.2 Enrichment of key metabolites and proteins

Next, we conducted joint pathway analysis on the 79 metabolites and 63 proteins identified as key features in multi-omics models. Considering all pathways (both metabolic and gene-only), 21 were significantly enriched (FDR-adjusted p<0.05; Fig. 3A, Supplementary Table S5). Of these, only two were enriched by both protein and metabolite hits (Central carbon metabolism in cancer and HIF-1 signaling pathway). Notably, the ErbB signaling and EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitor resistance pathways exhibited the highest topological impact scores of 1.42 and 1.30 (Fig. 3B). Restricting to the metabolic-pathway pool, the "Combine P-Values" method revealed seven significantly enriched pathways (Fig. 3C; Supplementary Table S6). Among these, Riboflavin metabolism had the highest topological impact score (0.88; Fig. 3D). Only Purine metabolism was enriched by both protein (i.e., adenosine deaminase [ADA]) and metabolites.

To further investigate the functional interplay between key proteins and metabolites, we performed Gene-Metabolite Interaction Network analysis, which identified a core functional module centered on Purine metabolism (Fig. 3E). Interestingly, this metabolic hub connected with a cluster of chemokine genes, suggesting a coordinated metabolic-inflammatory axis involving adenosine and nicotinic acid.

3.3 Differential analysis of molecules within twin pairs

We then investigated associations between molecular levels and NDC diagnosis utilizing the twin design further for the molecules mapped to seven enriched metabolic pathways (FDR-adjusted p<0.05; Fig. 3C) and the lysine degradation pathway, including one protein (ADA; Supplementary Figs. S7-S8), 21 fecal metabolites (Supplementary Fig. S9), and two urine metabolites (Supplementary Fig. S10). Serum ADA levels were consistently and significantly elevated in individuals with NDCs in the whole sample (GEE p=0.0002; CGEE p=0.015), the MZ subset (GEE p=0.0013; CGEE p=0.0048), and notably the discordant MZ twins (GEE p=0.004; CGEE p=0.0009) (Table 2). However, ADA measured in CSF showed no significant difference (Supplementary Table S7).

For fecal metabolites, across-individual GEE models in whole samples (n=189) and MZ twin subset (n=134) identified a significant negative association between flavinmononucleotide (FMN) and NDC diagnosis (FDR-adjusted p=0.036 and 4.8 × 10⁻⁵; Supplementary Table S8). However, this association was absent in the within-pair CGEE models (90 and 64 clusters for whole and MZ subsets, respectively), suggesting the FMN signal may be influenced by familial background. Interestingly, for NDC-discordant MZ subsample, within-pair analysis indicated a distinct metabolic signature in which twins with NDC had significantly lower levels of thymidine, glutamine, and 2'-deoxyuridine than their co-twins without NDC (FDR-adjusted p=0.029, 0.029, and 0.039, respectively). Results for all fecal molecules are shown in Supplementary Table S8. For urine metabolites, no significant differences were found (Supplementary Table S9).

We also evaluated alterations in 13 blood metals in relation to NDC diagnosis (Supplementary Fig. S11, Table S10). Mn showed a positive association with NDC diagnosis in the MZ subset (FDR-adjusted p=0.0039), while Cu showed a significant elevation specifically in NDC-affected twins in the discordant MZ within-pair model (FDR-adjusted p=0.036; Table 2). Additional nominally significant results are described in Supplementary Results S3.

Exploratory analyses stratified by ADHD and autism showed heterogeneous modification patterns, reflecting the molecular complexity of individual NDC diagnoses. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously given the modest and varied sample sizes available across omics datasets and models, and individuals with comorbid diagnoses contributed to subgroup analyses (Supplementary Results S3, Table S11).

3.4 Metal exposure and NDC-related pathways

To investigate the functional relevance of the metallomics profile, we focused on metals strongly correlated with other omics layers (Mg, Fe, Zn, Mn, Co, Ca, and Cd; correlation coefficients >0.6) and performed KEGG pathway enrichment using genes associated with both NDCs and at least one metal.

Using a set of 306 metal-NDC-associated genes with curated evidence in CTD (Ca-NDC-genes excluded, lacking curated evidence), 100 pathways were significantly enriched (Supplementary Fig. S12, Table S12). More details are described in Supplementary Results S4. Notably, ADA was among the genes associated with autism-related Cd and Zn, and autistic disorder-related Zn.

3.5 Pathway enrichment for genes harboring unique and shared rare variants in 14 MZ twin pairs

KEGG pathway enrichment of genes harboring NDC-specific unique and shared rare variants revealed convergence with pathways identified in other omics layers (Fig. 4). For 810 genes harboring NDCexclusive rare unique variants, 15 pathways were significantly enriched (FDR-adjusted p<0.05; Supplementary Fig. S13A, Table S13). Among these, the MAPK signaling pathway (FDR-adjusted p=0.032) was consistently implicated across enrichment analyses of both proteo-metabolomic and metallomic layers. The chemokine signaling pathway (FDR-adjusted p=0.039) was also enriched in the joint pathway analysis of proteins and metabolites. Additionally, several previously significant pathways reappeared at a nominal significance level here (e.g., purine metabolism [p=0.028] and PI3K-Akt signaling pathway [p=0.035]). Analysis of 491 genes harboring only rare exonic shared variants in concordant and discordant NDC twins identified nine significantly enriched pathways (Supplementary Fig. S13B, Table S14). The lysine degradation pathway (FDR-adjusted p=0.013) overlapped with previous findings for both key proteins-metabolites and metals. Further narrowing the analysis to genes with variants shared exclusively in concordant NDC twins and absent from all noNDC individuals, the calcium signaling pathway was the sole significantly enriched pathway (FDR-adjusted p=0.0097). More results about rare variants identified in WGS data are described in Supplementary Results S5.

4 Discussion

In this study, we applied an integrative multi-omics strategy within a twin cohort, combining data-driven multiblock sPLS-DA models with knowledge-based pathway enrichment to investigate molecular signatures and biological pathways associated with NDC. Convergent signals implicated several metabolic and signaling pathways across omics layers. Twin-model-based analyses provided additional context for the molecules prioritized by the integrative framework, indicating that their associations with NDC arose from a combination of shared genetic/familial influences and non-shared individualspecific factors across different molecular features. ADA protein and blood copper showed independent associations with NDC status, while thymidine, glutamine, and 2'-deoxyuridine, involved in pyrimidine metabolism, were consistently decreased in the NDC-discordant MZ subset. Flavin mononucleotide and blood manganese were also associated with NDC, though their associations were modulated by shared genetic or familial environmental influences.

A key value of the integrative approach is that some molecules' relevance to NDC becomes apparent only through their correlation with other omics layers rather than from single-omics testing alone [54].

In contrast to approaches that enrich each layer separately and overlap the results post hoc [29, 55], our convergent pathway findings reflect coordinated cross-layer relationships captured through joint modeling. This value also stems from the distinct position each omics layer occupies along the genotypeto-phenotype path and its tissue origin [56]. Pathways converge from molecules spanning these compartments, and modeling cross-omics correlation enables more complete identification of coordinated dysregulation. WGS, although available only in 14 MZ twin pairs, complements these insights by capturing rare genetic variation at both somatic and germline levels, offering a genetic-layer perspective on processes also highlighted at the physiological level. Molecules highlighted by both the integrative models and individual-level differential analysis are considered doubly supported and given particular weight in our discussion.

Several pathways were consistently and significantly implicated across different omics layers, highlighting their potential involvement in NDC physiology. Among metabolic pathways, lysine degradation emerged as the most consistently enriched process. Numerous studies have associated lysine degradation with mitochondrial energy metabolism and redox homeostasis; however, direct evidence linking it to NDC remains limited and mainly focused on autism. A recent systematic review reported that autism-associated microRNAs are significantly involved in lysine degradation [57].

Emerging evidence also points to microbiome-mediated mechanisms. Zou et al. found that altered gut microbiota in autistic children was associated with reduced lysine degradation [58], while Hicks et al.

observed upregulated microbial RNA enriched in this pathway in the autism oropharynx [59]. Altered lysine metabolism may also affect microbial degradation products such as 5-aminovalerate, which has been implicated in autism-related behaviors [60].

We further observed that one key metabolite in the lysine pathway, 2-aminoadipate, was nominally elevated in individuals with NDCs in the all MZ twin subsamples. Consistent with this, a recent clinical study found higher levels of α-aminoadipic acid in autistic children under five years compared with nonautistic children [61]. The accumulation of 2-aminoadipate is of particular interest because it can act as a neurotoxin by inhibiting the synthesis of kynurenic acid, a neuroprotective metabolite. Such accumulation may occur when aminotransferases that normally metabolize 2-aminoadipate are impaired, a process dependent on the cofactor pyridoxal 5'-phosphate (PLP, vitamin B6). Reduced or highly variable PLP levels have been reported in some autistic children compared with the neurotypical group [60]. Also, early postnatal pyridoxine supplementation has been shown to improve social and repetitive behaviors in the BTBR mouse model [62]. Together, these findings highlight a potential microbial-host metabolic axis linking lysine degradation, vitamin B6-dependent enzymatic activity, and neurobehavioral regulation in NDCs.

The purine metabolism pathway also emerged as a key metabolic focus in our study, enriched across multiple layers. This pathway is critically involved in neurodevelopment, particularly through its regulation of neuroimmune interactions and oxidative stress responses [63]. One of its core enzymes, ADA, regulates extracellular adenosine levels and mediates neuroprotective and anti-inflammatory functions [64]. The ADA gene has been listed in the SFARI Gene database as a strong candidate for autism susceptibility [52], with previous genetic studies linking specific allelic variants to ADA deficiency and increased autism likelihood [65, 66]. More recently, transcriptomic evidence has suggested an upregulation of ADA expression in autism [67]. Our finding of elevated serum ADA in NDC group parallels this molecular trend and remained robust even after controlling for family-constant confounding. Notably, CSF ADA levels did not differ between groups, reflecting region-specific regulation of ADA in peripheral metabolic disturbances. Together, we extend prior genetic-and transcriptomiclevel observations to the protein level and broaden ADA's relevance across NDC diagnoses, highlighting ADA as a potential peripheral biomarker of purine metabolic imbalance in NDC.

The purine metabolism pathway also appears to interact with broader neuroimmune signaling networks implicated in NDCs. In our metabolite-protein interaction network, adenosine, the central node of purine metabolism, exhibited close topological proximity to several members of the chemokine family.

Consistent with this, the chemokine signaling pathway was also significantly enriched in our proteinmetabolite joint pathway analysis and in the gene set harboring rare unique variants found in NDCs.

Chemokines are well-established mediators of neuroimmune signaling, and their dysregulation during early development has been linked to altered neurodevelopmental trajectories in autism [68]. These findings suggest a potential mechanistic convergence between purinergic signaling and immune modulation in the context of NDCs.

Our findings also highlighted several other key cell-signaling pathways, most notably the PI3K-Akt and MAPK signaling. These pathways showed consistent associations across multiple data layers, involving proteins, metals, and genes carrying NDC-specific rare unique variants. Both pathways are welldocumented regulators of neurodevelopmental processes, including neuronal survival, synaptic plasticity, and neuroimmune signaling [69, 70]. Genetic and functional studies have linked their dysregulation to a range of NDCs, with NDC-associated risk genes converging on these signaling cascades [11, [70] [71] [72], and perturbation correlating with symptom severity in idiopathic autism and cognitive impairments in intellectual disability [73, 74]. Among the key proteins we identified, EIF4EBP1 serves as a molecular marker of mTOR signaling [72], suggesting that downstream signals closely connected to PI3K-Akt, such as the mTOR axis, may also be implicated. Our findings add multi-layered evidence supporting PI3K-Akt/MAPK involvement in NDCs, though the underlying mechanisms warrant further investigation.

Among metabolites mapped to significantly enriched metabolic pathways, only thymidine, glutamine, and 2'-deoxyuridine, all involved in pyrimidine metabolism, were significantly lower in twins with NDCs than their unaffected co-twins in the within-pair CGEE model. This pattern suggests that alterations in specific components of pyrimidine metabolism may contribute to NDC liability independently of genetic or shared environmental influences. Glutamine, which is also modulated downstream of purine metabolism, has received considerable attention in NDC research due to its role in neurotransmitter balance. It cooperates with glutamate and GABA, which are crucial for maintaining excitatory-inhibitory homeostasis in the developing brain [75].

Among other differential findings, FMN levels were strongly negatively associated with NDC in the across-individual models, but not in the within-pair analysis, suggesting that shared genetic factors may mediate the relationship. While direct links between FMN and NDC are currently lacking, FMN serves as a critical cofactor for redox-active enzymes and ribonucleotide reductase [76] and may indirectly impact biological processes relevant to NDC physiology. In the within-pair model, the only metal that remained significantly associated with NDC was copper. Previous studies have reported elevated serum copper in autistic individuals, possibly contributing to oxidative stress [77]. However, our within-pair CGEE model employed a reduced sample size, especially for the discordant MZ twin subset, which diminished statistical power and increased the risk of false negatives.

Incorporating metallomics into the multi-omics framework offers valuable insights into environmental contributions to NDC mechanisms. In the differential metal profiles, we found that blood manganese levels were elevated across NDC individuals within the MZ subsample but were influenced by shared genetic or environmental factors. This may help explain part of the inconsistency in epidemiological studies of manganese exposure and NDC liability [78]. Indeed, polymorphisms in Mn-transporter genes have been shown to modulate manganese homeostasis and be associated with neurodevelopmental outcomes [79]. When genetic influence is not considered, blood manganese concentrations are generally not correlated with ingested manganese through drinking water and food [80]. Associations between blood manganese and NDC also converge with pathways highlighted earlier. In newborn rats, early-life oral manganese intake has been linked to attention deficits via dysregulation of gene expression in the prefrontal cortex, potentially involving mTOR signaling and neuroinflammatory mechanisms [81]. In addition, manganese overexposure may disrupt neurodevelopment through oxidative stress and neuroinflammation, which are also implicated in ADA function and purine metabolism [78]. Our findings underscore the complex and multilayered involvement of metal biology in pathways relevant to NDCs.

Our study has several limitations. First, our integrative multi-omics approach provides insights into NDCassociated candidate pathways. Still, it does not directly identify validated biomarkers or provide a predictive clinical model. Second, although substantial for a multi-omics twin design, our sample size limits our power to detect subtle cross-omics effects and to generalize to diverse populations, particularly within NDC diagnostic subgroups. Third, the scope and complexity of multi-omics outputs preclude exhaustive reporting, introducing potential selective emphasis in our discussion [82]. Fourth, WGS was limited to 14 MZ twin pairs, constraining variant-discovery power and generalizability, while proteomics was restricted to targeted Olink panels, potentially biasing findings toward represented pathways. Discordance status may also change over time, so some "discordant" pairs could reflect differences in NDC onset timing rather than stable biological discordance. Although no significant associations emerged between DIABLO variate scores and covariates after FDR correction, nominal associations were observed between age and variate 1, and between medication and variate 2; we cannot entirely rule out these as potential sources of residual confounding in the multi-omics signature.

Future studies would benefit from untargeted proteome-wide screens and additional omics layers (e.g., transcriptomics, epigenomics) alongside richer phenotypic data.

In conclusion, this study presents a multi-omics framework embedded within a twin-cohort design to uncover potential coordinated molecular alterations underlying NDC. The convergence of signals across metabolomic, proteomic, blood metallomic, and genetic layers points to shared biological processes involving systemic metabolic dysregulation and immune-related signaling cascades. Notably, we observed several molecules that have not been well characterized in the NDC context (e.g., the ADA protein and FMN), and emphasized that differential molecular levels may reflect underlying genetic or shared environmental influences rather than direct effects of NDCs.
