Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

P-Value Text
P ≥0.05 None of these risk differences differed significantly from that in the eGFR ≥60 group (all P ≥0.05) (Table 3; Figure 2).

The APA manual states: Report exact p values (e.g., p = .031) to two or three decimal places. However, report p values less than .001 as p < .001. However, 2 decimals is too imprecise for many use-cases (e.g., a p value meta-analysis), so report p values with three digits.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 1 non-significant p value that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Text Sentence
P ≥0.05 None of these risk differences differed significantly from that in the eGFR ≥60 group (all P ≥0.05) (Table 3; Figure 2).

For metascientific articles demonstrating the rate of misinterpretations of non-significant results is high, see:

Aczel B, Palfi B, Szollosi A, Kovacs M, Szaszi B, Szecsi P, Zrubka M, Gronau Q, van den Bergh D, Wagenmakers E (2018). “Quantifying Support for the Null Hypothesis in Psychology: An Empirical Investigation.” Advances in Methods and Practices in Psychological Science, 1(3), 357–366. doi:10.1177/2515245918773742.

Murphy S, Merz R, Reimann L, Fernández A (2025). “Nonsignificance misinterpreted as an effect’s absence in psychology: Prevalence and temporal analyses.” Royal Society Open Science, 12(3), 242167. doi:10.1098/rsos.242167.

Power Analysis Check

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background: Socioeconomic position is associated with kidney disease, but it remains unclear whether these differences are greater for worsening kidney function than for a first recorded diagnosis. We examined how household income, education, and neighborhood deprivation relate to mortality and kidney outcomes.

We conducted a retrospective cohort study involving 218,098 individuals using data from the All of Us Research Program, with follow-up to January 1, 2025.

Primary outcomes involved all-cause mortality and sustained 40% estimated glomerular filtration rate decline. Secondary outcomes were incident recorded chronic kidney disease (CKD) diagnosis, incident recorded CKD category G3a or worse, and kidney failure. We also evaluated income inequalities and estimated standardized 3-year mortality risks for each baseline kidney state.

Median follow-up was 3.62 years for mortality and 2.76 years for kidney outcomes. Among 218,098 individuals, 4,777 deaths, 6,423 sustained declines, 7,870 incident recorded chronic kidney disease diagnoses, 4,716 incident category G3a-or-worse events, and 1,799 kidney failure events were observed. Adjusted hazard ratios (95% CIs) for household income below $25,000 compared with $200,000 or more were 2.68 (2.31-3.12), 3.57 (3.08-4.13), 2.13 (1.90-2.38), 2.15 (1.86-2.49), and 3.08 (2.30-4.11), respectively. Relative index of inequality estimates were approximately twice as large for sustained decline and kidney failure than for recorded CKD diagnosis. Within baseline kidney states, standardized 3-year absolute mortality differences by income did not differ significantly across states. The main limitations are the observational design, baseline creatinine measured up to a year after the survey, and outcome ascertainment from health records that may differ by income.

Conclusions: Lower income was associated with higher mortality and greater risk of all kidney outcomes. Education and neighborhood deprivation showed different patterns of association, which supports considering individual and neighborhood circumstances together.

Introduction

Health inequities are systematic, avoidable, and unfair differences in health outcomes and access to healthcare between social groups, rooted in unequal social, economic, and environmental conditions (1). Globally, an estimated 2.1 billion people, which represents 26% of the global population, experienced financial hardship from out-of-pocket healthcare spending in 2022.

Approximately 75% of people in the poorest groups faced financial hardship from healthcare costs compared with fewer than 4% in the richest groups (2). These inequalities are particularly relevant to kidney health (3, 4). Among 14,086 participants in the Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities (ARIC) study with a median follow-up duration of 23 years, lower income was associated with a 15% steeper decline in estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) and a higher risk of kidney failure (adjusted HR, 1.50; 95% CI, 1.14-1.98) compared with high income (5).

Similarly, among 5.6 million Japanese working adults, the lowest income decile, compared with the highest, was associated with rapid kidney function decline (adjusted OR, 1.70; 95% CI, 1.67-1.73) and higher risk of renal replacement therapy initiation (adjusted HR, 1.65; 95% CI, 1.47-1.86) (6). In the Scottish GLOMMS-CORE study, people with early kidney disease living in unemployed households had higher mortality than those in professional households (adjusted HR, 1.77; 95% CI, 1.60-1.96) (7). Both household and neighborhood deprivation were associated with higher mortality, with the poorest outcomes among people experiencing both. These findings highlight the role of socioeconomic inequalities within adverse renal outcomes and mortality. Nevertheless, limited evidence exists about the association of income inequalities in recorded chronic kidney disease and sustained kidney function decline. We therefore conducted a large-scale cohort study using the All of Us Research Program Controlled Tier Dataset examine the role of socioeconomic inequalities across adverse renal outcomes and mortality.

Materials and Methods

Study design and population

We conducted a retrospective cohort study using the All of Us Research Program Controlled Tier Dataset (version 9, C2025Q4R6) which enrolls adults across the United States with deliberate recruitment of underrepresented populations in biomedical research (8). Participants provided informed consent, and the All of Us program protocol was approved by its Institutional Review Board (protocol 11-01139). The Yale University Institutional Review Board determined that this analysis was not human subjects research (protocol 2000043320; July 14, 2026). Counts of 1 to 20 were suppressed in accordance with All of Us policy. This study followed the STROBE reporting guideline (9).

The study included participants aged 18 years or older with linked electronic health record data, determined baseline kidney state, positive mortality follow-up after the index date, and provided substantive responses to both household income and educational attainment. The index date was the earliest response to either socioeconomic item and could precede the other response.

Participants without a creatinine measurement from which estimated glomerular filtration rate could be calculated were eligible only if kidney failure or kidney transplantation could be identified. Missing responses, including "Skip" and "Prefer not to answer," were excluded.

Socioeconomic measures

Household income and educational attainment were obtained from participant surveys. Annual household income was categorized as <$25,000, $25,000-49,999, $50,000-99,999, $100,000-199,999, or ≥$200,000. Educational attainment was categorized as less than high school, high school or equivalent, some college, or college graduate or above. Income was not adjusted for household size. Neighborhood deprivation was the program-supplied index based on 3-digit ZIP code and was categorized into tertiles using the otherwise-eligible source cohort. Neighborhood deprivation values that were unavailable were coded separately as a different category. Income, education, and neighborhood deprivation were included in the primary models.

Baseline kidney state

Estimated glomerular filtration rate was calculated from serum creatinine using the race-free 2021 CKD-EPI equation (10). Baseline creatinine was the measurement closest to the index within 365 days before or after that date. Creatinine values between 0.1 and 20 were retained and treated as mg/dL; recorded units were not used to filter or convert values. Participants without kidney failure or transplantation were classified into GFR categories G1 (≥90), G2 (60-89), G3a (45-59), G3b (30-44), G4 (15-29), or G5 (<15 mL/min/1.73 m²). Kidney failure and transplantation were classified as separate baseline states, with transplantation taking precedence. Kidney failure was identified from diagnostic codes indicating chronic kidney failure or dialysis dependence or from dialysis records on at least 2 dates separated by at least 90 days. Kidneyfailure qualification used the available longitudinal record, with onset assigned to the earliest dialysis/ESKD record; later evidence could retrospectively qualify a date before index. Kidney transplantation was identified from status or procedure records. Detailed information is provided in Supplementary Methods.

Outcomes and follow-up

The primary outcomes were all-cause mortality and a sustained 40% decline in eGFR. Decline required an eGFR ≤60% of baseline after the baseline measurement, confirmed by another qualifying value at least 30 days later. The event was dated to the first qualifying measurement.

Qualifying low eGFR values did not need to be consecutive, and the confirmation period was not explicitly restricted by death or the administrative end of follow-up. Prevalent recorded CKD was identified when the assigned first-record date was on or before the index date, although confirmation of CKD could occur after the index date. Further details are provided in Supplementary Methods. Secondary outcomes were a first recorded diagnosis of chronic kidney disease (CKD), first recorded CKD category G3a or worse, and kidney failure. Recorded CKD outcomes required one inpatient diagnosis or qualifying diagnoses on at least two different dates, with the event dated to the first record. The kidney-failure outcome was the first qualifying dialysis or ESKD record, as defined above, or kidney transplantation, whichever occurred first.

Participants with recorded CKD, kidney failure, or transplantation at baseline were excluded from the recorded-diagnosis analyses. Participants with baseline kidney failure or transplantation were excluded from the decline and kidney-failure analyses. The decline analysis also required a baseline eGFR estimate. Each analysis required positive follow-up. For kidney outcomes, follow-up ended at qualifying outcome, death, last qualifying clinical contact, or January 1, 2025, whichever occurred first. Mortality follow-up ended at death or January 1, 2025, without censoring at the last EHR contact. Deaths were identified from the program's death records, using the earliest recorded date for each participant.

Statistical analysis

Adjusted hazard ratios and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated using Cox models with the Efron method of handling ties. Household income, education, and neighborhood deprivation were included in primary Cox models as categorical variables. Models were adjusted for age, sex, race, and ethnicity. Race and ethnicity were self-reported in the program's basics survey and were included as social categories to account for social patterning of socioeconomic position, kidney disease, and care; the categories are listed in the Supplementary Methods. Age was modeled linearly. Remaining variables were treated as categorical. Reference groups were income ≥$200,000, college graduate or above, and the least deprived neighborhood tertile. For nonfatal outcomes, cause-specific models censored participants at death. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed with scaled Schoenfeld residual tests. Follow-up was split at 2 years, and income associations were estimated separately within each period with the same covariate set. When the assumption was not met, overall HRs were interpreted as summaries of the associations over follow-up. Income inequalities were also summarized using the relative index of inequality (RII) and slope index of inequality (SII) (11). For each outcome cohort, income groups were ranked from highest to lowest. Each group received a ridit score equal to the proportion of participants in higher-income groups plus half the proportion in that group. RII compared the extremes of income rank using Cox models adjusted for sex, age, race, and ethnicity. The RII is the hazard ratio for a one-unit change in ridit score, the modeled contrast between the lowest-and highest-income extremes of the distribution; values above 1 indicate higher hazard with lower income. The indices summarize the total income gradient and were therefore not adjusted for education or neighborhood deprivation. SII used population-weighted regression of the five crude income-category rates on ridit scores and was expressed per 1000 person-years. Further details are provided in Supplementary Methods. In exploratory analyses, we estimated the income trend per $10,000 within subgroups defined by race, sex, age, diabetes, and baseline kidney state, and the categorical socioeconomic associations with mortality within sex, race, and neighborhood-deprivation strata; a descriptive analysis repeated the income models among participants with prevalent recorded CKD at baseline. SII confidence intervals were calculated using a t-distribution with three residual degrees of freedom. Aalen-Johansen estimator was used for unadjusted 5-year kidney outcome cumulative incidence, with death treated as a competing event. Associations between income and mortality within kidney function states were assessed using Cox models adjusted for the primary covariates, and an income-by-kidney-state interaction was tested using a likelihood-ratio test.

Standardized 3-year mortality risks were estimated separately for each kidney state using Kaplan-Meier-based infinitesimal-jackknife pseudo-observations and identity-link regression adjusted for the primary covariates. HC1 robust standard errors were used to calculate Wald confidence intervals, without restricting their limits to the 0%-100% range. Absolute risk differences were calculated by subtracting the estimated risk in the highest-income group from that in the lowest-income group. Differences in risk differences across kidney states were assessed using eGFR ≥60 mL/min/1.73 m² as the reference, with variances summed from the separate, nonoverlapping state-specific models. These cross-state comparisons were considered descriptive. A pooled Cox model with income-by-state interaction provided standardized 2-and 3-year risks as a sensitivity analysis, with percentile intervals from 300 participant-level bootstrap replicates. Other sensitivity analyses changed mortality censoring, kidney-failure chronicity requirements, or eligibility based on numeric baseline eGFR.

Results

Study Population

Among 481,849 individuals who met the age and follow-up criteria, 218,098 were included in the study. Exclusions were 207,335 individuals whose baseline kidney state could not be determined, 54,482 without a household income response, and 1,934 without an education response (Supplementary Table S1; Supplementary Figure S9). Mean age was 54.3 years, and 132,327 individuals (60.7%) were female. Median follow-up was 2.76 years for kidney outcomes, and 3.62 years for mortality. Compared with individuals in the highest income category, those in the lowest category were younger, had lower educational attainment, and more often lived in deprived neighborhoods (Table 1). Baseline characteristics by educational attainment and by kidney state are shown in Supplementary Tables S2 and S3, and the availability of the neighborhood deprivation index in Supplementary Table S4. At baseline, 2,440 individuals had kidney failure, and 1,955 had received a kidney transplant.

Socioeconomic position, mortality, and kidney outcomes

During follow-up, 4,777 individuals died. Among the 209,916 individuals included in the kidney-function decline analysis, 6,423 experienced a sustained 40% decline in eGFR.

Compared with participants with household income of $200,000 or more, those with income below $25,000 had higher hazards of mortality (adjusted HR, 2.68; 95% CI, 2.31-3.12) and sustained kidney function decline (adjusted HR, 3.57; 95% CI, 3.08-4.13).

Among 194,814 individuals included in the recorded CKD analyses, 7,870 had a first recorded CKD diagnosis and 4,716 had a first recorded diagnosis of CKD category G3a or worse. Kidney failure occurred in 1,799 of 209,916 eligible individuals. Comparing the lowest with the highest income category, adjusted HRs were 2.13 (95% CI, 1.90-2.38) for recorded CKD diagnosis, 2.15 (95% CI, 1.86-2.49) for recorded CKD category G3a or worse, and 3.08 (95% CI, 2.30-4.11) for kidney failure (Table 2; Figure 1). Among individuals who had not completed high school, the adjusted HR for mortality was 1.41 (95% CI, 1.25-1.60) compared with college graduates. The corresponding adjusted hazard ratio for sustained eGFR decline was 1.42 (95% CI, 1.29-1.58). Both models were adjusted for household income, neighborhood deprivation, and demographic covariates. Individuals living in the most deprived neighborhood tertile had higher hazards of all four kidney outcomes than those in the least deprived tertile. Among the four kidney outcomes, the HR was lowest for sustained eGFR decline at 1.09 (95% CI, 1.02-1.17) and highest for kidney failure at 1.42 (95% CI, 1.25-1.61). No association with mortality was observed for this comparison (HR, 1.00; 95% CI, 0.93-1.08) (Supplementary Table S5; Supplementary Figure S1).

Income inequalities across outcomes

Inequality was greater for sustained eGFR decline and kidney failure than for outcomes based on recorded CKD diagnoses. The relative index of inequality was 6.84 (95% CI, 6.15-7.61) for sustained eGFR decline and 6.06 (95% CI, 4.94-7.44) for kidney failure. Lower estimates were observed for recorded CKD diagnosis (3.02; 95% CI, 2.76-3.30) and CKD category G3a or worse (2.94; 95% CI, 2.62-3.31). For mortality, the relative index of inequality was 4.70 (95% CI, 4.17-5.29). Sustained eGFR decline also showed the largest crude absolute inequality, with a slope index of 16.98 additional events per 1,000 person-years (95% CI, 10.27-23.70).

Differences in inequality estimates across outcomes were described but not formally tested (Supplementary Table S5; Supplementary Figure S10).

Mortality by baseline kidney state

Among individuals with baseline eGFR ≥60 mL/min/1.73 m², those in the lowest income category had a higher risk of mortality than those in the highest category (adjusted HR, 2.73; 95% CI, 2.28-3.26). The corresponding HR was 1.86 (95% CI, 1.32-2.61) for eGFR 30-59 mL/min/1.73 m², 1.24 (95% CI, 0.50-3.05) for eGFR <30 mL/min/1.73 m², 1.31 (95% CI, 0.58-2.94) for kidney failure, and 1.71 (95% CI, 0.70-4.16) for kidney transplantation. Estimates were less precise in the latter three groups, with wide confidence intervals. The overall interaction between income and baseline kidney state was nominally significant (likelihood ratio χ² = 26.5; 16 degrees of freedom; P = 0.047), although the state-specific estimates were imprecise (Table 3).

Among individuals with eGFR ≥60 mL/min/1.73 m², standardized 3-year mortality risks were 1.77% in the lowest income category and 0.73% in the highest, a difference of 1.04 percentage points (95% CI, 0.81-1.27). Among those with eGFR 30-59, risks were 5.09% and 3.21%, respectively, a difference of 1.88 percentage points (95% CI, 0.44-3.33). Risk differences were 7.51 percentage points (95% CI, 0.16-14.85) for eGFR <30, 2.10 percentage points (95% CI, -5.50 to 9.70) for kidney failure, and 2.20 percentage points (95% CI, -2.33 to 6.73) for kidney transplantation. None of these risk differences differed significantly from that in the eGFR ≥60 group (all P ≥0.05) (Table 3; Figure 2).

Sensitivity and exploratory analyses

The proportional hazards assumption was not met for mortality, sustained eGFR decline, or either recorded CKD outcome. However, the association between lower income and higher hazards remained during both the first 2 years and subsequent follow-up. For mortality, the HR comparing the lowest with the highest income category was 2.18 (95% CI, 1.74-2.73) during the first two years and 3.12 (95% CI, 2.55-3.81) thereafter. The corresponding HRs for sustained eGFR decline were 4.21 (95% CI, 3.42-5.18) and 2.95 (95% CI, 2.40-3.64), respectively (Supplementary Table S8).

With death treated as a competing event, the unadjusted 5-year cumulative incidence of all kidney outcomes was higher in the lowest income category than in the highest. The 5-year cumulative incidence in the lowest and highest income categories was 8.00% and 1.95%, respectively, for sustained eGFR decline; 7.49% and 3.84% for recorded CKD diagnosis; 4.33% versus 2.23% for recorded CKD category G3a or worse; and 2.32% versus 0.46% for kidney failure (Supplementary Table S7; Supplementary Figure S2).

In sensitivity analyses, censoring mortality follow-up at the last recorded EHR contact yielded an HR of 2.95 (95% CI, 2.38-3.65), whereas the primary analysis included 2,256 deaths occurring after the last EHR contact. Restricting the analysis to individuals with a numeric baseline eGFR yielded a mortality HR of 2.70 (95% CI, 2.32-3.14). When the chronicity requirements for kidney failure were removed, the number of events increased to 2,564, with an HR of 2.78 (95% CI, 2.21-3.51) (Supplementary Table S10; Supplementary Figure S4). In the pooled Cox sensitivity analysis, which assumes proportional hazards that the diagnostics rejected, differences in standardized risk differences versus eGFR ≥60 mL/min/1.73 m² were larger and their percentile intervals excluded zero for eGFR 30-59 (1.43 percentage points; 95% CI, 0.17-2.46) and kidney transplantation (3.95 percentage points; 95% CI, 0.52-6.93) at 3 years (Supplementary Table S12; Supplementary Figure S8). Exploratory subgroup analyses are shown in Supplementary Table S9 and Supplementary Figure S3, categorical associations within sex, race, and deprivation strata in Supplementary Table S11 and Supplementary Figures S5 to S7, and income associations among participants with prevalent recorded CKD in Supplementary Table S6.

Discussion

Our analysis of this large-scale observational All of Us cohort revealed that lower income was associated with higher mortality and all four kidney outcomes. In particular, income-gradient estimates were numerically larger for sustained kidney function decline and kidney failure than for a first recorded CKD diagnosis, with substantial differences also evident in the 5-year cumulative incidence of kidney outcomes. Education and neighborhood deprivation demonstrated different patterns of association, highlighting the importance of considering individual and neighborhood socioeconomic circumstances together. Interestingly, incomerelated mortality hazard ratios were numerically lower in more advanced baseline kidney states.

Income associations also varied over follow-up, with mortality hazard ratios numerically larger later and hazard ratios for sustained kidney function decline larger earlier.

Previous large-scale cohort studies have already linked socioeconomic disadvantage to kidney function decline, mortality, and kidney failure. In a nationwide Korean cohort of 7,405,715 adults with a median follow-up duration of 4.8 years, individuals in the lowest income decile had a higher risk of incident CKD than those in the sixth decile (adjusted HR, 1.30; 95% CI, 1.26-1.33) (12). The association was similar when incident CKD required both a ≥25% decline in eGFR and an eGFR below 60 mL/min/1.73 m² (adjusted HR, 1.32; 95% CI, 1.27-1.37).

Similarly, among 1,405,016 English primary-care patients followed for a median of 7.5 years, those living in the most deprived 20% of areas had higher hazards of CKD stages 4-5 and ESRD than those in the least deprived 20% (age-and sex-adjusted HRs, 1.76; 95% CI, 1.68-1.84 and 1.82; 95% CI, 1.56-2.12, respectively) (13). Furthermore, among 2,761 individuals with prevalent CKD stage 3 or 4, annual household income below $20,000 was associated with higher all-cause mortality than income of $20,000 or more (adjusted HR, 1.58; 95% CI, 1.24-2.00) (14).

Our study further extends prior findings in a large, ethnically diverse U.S. cohort with linked longitudinal electronic health records by jointly examining income, education, and neighborhood deprivation in relation to sustained kidney function decline, recorded CKD, kidney failure, and mortality, while quantifying 5-year kidney-event incidence across income groups and evaluating mortality inequalities across baseline kidney states.

Importantly, the income gradients were accompanied by substantial differences in 5-year kidney outcomes. At 5 years, estimated cumulative incidence of sustained kidney function decline was 8.00% in the lowest-income group, compared with 1.95% in the highest-income group. The corresponding estimates for kidney failure were 2.32% and 0.46%. These findings are consistent with previous U.S. studies showing greater burden of kidney failure among individuals with lower incomes and in counties with greater poverty. Among 79,943 individuals in the Southern Community Cohort Study, annual household income below $15,000 was associated with a higher risk of kidney failure than income of $15,000 or more (adjusted HR, 1.5; 95% CI, 1.2-1.8) (15).

Similarly, a national U.S. analysis also found that the excess incidence of kidney failure in highpoverty compared with low-poverty counties increased from 42.8 cases per million in 2000-2005 to 100.1 cases per million in 2012-2017, despite an overall national decline in kidney failure incidence (16). Our study extends these reports on kidney failure by also quantifying income inequalities in sustained kidney function decline confirmed by repeated measurements.

Incorporating all income categories demonstrated that sustained kidney function decline had numerically the largest relative and absolute income inequalities (RII, 6.84; SII, 16.98 events per 1,000 person-years). Furthermore, education and neighborhood deprivation demonstrated different patterns of associations across outcomes. Lower education was associated with higher risks of mortality and sustained kidney function decline. On the other hand, greater neighborhood deprivation was associated with adverse kidney outcomes despite demonstrating no clear associations with mortality. These findings highlight the importance of considering both individual and neighborhood socioeconomic circumstances when examining inequalities across kidney outcomes. Among 5,095 individuals in the German CKD cohort with a follow-up duration of 6.5 years, lower education was associated with higher mortality (age-and sex-adjusted HR, 1.48; 95% CI, 1.16-1.90) and kidney failure requiring dialysis (HR, 1.54; 95% CI, 1.15-2.05) compared with higher education (17). Nevertheless, these associations were no longer statistically significant after further adjustment for kidney disease severity and potential mediators, including income. In the Cardiovascular Health Study of 4,735 individuals aged 65 years or older, those living in neighborhoods with the lowest socioeconomic status had a 50% higher risk of progressive CKD compared to those in the highest socioeconomic quartile, even after adjustment for demographic characteristics, baseline creatinine, and individual-level socioeconomic factors (18). This association persisted after further adjustment for lifestyle factors, diabetes, and hypertension, whereas individual income and education were not independently associated with progressive CKD after accounting for the other SES measures. In our broader large-scale U.S. adult cohort, lower education remained associated with mortality and sustained kidney function decline after accounting for income and neighborhood deprivation.

These findings suggest that income alone may not fully capture socioeconomic inequalities across kidney outcomes.

Moreover, relative income associations with mortality were numerically smaller among individuals with more advanced kidney disease. Specifically, the adjusted mortality hazard ratio comparing the lowest-with the highest-income group was 2.73 (95% CI, 2.28-3.26) among participants with baseline eGFR ≥60 mL/min/1.73 m², compared with 1.24 (95% CI, 0.50-3.05) among those with eGFR <30 mL/min/1.73 m². Nevertheless, evidence of heterogeneity across kidney-function strata was weak, and estimates among participants with advanced kidney dysfunction were imprecise. Furthermore, the standardized 3-year analysis did not establish differences in absolute mortality gaps across states, although the intervals were wide and the pooled Cox sensitivity analysis, which depends on a rejected proportionality assumption, suggested larger gaps in two states. A similar numerical pattern was reported in a Grampian population study. The adjusted mortality hazard ratio comparing residents of the most deprived areas with those in all other areas was 1.21 (95% CI, 1.14-1.28) among people presenting with eGFR <60 mL/min/1.73 m² and 1.11 (95% CI, 1.04-1.19) among those presenting with eGFR <30 mL/min/1.73 m² (3). Our study complements this evidence by estimating standardized absolute differences in three-year mortality between income groups across baseline kidney states.

Income associations also varied over follow-up. Comparing the lowest-with the highest-income group, the adjusted hazard ratio for mortality was 2.18 during the first two years of follow-up and 3.12 thereafter. The corresponding hazard ratios for sustained kidney function decline were 4.21 and 2.95. Although the strengths of these associations varied over time, income-related inequalities remained evident in both early and later follow-up. These findings suggest that a single overall hazard ratio may obscure changes in the magnitude of income associations during follow-up. Differences in participants' health at baseline or their use of healthcare potentially could explain these patterns; nevertheless, we did not test those explanations. Future studies could potentially clarify why the associations varied over time.

Although our large-scale observational cohort study provides valuable insights into socioeconomic inequalities across kidney outcomes and mortality, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, given the observational nature of the study, the observed associations should not be interpreted as causal relationships. Second, socioeconomic circumstances were assessed only at baseline, so subsequent income changes were not captured, while neighborhood deprivation measured using three-digit ZIP codes may obscure local differences. Third, incomplete EHR coverage and differences in testing and coding may have affected outcome detection. Incident CKD was identified from diagnosis records and a laboratory definition based on repeated low eGFR values was not applied; recorded creatinine units were not verified; and deaths near the end of the data period may be incompletely captured because of reporting lag.

Some creatinine measurements used to assess baseline kidney function were obtained after the socioeconomic survey, creating uncertainty about kidney status at the time of income assessment. Fourth, only 218,098 of 481,849 otherwise eligible participants had a determined kidney state and complete socioeconomic responses, so selection related to income, illness, and healthcare use is possible. Finally, as the All of Us Research Program is a volunteer-based cohort, participants may differ from the general population, potentially limiting the generalizability of our findings. Therefore, further studies with repeated socioeconomic assessments, more detailed neighborhood information, and standardized kidney function measurements are needed to clarify these associations and identify potential ways to reduce socioeconomic inequalities in kidney health.
