How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. Abstract

Background: Heterogeneity in eligibility criteria, intervention content, and exercise-progression rules has limited the reproducibility of research on upper crossed syndrome. This trial will examine the added effect of a structured, individualized, staged corrective-exercise program on adults who meet prespecified criteria for this phenotype. Methods: In this parallel randomized controlled trial, 36 women and men aged 18-40 years with forward head and shoulder posture and increased thoracic kyphosis will be allocated 1:1, stratified by sex. Over 12 weeks, both groups will attend three sessions per week and receive stationary cycling, indication-based stretching and mobilization, and education on healthy movement habits. The intervention group will additionally receive a systems-informed program encompassing restoration of range of motion, muscleactivation retraining, correction of movement patterns, progressive load tolerance, and preparation for health-enhancing physical activity. The primary outcome is the baseline-adjusted week-12 between-group difference in a multivariate profile comprising the forward-head, forward-shoulder, and thoracic-kyphosis angles. Scapular-muscle electromyography, range of motion, and balance are secondary outcomes. The primary exploratory electroencephalographic outcome is change in central relative alpha power during quiet standing with eyes open. Posture, range of motion, and balance will be assessed at baseline, week 12, and four-week follow-up; electromyography and electroencephalography at baseline and week 12. The primary analysis will use MANCOVA adjusted for baseline and sex, with Pillai's trace; longitudinal analyses will use linear mixed models. Effect estimates and 95% confidence intervals will be reported. Conclusions: This study will test the added effect of a reproducible corrective-exercise framework beyond the shared active-control components. The four-week follow-up will not support inference about long-term prevention of recurrence. Ethics and trial registration: Ethics approval: IR.UI.REC.1404.053. Introduction

Upper crossed syndrome (UCS) refers to a pattern of postural and neuromuscular changes involving the head, thoracic spine, and shoulder girdle, with forward head posture, forward or rounded shoulders, and increased thoracic kyphosis among its most prominent features. These alterations may occur separately or in different combinations, and previous studies have not used consistent angular thresholds or eligibility criteria to define UCS [1] [2] [3]. To avoid making diagnostic claims beyond the available evidence, the present study operationalizes UCS as a postural phenotype requiring the concurrent presence of forward head posture, forward shoulder posture, and increased thoracic kyphosis. This definition is intended to increase sample homogeneity and permit evaluation of a combined postural pattern; it is not equivalent to a validated diagnostic criterion.

Conventional corrective-exercise programs have largely been based on the muscle-imbalance model and have used stretching, strengthening, scapular-stabilization exercises, and postural education [2] [3] [4] [5] [6] [7]. A 2024 meta-analysis indicated that therapeutic exercise could improve the three postural components associated with UCS, although substantial heterogeneity existed among studies [2]. A 2026 meta-analysis of 28 trials and 901 participants likewise reported large effects on forward head posture, forward shoulder posture, and thoracic kyphosis, but between-study heterogeneity ranged from 78% to 88% [3]. Pooled findings for muscle activity, balance, range of motion, function, and pain remain uncertain or inconsistent. Thus, the unresolved question is not simply whether exercise can change postural angles, but whether a prespecified and structured framework can improve all three components concurrently and whether these changes are accompanied by neuromuscular responses and maintained after the intervention [2, 3]. Although the muscle-imbalance model offers an understandable basis for exercise selection, postural control cannot be reduced to the length, strength, or activity of a small number of muscles [5, 6, 8]. In this study, a systems perspective provides the theoretical basis for explaining postural and movement control. From this perspective, postural control emerges through continuous interactions among sensory information, central nervous system processing, neuromuscular responses, the body's mechanical capacities, and the demands of the task and environment, and it adapts to individual and environmental conditions through feedback [9] [10] [11].

Drawing on this foundation and on components used in previous comprehensive interventions [6] [7] [8] [9] [10] [11] [12] [13], the investigators developed a structured, staged, and individualized framework for the present trial. The intervention is not confined to correcting a single muscle, joint, or postural angle. Instead, modifiable factors within the scope of the intervention are organized into overlapping stages that begin with breathing retraining and trunk control, restoration of mobility, regulation of muscle activation and coordination, and control of movement patterns, and progress toward load tolerance, health-related physical fitness, self-management, and sustained participation in physical activity. Transitions between stages are governed by prespecified criteria relating to individual capacity, movement quality, compensatory movement, and response to loading rather than by elapsed time alone [6, 7, [9] [10] [11].

Accordingly, the novelty of the intervention does not lie in an entirely new exercise or technique, but in how established components are organized, progressed, and transferred [2, 3, 5, 13]. Goals and decision rules are standardized in advance, whereas exercise level is adjusted according to individual capacity, range of motion, execution quality, and compensatory movement. Standardization therefore refers to the use of a shared decisionmaking framework rather than an identical prescription for every participant. The gradual transition from simple, low-load positions to weight-bearing and daily activities is intended to enhance transfer of learning [14, 15].

Findings concerning shoulder-girdle muscle activity reinforce the need to distinguish postural change from neuromuscular response [3, 6, 7]. Some trials have reported increased serratus anterior activity, reduced upper trapezius activity, or improved scapular-muscle ratios, whereas the available meta-analysis did not confirm consistent changes in selected muscles [3]. Surface electromyography (EMG) is therefore included as an objective secondary outcome, and any change will not be considered definitive evidence of the mechanism of effect.

The contribution of the central nervous system to postural control provides the theoretical rationale for the electroencephalographic (EEG) assessment [9, 11, [16] [17] [18]. Studies of standing and balance learning have reported changes in sensorimotor activity and cortical oscillations, but findings depend on the task, population, and analytical method [16] [17] [18]. One study reported EEG differences between forward and neutral head postures [17]; however, an acute change in head position is not equivalent to a UCS-specific brain pattern [18]. Because no UCS-specific EEG biomarker has been established, central relative alpha power at C3, Cz, and C4 during quiet standing with eyes open was prespecified as a single exploratory outcome, based on the sensorimotor relevance of these electrode locations, reported associations between alpha oscillations and postural control, and the lower susceptibility of quiet standing to movement artifacts than more demanding postural tasks. Restricting the analysis to one prespecified band-region-condition combination also reduces multiplicity.

EEG is therefore included solely as an exploratory, hypothesis-generating outcome and will not be used as a diagnostic criterion or primary effectiveness outcome. In the absence of source localization, sensor-level findings will not be attributed to specific cortical structures or interpreted as definitive evidence of neural reorganization.

The primary objective of this trial is to determine the added effect of a structured, staged corrective program, compared with a foundational active intervention comprising cycling, stretching, mobilization, and education on healthy movement habits, on the postural profile of adults with the operational UCS phenotype. Study design and setting

This is a two-arm, parallel-group randomized controlled trial with 1:1 allocation. It is designed to estimate the added effect of a 12-week structured, individualized, staged corrective-exercise program relative to a foundational active intervention. Individualization and progression

Exercise variants will be selected using prespecified criteria for alignment, continuous breathing, control of the target region, pain, compensatory movement, and cue dependence.

Progression requires at least eight correct repetitions or breathing cycles in every prescribed set; if these criteria are not met, the exercise will be modified or regressed. All decisions and their rationales will be documented. The complete selection, progression, regression, and manual-technique algorithm is provided in S4 File.

Progression will move from low-load positions to sitting, standing, weight-bearing, and functional tasks, with task-specific adaptations when relevant [12, 13, 28].

Feedback

Feedback will initially be explicit and multimodal and will be progressively faded as performance improves; the complete feedback hierarchy is provided in S4 File [12, 13, 28].

Recumbent stationary cycling

The warm-up will use a SEG BG-8229 recumbent stationary bicycle manufactured in Taiwan.

In the initial sessions, the objectives will be to learn appropriate sitting posture, lower-limb alignment, and pedaling technique, followed by integration of breathing coordination. The bicycle has resistance settings from 1 to 20. The program will begin with alternating one minute at level 1 and one minute at level 2 for 10 minutes. The functional target is to cover 4 km in 10 minutes with appropriate technique, without overt compensation, and while retaining a perceived capacity to continue. Once this target is achieved consistently, the intervals will progress to levels 2 and 3 and subsequently to higher levels. Progression will be based on distance, the 0-10 Borg rating of perceived exertion, breathing, and movement quality rather than solely on the resistance setting.

Stretching and manual techniques

Indication-based stretching and joint or soft-tissue techniques will be available to both groups.

A static stretch will be held once per target muscle for 30 s when a restriction is present and for 20 s after the target range has been attained. When indicated, mobilization or soft-tissue techniques will be delivered as one set of 10 repetitions followed by immediate reassessment.

The target, dose, response, and any adverse event will be documented; full procedures are provided in S4 File [29] [30] [31].

Adherence and intervention fidelity

Attendance, exercises, sets, repetitions, resistance, modifications, feedback, manual techniques, and adverse events will be recorded at every session. Attendance adherence in both arms is defined as participation in at least 33 of the 36 sessions. This criterion reflects adherence to scheduled visits only and does not imply equal contact time or intervention dose between groups. Actual session duration, cumulative contact time with the provider, and the proportion of planned arm-specific components received will be recorded and reported separately. The per-protocol population will include participants who attend at least 33 sessions, receive at least 80% of the planned dose for their allocated arm, and have no major protocol deviation or prohibited concurrent intervention. Permitted, documented modifications made within the individualized framework will not be considered deviations. The threshold will be identical for women and men; menstrual symptoms may justify rescheduling or temporary modification. When a session is missed, a replacement time will be offered while preserving session order. Each month, a random 10% sample of intervention records will be audited by a researcher not directly involved in intervention delivery, and intervention details will be documented in accordance with TIDieR-Rehab and CERT [22, 23].

Active comparator and concomitant interventions

Participants in the active-comparator group will attend the same total of 36 sessions over 12 weeks and receive the general components shared by both groups: 10 minutes of stationary cycling, stretching of target muscles, prespecified mobilizations, and education on healthy movement habits. Each comparator session will last approximately 20-30 minutes. The type, sequence, dose, and delivery of shared components will be the same as the corresponding components in the intervention group. Education will address reducing prolonged time in static postures, increasing variability in daily movement, taking regular movement breaks, and applying basic postural principles during occupational and daily activities.

The active-comparator group will not receive the specific main component of the intervention: exercises based on the structured, staged model; retraining of muscle activation and coordination; training of basic and complex movement patterns; individualized progression of load and task; or the specific transition program toward health-related physical fitness. Attendance, actual contact time, components delivered, stretching and mobilization . dose, educational content, and interventions received outside the study will be recorded in both groups.

The comparator was selected to control for the shared foundational components, number of visits, and exposure to the treatment environment, but it will not fully equalize providercontact time or the volume of supervised activity. Equalizing time would have required sham activity, prolonged education, or general exercise whose absence of effects on posture, balance, physical activity, and participant expectations could not be guaranteed and could have changed the study question into a comparison of two exercise programs. The betweengroup contrast will therefore be interpreted as the added effect of the complete specific component of the systems-informed approach under pragmatic delivery conditions, not as the isolated effect of its exercise content independent of contact time, provider attention, or supervised-activity dose.

Participants in both groups will be asked to continue their usual activities and not begin a new structured exercise program. Sessions will be delivered individually or in groups of no more than three participants, with one provider for every three participants. Actual dose, durations of session components, completed sets, prolonged rest periods, and non-exercise interruptions will be recorded on the intervention-fidelity form.

The forward-head and forward-shoulder angles will be defined as the angles formed by the C7-tragus and C7acromion lines, respectively, relative to the vertical reference. Angles will be measured using AutoCAD 2025, and the average of the three images will be used for analysis. The dominant side will be determined before baseline and used consistently across all assessments [7, 24].

The flexible ruler will be used only to determine whether the kyphosis threshold is met at screening because it is practical, inexpensive, and has acceptable reliability and validity. After C7 and T12 have been marked, the thoracic curve will be traced onto paper. In the equation θ=4arctan(2H/L), L is the straight-line distance from C7 to T12, and H is the maximum perpendicular distance from the curve to line L. At baseline, week 12, and follow-up, the . kyphotic angle (ICT-ITL) will be measured with DIERS formetric 4D and DICAM 3 software. The flexible ruler and Formetric serve different purposes, and their values will not be treated as directly interchangeable. Eligibility will be determined solely by screening; the Formetric result will not be used to reclassify participants. Longitudinal change will be assessed only with the same Formetric output. At each assessment, six scans will be acquired, with the participant stepping out of and then returning to the standardized position between scans, and their mean will be reported [32].

Surface electromyography

Activity of the upper, middle, and lower trapezius and serratus anterior on the dominant side will be recorded using an eight-channel wireless Desktop DTS system (Noraxon USA Inc., Scottsdale, AZ, USA) and acquired using MyoResearch 3 (MR3) software. Four channels will record the target muscles, and a fifth channel placed over the middle deltoid will provide a temporal reference for movement onset. When required, the skin will be shaved, gently abraded, and cleaned with 70% alcohol. Disposable bipolar Ag/AgCl electrodes will be placed with a center-to-center distance of 20 mm, parallel to the muscle fibers and in accordance with SENIAM recommendations. Locations will be documented by measurement from anatomical landmarks and with a non-identifiable photograph [33, 34].

Before recording, the resting signal and a brief contraction will be inspected. Raw signals will be processed using a fourth-order, zero-phase Butterworth band-pass filter from 20 to 450 Hz.

A 50-Hz notch filter will be applied and documented only if persistent contamination remains after electrode connections have been corrected. After full-wave rectification, an RMS envelope will be calculated using a 50-ms moving window. Processing settings will remain constant across participants and time points.

. For normalization, four maximum voluntary isometric contraction (MVIC) positions will be tested: a unilateral shoulder shrug in sitting; prone horizontal shoulder abduction with external rotation at 90 degrees of abduction; prone arm elevation along the line of the lower trapezius fibers at approximately 135 degrees of abduction; and resisted elevation in the scapular plane at 120-125 degrees. Each position will be performed three times for five seconds, with 60 seconds of rest between repetitions and two minutes between positions. Position order will be randomized, and resistance, stabilization, and instructions will be standardized. For each muscle, the highest stable one-second mean across all valid contractions will be used as the MVIC reference [33].

After five familiarization repetitions, participants will complete three recorded repetitions of active arm elevation in the scapular plane without external resistance and through their maximal pain-free range: a three-second raising phase, a one-second hold, and a three-second lowering phase, paced by a metronome, with 60 seconds of rest between repetitions. The terminal angle recorded at baseline will be reproduced at week 12. Synchronized video and an event marker will be used to verify timing and identify compensation. The principal amplitude metric will be the mean normalized RMS for the entire seven-second task, averaged across the three valid repetitions. Phase-specific values will be supportive analyses.

The UT/MT, UT/LT, and UT/SA ratios will be calculated from the normalized amplitude over the full task.

For onset timing, the baseline mean and standard deviation will be calculated from the 500-ms window preceding movement onset. Muscle onset will be defined as the first point at which the signal envelope exceeds the baseline mean plus three standard deviations for at least 25 ms. Middle-deltoid onset will define time zero. A blinded analyst will verify detections using the raw signal, envelope, and synchronized video; if no reliable onset can be identified, the value will be treated as missing [7, 35]. The normalized amplitudes of the four muscles and the three muscle ratios will form two principal EMG families. Phase-specific data and onset timing will be supportive analyses.

Range of motion

Active flexion and external rotation of the dominant shoulder and active cervical flexion will be measured with a 360-degree universal goniometer with one-degree resolution [36]. For shoulder flexion, the participant will lie supine with the elbow extended and forearm neutral while the trunk and scapula are stabilized. The fulcrum will be positioned over the lateral aspect of the greater tubercle, the stationary arm parallel to the lateral midline of the thorax, and the moving arm along the humerus toward the lateral epicondyle.

Shoulder external rotation will be assessed in supine with the shoulder abducted to 90 degrees, the elbow flexed to 90 degrees, and the forearm neutral. A towel of standardized thickness will be placed beneath the humerus, and the scapula will be stabilized. The fulcrum will be positioned over the olecranon, the stationary arm perpendicular to the floor, and the moving arm along the dorsal surface of the ulna toward the ulnar styloid process.

Active cervical flexion will be assessed in sitting with the feet on the floor and the thoracic region in contact with the backrest. The fulcrum will be positioned over C7, the stationary arm vertical, and the moving arm along the line from C7 to the earlobe. The participant will bring the chin toward the chest without compensatory thoracic flexion. End range will be defined by the first compensatory movement, end-range resistance, or pain. Each movement will be performed three times with consistent instructions and speed, and the mean of the three measurements will be recorded [37].

Static and dynamic balance

Static balance will be measured using DIERS pedoscan during the Romberg test with eyes open and eyes closed. The participant will stand barefoot with the feet in a comfortable, recorded position, arms by the sides, and gaze directed at a target at eye level. Three 35second trials will be performed in each condition, with 60 seconds of rest between trials. The first five seconds will be discarded and the subsequent 30 seconds analyzed [38]. The mean of the three valid trials will be reported. Mean center-of-pressure velocity is the key measure; path length, sway area, and anteroposterior and mediolateral excursion are supportive measures. Dynamic balance will be assessed using the Lower Quarter Y-Balance Test on the dominant limb. The dominant leg will be identified using the ball-kick test and will remain the assessed limb at every time point. Limb length will be measured from the anterior superior iliac spine to the medial malleolus. After six familiarization attempts in each direction, three valid trials will be recorded in the anterior, posteromedial, and posterolateral directions. Lifting the stance foot; leaning on or kicking the reach indicator; touching the floor with the reaching foot; or failing to return under control will invalidate the trial. Participants will rest for at least 10 seconds between trials and 30 seconds between directions. The greatest valid reach in each direction will be divided by limb length and multiplied by 100. The composite score will be the sum of the maximal reach distances in the three directions divided by three times limb length [39]. Results pertain only to performance of the dominant limb and will not be used to infer bilateral symmetry.

Electroencephalography

EEG will be recorded using a portable EEG 3840 system (NR Sign [Negar Andishgan], Iran) with 24 channels and a sampling rate of 500 Hz. Nineteen scalp electrodes will be placed according to the international 10-20 system, with A1/A2 as references and horizontal and vertical electrooculography channels [40, 41]. Electrode impedance will be maintained below 10 kΩ. After a five-minute adaptation period, recordings will be obtained during eyes-open sitting, eyes-closed sitting, eyes-open standing, and an eyes-open modified Matthiass test. Each condition will comprise three 60-second trials separated by 60 seconds of rest. Gaze instructions, target distance, foot and hand positions, and the procedure for marking the start and end of each trial will be standardized, and events will be marked in both the EEG file and synchronized video.

Preprocessing will be performed in EEGLAB 2026.0.0 using locked versions of clean_rawdata and ICLabel [41] [42] [43]. Flat-line segments will first be identified, followed by zero-phase band-pass filtering from 1 to 40 Hz. Bad-channel criteria will be a flat line longer than five seconds, correlation below 0.80 with a robust estimate based on the remaining channels, or line noise more than four standard deviations above the distribution. If more than 20% of scalp channels are classified as bad, that condition will be treated as missing.

Otherwise, bad channels will be removed temporarily before independent component analysis (ICA), the data will be re-referenced to the average of the remaining channels, and the four conditions will be concatenated for extended Infomax ICA. Data rank will be calculated before ICA, and the number of components will be restricted to the effective rank.

Components classified by ICLabel as ocular or muscular with a probability of at least 0.80 will be removed only after confirmation from their spatial pattern, time course, and EOG channels. Removed channels will subsequently be reconstructed by spherical interpolation, and all decisions will be documented. The signal will be divided into non-overlapping two-second epochs. An epoch will be rejected if the amplitude of any channel exceeds ±100 μV or synchronized video shows overt movement. At least 60 acceptable epochs, equivalent to 120 seconds, will be required for each condition. Power spectral density will be calculated using Welch's method with a one-second Hamming window and 50% overlap. Relative power will be calculated for delta (1 to <4 Hz), theta (4 to <8 Hz), alpha (8 to <13 Hz), and beta (13-30 Hz), relative to total power from 1 to 30 Hz. Regions will comprise frontal (F3/Fz/F4), central (C3/Cz/C4), and parietal (P3/Pz/P4) electrodes.

The primary exploratory EEG outcome is the change from baseline to week 12 in central relative alpha power during quiet standing with eyes open. Other conditions, regions, and frequency bands will be supplementary exploratory analyses. A sensitivity analysis will exclude recordings in which any central electrode was interpolated. The numbers of rejected channels, components, and epochs will be reported. Interpretation will focus on effect estimates, confidence intervals, and consistency of patterns; statistical significance alone will not be considered evidence of neural reorganization.

For the modified Matthiass test, the participant will stand barefoot with the feet parallel and hip-width apart, elevate both arms to 90 degrees of shoulder flexion with elbows extended and palms facing downward, and maintain the position for 60 seconds. Recording will begin after the arms reach 90 degrees. A step, displacement of either foot, contact with a support, or lowering of the arms will invalidate the trial, which will be repeated after rest [44].

Sample-size justification

The joint covariance matrix of the three postural components and sufficient information on the correlations between baseline and week-12 values have not been published for this population. Consequently, the parameters required to calculate sample size directly for the planned MANCOVA on the basis of a prespecified clinically important effect were unavailable. The recruitment target was therefore determined before access to outcome data . on the basis of resource constraints, operational feasibility, and the requirements for maintaining intervention fidelity.

The intervention is individualized, and during each session the provider must evaluate and document the quality of movement-pattern execution, compensatory movements, response to load, need for exercise selection, regression, or progression, and use of manual techniques for every participant. To preserve adequate opportunity for observation, feedback, and exercise modification, no more than three participants will be supervised concurrently by one provider, resulting in a provider-to-participant ratio of 1:3. With six delivery slots per day, a maximum of 18 participants can receive the intervention in groups of three over the course of a day.

After randomization, each study arm will be assigned three non-consecutive days per week.

The center can therefore provide all scheduled sessions to 36 participants, 18 per group, over the 12-week period.

Recruiting more participants would require increasing the number supervised concurrently, reducing the time devoted to observation and feedback, or increasing staffing and operating hours, any of which could weaken intervention fidelity. The fixed study period also precluded successive cohorts. Thus, the operational capacity of one cohort defined the prespecified ceiling, and the sample of 36 was not selected solely on the basis of sample sizes in earlier studies. Justification of sample size by resource constraints is methodologically acceptable when the study's informational value and inferential limitations are reported transparently [45, 46].

To describe the statistical sensitivity of this fixed sample size, an analysis based on the approximation of a two-group MANOVA with three dependent variables and an alpha level of 0.05 was conducted before access to outcome data. If evaluable data are available for all 36 participants, the design provides 80% power to detect a large multivariate effect of . approximately f²(V) = 0.34, corresponding to Pillai's V ≈ 0.25. If 29 evaluable participants remain after approximately 20% attrition or unusable data, the effect detectable with 80% power is approximately f²(V) = 0.44, corresponding to Pillai's V ≈ 0.31. These are statistical sensitivity thresholds and should not be interpreted as expected effect sizes or thresholds of clinical importance. Because the covariance matrix and baseline-outcome correlations are unavailable, these calculations are only approximations for the planned MANCOVA.

The large effects reported by Seidi et al. are considered only contextual evidence that comprehensive corrective interventions may produce large responses. Owing to differences in the primary outcome and effect-size metric, those results were not used as the direct basis for the present sample size [7]. This study is not powered to detect small or moderate multivariate effects, perform subgroup analyses, or draw definitive conclusions regarding secondary outcomes or EEG. All results will be accompanied by effect estimates and 95% confidence intervals to show the range of values compatible with the observed data. Restricting the primary outcome to one omnibus multivariate test also addresses multiplicity in the primary analysis, but does not substitute for adequate statistical power. Effect estimates, the covariance structure, and their uncertainty from this study may inform more precise samplesize planning for subsequent trials.

Statistical analysis

Analyses will be conducted in R according to the intention-to-treat principle. The R version, statistical packages, and analysis code will be documented and archived in the statistical analysis plan before the group code is broken. Baseline characteristics will be summarized using appropriate descriptive statistics, without significance testing for baseline betweengroup differences. All tests will be two-sided, and alpha for the primary test will be 0.05. Sex will be included in the models because it is used for stratified randomization; no subgroup analysis or intervention-by-sex interaction test will be performed.

The primary estimand is the adjusted difference between the systems-informed intervention group and the active-comparator group in the three-variable postural profile at week 12. This contrast estimates the added effect of the specific staged component of the systems-informed intervention beyond the shared components of stationary cycling, stretching, mobilization, and general education on movement habits. The three week-12 angles will be entered as dependent variables in a MANCOVA, with group and sex as fixed factors and the baseline values of the same three angles as covariates. The omnibus test will use Pillai's trace. A significant omnibus test does not by itself indicate improvement in all three components. Only if the omnibus test is significant will three outcome-specific ANCOVAs be performed with the same adjustment variables, with P values corrected using the Holm method. Concurrent improvement will be claimed only if all three effect estimates favor correction and all three component tests remain significant after Holm correction. If assumptions are seriously violated, a permutation-based multivariate analysis that preserves the covariates and restrictions arising from stratified randomization will be performed as a sensitivity analysis.

. Longitudinal analyses of posture, range of motion, and balance will use constrained linear mixed models. Baseline, week 12, and follow-up measurements will be entered as repeated responses. Categorical time, sex, and group-by-time interactions for week 12 and follow-up will be fixed effects; omission of the group main effect will constrain baseline group means to equality. A random intercept will be included for each participant. Models will be estimated using restricted maximum likelihood, with Satterthwaite degrees of freedom. Prespecified contrasts are the between-group differences at week 12 and follow-up. Week-12 postural contrasts from these models are supportive of the primary analysis; primary component-level inference will follow the hierarchical MANCOVA-ANCOVA procedure. The three postural contrasts at follow-up will constitute one family and will be Holm-adjusted. At each postbaseline time point, the three range-of-motion outcomes will form one family, and mean center-of-pressure velocity and the Y-Balance composite score will form a second family; Holm correction will be applied within each family. Other center-of-pressure measures and direction-specific Y-Balance scores will be supportive analyses.

Week-12 EMG outcomes will be compared using ANCOVA adjusted for baseline values and sex. The normalized amplitudes of the four muscles and the three muscle ratios will form two separate families, with Holm correction applied within each family. Skewed ratios will be transformed using the natural logarithm, and results will be back-transformed and reported as ratios of geometric means with 95% confidence intervals. Phase-specific values and onset timing are supportive analyses. The primary exploratory EEG outcome will be analyzed using ANCOVA adjusted for baseline values and sex. All other condition-by-region-by-band combinations will form one exploratory family, for which the false-discovery rate will be controlled at 5% using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure.

For individual outcomes, adjusted mean differences, 95% confidence intervals, and Hedges' g will be reported. Hedges' g will be calculated from the adjusted between-group difference and pooled baseline standard deviation. If primary-outcome data are missing, multiple imputations by chained equations will be performed with at least 50 imputed datasets. The imputation model will include group, sex, postural values at baseline and post-baseline time points, and a parsimonious set of prespecified auxiliary variables. Predictive mean matching will be used for continuous variables. In each imputed dataset, a full multivariate model and a reduced model without the group effect will be fitted, and the omnibus group effect will be pooled using a multivariate Wald test with the D1 method. Estimates for individual outcomes will be pooled using Rubin's rules. Pillai's V will be reported in the complete-case analysis.

Complete-case and per-protocol analyses will be presented as sensitivity analyses. The perprotocol population will comprise participants who attend at least 33 of 36 sessions, receive at least 80% of the planned dose for their assigned arm, and have no major protocol deviation, including initiation of a structured exercise program outside the study. Permitted and documented modifications within the individualized intervention rules will not be considered protocol deviations. Because session duration and content differ between the two arms, dose adherence will be calculated relative to the planned program for the assigned arm; cumulative contact time, actual session duration, and percentage of planned dose received will also be reported separately by group. If more than 10% of primary-outcome data are missing, a sensitivity analysis incorporating departures from the missing-at-random assumption will be performed. Last observation carried forward and an interim efficacy analysis will not be used.

Safety, adverse events, and insurance

Before each session, participants will be asked about pain, new injury, and changes in medical status. Potential adverse events include transient muscle soreness, subacute symptom aggravation, tenderness or bruising after soft-tissue release, and abdominal discomfort from contact with the exercise ball. Pain intensity will be recorded on a 0-10 scale. Pain greater . than 5 or pain that prevents correct execution will prompt cessation of the exercise, assessment of load, range, and involved tissue, and appropriate modification. Persistent pain, neurological symptoms, unusual dyspnea, dizziness, or another warning sign will result in termination of the session and medical referral. The intervention will be discontinued when medically indicated or when risk persists, but a single report of pain will not by itself lead to exclusion from the study. All adverse events will be documented with their severity, possible relationship to the intervention, and outcome [22]. Sports insurance will remain valid for all participants from before formal assessment until completion of follow-up, and the intervention center holds public-liability insurance. Any incident will be reported within the required period. Reimbursement will be governed by the terms of the insurance policy, and complete coverage of all expenses cannot be guaranteed.

Trial monitoring and stopping rules

Given the low-risk intervention, limited sample size, short study duration, and absence of an interim efficacy analysis, a formal data monitoring committee will not be established. An independent trial-safety and conduct monitor will nevertheless be appointed before recruitment begins. This person will have no role in intervention design, recruitment, exercise delivery, outcome assessment, data analysis, or manuscript preparation, and will have no financial, employment, or professional dependency relationship with the principal investigator, the intervention clinic, or the funder.

The independent monitor will review coded reports on recruitment and informed consent, adverse events, withdrawals, medical referrals, major protocol deviations, and completeness of safety records after the first six participants have enrolled and monthly thereafter. Efficacy results will not be made available to the monitor, and group allocation will be revealed only if required to evaluate the safety of an adverse event. Any serious adverse event potentially . related to the intervention will be reported to the monitor within 24 hours, and recruitment and intervention delivery will be suspended temporarily pending initial review. Two unexpected moderate or severe adverse events with a similar pattern and a possible relationship to one intervention component will likewise result in temporary suspension and review of that component.

The independent monitor may recommend temporary suspension of recruitment or intervention delivery, protocol modification, medical referral, or immediate reporting to the ethics committee. A temporary safety suspension will be implemented immediately.

Resumption, modification, or termination of the study will be decided after the monitor's review and ethics-committee approval. Monitor reports will be sent directly to the ethics committee and research team and archived until study completion. The study may also be suspended because of major disruption in access to the center, security conditions, prolonged equipment failure, or an ethics-committee directive.

Discussion

Available evidence indicates that corrective exercise can improve postural alignment in people with UCS, but heterogeneity in eligibility criteria, intervention components, and exercise-progression rules limits interpretation and reproducibility [2] [3] [4] [5]. By requiring the concurrent presence of three postural components and defining a multivariate primary outcome, the present study will evaluate the response of the combined pattern. This phenotype is an operational research definition and not a validated diagnostic criterion for UCS. The program's innovation does not lie in a single new exercise, but in organizing established components within the criterion-based staged model, applying explicit selection and progression rules, and documenting provider decisions. This structure permits individualization within a shared framework and is consistent with recommendations for reporting complex and rehabilitation interventions [22, 23, 47, 48]. EMG, range of motion, and balance are assessed to describe whether postural change is accompanied by neuromuscular and functional responses, not to prove a mechanism. EEG has . likewise been restricted to one primary exploratory outcome; a positive finding will therefore be hypothesis-generating and will not be sufficient to claim neural reorganization.

Strengths and limitations

A distinctive feature of this study is that corrective exercise is treated as one stage within a clinical continuum rather than as an intervention that ends when postural angles change.

Restoration of range of motion, retraining of muscle activation, and control of movement patterns are intended to provide a foundation for progression to load tolerance, health-related physical fitness, self-management, and more sustained participation in physical activity. This approach assumes that physical inactivity, prolonged static postures, and repeated exposure to monotonous movement patterns may contribute to the persistence of unfavorable movement strategies Methodological strengths include the prespecified phenotype definition, multivariate primary outcome, stratified randomization, allocation concealment, blinding of assessors and analysts, objective measurement tools, and explicit separation of confirmatory, secondary, and exploratory analyses. Conversely, participants and the intervention provider cannot be blinded; the small sample is expected to be adequate only for large omnibus effects; and the . single-center setting, age range of 18-40 years, and restriction to participants with BMI in the normal range limit generalizability.

Use of an active comparator permits estimation of the added effect of the specific progressive component beyond stationary cycling, stretching, mobilization, and general education, and provides a more conservative test than comparison with no intervention. However, providercontact time and the volume of supervised exercise are greater in the intervention group.

Between-group differences may therefore reflect the combined effects of the specific intervention content, a greater exercise dose, provider attention, individualized feedback, and participant expectations; the independent contributions of these factors cannot be determined.

Blinding of assessors and analysts, objective outcomes, and documentation of actual contact time may reduce some sources of bias but do not eliminate this design limitation. Findings should therefore not be interpreted as the specific effect of the systems-informed approach independent of time and provider attention, or as the effect of the entire package compared with no treatment. Separating these effects will require future trials with time-and attentionmatched control conditions or designs that dismantle intervention components. Individualization is both a strength and a limitation. Although common rules govern the intervention, progression pathways and the use of manual techniques may differ among participants. The trial will therefore evaluate the effectiveness of the complete framework but will not determine the independent contribution of any exercise or manual technique. In summary, this trial will determine whether adding a 12-week structured and staged program to shared cycling, stretching, mobilization, and education produces greater improvement in the postural profile and whether any change is accompanied by neuromuscular responses, range-of-motion changes, and balance changes. Regardless of the . direction of the findings, transparent reporting of decision rules and actual dose will support replication and critical appraisal of this approach.
