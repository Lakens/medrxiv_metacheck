Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Abstract

NOTE: This preprint reports new research that has not been certified by peer review and should not be used to guide clinical practice.

Question: Is anorexia nervosa (AN) severity associated with long-term social and occupational functioning outcomes? Findings: In this population-based cohort study, greater AN severity was associated with poorer long-term functioning, including education, occupation, and cohabitation status, across adulthood. AN severity showed graded, dose-response-like relationships with those outcomes, and the gradient persisted regardless of whether individuals had active eating disorder diagnoses.

Meaning: Individuals with severe AN show substantial social and occupational impairment, reflected in shorter education, weaker labour-market attachment, lower income, and greater likelihood of living alone, compared with the general population, with these disparities becoming more pronounced over time.

Introduction

Anorexia nervosa (AN) is a serious psychiatric disorder associated with a high likelihood of chronicity, multimorbidity, and premature mortality. [1, 2] Beyond its clinical impact, AN disrupts key developmental stages and impairs psychosocial functioning, often generating lasting socioeconomic and functional impairment. [3] [4] [5] While Global Burden of Disease data indicate that years lived with disability (YLD) for many mental disorders are stable or declining, YLD attributable to AN has increased over the past three decades. [6, 7] Most prior research has examined educational attainment and socioeconomic status as risk factors preceding AN onset, [8, 9] whereas few studies have examined them as consequences of AN. Register-based studies consistently report adverse outcomes following AN diagnosis, though findings vary in magnitude. A Swedish cohort study found that 58% of individuals with AN were employed and 21% received disability pension or other sickness benefits 9 to 14 years after hospital admission. [4] A Finnish 30-year followup study similarly reported that 22% of individuals with AN received disability pension, alongside reduced employment days and lower cumulative income relative to population controls. [3] Educational trajectories may also be altered: females with AN being overrepresented at both the lowest and highest levels of attainment compared to matched controls. [10] A separate community-based cohort found that more women with AN experienced delayed education, remaining enrolled in higher education into their early thirties. [11] Past studies have relied on AN diagnosis alone without accounting for heterogeneity in symptom severity, chronicity, and treatment response. Nonetheless, some evidence suggests the severity itself matters: higher eating disorders (EDs) symptom levels were associated with worse occupational functioning in a female sample. [12] Consistent with this, researchers observed that adults with severe and enduring AN had poor baseline work and social adjustment, followed by limited improvement in the short term. [13] Despite this evidence, it remains unclear whether these associations reflect a severity gradient or a disproportionate burden specific to the most severely affected, and/or whether a potential gradient persists after remission. Understanding this relationship could potentially identify patients at greatest risk of long-term disadvantage, with relevance for service provision.

Understanding the impact of severe AN has been limited by the lack of a validated severity measurement in epidemiological research. [14] [15] [16] To remedy this, we derived a continuous composite score using information from the Danish national registers. The Anorexia Nervosa Register-based Severity Index (AN-RSI) enables quantification of illness severity in population-based register studies [17] and significantly predicts mortality [17] , supporting its utility as an indicator of overall cumulative illness burden. The features it may reflect, including prolonged undernutrition, frequent comorbidity, and repeated treatment exposure, also plausibly drive long-term socioeconomic disadvantage, [18] [19] [20] making AN-RSI well-suited to examining how illness severity shapes long-term sociooccupational functioning in AN.

The primary aim of our study was to examine the severity-stratified relationship with longterm social and occupational outcomes in AN. We hypothesized that more severe AN would be associated with greater cumulative impairment in educational, occupational, and social functioning. Specifically, we: (1) quantified the burden of AN across severity levels by comparing multiple outcomes including educational attainment, employment status, income level, and cohabitation status against the general population; and (2) evaluated the association between illness severity and functional outcomes among individuals with AN.

Methods

Study design and data

This register-based cohort study used individual-level data from Danish national registers, linked via the unique personal identification numbers assigned to all Danish residents through the Danish Civil Registration System. [21] All live-born individuals in Denmark between 1963 and 1994 were followed from age 25 until age 55 or through December 31, 2024. Individuals with a first AN diagnosis (International Statistical Classification of Diseases (ICD), including ICD-10: F50.0-F50.1 and ICD-8: 306.50) were identified from the Danish National Patient Register and the Danish Psychiatric Central Research Register. [22, 23] Given the timing of education completion and career entry, we excluded individuals older than 25 years at first AN diagnosis. To ensure comparable exposure time for the AN-RSI calculation (eMethods), we also excluded individuals younger than 10 years. Individuals who died or emigrated within 5 years of first diagnosis were excluded, since AN-RSI requires 5 years of complete follow-up. Educational attainment was obtained from the Danish Education Registers, [24] and employment and income data were obtained from the Employment Classification Module and the Integrated Database for Labor-market Research. [25] Cohabitation status was derived from the Danish Population Statistics Register. [26] Full register descriptions appear in the eMethods. This study followed the STROBE reporting guideline. [27] The AN-RSI incorporates age of onset, illness duration, treatment length, and healthcare utilization. Illness severity was quantified using the AN-RSI five years after the first AN diagnosis following the weighting procedure described elsewhere [17] and detailed in the eMethods. Individuals with AN were stratified in two ways. In the binary classification, cases scoring in the top quintile of the AN-RSI were classified as severe AN and the remainder as less severe AN, consistent with the proportion of individuals who experience a severe illness course reported in previous studies. [28] The severity scores were further divided into quintiles.

We examined four socio-occupational domains. In Denmark, upper-secondary education is typically completed by age 20, and most people have achieved their highest educational attainment by age 30. [29] To account for minor delays, we measured: (1) completion of upper-secondary education at age 25 (binary); and (2) highest educational attainment at age 35 (four levels, ordinal). For labour-market and social outcomes, we measured employment status (unemployment or employment), income deficiency (lowest quintile of annual wages, relative to the age-, sex-, and calendar year-matched general population), and cohabitation status (living alone or not) as trajectories from ages 30 to 55. Detailed outcome definitions and rationales are provided in eMethods.

Statistical analyses

To quantify the absolute burden in relation to functioning outcomes, we compared the AN severity group against the reference group (all individuals from the general population without a diagnosis of AN). We examined the associations between education outcomes and illness severity at ages 25 and 30 with logistic regression for binary outcomes and multinomial logistic regression for categorical outcomes. Trajectories of employment status, income level, and cohabitation status were analyzed by fitting separate regressions for every year between 30 and 55. To evaluate whether long-term sociooccupational impairments were driven by ongoing, active diagnoses, we conducted within-AN analyses using three nested models: a crude model adjusted for baseline covariates; a model additionally adjusted for active AN, defined as an inpatient or outpatient contact with an AN diagnosis recorded during the calendar year in which the outcome was measured; and an alternative model adjusted for active ED, defined analogously using any ED diagnosis. The latter adjustment was performed as a sensitivity analysis to address the potential diagnostic crossover. To examine whether illness severity showed a graded association with functional outcomes, additional analyses were performed using the AN-RSI quintiles as exposure.

All models were adjusted for sex, birth year, parental psychiatric history, and socioeconomic position, which were selected a priori based on previous literature and confounding (eMethods). Complete-case analysis was used after excluding missing covariate data. Statistical tests were two-sided with Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate correction per outcome, performed in R version 4.4.3. [30]

Results

In total, 2,442,916 individuals were included in our study, of whom 9,465 were diagnosed with AN between ages 10 and 25; 1,996 (21.1%) met the AN-RSI threshold for severe AN (eTable 1-2). The AN cohort was predominantly female (93.8%), with a mean age at first diagnosis of 17.9 years (standard deviation [SD]=3.5). Parents of the AN cohort had higher income, higher secondary education completion, and more frequent psychiatric history compared to the general population (Table 1).

Educational outcomes

By age 25, 527 individuals (35.9%) with severe AN had not completed upper-secondary education compared with 1,198 (23.0%) with less severe AN and 557,760 (22.9%) in the general population. (Table 2a) Both AN groups had a higher likelihood of not completing upper-secondary education than the general population, but the risk was twice as high in the severe group compared to the less severe group (odds ratio [OR]=3.07; 95% CI:2.72-3.46; OR =1.39; 95% CI:1.29-1.50, respectively).

Socio-occupational functioning trajectories

Figure 1 shows associations between illness severity and unemployment, income insufficiency, and living alone. Both severity groups showed worse functioning than the general population across all three domains. Unemployment remained elevated throughout follow-up in both severity groups but followed distinct trajectories. In the less severe group, unemployment odds peaked in midlife (OR =2.66; 95% CI:2.34-3.04 at age 44) and gradually declined towards population levels by age 55 (OR =1.53; 95% CI:1.09-2.15). In contrast, the severe group showed progressively worsening unemployment across adulthood, with ORs increasing from 3.42 (95% CI:3.03-3.86) at age 30 to 4.65 (95% CI:3.54-6.10) at age 45 and rising further later. Income insufficiency risk also diverged over time: in the severe group, odds of low income were stable from age 30 to age 45 (3.84 [95% CI:3.42-4.31] vs. 3.98 [95% CI:3.08-5.14]) and then increased substantially to 7.51 (95% CI:3.85-14.62) at age 55. For cohabitation, both groups consistently showed a higher likelihood of living alone, with a greater magnitude of effect in severe AN (OR = 2.43 (95% CI:2.17-2.71) at age 30; 3.12 (95%CI:1.60-6.05) at age 55). In the joint multinomial model of labour-market status (employment, disability pension, or other unemployment type), with employment as the reference outcome and individuals without AN as the reference group, disability pension risk was significantly elevated in the severe AN group (OR =14.78; 95% CI:12.39-17.64) at age 30, remaining persistently high across adulthood; this pattern was less pronounced in the less severe group (OR =4.78; 95% CI:4.19-5.45, eFigure 1).

Within-AN severity contrast

Within-AN comparison showed that severity was consistently associated with worse functioning, partly explained by illness status. (Figure 2) Across all three domains, accounting for active illness status marginally attenuated the point estimates toward the null, yet the clear severity gradient persisted independent of current diagnostic status. At age 30, the severe AN group had significantly higher unemployment than the less severe group in the crude model (OR = 1.90; 95% CI:1.65-2.18), which remained robust after adjusting for active AN status (OR = 1.50; 95% CI:1.29-1.74) and active ED status (OR = 1.46; 95% CI:1.26-1.69). A comparable pattern of increased risk held across the followup period (ages 30-50), and the severity contrast persisted independent of current diagnostic status (eTable 4). For income insufficiency, the severe-versus-less severe odds at age 30 were attenuated after adjusting for active AN and ED status (OR = 1.98 (95% CI:1.73-2.26); 1.66 (95% CI:1.44-1.93); 1.60 (95% CI:1.38-1.86), respectively). The severity contrast in cohabitation was more modest but followed a similar attenuation pattern (OR = 1.54 (95% CI:1.36-1.75); 1.31 (95% CI:1.14-1.50); 1.29 (95% CI:1.12-1.48), respectively).

Dose-response analyses

To further examine the dose-response relationship, we evaluated outcomes relative to the general population across AN-RSI severity quintiles (Q1-Q5, from least to most severe). Across all domains, risk estimates followed a similar pattern (Figure 3): ORs were lowest at Q2 and increased monotonically through Q5. For education (not completing upper-secondary), the OR at Q2 was 1.15 (95% CI:0.91-1.45), not significantly different from the general population. For unemployment, income insufficiency, and living alone, ORs remained above 1.0 at every quintile, including the lowest risk point (Q2) (1.35 [95% CI:1.10-1.65], 1.41 [95% CI:1.16-1.72], and 1.37 [95% CI:1.17-1.59], respectively). From Q2 onward, a clear dose-response relationship emerged, with the highest risk for all adverse outcomes concentrated in the most severe quintile (eTable 5).

Discussion

Utilizing the comprehensive national registers and longitudinal data, we examined a severity-outcome association in AN spanning education, employment, income, and cohabitation. Severe AN carried a substantially greater functioning deficit than less severe AN, and the general population, which persisted-and in some cases widenedover time. Social and occupational impairment observed over time was proportional to illness severity. The association was attenuated, but persisted, after adjustment for concurrent AN or ED hospital contact.

Educational attainment showed a shifting pattern of impairment across ages. By age 25, fewer individuals with AN completed upper-secondary education than the general population. This was more pronounced in the severe AN group and consistent with prior evidence on lower educational attainment in individuals with AN by their late twenties. [3] This deficit coincided with the typical age range for upper-secondary education; severe AN cases had more hospital admissions and longer illness duration, likely disrupting schooling. By age 30 and 35, severe AN cases had a higher likelihood of having completed basic education only and were less likely to have completed higher education. In contrast, less severe AN cases instead showed both increased patterns, at the low and high ends, matching the U-shaped distribution reported in a previous study. [10] This difference may reflect a combination of psychological, cognitive, and familial factors. First, premorbid perfectionism and high achievement orientation may support continued academic engagement among those with less severe AN once they are in remission. [31] In severe AN, the same traits may be present, but their academic advantages may also be attenuated by illness severity and prolonged disruption to continuous education. Second, these academic advantages have been linked to genetic factors associated with AN risk. [32, 33] Individuals with less severe AN may achieve educational levels closer to their familial and genetic potential, whereas greater illness severity may override the advantages. Third, higher familial socioeconomic status may offset the disruption caused by adolescent illness and support return to the educational track. [8, 34] Together, these factors may explain how illness severity shapes long-term educational outcomes.

In line with previous studies [3, 4] , individuals with AN had poorer functioning across labour-market outcomes at younger ages, regardless of illness severity. However, this pattern diverged over time: the less severe group became increasingly comparable to the general population, whereas the individuals with severe AN experienced persistent and progressively widening impairment with age. This divergence may explain the inconsistent findings on long-term labour-market outcomes in AN. [3, 4, 11, 35] Prior studies have generally examined AN as a single group and did not account for illness severity. Community-ascertained AN samples, [11] often identified through self-report questionnaires, are more likely to include untreated individuals with mild to moderate illness, which may explain their more favourable outcomes. Clinical cohorts, by contrast, tend to capture individuals with greater severity, longer illness duration, and higher psychiatric comorbidity, resulting in poorer reported outcomes. [35] The widening gap over time points to a process of cumulative disadvantage. Individuals with less severe AN may experience the illness as a temporary disruption; one possibility is that once their illness stabilizes, they may be able to draw upon personal and social support to return to work, eventually catching up to the general population. Conversely, for those with severe AN, prolonged illness duration and repeated treatment or hospitalization often occur during critical periods for entering higher education and building early-career employment, and missing these developmental milestones could potentially result in limited skill development, work experience, and income accumulation, making it increasingly difficult to catch up over time. These divergences suggest that treating AN as a single diagnostic category may mask these severity-dependent differences in longterm functional recovery. [36] When adjusted for hospital contacts of active AN or ED, the association between severe AN and labour-market outcomes was only slightly attenuated at younger ages, with further minimal attenuation at older ages. This suggests that the contribution of concurrent AN/ED illness activity declined steadily across the observation period. The gaps likely reflect a divergence between clinical diagnostic remission and functional recovery. [37, 38] In register-based settings, discharge from active diagnostic status is commonly driven by physical improvement and symptomatic remission.

Using the absence of hospital contact as a proxy for illness remission, our longitudinal data, however, suggest that functional recovery progresses independent of core ED symptoms; diagnostic remission does not appear to reverse the functional vulnerability quickly. This persistence may be compounded by a high burden of psychiatric comorbidity, as previously reported in our AN-RSI validation study. [17] Even after an individual no longer has an ED diagnosis, frequent and long-lasting comorbid psychiatric conditions likely continue to drive functional impairment. [39] Baseline familial disadvantage is an unlikely explanation for this persistence: both severity groups had higher parental socioeconomic status than the general population, which is more pronounced in the severe group. Instead, the persistent impairment is better explained by the timing of AN onset: median AN age at onset is mid-adolescence, a critical period for rapid neurodevelopment and maturation of cortical and subcortical networks. [40] Chronic malnutrition compromises cognitive development, including executive functioning, cognitive flexibility, and decision-making. [19] This developmental stage also coincides with key transitions in education, identity formation, and social development. More severe illness likely reflects greater neurodevelopmental vulnerability, disrupting these transitions and leading to long-term impairment that persists even after remission.

We observed that the severity gradient followed a monotonic pattern, with outcomes worsening consistently at each higher severity level, rather than reflecting only a difference between two broad categories. Functioning outcomes declined stepwise among those with the top 80% scores, consistent with a dose-response relationship. This pattern aligns with the findings from the AN-RSI development study, where increasing AN-RSI scores were associated with a graded increase in mortality risk, supporting the index's internal construct validity and capacity to capture severity along a continuous gradient rather than a binary distinction. [17] Clinical studies have shown that individual ED symptom severity directly predicts the trajectory of socio-occupational disability and health-related quality of life, [5] and our findings extend this into a population-based setting and demonstrate that the association between clinical severity and functional impairment is not transient but a persistent impact on life-course trajectory. Conversely, the outlier pattern observed at the bottom 20% is most plausibly explained by heterogeneity within the lowest register-severity group. This group likely comprises both genuinely mild cases and individuals with limited health care contact, potentially reflecting unwillingness to seek treatment-which is not uncommon in AN given its ego-syntonic nature [41] -or other unmeasured factors such as later differential diagnosis. This heterogeneity limits interpretation of the bottom 20% as a true mild-illness reference category.

Strengths and Limitations

Our study benefits from register-based data from Denmark, which provide detailed longitudinal information on health and societal outcomes for all individuals, allowing us to study multiple functional domains over more than 25 years without self-report bias and with minimal loss to follow-up. Another major strength is the use of AN-RSI to measure illness severity, both in terms of binary and gradient effects. We used complementary comparison groups to quantify the absolute burden of AN and isolate the relative effect of illness severity itself. Our nested adjustment strategy allowed us to assess whether the association with illness severity persisted after accounting for current illness status, approximated by register-recorded active AN and ED hospital contacts. This study also has limitations that warrant caution. First, with register-based measurement of illness severity, as discussed above, the heterogeneity within the bottom 20% for AN severity complicates the interpretation of some of the findings in the gradient analysis. Second, due to data availability, we cannot measure clinical presentation and other factors related to AN risk and severity at baseline and at the severity assessment time window, leaving unmeasured or residual confounding factors to consider. Third, this observational study design precludes causal inference. The graded association observed across severity quintiles may indicate that illness severity contributes to observed functional impairment but does not confirm it. Finally, the generalizability of this study may be limited due to differences in clinical practices and welfare systems; validation in other countries is warranted.

Conclusions

Individuals with lifetime severe AN, regardless of current illness status, showed impaired functioning across multiple domains and increasingly poorer labour-market outcomes across the entire work-life course, indicating a pattern of accumulation rather than static disadvantages. Individuals with less severe illness tend to approximate the general population over time, showing a promising chance of long-term socio-occupational recovery. These findings indicate that register-defined remission does not necessarily correspond to functional recovery. Data availability

Data for this study were obtained from Danish national registries via Statistics Denmark and the Danish Health Data Authority. Individual-level data cannot be shared due to Danish data protection legislation and registry access agreements. Qualified researchers may apply for data access through the appropriate Danish authorities, subject to approval of research protocols and compliance with data protection requirements. Statistical analysis code is available from the corresponding author upon request.

Figure 2. Association between illness severity and social functioning outcomes within individuals with anorexia nervosa. Odds ratios (95% CI) for unemployment, income insufficiency, and living alone among individuals with severe AN relative to those with less severe AN.
