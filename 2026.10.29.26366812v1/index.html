Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

Background: Human-centered design (HCD) is a co-creation approach that is increasingly being utilized by implementation science researchers to engage key stakeholders in developing contextually appropriate health interventions and implementation strategies. The exact role of HCD in implementation research, however, remains uncertain. We describe the process, outcome, and experiences of using HCD to develop the operational details of an implementation strategy based on a pre-existing scaffolding that consisted of combining community-based peer medication delivery with health information technology (HIT) to improve medication adherence among hypertension patients in rural western Kenya.

Methods: A design team of 22 members was formed representing key stakeholders: hypertension patients, healthcare workers, pharmacists, peers, HIT personnel, policy/program leaders, and implementation researchers. We conducted six meetings utilizing established HCD principles and processes (inspiration, brainstorming, conceptualization, creation, iteration, and finalization), aimed at developing a final implementation strategy with full operational details.

Quantitative assessment of acceptability and appropriateness was conducted among healthcare workers, patients, and peers, using the Acceptability of Intervention Measure and Intervention Appropriateness Measure.

Results: The design team developed key operational details across five domains: peer recruitment, training and monitoring; HIT; medication delivery; medication payment; and stakeholder identification. Acceptability and appropriateness scores (maximum of 20) were favorable among all participants, but mean scores were notably higher among patients with hypertension (19.3, 19.2) and peers (19.2, 19.2) compared to healthcare workers (17.7, 17.3).

Specific recommendations were made to improve the acceptability and appropriateness of the strategy. These findings were subsequently used to develop a final iteration of the implementation strategy.

Conclusion: HCD was feasible and useful in optimizing the operational details of our implementation strategy, aligning it with the healthcare goals and needs of the community. It allowed end-users to co-create with researchers, ultimately yielding high pre-implementation acceptability and appropriateness, which we anticipate will ultimately enhance implementation success.

BACKGROUND

Despite an increasing burden of hypertension in low-and middle-income countries (LMIC), blood pressure control rates in LMICs are low. 1 These low control rates are concerning given the adverse cardiovascular effects and death resulting from complications of uncontrolled elevated blood pressure, with elevated systolic blood pressure ranking as a top contributor to disabilityadjusted life years in LMICs. 1 In Kenya, hypertension prevalence is estimated at 24% and control rates are less than 20%. 1 Medication nonadherence in Kenya is a significant cause for these low control rates, with approximately 71% of participants with hypertension and/or diabetes reporting sub-optimal medication adherence in a recent cross-sectional study from western Kenya. 2 Significant barriers to adherence, such as transportation costs that influence medication access and possession, as well as inadequate psychosocial support, need to be addressed to improve health status. 2 Novel interventions leveraging community health workers and peer networks have been effective previously in improving diabetes and HIV control in Africa, and have the potential to address the aforementioned barriers to ultimately improve medication adherence and blood pressure control rates. 3, 4 In addition, health information technology (HIT) and digital health interventions incorporating reminders, decision support, patient tracking, and electronic medical records have been shown to be effective at improving medication adherence. 5 However, the combination of peer coaching, community-based medication delivery, and HIT support to improve medication adherence and clinical outcomes for people living with hypertension has not been well studied.

Thus, our team plans to implement and evaluate an implementation strategy, known as the Peers and Technology for Access, Adherence, Accountability and Analytics (PT4A), to address this gap.

In order to create successful interventions and implementation strategies that will lead to high end-user satisfaction and engagement, the desires and interests of the end-users must be taken into account, especially for interventions and implementation strategies involving multiple community stakeholders. 6 The human-centered design (HCD) approach is gaining popularity and has shown success in implementation research studies in low-and middle-income countries. 7, 8 The HCD approach involves deep discussion and exploration with end-users utilizing interactive workshops and interviews to generate tailor-made solutions that fit their needs and are feasible and appropriate for society. By involving stakeholders and community members in the design process, interventions and implementation strategies tend to be more appropriate and acceptable for the end-users for whom they are developed, leading to better outcomes. 9, 10 In prior published studies of the HCD process, most interventions and implementation strategies were already designed or in place, and the HCD process was used to refine these interventions and provide researchers with insights into public perceptions of their interventions. For example, the BIGPIC study in western Kenya applied the HCD process to refine a hypertension and diabetes care delivery model combining microfinance and group medical visits that had been previously developed and piloted. 7, 11 HCD has been used in a similar manner for a number of other interventions in several African countries. [12] [13] [14] [15] [16] However, it has not yet been described how to leverage HCD to specify operational details for a planned implementation strategy. Therefore, we aimed to use the HCD process to generate operational details for the PT4A strategy.

METHODS

Study setting

This study was conducted in western Kenya within the context of the Academic Model Providing Access to Healthcare (AMPATH), chronic disease management (CDM) program. AMPATH is an academic partnership between Moi University College of Health Sciences (MUCHS), Moi Teaching and Referral Hospital (MTRH), and a consortium of North American universities. 17 AMPATH established a CDM program in 2011 that includes several components such as task redistribution, clinical decision support using HIT, consistent and secure medication supply, linkage and retention activities, community stakeholder engagement, and social support including MF groups. 9, [18] [19] [20] [21] CDM has enrolled more than 60,000 patients in over 150 health facilities in its catchment area in western Kenya. 22 Our study took place in three of the AMPATH catchment areas: Webuye in Bungoma County, Kitale in Trans-Nzoia County and Turbo in Uasin Gishu County.

Core components of the implementation strategy

The proposed implementation strategy has three core components: peer coaching, communitybased medication delivery and HIT (Figure 1). Peers are lay individuals with shared disease experience that can be leveraged to provide peer coaching consisting of practical, social, emotional, and motivational assistance to patients. 23 Peer coaching will address individual-level adherence barriers, provide psychosocial support, and enhance patient activation. This peer coaching will be offered during the door-to-door community-based delivery of medicines to patients with hypertension at convenient locations and time collaboratively decided by patients and peers. The HIT component will be aligned with AMPATH informatics infrastructure to provide decision support and tailored adherence counseling. 24, 25 This component will also facilitate tele-consultation between patients and clinicians and will be used for tracking and accountability by providing prompts to peers when refills are near due. and HIT (health information technology) components of the PT4A implementation strategy as well as how the implementation strategy functions to improve medication delivery and adherence.

Design team facilitator training

The study team received a two-month online training on 'Introduction to Human-Centered Design' from Acumen Academy, which is supported by IDEO. 26 Each module had activities and discussions that were required to be completed in groups and updated before moving to the next module. The study team held weekly online discussions that lasted approximately one hour each.

In addition to online meetings, the group created online boards that were accessible to each team member to share their perspectives for further discussion. Every member of our study team completed all four modules of the IDEO training course.

Design team formation

The design team was comprised of 22 members representing the targeted end users: physician (1), clinical officers (2), nurse (1), pharmacists (2), peer navigators (3), patients with hypertension (2 male, 2 female), HIT personnel (3), national health insurance fund representatives (2), and research team members (4). Patients were randomly selected during their routine clinic visits at the various facilities, one from Kitale, one from Turbo, and two from Webuye. After approval from hospital administration had been obtained, healthcare workers were selected from those who were currently engaged in patient care in the CDM clinics of the various facilities within the study sites. HIT personnel were selected from those who had experience with the AMPATH medical records system. Peers had to be active in their peer roles within various programs in AMPATH. Representatives from the national health insurance fund (the Kenyan national public health insurance scheme), were also included.

Once identified, the research team briefed them on the HCD process, the number of expected sessions, and the duration of each session. All participants who were willing and able to attend all the sessions were invited. They were from different health facilities across the three study sites. For logistical purposes, the design team members from all the study sites were convened in one central geographical location which enabled in-person participation of the design team members.

Design process

We conducted a total of six design team meetings (Figure 2): inspiration (meeting #1), brainstorming (#2), conceptualization (#3), creation (#4), iteration (#5), and finalization (#6). This paper describes the first five meetings and presents the results of acceptability and appropriateness testing conducted after meeting #4, focusing on how we leveraged HCD to optimize the operational details of the implementation strategy in preparation for the pilot study.

The pilot study implementation and feasibility testing, as well as the outcomes of meeting #6 (finalization of the implementation strategy), will be described and reported separately in an independent manuscript. including the various meetings that occurred, testing that was done, and prototypes created. The first four meetings were weekly meetings, each approximately five hours long, anchored on the core components of the implementation strategy as described above. The prototype generated after meeting #4 underwent acceptability and appropriateness testing. This testing process and analysis took approximately three months, after which meeting #5 was conducted. Each meeting's agenda was developed and led by two facilitators (JA, EN) who had received training and had prior experience in HCD implementation. All design team meetings were collaborative and interactive.

Design meetings

Meeting 1-Inspiration

The goal of this meeting was to introduce the team to HCD, present baseline findings and identify key refinement areas. To better understand HCD, team members were paired up and asked to identify and discuss the challenges they faced during their commute to the workshop that morning, and to explore possible solutions. This exercise laid the groundwork for a discussion on the meaning and application of HCD.

The study team then presented the findings from the baseline contextual assessment, which aimed to identify factors that could influence the operational details of the implementation strategy. 27 The findings highlighted several perceived benefits of the planned strategy, including improved hypertension management, enhanced psychosocial support, better community-based follow-up for patients, and reduced healthcare costs. The assessment also identified areas requiring further refinement, including the ideal qualifications of peers, medication payment processes, delivery procedures, and specifications for the HIT component. Following the presentation, team members reviewed the findings and shared their personal experiences and insights on aspects of the implementation strategy that needed further development. Each member documented their reflections on Post-it notes, which were then organized on a board to guide discussion and prioritization.

Meeting 2-Brainstorming

The goal of this meeting was to translate insight statements into 'How Might We' questions to stimulate creative thinking and to guide generation of potential solutions. The team used an iterative process to refine the initial insight statements into final core statements that were relevant to the implementation strategy. These final core insight statements were then reframed as 'how might we' questions which created an exploratory space for identifying and discussing possible solutions.

Meeting 3-Conceptualizing

The goal of this meeting was to evaluate and refine the proposed solutions in order to develop implementation components that were acceptable, feasible and aligned with the implementation strategy. Members reviewed solutions generated in meeting 2 and assessed each for anticipated feasibility, potential advantages, and potential disadvantages. During the meeting, the team continued to generate additional ideas and solutions while also adding elements to existing ones.

Any ideas that were deemed unfeasible or not aligned with the core components of the implementation strategies were discarded. Similar concepts were grouped together. This process was cyclical and iterative, continuing until members reached agreement on final solutions with feasible operational components.

Meeting 4-Creation

The goal of this meeting was to synthesize the refined concepts from previous meetings into a visual workflow and to prepare the first prototype of the implementation strategy for acceptability and appropriateness testing. Research team members presented the detailed concepts of the proposed solutions, while other members provided feedback, either agreeing with the proposed recommendations or suggesting further modifications based on their potential to strengthen the implementation strategy.

During the meeting, the study informatics team also presented the proposed structure of the HIT tool, developed based on input from Meeting 3. Design team members reviewed the proposed workflow and provided feedback on the flow structure, required fields, and overall usability of the tool. By the end of the meeting, recommendations across all domains had been incorporated into a visual depiction of the first prototype implementation strategy, which was then ready for acceptability and appropriateness testing.

Acceptability and Appropriateness testing

The prototype implementation strategy was then tested for acceptability and appropriateness based on an adapted version of the psychometrically validated and reliable Acceptability of Intervention Measure (AIM) and Intervention Appropriateness Measure (IAM). 27 Acceptability is a measure of how innovation is perceived as agreeable or satisfactory whereas appropriateness measures its perceived relevance and compatibility.

A total of 39 participants were enrolled across all study sites: 15 patients with hypertension, 15 clinicians, 6 peer providers, and 3 pharmacists. Patients were recruited through random sampling during routine clinic visits. Clinicians, pharmacists, and peer providers were required to be actively involved in the clinical management of hypertensive patients, while all participants were required to have had no prior involvement in the HCD process. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment.

Research assistants described the intervention in detail while displaying the visual depiction created in meeting #4 for better understanding. Thereafter, each participant rated the acceptability and appropriateness of the intervention. Acceptability and appropriateness scores were summed separately. Each of these domains has four items with a 5-point response scale ranging from 'completely disagree' (a score of 1) to 'completely agree' (a score of 5). Total scores for each domain thus range from 4-20.

Meeting 5-Iteration

This meeting followed the acceptability and appropriateness testing, and the goal was to refine the prototype based on feedback from the testing. The study team presented their findings, focusing on areas with low scores on either acceptability or appropriateness measures. The team then formed 'how might we' questions to prompt further brainstorming. For example, if group 'x' scored low on the overall acceptability measure, the HMW question was framed as "How might we make the implementation more acceptable among group 'x'?" The design team then brainstormed possible solutions. At the end of this meeting, we had the second prototype implementation strategy for pilot implementation.

Ethical considerations

This study obtained ethical approval from the Institutional Research and Ethics Committee at Moi University, College of Health Sciences in Eldoret, and the Institutional Review Board at NYU Grossman School of Medicine. ClinicalTrials.gov identifier (NCT number) is NCT05051124. All participants provided written informed consent before enrollment in this study.

RESULTS

Design meetings 1-4.

In meeting 1, the deliberation process resulted in the organization of team members' inputs into five operational domains that addressed the key factors identified during the baseline contextual assessment. These domains were peer recruitment, training, and monitoring; HIT; medication delivery; medication payment; and stakeholder identification (Table 1). These domains were further developed through reframing and solution brainstorming during meeting 2 and 3. In meeting 4, all recommendations were synthesized, and operational details for different domains were finalized, creating the 1 st prototype (Table 1). These refinements built on the originally conceptualized strategy by adding more specific details to improve feasibility, acceptability, and responsiveness to end users' needs.

Domain 2: HIT.

Participants identified the need for a reliable, user-friendly system that could support peers during medication delivery, particularly in settings with limited internet connectivity. Key requirements included timely medication refill reminders, easy data entry and retrieval, audio and video teleconsultation, and alerts for blood pressure readings outside recommended thresholds. In response, the team proposed an easy-to-navigate tablet-based system with offline data entry and synchronization once internet connectivity became available.

The system would generate refill reminders approximately seven days before medications were due and alerts for blood pressure readings above or below predefined thresholds. It would also capture blood pressure readings, pill counts, medication information, patient complaints or side effects, peer observations, referrals, and delivery information, while support stock monitoring and tracking of refill and clinic appointment dates.

Domain 3: Medication delivery.

Participants emphasized the need for a safe, reliable, and patient-centered process that ensured convenience, appropriate medication handling, and accurate and timely delivery. These considerations informed the development of a detailed SOP to standardize the delivery process. Peers and patients would communicate in advance regarding the delivery location and timing, and payment would be confirmed before medications were dispensed. Medication safety would be supported through appropriate packaging and storage.

Patient feedback would be used to assess whether the correct medications were delivered on time and the quality of their interaction with the peer. Proof of delivery would be documented by the peer in the HIT system at the time of the delivery encounter and confirmed through the patient's signed acknowledgment.

Domain 4: Medication payment.

Participants identified the need for a secure, traceable, and convenient cashless payment process with minimal additional transaction costs. They also emphasized the importance of explaining payment procedures during informed consent so that participants understood the requirements before medication delivery began. The proposed approach was to use mobile payments exclusively, with payments made directly to hospital pharmacy till numbers to improve accountability and reduce transaction charges. Family support and participation in economic empowerment initiatives, such as table banking or farming groups, were suggested as potential mechanisms for patients who experienced difficulty paying for medications.

Domain 5: Stakeholder engagement.

Participants emphasized early and continuous involvement of local authorities, communities, health facilities, county leadership, and relevant departments to strengthen acceptance and implementation of the intervention. Sustainability beyond the study period was also identified as an important consideration. The proposed stakeholders included county health leadership, health facility in-charges, county public health officers, clinicians, community health workers, chiefs, village elders, and representatives from similar AMPATH programs. Engagement meetings would introduce PT4A, clarify stakeholder roles, and establish mechanisms for continued involvement through meetings, visits, and telephone communication.

For longer-term sustainability, the design team proposed engaging the Ministry of Health and county leadership regarding potential adoption of the intervention, including peer integration, medication supply arrangements, and continuation of the technology platform, while linking the intervention with existing community-based health programs.

Among the healthcare workers (n = 18), the average age was 36.4 years, and the majority (56%) were female. Design meeting 5

In meeting 5, additional recommendations were generated to improve the acceptability and appropriateness of the implementation strategy for patients, peers, and healthcare workers. To strengthen peer support and oversight, the design team recommended that peers' scope of work be clearly defined during training and shared this with clinicians to support monitoring and evaluation. The HIT tool was also refined to support oversight by enabling clinicians and the research team to monitor peer activities, track missed deliveries.

The team also recommended issuing identification badges to peers, clearly indicating their role as hypertension peers, to improve recognition and reduce stigma associated with home medication delivery. Additional recommendations included establishing clear procedures for medication delivery and developing a protocol to minimize loss to follow-up, including reengaging patients through caregiver contact or home visits and specifying the number of recontact attempts before classifying a patient as lost to follow-up.

The team also proposed more details on medication packaging, supply coordination, and healthcare worker engagement. Recommendations on medication packaging included: detailed receipts that would indicate the source of medications, medications dispensed, dispenser identity, and amount paid all to support transparency and accountability. Regular communication with pharmacists was also recommended to support consistent medication availability. To improve healthcare worker acceptability, the team recommended briefing clinicians on the study, patient eligibility criteria, peer qualifications, and peer training so that they could remain engaged throughout implementation. Non-monetary incentives, such as special training sessions, were also recommended to enhance clinician engagement.

DISCUSSION

The PT4A strategy was developed in order to address significant barriers to hypertension medication adherence in western Kenya, specifically related to lack of access to and possession of medications, transportation-related challenges, and unmet psychosocial needs. 2, 28 Using a design team comprised of multiple different stakeholders, the PT4A strategy underwent refinement of operational details as well as appropriateness and acceptability testing, ultimately resulting in a prototype ready for pilot testing and implementation. The final prototype included the core components of the strategy from its inception--peer coaching, community-based medication delivery, and HIT--with additional refinements such as training requirements for peers, HIT usability improvements, and stipulations on financing and payment processes, to increase the likelihood of the strategy's implementation success in the community. Overall, appropriateness and acceptability testing revealed that a majority of participants found that the revised implementation strategy was both appropriate and acceptable.

The HCD approach has proven useful in a number of settings to address several different healthcare delivery challenges and issues while increasing community and stakeholder participation in the design and implementation process. The pre-implementation and implementation "pipeline" encompass many different elements and stages, and HCD has been utilized at different stages of this process. Some studies have employed HCD at early time points, engaging stakeholders sometimes during the earliest stages of the design process in order to "co-design" the intervention. 12, 30, 31 In contrast, other studies have involved HCD at later time points, often using stakeholder feedback to refine existing interventions. 32, 33 The optimal timing and duration of HCD in implementation research remains an area of debate and diversity of opinion. While there is consensus regarding the importance of a participatory approach and stakeholder involvement, there can be challenges for both stakeholders and investigators. Our team's previous work in western Kenya has highlighted that there can sometimes be tension between implementation researchers and community stakeholders, especially when the "scaffolding" or initial specifications of an implementation strategy are "too fully" developed at the time that the HCD process is initiated. 10 Our current approach, summarized here, involved clear communication throughout the design process between study investigators and design team members about the role of HCD, in this instance, to guide the development of operational details for the PT4A implementation strategy. This transparent communication helped to mitigate any potential tension and gave stakeholders a defined purpose in the process. As described above, our HCD process yielded valuable operational details regarding peer legitimacy, medication financing, and HIT that would have otherwise potentially gone un-or under-addressed without stakeholder perspectives. We believe that bringing together a diversity of stakeholders allowed for the entire group to feel activated to enact change collectively. 32 Interestingly, however, despite the overall general success of the HCD process in supporting a diverse set of stakeholders to operationalize the PT4A strategy, healthcare workers gave markedly lower scores for acceptability and appropriateness compared to peers and patients.

Reasons for this difference in acceptability and appropriateness may be attributed to the perception held by clinicians and healthcare workers that utilization of peers could be risky given the relative lack of extensive medical training that peers have compared to clinicians and other healthcare workers. To allay these concerns, several modifications were made in order to make the strategy more acceptable to clinicians by bolstering peer training and recruitment requirements, as described above. The differential perceptions across stakeholder groups thus did influence our team's response to be more accommodating to the specific concerns of the different stakeholder groups. We anticipate that this approach will help to ensure that the implemented strategy maximizes buy-in by all stakeholder groups.

HCD as a form of stakeholder engagement is not without its critics, and we recognize that HCD is one among many potential participatory approaches that aim to secure stakeholder engagement and involvement. Alternative methods such as community-based participatory research, Delphi Method, and RAND/UCLA Appropriateness Method have been used in the past with success. 34- 36 Each approach has its own specific benefits and challenges; ideally, investigators and community stakeholders together can discuss and choose which approach is most appropriate for each particular situation, in order to optimize innovation, effectiveness, and equity in the context of resource limitations and other structural challenges.

In our situation specifically, we had scaffolding and a framework for our PT4A implementation strategy that we wanted to operationalize and implement in a specific region of western Kenya.

When choosing among different participatory approaches, we prioritized stakeholder engagement in the design and refinement process of the PT4A strategy, with the eventual hope that we would foster stakeholder engagement for long-term success. We acknowledge, however, that the HCD process might not foster sustained engagement from stakeholders, potentially due to the duration and demand of long design phases and cycles that have previously been reported to result in stakeholder attrition. 7, 37 Stakeholder attrition becomes a particularly significant concern when one considers that not all participants are able to allocate the same amount of time to the design process, leading to situations where design teams may ultimately become less representative of the community they are serving by effectively selecting for those who are able to afford to dedicate time to the study. 38 Because of the potential for stakeholder attrition and our understanding of the importance of clear communication with community members, we opted for a shorter HCD duration with involvement at a later time point in the design/operationalization process, recognizing potential shortcomings as described above. While it was a demanding experience that required flexibility and cooperation amongst a diversity of participants, we feel that our HCD approach ultimately was able to strike a balance between involving stakeholders to generate critical elements of the implementation strategy while not unduly burdening them.

We acknowledge the following limitations of our study. First, we recognize that while we did have a diverse group of stakeholders involved, not all potential stakeholder groups were represented. Future efforts could incorporate patient family members or caregivers who could provide input and insight regarding the impact of PT4A on patients and families. In addition, health facility-level administrators involved in scheduling appointments and staff assignments could also be included to provide the perspective of facility-level logistics and implications.

Second, as we have discussed above, our choice of HCD is not the only participatory approach that could have been selected, and we recognize that other approaches could have their own benefits and drawbacks. Finally, our approach and the final PT4A implementation strategy might not be fully generalizable to other populations and other settings. However, we feel that the considerations raised by the design team, the operational details that were incorporated, and the final PT4A strategy can be relevant for populations and communities worldwide that are facing similar challenges of fragmented health systems, poverty, and transportation difficulties.

CONCLUSION

We successfully used an HCD process to operationalize an implementation strategy to improve medication possession and adherence for hypertension management in western Kenya. The HCD process yielded valuable feedback from stakeholders, strengthening the implementation strategy and increasing its likelihood of long-term success, while also strengthening relationships between the research team and community stakeholders that can increase engagement and trust.

We anticipate that future program planners, implementers, and implementation science researchers can build off our current work to continue engaging in successful participatory approaches to design and implement stronger, effective interventions to improve population health worldwide.

This led to consideration of which competencies should be prioritized during training. Proposed recruitment strategies included advertising positions through health facilities and community leadership structures, as well as clearly communicating eligibility criteria.
