How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

The performance of infectious disease forecasting models is commonly calculated under the implicit assumption that forecast accuracy is equally important across time. This approach may obscure or under-weight differences in model performance during the epidemic periods that matter most for public-health decisions. Here, we introduce the weighted prequential score (WPS), a forecast evaluation framework that can emphasize epidemic periods of interest. The WPS is a weighted average of scores over time, where weights are constructed through entropic tilting which up-weights priority periods while keeping the WPS as close as possible to the arithmetic average. We demonstrate the WPS on forecasts of incident hospitalizations submitted to the CDC FluSight challenge, focusing on two priority periods: weeks of rapid growth in incidence and weeks near the seasonal peak. Across season-location-horizon pairs, doubling the weight assigned to priority periods changes the top-ranked model in 10%-26% of eligible settings, while the top-ranked model remains unchanged regardless of the amount of weight assigned in 37%-68% of settings. We demonstrate that for suitable weights, the models favored by the WPS yield an average gain in accuracy during priority periods that is at least as large as the average accuracy lost during the remaining weeks. Because weights can be defined using observed outcomes, the WPS is not a proper scoring rule. Hence, we recommend using the WPS as a retrospective evaluation diagnostic. The WPS can guide public-health practitioners in selecting models that perform well during periods most relevant to their decisions, while encouraging forecasting teams to improve accuracy during these critical weeks.

1 Introduction

Quantitative evaluation of probabilistic forecasts of infectious disease incidence requires a scoring rule. In infectious disease modeling, commonly used rules include the weighted interval score (WIS) [1, 2], continuous ranked probability score (CRPS) [3, 4], and logarithmic score [5, 6]. Each of these scoring rules is proper, meaning that the expected score is minimized when the forecaster issues their true beliefs about the data-generating distribution [1, 4, 7]. To compare the performance of forecasting models over multiple forecasts (e.g., a respiratory disease season), a common approach is to report the arithmetic average of the scores for each forecast-observation pair; see [1, 2, 8] for examples. This method provides an interpretable summary of forecast performance and has well-understood theoretical properties [7, [9] [10] [11] [12]. This arithmetic average assigns equal weight to each individual forecast-observation pair; however, the value of an accurate forecast may vary over the course of an epidemic. For example, errors during periods of rapid growth or high incidence might carry greater consequences than errors during stable or decreasing phases, as they may lead to underprepared hospitals and delayed responses. Consider the following example: Fig 1 shows two forecasts for the 2023/2024 influenza season in Michigan submitted to FluSight, a Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC)-run project that aggregates influenza forecasts for U.S. states from multiple teams [13]. Over the entire season, the UMass-flusion model ranked 1st by average WIS among all models that submitted more than 50% of the target weeks, but its 90% prediction intervals did not contain most of the observed incident hospitalization counts near the seasonal peak. Conversely, the UMass-trends ensemble model, which ranked 7th, contained the observed count within its 90% prediction intervals for nearly all of these weeks. If we are concerned about public-health or operational decisions that are made when cases are high, the UMass-trends ensemble model might be more useful than the UMass-flusion model, despite the latter achieving a lower average WIS. This suggests that a modified scoring criterion could more accurately reflect the utility of a forecast in many contexts.

The adaptation of proper scoring rules to public-health decision making problems has been explored previously [14] [15] [16] with approaches that connect forecast evaluation to an explicit definition of utility. However, specifying a utility function can, in many November 12, 2026 2/17 cases, be difficult or impractical. Instead, it may be easier to identify periods when forecast accuracy matters most. These periods of interest, such as weeks surrounding the seasonal peak, may only be identifiable retrospectively. In such cases, a scoring rule emphasizing performance during these periods is generally not proper as it relies on information unavailable at forecast time [17]. Nonetheless, such evaluation methods provide a useful performance diagnostic. In weather forecasting, for example, the U.S.

National Weather Service evaluates ensemble forecasts of precipitation conditional on

observed precipitation exceeding a chosen threshold [18] [19] [20]; similar approaches have also been used to evaluate forecasts for other high-impact weather events [21], including heat waves [22].

Here, we introduce the weighted prequential score (WPS), a scoring rule that allows emphasis on forecast performance during designated periods. To do so, the WPS evaluates models using a weighted average of their weekly scores. We construct weights using entropic tilting (ET) [23] [24] [25], so that the WPS deviates as little as possible from the arithmetic average. We illustrate the WPS using influenza hospitalization forecasts submitted to the CDC FluSight Forecast Hub during the 2023/2024, 2024/2025, and 2025/2026 seasons. We consider two illustrative priority periods: weeks surrounding the seasonal peak and high-growth weeks. We explore the sensitivity of the rankings of FluSight models to the emphasis ratio r, defined as the weight assigned to an event week relative to a non-event week, and how the contribution of weekly scores to the WPS changes under different priorities. Finally, we revisit the forecaster's dilemma [17] and discuss its implications for our approach.

2 Weighted forecast evaluation framework

In this section, we introduce the weighted prequential score (WPS) and derived quantities that summarize its effect on model ranking. For simplicity, we describe our method assuming single-week-ahead forecasts, although it generalizes to any forecast horizon.

2.1 Weighted prequential score

Let Y 1:T = (Y 1 , . . . , Y T ) be a sequence of weekly observations over a season of duration T weeks (e.g., incident hospitalization counts during a flu season). Each week, a forecaster issues a forecast distribution P t for Y t using information available up to week t -1. We denote the score of this forecast by S(P t , y t ), where S is a proper score, such as the WIS or CRPS, and y t is the observed count.

A common way to evaluate model forecast performance is using the arithmetic average of the scores (see e.g., [2, 8]). We refer to this score as the prequential score (PS) as in Dawid and Musio [11]: S(P 1:T , y 1:T ) := 1 T T t=1 S(P t , y t ).

Here, we propose to instead evaluate forecasts using the weighted prequential score (WPS), Sπ (P 1:T , y 1:T ) := T t=1 π(t; y 1:T )S(P t , y t ), where π(t; y 1:T ) is the weight for time t depending on the observed sequence y 1:T .

Note that the PS is a special case where the weight distribution is uniform π 0 (t) ≡ 1/T.

2.1.1 Weight construction using entropic tilting

For simplicity, we choose a binary rule for assigning weights to forecast-observation pairs, but our approach can be extended to more flexible weighting rules (see Supplementary Material). In particular, a set of event weeks E is assigned a higher weight relative to weeks in the non-event week set E c . Specifically, the user selects an emphasis ratio r ≥ 1 that determines how strongly the evaluation prioritizes accuracy during the event weeks relative to the rest of the season. Setting r > 1 emphasizes accuracy on weeks in E relative to the PS, while r = 1 recovers the PS.

Entropic tilting [25, 26] is demonstrated in Fig 2: the weights obtained under ET assign higher probability to the event weeks than the PS weights while remaining uniform within E and E c . More formally, each choice of r corresponds to a desired total weight w = r|E| (T -|E|) + r|E| assigned to the event week set. ET allows us to construct the unique set of score weights that satisfies the constraint t∈E π(t; y 1:T ) = w while minimizing the Kullback-Leibler divergence from the PS weights. The WPS weights obtained by ET (derived in the Supplementary Material) lead to a WPS of the form: Sπ (P 1:T , y 1:T ) = w |E| t∈E S(P t , y t ) + 1 -w T -|E| t∈E c S(P t , y t ). (3) This formula can be understood as a two-stage average: first, compute the PS restricted to the event weeks E and the non-event weeks E c , then combine these scores as a weighted average with weights w and 1 -w.

Importantly, it requires only a definition of event weeks and the emphasis ratio, r, making it particularly useful when an explicit utility function is difficult to specify. We demonstrated on the CDC FluSight challenge that the WPS is a flexible method to underscore priority periods in model evaluation, and that under suitable weights it selects models whose improvements during event weeks exceed losses during non-event weeks.

The WPS is closely related to existing scoring rules that connect the quality of a forecast to its value for public health decision making. Notably, Gerding et al. [15] derive a proper scoring rule that evaluates forecasts based on their ability to effectively allocate limited medical resources; Marshall et al. [16] propose a modification of WIS that normalizes and caps the interval score according to a utility-based error tolerance. These approaches provide a valuable framework for connecting a given scoring rule to an explicit utility function. The WPS takes a complementary approach by changing how an unmodified score is aggregated over a season. The benefit of this approach is that neither a loss function nor a utility threshold needs to be defined explicitly, as these may be difficult to define or elicit. Both strategies are nonetheless closely related, since a utility-based scoring function implicitly emphasizes a particular set of weeks. For example, the allocation score is largest when incidence is close to the amount of available resources, which in the application of Gerding et al. occurred during the growth and decline periods of the epidemic curve [15]. Another alternative scoring rule proposed by Bosse et al. involves scoring epidemic forecasts on a log-transformed scale [27]. This approach results in a proper scoring rule which mitigates the influence of high-incidence weeks or highly populated locations on the aggregate seasonal score. At the expense of propriety, the WPS can achieve a similar effect for an appropriately defined set of event weeks, additionally allowing more control over the amount of emphasis (e.g., giving more weight to less populated locations while still emphasizing performance on high incidence weeks).

The main limitation of the WPS is that, when weights depend on observed outcomes, it is not proper for the true joint data-generating distribution. Instead, the WPS incentivizes forecasters to assign higher probability to outcome sequences that receive larger weight under the WPS. This issue is closely related to the forecaster's dilemma identified by Lerch et al. [17], who showed that when evaluation is restricted to extreme observations, forecasters do not need to be skillful with respect to the true data-generating distribution. Consequently, we recommend using the WPS as a retrospective performance diagnostic rather than as a replacement for PS-based evaluation. Nonetheless, we show in the Supplementary Material that the WPS can be properized using a Bayes-act construction [28]. This properized WPS can be approximated using Monte Carlo integration when simulations from the forecast distribution are available. However, this approach cannot currently be applied to submissions from forecasting hubs such as FluSight, as they collect forecast quantiles instead of trajectories. Nonetheless, the increasing availability of trajectory-based forecast submissions could make properized versions of the WPS feasible in future applications [29].

Forecast accuracy has been shown to degrade during periods of rapid epidemic change [8], and, more generally, to vary across epidemic phases [30]. Yet these differences may be obscured when models are evaluated using seasonal average scores, particularly when important event weeks represent only a small fraction of the evaluation period. By allowing users to explicitly prioritize the weeks most important to their decisions, the WPS provides a flexible framework that makes the temporal priorities underlying aggregate evaluation explicit and better connects predictive performance to its downstream value for decision making. Consequently, we recommend using the WPS with moderate weighting alongside proper scoring rules to support November 12, 2026 11/17 model selection that better reflects public-health priorities.
