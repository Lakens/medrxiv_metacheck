Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Helicobacter pylori (Hp) infects over 40% of the world's population and causes most cases of gastric cancer worldwide, yet fundamental questions about its within-host genetic diversity remain unresolved. Decades of work on Hp genomics depends on culturing clinical isolates, which can distort within-host genetic heterogeneity that informs Hp virulence and antimicrobial resistance. Culture-free alternatives have been limited by the extremely low Hp abundance in gastric tissue (usually 10 -3 to 10 -7 of total genomic DNA), making direct metagenomics prohibitively expensive and existing enrichment strategies insufficient for whole-genome.

Introduction

Helicobacter pylori (Hp) infects over 40% of the world's population and is the leading infectious cause of gastric cancer 1 , the fifth most common cancer and the fifth leading cause of cancer death worldwide 2 . Hp strains differ markedly in their capacity to cause disease, driven by variation in key virulence loci: the cag pathogenicity island, cagA EPIYA configuration, vacA alleles, and adhesin repertoires that together determine the intensity of host interaction and the risk of malignant progression [3] [4] [5] . Identifying patients at highest risk therefore depends on knowing the virulence configuration of the strain they carry. Importantly, selecting an effective eradication regimen requires knowing the strain's antibiotic resistance genotype [6] [7] [8] , as rising antimicrobial resistance has driven first-line treatment success below 90% in most regions, as low as 60% for certain antibiotics in certain regions [9] [10] [11] [12] . Despite four decades of Hp genomic research, this information cannot be efficiently obtained for individual patients in clinical practice.

The barrier is technical rather than conceptual. Hp is exceptionally diverse and individual strains evolve within hosts over years 13 , making whole-genome resolution essential for reliable biological and clinical inference. Nearly all Hp genomic insight has come from culture of clinical isolates. However, culture introduces bias by favoring strains that grow better under laboratory conditions, collapses complex multi-strain infections to a single strain, misses single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and structural variations (SVs), and misrepresents the genomic landscape of Hp as it exists at the gastric mucosa.

Culture-free alternatives have been attempted, but each is bounded by the fundamental challenge of the Hp scarcity of Hp in gastric tissue: Hp constitutes only 10 -3 to 10 -7 of total genomic DNA, with the vast majority derived from human cells 14, 15 . Conventional shotgun metagenomics is cost prohibitive to achieve sufficient Hp coverage. Raman-spectra-based sorting and laser capture microdissection can recover Hp cells prior to sequencing but require specialized instrumentation, scale poorly to clinical throughput 16, 17 . PCR-based clinical assays detect a handful of resistance hotspots and virulence markers but miss important variants because they cannot resolve full genetic variations that shapes infection outcome [18] [19] [20] [21] . Although probe-based enrichment is a generally applicable approach, it is often limited by hybridization bias 22, 23, 24.

Here we report uHp-seq, a culture-free workflow that reconstructs Hp genomes directly from host-dominated clinical specimens by coupling methylation-guided depletion of non-Hp DNA with ultra-low-input sequencing optimized for the trace quantities of the enriched Hp DNA that remain. uHp-seq achieves a thousand-fold enrichment and recovers near-complete genomes from gastric biopsies and minimally invasive gastric string samples across a wide range of Hp abundance. We show that culture systematically misrepresents within-host Hp heterogeneity, missing mixed infections, collapsing co-existing cagA EPIYA variants with distinct oncogenic potential, and flattening gastric regional Hp genomic structure. In contrast, uHp-seq resolves these heterogeneity. Genome-wide profiling of antibiotic resistance variants via uHpseq outperforms PCR-based and probe-based commercial panels. Applied to gastric string samples from patients with refractory infections, uHp-seq delivers comprehensive antibiotic resistance profiles without endoscopy, establishing a practical route to genome-guided eradication therapy.

Results

uHp-seq enables robust culture-free recovery of near-complete Hp genomes

To highlight the challenge of recovering Hp genomes without culturing, we showed that Hp constitutes 10⁻³ to 10⁻ of total sequencing reads in gastric biopsies, with the remainder dominated by host DNA (>99.99%) and a complex non-Hp microbial background, consistent across both public metagenomes (Extended Data Fig. 1a) 14, 15 and our in-house gastric biopsy data (Fig. 1a-b). Direct shotgun metagenomics is prohibitive at this abundance: reaching ≥10×

Hp coverage from a 10⁻ sample requires thousands of gigabases per sample.

We reasoned that a more effective solution would exploit a feature intrinsic to the Hp genome yet less dependent on specific sequences than PCR or probe-based approaches. Hp strains encode on average ~20 DNA methyltransferases (MTases), three of which target highly conserved 4-mer motifs across nearly all clinical isolates: M.HpyAVIII (G5mCGC), M.HpyAI (C6mATG), and M.HpyAIII (G6mATC) (Fig. 1c, Extended Data Fig. 1b), as confirmed in more than 2,000 Hp strains using public datasets [25] [26] [27] and Helicobacter pylori Genome Project (HpGP) analysis 28 . These methylation marks shield Hp DNA from cleavage by motif-matched, methylation-sensitive restriction endonucleases (REases, HhaI, NlaIII, and MboI), while unmethylated host and non-Hp microbial DNA is digested 29 . Human DNA cutting and in silico digestion of ~4,000 reference bacterial genomes confirmed that combined three-REase, HhaI, . NlaIII, and MboI maximized cleavage of background DNA while preserving the largest fraction of Hp DNA as long fragments (Extended Data Fig. 1c-d).

Building on this principle, we developed uHp-seq (Fig. 1d). Genomic DNA from clinical specimens is co-digested with HhaI, NlaIII, and MboI, fragmenting unmethylated background DNA to <1.5 kb while leaving methylation-protected Hp DNA intact at >10 kb (Extended Data Fig. 1d). Long Hp fragments are then isolated by automated size selection on the BluePippin platform. Recovered Hp DNA is barcoded by transposase-mediated tagmentation using MuA transposase for nanopore sequencing (Extended Data Fig. 1g-h) or Tn5 for short-read sequencing (Extended Data Fig. 2).

To confirm that uHp-seq reconstructs the correct genome rather than a chimera or contaminant assembly, we applied uHp-seq to 17 gastric biopsy samples from nine patients and performed whole-genome sequencing (WGS) on paired cultured isolates from each using the same library preparation protocol, but without restriction enzyme digestion or size selection. uHp-seq increased the Hp abundance by over 1,000-20,000-folds (Fig. 1e), and the uHp reads achieved 100% genome completeness for 16 of the 17 samples when mapped to each of the corresponding paired isolate genomes (Fig. 1f). uHp genomes showed >99.9% average nucleotide identity (ANI) to their corresponding isolates (Fig. 1g, Extended Data Fig. 3a).

Importantly, the uHp-recovered genomes reveal greater genomic diversity than present in the paired isolates (Extended Data Fig. 3b). For example, patient P9 harbored a second distinct Hp strain detected in the biopsy but missed by culture (Fig. 1g).

Further applied at a larger scale to 94 gastric biopsies from five collections, including the 17 samples described above (Supplementary Table 1). uHp-seq delivered a median of ~2,500fold enrichment, significantly better than the microbial-enrichment method (MEM) 30 and saponinbased enrichment 31 , two culture-free methods for enriching bacterial genomic DNA from tissues (Extended Data Fig. 4a, b, Supplementary Table 2). uHp-seq recovered Hp genomes.

Notably, uHp-seq recovered Hp genomes from all nine Hp-positive biopsy samples for which culture was not successful. uHp-seq consistently recovered genomes from samples with Hp abundance as low as 10⁻ (100 Hp genome copies in 200 ng of biopsy DNA), a threshold encompassing approximately 91% of gastric biopsy samples based on the observed abundance distribution (Fig. 1b). Reaching ≥10× genome coverage required only ~0.2 Gb of sequencing per sample (Extended Data Fig. 4e), confirming compatibility with population-scale studies on standard sequencing budgets.

uHp-seq reveals within-host Hp strain diversity and virulence heterogeneity

uHp-seq uncovered an additional co-existing Hp strain in patient P9 that had been missed by colony picking and culture (Fig. 1g), prompting a systematic characterization of within-host Hp heterogeneity across the broader samples. Among five patients with paired antrum and fundus biopsies, only one carried near-identical strains across both sites (patient P10, 99.9% ANI). The remaining four showed marked between-site divergence, including distinct strains in antrum versus fundus (patient P12) and mixed infections within a single biopsy (patients P13 and P14, with patient P14 carrying three distinct strains in one sample) (Fig. 1h).

Antrum-fundus pairs showed greater divergence than antrum-corpus pairs (Extended Data Fig. 5a-b), consistent with stronger biogeographic structuring along the proximal-distal axis of the stomach. These findings indicate that Hp genomics of single-site, single-colony, or pooled colonies picked from the primary isolation plates underestimates within-host strain heterogeneity, and that uHp-seq, by recovering native (in vivo) genomes directly from tissue, provides the resolution needed to characterize it.

. To define within-host Hp genomic heterogeneity at SNP resolution, we compared uHpseq genomes against cultured isolates across samples with paired uHp-recovered genomes (single strain) and isolate genomes (Fig. 2a, Supplementary Table 3). SNPs present in vivo but absent from culture comprised ~21,844 positions across the cohort, of which 7,550 were protein-changing (Fig. 2b). When mapped to reference strain Hp26695, most in vivo variants missed by culture were subject-specific, while 576 recurrent SNPs were detected across at least two patients (Fig. 2c-d, Supplementary Tables 4 and 5), including several genes associated with host interaction and gastric adaptation such as TonB-dependent transport genes (frpB2 and tonB2), outer membrane proteins (sabA, hopQ, and babA), flagellar assembly (fliF), and established virulence determinants. uHp-seq also captured ~686 SNPs that differ between gastric regions, with 10 recurrent variants detected across at least two patients (Supplementary Table 6, Fig. 2b; Methods).

We next focused on examining sequence heterogeneity that involves known virulence loci with clinical significance. Virulence-based risk stratification for gastric cancer rests on a set of Hp genes, most prominently the integrity of the cag pathogenicity island (cagPAI) 32 , the EPIYA motif configuration of cagA 4 , and the vacA signal / intermediate / middle-region subtype 33 (Fig. 3a). uHp-seq recovered the full cagPAI and resolved cagA EPIYA configurations in all the 17 biopsy samples with paired isolates. While 14 of the 17 (82.4%) cases showed concordant cagA subtypes between those revealed by uHp and in the paired isolate genomes, patients P2c, P3a and P8c showed different cagA subtypes due to differences in EPIYA-C copy number (Fig. 3b). This discordance was driven by structural variation within cagA itself rather than the presence of distinct strains: a 102 bp insertion altering EPIYA-C copy number generated mixed (EPIYA-C)n (e.g. ABC/ABCC) configurations captured by uHp-seq but collapsed to a single form by culture (Fig. 3c). In addition, a 57 bp deletion converted an EPIYA-AB motif into an EPIYA-B configuration, producing co-existing EPIYA-B and EPIYA-AB forms in the in vivo sample that culture reduced to a single dominant form (Extended Data Fig. 6a). Although patient P2c was classified as a single-strain infection based on isolate genome, uHP-seq data shows that it harbored four distinct cagA subtypes (ABCC, ABCCC, BCC, and BCCC), including variants containing two or three EPIYA-C repeats (Fig. 3d and Extended Data Fig. 6b), whereas the matched cultured isolate yielded only the ABCC subtype with two EPIYA-C repeats. To validate this finding, we performed deep long-read sequencing (~110 Gb) of native genomic DNA from P2c, without Hp enrichment or DNA amplification. This recovered three of the four cagA subtypes (ABCCC, BCCC and ABCC), in agreement with uHp-seq, and missed only the rare minor variant BCC (Extended Data Fig. 6c,d). Importantly, increased EPIYA-C repeat numbers are associated with elevated gastric cancer risk 4 , while emerging evidence suggests that mixed cagA EPIYA genotypes may also be associated with gastric cancer 34, 35 , highlighting the potential clinical relevance of detecting within-host cagA heterogeneity. By directly resolving distinct EPIYA configurations at the single-molecule level, long-read uHp-seq reveals coexisting virulence variants that may be missed by culture-based sequencing. Two additional patterns of cagA heterogeneity were identified by uHp-seq. In patient W3, cagA was detected in only a subset of the Hp population, indicating the coexistence of cagA-positive and cagA-negative strains. In patient P12f, approximately 80% of the Hp population carried two copies of cagA, with the second copy being truncated (Extended Data Fig. 6e). Across a total of 26 cagA-positive biopsy samples, five had mixed cagA subtypes detected by uHp-seq (Fig. 3e, Extended Data Fig. 6f).

The in vivo heterogeneity of cagA and vacA can also be assessed using cultureindependent PCR-based methods, such as the multiplex PCR assay Deeplex HelP (HelP-PCR), a commercial assay targeting both Hp cagA/vacA genotyping and antimicrobial resistance (AMR) detection 21 . To evaluate this, a separate 28 biopsy samples from 28 patients were analyzed using both uHp-seq and HelP (Fig. 3f). Twelve samples (43%) showed discordant cagA . genotyping results. Among these, three appear to be false-positive calls by HelP due to contamination, PCR or the reliance on short read sequencing. Compared to uHP-seq that can directly readout cagA genotypes by long sequencing reads, HelP had one sample carrying an EPIYA-ABC genotype misidentified as EPIYA-AC, and another carrying an EPIYA-BC genotype misidentified as EPIYA-ABC. Six samples harbored two EPIYA-C repeat counts that could not be correctly resolved by HelP-PCR likely because the repeat region could not be accurately resolved by the Illumina short-read sequencing used in HelP-PCR. One mixed cagA subtype infection was also missed by HelP-PCR because of the limited ability of short-read sequencing to resolve mixed repeat structures.

Three samples showed discordant vacA genotypes because HelP-PCR failed to amplify the s, i, or m region, resulting in incomplete genotyping due to primer biases, a long-standing limitation of PCR-based vacA typing 36 . These results demonstrate that culture-independent uHp-seq outperforms PCR-based methods for cagA and vacA genotyping by providing more accurate characterization of virulence gene diversity and heterogeneity.

We next compared uHp-seq with probe-based target enrichment using publicly available sequencing data generated with the Qiagen xHYB (xHYB-probe) Hp whole-genome capture panel 24 . Although both approaches recovered cagA and vacA, their read-depth profiles differed substantially. uHp-seq showed relatively uniform read depth across cagA with both ONT and Illumina sequencing, whereas xHYB showed pronounced troughs at two cagA loci, one of which encompassed the EPIYA region (Fig. 3g). Samples with coverage troughs at these two loci result in more fragmented assembly of cagA (Fig. 3h), indicating that uneven target enrichment may contribute to incomplete cagA recovery and preclude reliable resolution of EPIYA configurations. A similar coverage bias was observed for vacA: xHYB showed reduced read depth in the m-region, particularly in m2 strains, whereas uHp-seq maintained more uniform read depth across this region (Fig. 3i). As a result, none of the 51 m2 strains had complete . vacA recovered by xHYB (Fig. 3j). These results indicate that probe-based enrichment can introduce region-and genotype-dependent coverage biases that affect the recovery and genotyping of Hp virulence genes. Beyond cagA, uHp-seq recovered genomes also enable assessment of other virulence genes such as vacA, babA, sabA, hopQ, and htrA (Extended Data Fig. 6g).

uHp-seq enables full-length AMR gene profiling across six antibiotics used for Hp eradication

Selecting an effective Hp eradication regimen depends on knowing the strain's antibiotic resistance profile [6] [7] [8] . Standard triple therapy and bismuth quadruple therapy combine a proton pump inhibitor with two of the following six antibiotics: amoxicillin (AMX), clarithromycin (CLR), metronidazole (MNZ), tetracycline (TET), levofloxacin (LEV), or rifabutin (RFB) 37 . However, rising resistance has driven first-line success rates below 80% in many regions 9 , and secondand third-line regimens require resistance information that cannot reliably be obtained without culture. For AMR-associated variants, the diagnostic gap is created by limited locus coverage of PCR as well as primer failure with highly variable resistance genes. For CLR and LEV, resistance is dominated by a small number of well-characterized mutations in 23S rRNA and gyrA that hotspot PCR panels cover reliably. For AMX, MNZ, TET, and RFB, however, resistance is determined by variants distributed across multiple loci, and predefined hotspot panels including the widely used commercial multiplex-PCR panels (PyloriAR 20 , HelP 21 ) leave substantial resistance signal undetected. Here we evaluated whether genome-wide recovery via uHp-seq could close this gap across the full eradication antibiotic resistance genes. First, to evaluate the impact of target coverage on AMR prediction, we compared resistance prediction accuracy between the variant set targeted by PyloriAR 20 (a commercial test) and all AMR-associated variants recovered from complete genomes, using 96 isolates with paired whole-genome sequencing and MIC data from five previous studies [38] [39] [40] [41] [42] (Fig. 4a; Methods). For CLR and LEV, both strategies performed equally well because the four canonical hotspot mutations in 23S rRNA and gyrA are well covered by PCR. For MNZ and TET, incorporating additional variants not covered by hotspot loci targeted by PyloriAR significantly improved sensitivity while maintaining the level of specificity (Fig. 4b). For AMX, all 4 resistance-associated variants across the full pbp1A coding sequence can be recovered from complete genomes, whereas PyloriAR's targeted panel covers only 3 of them; the additional variants captured drove a significant improvement in sensitivity and accuracy against phenotypic MIC (Fig. 4b). This gain in sensitivity is accompanied by a slight decrease in specificity, reflecting the inclusion of variants with lower individual effect sizes; however, overall accuracy against phenotypic MIC is significantly higher than the targeted PCR approach (Fig. 4b). For RFB, both strategies performed similarly because they used the same rpoB resistanceassociated mutations, although both showed relatively low sensitivity. Strong RFB resistance mutations are rare and were not observed in this cohort. Additional resistance-associated mutations may be identified in larger studies and can be readily incorporated into the uHp-seq analysis pipeline based on its reliable recovery of near complete Hp genomes (Fig. 1).

A second limitation of PCR-based commercial panels is the potential for primer-bindingsite variation at resistance loci to impair amplification. Among the six major protein-coding resistance genes, four (gyrA, pbp1A, rdxA, rpoB) carry extensive sequence diversity that compromises multiplex PCR amplification across diverse strains (Extended Data Fig. 7a).

Consistent with this, multiplex PCR assays frequently fail to recover rdxA and pbp1A, and incorporating additional targets is constrained by the fundamental complexity of multiplex primer design. In contrast, uHp-seq consistently recovered all eight major resistance loci from genomes with >70% completeness and at least seven of the eight loci from genomes with >50% completeness, covering approximately 90% of samples (Extended Data Fig. 7b).

To evaluate these limitations in a direct head-to-head comparison, we applied uHp-seq and HelP-PCR 21 , targeting a broader set of AMR loci than earlier panels such as PyloriAR, to an independent cohort of 28 biopsy samples from 28 patients with matched MIC measurements (Fig. 4c). Hp genomes were reconstructed from all 28 samples, with 26 achieving >99% genome completeness (Extended Data Fig. 7c-d). Recovery of resistance-associated genes was comparable between uHp-seq and HelP-PCR for CLR, LEV, TET, and RFB. In contrast, uHp-seq successfully recovered the genes associated with MNZ and AMX resistance in 27 of 28 and 26 of 28 samples, respectively. For MNZ, either rdxA or frxA failed to amplify with HelP-PCR in 22 of 28 samples, while the AMX-associated resistance gene failed to amplify in 16 of 28 samples (Fig. 4d, Extended Data Fig. 7e). Consequently, uHp-seq recovered all AMRassociated genes and generated complete AMR profiles (resistance predictions for all six antibiotics) for 25 of 28 patients, compared with only 4 of 28 using HelP-PCR. (Fig. 4e, Extended Data Fig. 7f). We next compared the predicted AMR profiles with phenotypic MIC results. For CLR, LEV, TET, and RFB, both uHp-seq and HelP-PCR made concordant AMR calls against MIC. For MNZ and AMX, however, uHp-seq showed substantially higher concordance, primarily because the corresponding resistance genes were not successfully amplified by HelP-PCR (Fig. 4f). When samples with failed AMR calls were excluded, uHp-seq and HelP-PCR showed equivalent performance (Extended Data Fig. 7g), confirming that uHpseq's advantage lies in robust genome coverage and amplification reliability rather than variant calling accuracy.

To further evaluate the performance of uHp-seq in AMR detection, we compared it with the Qiagen xHYB (xHYB-probe) kit, a probe-based hybridization capture method that attempts to recover the whole Hp genome, based on the publicly available sequencing data 24 . Unlike PCR-based AST, which targets predefined resistance-associated mutations, probe-based enrichment enables broader recovery of AMR-associated genes. uHp-seq and xHYB-probe showed comparable read depth at AMR-associated SNP sites in 23S, gyrA, rdxA, HP0399, and rpoB (Extended Data Fig. 7h). However, xHYB-probe exhibited reduced read depth at resistance-associated SNP sites in frxA, pbp1A, and 16S compared with uHp-seq (Fig. 4g In contrast to virulence loci, where culture reshapes the in vivo variant landscape, AMR profiles were highly concordant between uHp-seq and paired culture-derived whole-genome sequencing in both single-strain and mixed-strain infections from the same gastric region, with no evidence that culture systematically selects for the SNPs used for determining resistance in this study (Fig. 4i, Extended Data Fig. 7k,l). AMR profiles were also largely concordant between paired uHp genomes from different gastric regions, except in some mixed-strain infections where regional differences in strain composition produced distinct resistance profiles (Fig. 4j), further reinforcing that within-host AMR heterogeneity reflects strain structure rather than regional genomic variation. Integrating AMR profiles across multiple gastric regions improved resistance determination for individual antibiotics (Fig. 4k), enabling complete resistance assignments for all 14 patients with biopsies collected from at least two gastric regions.

uHp-seq from minimally invasive gastric string samples delivers actionable AMR profiles

To broaden the use of uHp-seq, we performed a study of gastric string samples from esophageal string test (EST), in which a swallowed capsule retrieves gastric secretions and mucus along its length 43 , representing a minimally invasive alternative to endoscopic biopsy for Hp genomics. We applied uHp-seq to 19 string samples from patients including 11 refractory Hp infection following at least two failed eradication regimens. Paired isolates, MIC data, and clinical outcome data were available for retrospective validation in 10 of 19 patients (Fig. 5a).

Beyond reduced invasiveness, gastric strings traverse the full proximal-distal axis of the stomach, integrating Hp populations across fundus, corpus, and antrum simultaneously, potentially capturing regional heterogeneity that single or limited biopsy sampling misses. uHp-seq efficiently depleted host DNA and enriched Hp reads from all 19 string samples (Fig. 5b, Extended Data Fig. 8a), recovering high-completeness Hp genomes across all samples (Fig. 5c). Reconstructed string-uHp genomes showed high ANI to paired cultured isolates (Fig. 5d), confirming faithful genome recovery from gastric string samples. String samples also revealed mixed infections, including string sample D (Fig. 5d), consistent with the within-host strain heterogeneity documented in biopsy samples and further illustrating the advantage of stomach-wide sampling.

Virulence genotyping and AMR profiles derived from string sample-based uHp genomes were comprehensive and largely concordant with paired Hp isolates cultured from the same patient (Supplementary Table 7, Extended Data Fig. 8b). Predicted AMR profiles showed high consistency with phenotypic MIC measurements (Extended Data Fig. 8c). uHp-seq resistance predictions largely explained treatment failures and successes in longitudinally monitored patients, demonstrating that culture-free genomic profiling alone can provide clinically actionable information (Fig. 5e; Methods).

For example, in patient I, who underwent nine courses of Hp eradication therapy, the string-uHp AMR profile was consistent with the observed treatment history in the context of persistent colonization. The resistance profile explained failure of the eighth regimen by identifying resistance to CLR and further predicted failure of the ninth regimen due to persistent LEV and TET resistance, which was confirmed by clinical Hp testing (Fig. 5f).

In patient J, who underwent seven courses of treatment, genome comparison of string-uHp sample collected immediately after the sixth treatment with prior cultured isolates indicated mixed-strain infection, in which one strain was eradicated by the sixth treatment while the other persisted (Fig. 5g, Extended Data Fig. 8d). Based on the string-uHp resistance profile, the seventh regimen was predicted to succeed, which was in fact shown by the subsequent clinical Hp test outcome.

Discussion

uHp-seq enables highly sensitive, culture-free recovery of Hp genomes directly from clinical specimens, yielding near-complete genomes from biopsies and minimally invasive string samples. By recovering Hp genomes as they exist in vivo, uHp-seq opens new opportunities to study the native biology of Hp host interaction and within-host adaptation that culture has obscured, while also enabling more precise and scalable genome-guided eradication therapy.

Decades of virulence-factor catalogs [44] [45] [46] [47] [48] [49] , pangenome studies [50] [51] [52] [53] , Hp adaptation studies [54] [55] [56] [57] [58] and cancer risk associations [59] [60] [61] [62] [63] [64] , including large-scale resources such as HpGP 28 , have been built on cultured isolates and have therefore inherited the limitations of culture-based approaches. Beyond individual loci, within-host strain heterogeneity is substantially more complex than previously appreciated: mixed infections, regional structuring along the proximaldistal axis of the stomach, and individual informative loci such as cagA that harbor up to four distinct configurations within a single patient. In addition, uHp-seq identified truncating variants in multiple genes related to TonB-dependent iron and nickel acquisition that were absent from the corresponding cultured isolates. Because Hp encodes multiple TonB-ExbB-ExbD systems and FrpB/FecA-family transporters with potential functional redundancy 65 , disruption of individual components may attenuate rather than abolish metal acquisition, potentially reflecting adaptation to the nutrient landscape of the gastric niche. Their disappearance after culture further suggests that such in vivo-adapted variants may be selectively disadvantaged under laboratory growth conditions, illustrating how culture can erase biologically relevant within-host variation before genomic analysis. These findings argue that core questions of Hp population structure, host adaptation, and within-host evolution should be revisited with culture-free genomic approaches.

For virulence genotyping, uHp-seq has important advantages over existing approaches: it captures the in vivo virulence landscape that culture distorts, and it resolves full locus configurations including the intact ~40 kb cagPAI, structural variants within cagA, and the highly variable adhesin repertoire, which PCR panels do not reliably cover. In addition, given that EPIYA-C copy number is associated with CagA activity 66 , uHp-seq reliably resolved EPIYA-C copy-number variation that was missed by probe based target-enrichment due to hybridization biases. The most striking virulence finding is that individual hosts simultaneously harbor multiple co-existing cagA EPIYA variants with distinct oncogenic potential, a within-host heterogeneity that culture collapses to a single dominant type. EPIYA configuration is a primary determinant of CagA-mediated oncogenic signaling through SHP-2 67 , and the co-existence of variants with distinct oncogenic potential within a single host means that culture-based EPIYA typing misrepresents the true virulence landscape of the infection. A patient presumed by a clinician to carry a low-risk EPIYA configuration may simultaneously harbor a high-risk variant that was not recovered by culture. Current gastric cancer risk models built on culture-derived EPIYA classification may therefore be systematically underestimating risk in patients whose most oncogenic variant is culture-disadvantaged. uHp-seq provides the genomic substrate to build risk scores that reflect the full in vivo virulence distribution rather than its culture-selected proxy 61, 68.

The culture-biased view of Hp virulence extends beyond cagA. Whole-genome recovery across all virulence loci reveals that the adhesin repertoire (including babA, sabA, hopQ, and htrA) has been similarly affected. In addition, we found that genome-wide strain typing similarly exposes limitations of current practice: PCR-based 7-gene MLST 69 , the standard culture-free typing approach, recovered all seven housekeeping genes (atpA, efp, mutY, ppa, trpC, ureI, and yphC) in only 22% of samples using HelP-PCR, compared with 93% using uHp-seq. While HelP-PCR provided only coarse geographic population assignments, uHp-seq enabled highresolution genome-wide strain typing into 17 subpopulations as defined by the HpGP 28 . Using the high-resolution geographic population classification system, MLST achieved only 42-50% concordance with genome-wide geographic population structure analysis across the two cohorts.

(Extended Data Fig. 9), with implications for the population-level inferences about Hp diversity and cancer risk associations that MLST-based studies have generated. Taken together, these findings indicate that the full virulence landscape of Hp, across cagPAI, EPIYA configuration, adhesins, and strain identity, has been assessed through a culture-distorted lens, and that uHpseq provides the substrate to revisit it.

For AMR-associated variants, our study showed that the diagnostic gap here owes to the restricted locus coverage of commercial PCR and probe panels, as well as primer failure on highly variable resistance genes. uHp-seq genome-wide recovery outperforms targeted PCR panels for AMX and MNZ specifically, where resistance is determined by variants distributed across full gene sequences rather than a small number of canonical hotspots. Reliable determination of AMX susceptibility is increasingly important with the introduction of . vonoprazan-amoxicillin dual therapy as a first-line treatment option 37, 70 . This regimen uses a single antibiotic, reduces exposure to other antibiotics, and is generally well tolerated. Because its efficacy relies directly on AMX activity, accurate detection of AMX resistance before treatment could help guide appropriate use and avoid treatment failure. For CLR and LEV, where resistance is dominated by a handful of well-characterized mutations, targeted panels perform equivalently, confirming that whole-genome recovery adds value precisely where PCR's structural constraints have the largest effects. Another advantage of uHp-seq over multiplex PCR panels is that newly emerging resistance variants are readily incorporated into profiling as the genome is recovered whole, without primer redesign. In contrast, multiplex PCR panels must be deliberately redesigned to add new targets, a slow process constrained by primer complexity in variable regions like pbp1A and rdxA.

Beyond accurate resistance genotyping, the gastric string cohort reveals that stringbased uHp-seq is not merely a less invasive alternative to endoscopic biopsy but a more comprehensive one. By traversing the full proximal-distal axis of the stomach, the string integrates Hp populations across fundus, corpus, and antrum simultaneously. The regional virulence and AMR heterogeneity documented in biopsy samples, where distinct cagA subtype compositions and resistance profiles were identified across gastric regions in patients with mixed-strain infections (~10% of samples), highlights that single-site sampling is inherently incomplete for characterizing the true within-host Hp landscape. Multi-region biopsy sampling generated complete AMR profiles for all 14 patients versus single-site sampling, and the gastric string achieves the equivalent of that multi-region integration in a single procedure not requiring endoscopy. For both virulence risk stratification and resistance-guided eradication therapy, stomach-wide sampling is clinically advantageous, and the gastric string is currently the only practical way to achieve it without endoscopy.

. In this study, uHp-seq requires Hp at ≥10⁻ relative abundance to reconstruct Hp genomes, a threshold met by ~90% biopsies and string samples. While we demonstrated uHpseq on gastric biopsies and string samples, extension to other clinically relevant sample types, including fixed gastric biopsies, gastric juice, stool, and saliva, will be optimized in future work.

Although the gastric string cohort provides informative retrospective validation of uHp-seqguided therapy selection, prospective validation studies in independent cohorts with integrated treatment-outcome data will be required to establish the clinical utility of routine deployment of uHp-seq.

Despite these constraints, uHp-seq establishes a foundation for culture-free Hp genomics, making the in vivo within-host bacterial population accessible at clinical scale. By recovering genomes directly from host-dominated specimens, this approach opens a practical path toward precision eradication therapy, more accurate gastric cancer risk stratification, and potentially a broader framework for direct genome recovery from other clinical specimens where culture has long been the only option. Methods

Sample collection

Gastric biopsy and EST samples were obtained from multiple cohorts and studies. All samples were collected with informed consent and under the respective institutional review board (IRB) approvals, unless otherwise specified below. Collection 1, 2: 41 gastric biopsies were collected . at the University of Magdeburg (IRB no. 80/11) 72 and Collection 3:18 gastric biopsies were collected at the Veterans Affairs Medical Center in Nashville (IRB no. 5910) 73

Esophageal string test (EST) sample collection:

The EST is a minimally invasive, FDA-registered device (EnteroTracker; EnteroTrack) consisting of an adsorbent nylon fiber string housed within a weighted gelatin capsule, designed to collect biofluid from the upper gastrointestinal tract without endoscopy 75 . The 140-cm string was used for all samples, and the string was left in place for 1 hour prior to retrieval. Patient height was used to estimate the distance to the lower esophageal sphincter (LES) using the manufacturer-provided formula (6.7 + 0.226 x height [cm]). The portion of string distal to this point was used for analysis.

Hp isolation from gastric specimens and culture

Hp isolation at Vanderbilt University: Hp was isolated from antral and corpus biopsies, as previously reported 76 . Briefly, specimens were homogenized in sterile saline. Serial dilutions were plated onto selective Trypticase Soy Agar (TSA) with 5% sheep blood. Agar plates were incubated under microaerobic conditions at 37°C for 4 days. Hp was confirmed by positive oxidase and urease tests and typical appearance on a Gram stain. Hp isolation at Montefiore Medical Center: Hp culture was performed for routine clinical care at the Montefiore Medical Center Clinical Microbiology Laboratory, in accordance with standard operating procedures.

Gastric biopsy specimens were plated onto Columbia with nalidixic acid and 5% sheep blood agar plates (BD BBL, Sparks, Maryland) and incubated under microaerophilic conditions at 37°C for up to 7 days. Identification of Hp was confirmed by positive catalase, oxidase, and rapid urease testing.

Hp isolation at National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases:

After EST, gastric antral biopsies from patients with confirmed Hp infection were cultured on blood agar (BHI + 5% sheep blood) and on a selective CVA (cefsulodin-vancomycinamphotericin B) supplemented plate, both incubated under microaerophilic conditions at 35°C. Hp and H. bilis reference strains were run in parallel on BHI plates as quality controls. Colonies with morphology consistent with Hp were confirmed by MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry.

Hp isolates stock were recovered on TSA with 5% sheep blood at 37°C in 5% CO 2 for 2-3 days, all colonies were collected into one tube for DNA extraction.

DNA mocks

Human DNA (extracted from human lymphoblastoid cell line culture) and DNA extracted from Hp-negative human stool (confirmed by both the urea breath test and stool antigen test) were used separately as background DNA. Human DNA, Hp-negative stool DNA, and Hp26695 DNA were separately sheared to ~10 kb using g-TUBEs (Covaris, #520079). Mock mixtures were prepared by spiking Hp26695 DNA into either human DNA or Hp-negative stool DNA at Hp DNA abundances ranging from 1 to 1.0 × 10⁻ , with a total DNA input of 1 µg per mixture.

Restriction enzyme digestion and DNA fragment analysis

Human, Hp, or mock DNA (200 ng -3ug) was incubated with methylation-sensitive restriction endonucleases HhaI, NlaIII, and MboI alone or combination as indicated in corresponding figures (New England Biolabs, Cat # R0139S, Cat # R0125, Cat # R0147M) at 37 °C for 30 min.

The REase was then heat-inactivated at 65 °C for 20 min, and DNA fragment size distributions were analyzed using a TapeStation or 0.9% agarose gel electrophoresis.

QC of input DNA

Input DNA was required to be high quality, pure total genomic DNA meeting the following criteria: (a) purity measured by Nanodrop, OD 260/280 of 1.8 and OD 260/230 of 2.0-2.2; (b) . average fragment size measured by electrophoresis (>10 kb); (c) Input mass measured by Qubit, ideally 200 ng in total.

qPCR measure Hp abundance in input DNA

A standard curve was generated using human and Hp DNA mock samples. Mock DNA or Input DNA was diluted to 10 ng/µl for qPCR. Each 10 µl qPCR reaction contained 5 µl TaqMan Fast Advanced Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, Cat # 4444556), 0.25 µl forward primer (OYJL234) (5'-GAGCTGTCTCAACCAGAGATT-3'), 0.25 µl reverse primer (OYJL235) (5'-GCAGTGCTAAGTTGTAGTAAAGGTC-3'), 0.125 µl probe (OYJL236) (5'-FAM/TG GAG GTG AAA TTC CTC CTA CCC G/3'-TAMRA) 77 , 10 ng template DNA and nuclease-free water.

Reactions were run on a QuantStudio 6 Flex using the fast cycling program: 50 °C for 2 min; 95 °C for 20 s; followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 1 s and 60 °C for 20 s.

uHp-seq enrichment and library preparation

Genomic

Microbial-enrichment method (MEM)

MEM was performed using 100 mg of fresh gastric tissue as previously described 30 . Following bead beating, the homogenized tissue was divided into two 150µl aliquots. The first aliquot was subjected to the subsequent MEM enrichment steps. The second aliquot was processed using the QIAamp DNA Microbiome Kit (Qiagen, Cat # 51704), in which human cells remaining intact in the liquid fraction after bead beating were further selectively lysed with saponin, and the released human DNA was digested with Benzonase before bacterial cell lysis and DNA extraction.

Direct deep long-read sequencing

DNA from gastric biopsy or string samples was extracted as described above, without any host DNA depletion or enrichment procedures. ONT sequencing libraries were prepared using the Native Barcoding Kit (SQK-NBD114.24) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Libraries were sequenced on the PromethION platform to generate sufficient sequencing depth to obtain adequate Hp reads for whole-genome assembly.

ONT sequencing data processing

Raw ONT reads were base called and demultiplexed using Dorado (v0.4.1) 78 with the dna_r10.4.1_e8.2_400bps_sup.cfg model for R10.4 flow cells and the emit-fast flag to produce per-barcode FASTQ files. Barcodes yielding fewer than 10 Mb of sequence were excluded.

Sequencing statistics (total reads, read length N50, mean length) were computed using seqkit 79.

Metagenomic classification and community profiling

Demultiplexed reads were classified using Kraken2 (v2.0.9b) 80 against the PlusPF database (comprising RefSeq archaea, bacteria, viruses, plasmids, fungi, protozoa, and the human genome). Taxonomic reports were filtered to species-and unclassified-level entries. To obtain base-resolved community profiles, Kraken2 raw output was parsed to extract per-read base-pair . assignments; NCBI taxonomic lineage was appended using TaxonKit 81 with the NCBI taxonomy database (downloaded February 2023). Community profiles were visualized as stacked bar charts in R (v4.2.0) using the tidyverse and Polychrome packages.

uHp-seq enrichment analysis

Enrichment efficiency was quantified by comparing paired input and uHP-seq-enriched libraries for each sample using two orthogonal metrics: (1) Hp fractional abundance derived from Kraken2 classification reports 80 , and (2) Hp read mapping rate determined by aligning reads to the Hp26695 reference genome (GCF_000307795.1; NC_018939.1) with Minimap2 (v2.24; -ax map-ont) 82 , with mapping statistics extracted by SAMtools (v1.15.1) flagstat 83 . The enrichment fold was calculated as the ratio of the respective metric in the enriched library to that in the matched input library. Genome breadth of coverage and mean sequencing depth were computed with SAMtools coverage 83.

Hp read extraction and de novo genome assembly

Reads classified as Hp by Kraken2 were extracted per barcode to produce HP-only FASTQ files. De novo assembly was performed with Flye (v2.9) 84 using the nano-hq flag and 30 threads. For barcodes with evidence of mixed or heterogeneous Hp populations, assembly was additionally performed in metagenome mode (meta). Assembly completeness and contamination were assessed with CheckM (lineage_wf workflow, 8 threads) 85 . Read-based genome coverage was calculated as the fraction of the matched isolate genome (or the closest publicly available reference genome when a matched isolate was unavailable) covered by at least one read, using read depths in 100-bp bins.

Strain-level assembly refinement and ANI estimation

For each species-level haplotype-phased assembly, contigs were further resolved into strainlevel assemblies using Strainberry v1.1 86 , which separates strain-specific haplotypes using longread-supported phasing information. The resulting strain-resolved contigs were binned using the binning module of metaWRAP v1.3.2 87 , and the quality of reconstructed long-read metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) was assessed and refined using the bin_refinement module of metaWRAP. Pairwise average nucleotide identity (ANI) among Hp strain-level assemblies was calculated using fastANI v1.32 88.

Nucleotide diversity (pi / π) of uHp genomes and cultured isolates

Within-sample nucleotide diversity pi was compared between uHp genomes and cultured isolates using Illumina short-read data. Reads were deduplicated, screened against phiX, adapter-trimmed, quality-trimmed and length-filtered (minimum 75 bp), then aligned to the Hp26695 reference genome (GCF_000307795.1, NC_018939.1; 1,667,892 bp) with Minimap2 v2.30 (-a -x sr --secondary=yes) and retained at mapping quality ≥ 30 (SAMtools v1.23). To make samples directly comparable, each alignment was randomly downsampled to a common depth of 1,375,000 mapped reads with Picard DownsampleSam v3.4.0 (STRATEGY=Chained, ACCURACY=1e-4), and the analysis was repeated over 10 independent downsampling rounds.

At each reference position, pi was calculated from the per-base read counts as the proportion of pairwise differences among the reads covering that position. Genome-wide pi was the mean across all reference positions, coding pi the mean across coding positions, and per-gene pi the mean across the positions of each of the 1,485 annotated genes; reported values are means across the 10 rounds.

Geographic subpopulation assignment of Hp strains was performed using two complementary approaches. DAPC on core-genome SNPs. Each query assembly was aligned to the Hp26695 reference with snippy (ctgs), and a core-genome SNP alignment (632,909 informative positions) was generated with snippy-core together with 1,199 geographically annotated genomes from the HpGP 28 . Queries were projected into the discriminant space of a DAPC model pre-trained on the reference panel (200 principal components, 17-class LDA) and assigned to the nearest population centroid by Euclidean distance. Assignment confidence was quantified as the relative margin (d2 -d1)/d2, where d1 and d2 are the distances to the top-and second-ranked centroids; margins >0.5 were classified as high confidence, 0.2-0.5 moderate, and <0.2 low.

Subpopulation labels (17 subpopulations) were taken from the fineSTRUCTURE 94 -based classification reported by Thorell et al 28 MLST-based approach. For assembled genomes, 7gene MLST was performed using the standard Hp scheme (pubMLST Helicobacter scheme 1 95 ; loci: atpA, efp, mutY, ppa, trpC, ureI, yphC). Each assembly was queried against the pubMLST allele database by BLASTn (≥85% nucleotide identity, ≥90% allele-length coverage); the bestmatching allele per locus was selected by identity then alignment length, and the resulting profile was matched to the pubMLST sequence type (ST) table. Because STs are not themselves informative of geographic ancestry, subpopulation was inferred by allele sharing against the HpGP reference panel of 1,200 fineSTRUCTURE-labelled genomes, typed by the identical BLASTn procedure: allelic proximity to each reference strain was defined as 1 -(fraction of commonly typed loci with different alleles), proximity was averaged within each of the 17 subpopulations, and the subpopulation with the highest mean proximity was reported with a confidence margin (difference in mean proximity between the top and runner-up subpopulation).

Virulence factor analysis.

Virulence genes were identified from the Prokka-annotated assemblies by BLASTp (BLAST+ 2.16.0) of all predicted proteins against a reference panel of cagA (WP_267286128.1), VacA (AAD07935.1) and HtrA (AAD08063.1), with E-value < 1×10⁻ ; hits were retained at ≥70% amino-acid identity, alignment length ≥300 aa and bit score >500, and the corresponding gene and protein sequences extracted with seqkit. Antimicrobial resistance prediction

AMR-associated variants were detected at a curated panel of resistance loci targeting clarithromycin (CLR; 23S rRNA, both chromosomal copies), levofloxacin (LEV; gyrA), amoxicillin (AMX; pbp1), metronidazole (MNZ; rdxA, frxA), tetracycline (TET; 16S rRNA, both chromosomal copies, HP0399) and rifampin (RIF; rpoB) (Rifabutin is used for Hp treatment and rifampin is used for MIC test). Detection of any mutation in the curated resistance mutation panel was considered indicative of resistance to the corresponding antibiotic. Resistance positions were encoded in a curated BED file (see Supplementary table 8) distinguishing Tier-1 positions (allele-specific, e.g., 23S rRNA A2143G/A2144G) from Tier-2 positions (codon-level, requiring amino acid annotation to confirm pathogenicity, e.g., gyrA, rpoB). For ONT raw reads, reads were aligned to the Hp26695 reference with Minimap2 82 and variants at AMR loci called by BCFtools (v1.22) mpileup and call 83 in haploid mode (-m), with minimum mapping quality Q20, . minimum base quality Q20, and a maximum pileup depth of 100,000. Sites with depth < 20× were reported as indeterminate (ND) rather than wild-type. For Illumina data, BWA-MEM 90 alignment and LoFreq 91 calling were applied as described above. For genome assemblies, nucmer-based 96 whole-genome alignment to the reference was used. All calls were annotated with snpEff 71 and cross-referenced against the curated resistance amino acid table to resolve Tier-2 positions as confirmed resistant (R) or background variant. For CLR, 23S rRNA heteroplasmy was assessed by computing allele frequencies across both rRNA gene copies; positions with AF between 10% and 80% were flagged as heteroplasmic (R_HET), consistent with a single-copy mutation appearing at ~50% AF in haploid-equivalent ONT pileups.

Hp AMR detection with HelP-PCR

From the total DNA extracted, targeted amplification of Hp genomic content was performed using GenoScreen's Deeplex HelP kit as per manufacturer's instructions. The PCR amplicons were purified using AMPure XP magnetic beads (Beckman Coulter, USA) and quantified using the Qubit double-stranded DNA Broad Range Assay Kit (Invitrogen Inc.). Following the amplicon purification, library preparation was performed using the Nextera XT Library Preparation Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA) following the manufacturer's protocol with the following modifications -Hp DNA amplicons were diluted to 0.2 ng/uL in molecular grade nuclease-free water (Sigma-Aldrich, USA), for a 1 ng of DNA mass input into the library preparation workflow. AXP magnetic beads (Beckman Coulter, USA) was used to purify the Hp DNA libraries following library amplification. Final DNA library quantification was performed using the Qubit double-stranded High Sensitivity Assay Kit (Invitrogen Inc.). The libraries were then normalized to a final concentration of 2 nM each and pooled. Sequencing of these libraries was performed on the Illumina MiSeq Platform using the MiSeq v2 Micro Sequencing Kit (300 cycles; 2 x 150 bp) with 1% bacteriophage phiX control (Illumina, San Diego, CA). Sequencing FastQ files were uploaded to GenoScreen's secure server for analysis.

Comparison with probe-based capture enrichment xHYB-probe

To benchmark uHp-seq against hybridization-capture enrichment, we reanalyzed the public QIAseq xHYB Hp whole-genome sequencing dataset (ENA PRJEB110105) 24 , comprising 104 Illumina NextSeq 500 paired-end (2 × 151 bp) libraries prepared directly from 28 fresh and 70 formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) gastric biopsies. The deposited reads had been filtered to only preserve Helicobacter reads, so enrichment efficiency could not be compared; the comparison was restricted to resistance genotyping and virulence-gene recovery. The uHpseq comparator comprised the 87 biopsy genomes in Supplementary Table 2 that were sequenced by uHp-seq and yielded an assembly.

Read processing and assembly. Reads were trimmed with fastp (v0.23.2) 89 , aligned to Hp26695 (NC_018939.1) with BWA-MEM (v0.7.17) 90 ., and PCR duplicates were removed with SAMtools (v1.16) 83 . All 104 libraries were assembled without depth filtering, from all nonduplicate read pairs (downsampled to 150× where deeper), with SPAdes (v3.13.1; --careful).

Contigs <500 bp, and contigs assigned by Kraken2 to lineages outside Helicobacter, were removed from xHYB and uHp-seq assemblies alike. Libraries that failed to assemble were kept in all denominators.

Resistance variants. AMR-associated variants were called from xHYB reads with the uHp-seq Illumina pipeline (Antimicrobial resistance prediction), with sites below 20× reported as indeterminate; a 5× threshold was also tested as a sensitivity analysis.

Virulence genes.

Virulence genes were scored as described for uHp-seq (Virulence factor analysis). Read depth along cagA and vacA was profiled after assigning reads to their closest RefSeq allele with DIAMOND blastx (v2.2.0), using uHp-seq libraries as no-capture controls.

Selection of Hp eradication regimens

Five Hp eradication regimens were considered based on the 2024 American College of Gastroenterology clinical guideline: optimized bismuth quadruple therapy (BQT; PPI, bismuth, MNZ, and TET), rifabutin triple therapy (omeprazole, RFB, and AMX), PCAB dual therapy (vonoprazan and AMX), PCAB triple therapy (vonoprazan, CLR, and AMX), and levofloxacin triple therapy (PPI, LEV, and AMX). Because each of these regimens contains either AMX or MNZ, resistance information for both antibiotics was required to comprehensively evaluate regimen availability. A standard treatment regimen was considered available if all antibiotics included in the regimen were predicted to be susceptible. Samples for which no standard regimen was identified were further classified as having no available standard regimen due to multidrug resistance or an incomplete AMR profile.

String-sample validation cohort and treatment-outcome analysis

String samples were obtained from treatment-naive patients or refractory Hp patients following at least two failed eradication regimens, with clinical outcome data available. Treatment outcome was defined by stool antigen test. The biopsies were also collected for Hp isolation and phenotypic MIC testing. uHp-seq predicted resistance profiles were compared with phenotypic . MIC data. For non-RFB-containing regimens, a regimen was predicted to fail if resistance was detected to any antibiotic included in the regimen. For RFB-containing regimens, failure was predicted only when resistance to RFB or both RFB and at least one other antibiotic in the regimen was detected. Predicted treatment outcomes were then compared with the clinical eradication outcomes.

Statistics and reproducibility

Sample sizes were determined by specimen availability across the contributing sites. All genomic comparisons were paired within patients (biopsy versus matched isolate, or antrum versus corpus), so no randomization or blinding was applicable. Samples with evidence of multistrain co-infection were excluded from the SNP category analyses as listed above; no other data were excluded.

Statistical tests, sample numbers and the meaning of any centre line or error bar are stated in the corresponding figure legends. Where a family of related comparisons was made, P values were adjusted by the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure and asterisks denote FDR < 0.05 (*), < 0.01 (**) and < 0.001 (***). Concordance between genomic predictions and phenotypic or clinical outcomes is reported as counts and percentages of patients. Analyses were performed in R and Python, with versions given in the corresponding Methods sections.
