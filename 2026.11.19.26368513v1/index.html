Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Full text

Abstract

A diagnosis of mismatch repair deficiency (MMRd) can unlock immunotherapy treatment for a patient. However, existing sequencing-based detection methods require matched normal samples, panel-specific baselines or dedicated microsatellite loci, none of which are available to the tumour-only targeted panels that dominate routine clinical practice. Here we present captureMMRd, an open-source classifier that diagnoses MMRd using mutational signatures computed from the somatic variant calls any panel already produces. Applying a single locked model to 7,514 samples from 16 independent cohorts, spanning whole-exome, whole-genome, targeted panel and plasma cfDNA sequencing, captureMMRd achieved a pooled sensitivity of 94.36% and specificity of 99.02% against orthogonal MMR IHC and MSI-PCR testing, and outperformed microsatellite-based callers on panels with limited microsatellite content. We demonstrate that as few as 3 indels and 44 single-base substitutions are sufficient for reliable diagnosis, defining a minimum panel footprint that most clinically deployed assays already exceed. Our findings extend clinically actionable MMRd detection to the sequencing workflows of routine precision oncology, with the potential to broaden patient access to immunotherapy without new assays, baselines or matched normal samples.

Introduction

Loss of mismatch repair proficiency in a tumour leads to the accumulation of replication errors, particularly at microsatellite loci, generating a microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H) phenotype and a high burden of tumour neoantigens. Accurate identification of MMRd/MSI-H tumours therefore has direct therapeutic relevance, most notably for selecting patients who may benefit from immune checkpoint blockade [1] [2] [3] , for Lynch syndrome screening 4, 5 , and for prognostic and predictive information in several solid tumours 6, 7 . This has made MMRd/MSI-H testing an essential component of modern precision oncology, extending beyond colorectal and endometrial cancer into pan-cancer diagnostic workflows 8 . Clinical assessment of MMRd rests on immunohistochemistry (IHC) for loss of expression of the mismatch repair proteins MLH1, PMS2, MSH2 and MSH6, and/or PCR-based testing of microsatellite instability across a small set of markers [8] [9] [10] . These assays are well established and remain the clinical standard in many settings, but each captures only part of the biology of MMRd. IHC provides protein-level evidence of MMR pathway disruption yet can be affected by heterogeneous staining, retained expression of non-functional proteins and interpretative variability, while MSI-PCR directly measures microsatellite instability but interrogates a limited number of loci, with performance that varies across tumour types and sample contexts [11] [12] [13].

Next-generation sequencing (NGS) offers an opportunity to integrate MMRd assessment into the molecular profiling many patients already receive. Sequencing-based approaches can simultaneously evaluate MSI, tumour mutational burden (TMB), mutational patterns and pathogenic alterations in MMR genes, providing a more comprehensive readout than singlemodality testing [14] [15] [16] . These approaches span whole-genome (WGS) and whole-exome (WES) sequencing, through to comprehensive targeted panels, and MSI status is now reported by clinically implemented assays such as MSK-IMPACT and FoundationOne CDx as part of broader genomic profiling [17] [18] [19] . Yet each of these readouts is an imperfect proxy for MMRd. Classification based on MMR pathogenic variants can miss tumours driven by MLH1 promoter methylation, non-coding or regulatory alterations, or other epigenetic mechanisms not routinely captured by standard clinical panels [20] [21] [22] . TMB is enriched in MMRd tumours but is not specific to MMRd, as POLE/POLD1-deficient tumours can show extremely high TMB without MMRd 23, 24.

However, current sequencing-based MSI/MMRd detection remains anchored to the assay it was built for. Most methods infer instability by reading dedicated microsatellite loci, requiring matched normal sequencing 25, 26 , a panel-specific baseline of microsatellite-stable samples 27, 28 , models pre-trained on specific platforms 29 or on assay-specific sequencing reads 30 , or proprietary microsatellite content designed into commercial panels 19, 31, 32 ; even recent reference-free approaches require sufficient evaluable microsatellite loci on the panel 33 , with performance that varies with assay design and tumour type 30, 34, 35 . Mutationbased alternatives have so far been limited to single-feature heuristics, such as homopolymer indel counts calibrated against IHC in colorectal cancer within a single institutional assay 36 . The mutational consequences of MMRd are nonetheless directly visible in the somatic variant calls every panel already produces, and formal mutational-signature analysis capturing the full MMRd repertoire (SBS6, SBS14, SBS15, SBS20, SBS21, SBS26, SBS44, ID1, ID2 and ID7) [37] [38] [39] [40] [41] [42] [43] has not been developed into a validated pan-cancer diagnostic. Yet most routine clinical NGS is performed using tumour-only capture sequencing, often to reduce cost, tissue requirements and turnaround time [44] [45] [46] [47] , on panels that vary substantially in size, genomic content and microsatellite coverage, and that are processed through heterogeneous local bioinformatic pipelines. As a result, laboratories running tumour-only panels, particularly smaller or custom designs, have no validated route to MMRd assessment: they lack the matched normals, the baseline cohorts and the microsatellite content that existing methods assume. There is therefore a critical unmet need for an open, pan-cancer, panel-agnostic method that infers MMRd status directly from the somatic variant calls every targeted panel already captures, independent of microsatellite coverage, baselines or matched normal samples.

Here, we present captureMMRd, an open-source, capture-based classifier that reads MMRd-associated SBS and indel mutational signatures from routine tumour-only variant calls (Figure 1a). We train the model on tumour-normal WES data with orthogonal MSI-PCR reference testing and validate it, locked and without retraining, across 16 independent cohorts spanning tumour-normal WES, WGS and targeted panels, tumour-only institutional and custom panels, and plasma cfDNA. We benchmark captureMMRd against existing tumour-only MSI callers, assess its robustness to sample preservation method, and derive the minimum input for a reliable diagnosis (as few as 3 indels and 44 single-base substitutions) translating into a minimum panel footprint that most clinically deployed assays already exceed. By operating on the output sequencing workflows already generate, captureMMRd extends clinically actionable MMRd detection to the heterogeneous tumouronly panels used in routine precision oncology.

Results

Mutational signatures are sufficient to diagnose MMRd from targeted sequencing

To identify the minimal genomic readout required for tumour-only MMRd diagnosis, we assessed a broad set of candidate NGS-derived features capturing complementary consequences of MMR loss: MSI scores, TMB, pathogenic alterations in the canonical MMR genes MLH1, PMS2, MSH2 and MSH6, aneuploidy, and SBS, DBS and ID mutational signatures associated with MMRd. For model development, we used the TCGA colorectal and stomach adenocarcinoma tumour-normal WES cohorts (Supplementary Table 1), with clinical MSI-PCR labels as the reference standard, as MSI-PCR is principally validated in gastrointestinal tumours 48 . After excluding cases with indeterminate reference results and ultramutated tumours carrying POLE/POLD1 alterations (Methods), cohorts were split at the patient level into training (70%) and held-out test (30%) sets, with all preprocessing and feature selection restricted to the training set. MMRd-associated SBS (SBS6, SBS14, SBS15, SBS20, SBS21, SBS26 and SBS44) and ID (ID1, ID2 and ID7) activities were summed into two aggregate features, with activity set to zero below an assay-specific minimum mutation count (563 SBS and 19 indels for WES) derived by exhaustive threshold optimization in the training set (Methods; Supplementary Tables 2 and 3).

Recursive feature elimination with cross-validation identified a model using ID signatures alone as optimal (Methods), with ID activity the selected feature in 99.0% of 500 training-set resamples (Figure 1b). Given that SBS and ID signatures are computed concomitantly at no additional cost, and that adding the SBS signature activity left the calls unchanged in 477 of those 500 resamples without reducing sensitivity or specificity (Figure 1b), we retained both signature classes, and trained a logistic regression classifier on the two aggregate signature activities (ID1+ID2+ID7 and SBS6+SBS14+SBS15+SBS20+SBS21+SBS44), a model we named captureMMRd. In training data, captureMMRd achieved a sensitivity of 95.56% and specificity of 99.36% in colorectal cancer, and 100% for both in stomach cancer (Figure 1c). Sensitivity and specificity values throughout the study are displayed along Wilson binomial intervals, reflecting cohort size for the fixed classifier with no model retraining.

In the held-out TCGA test sets, captureMMRd classified every sample correctly in both cohorts (sensitivity = 100%, 95% CI 83.89-100.00 in colorectal and 85.69-100.00 in stomach; specificity = 100%, 95% CI 97.19-100.00 in colorectal and 96.37-100.00 in stomach; Figure 1d). captureMMRd exceeded the sensitivity of MSIsensor (colorectal: 90.00%; stomach: 73.91%) 25 , and matched MSIsensor-pro in colorectal and exceeding it in stomach (colorectal: 100%; stomach: 95.65%) 28 (Figure 1d). The final model was deliberately kept simple: two interpretable features and a linear classifier that can be locked, audited and deployed on any assay without retraining.

A single locked model generalises across cohorts and assays without retraining

To determine whether captureMMRd generalises beyond its training domain, we applied the locked model, without retraining or threshold adjustment, to a series of increasingly distant validation settings: a tumour type unseen during training, independent non-TCGA cohorts, lower-depth WGS, and clinically implemented targeted panels (Figure 2).

CaptureMMRd achieved a sensitivity of 99.26% (134/135; 95% CI 95.92-99.87) and specificity of 98.67% (296/300; 95% CI 96.62-99.48) in TCGA uterine corpus endometrial carcinoma, a tumour type unseen during training (Figure 2), exceeding the sensitivity obtained with MSIsensor (82.22%) and MSIsensor-pro (97.78%). Subsets of non-colorectal MMRd tumours, including endometrial carcinoma, show fewer or smaller detectable microsatellite shifts than classical MSI-H colorectal cancers 49 . Since most traditional MSI assays and thresholds were optimised in colorectal cancer, this can lead to reduced sensitivity or discordant MMR results in endometrial cancer 13, 50 . Reading mutational signatures instead of microsatellite loci appears to overcome this limitation.

To test generalisation beyond TCGA, we first evaluated captureMMRd in an independent tumour-normal WES validation cohort of 628 colorectal cancers, comprising 104 freshfrozen 51 and 524 FFPE 52 samples with MSI-PCR status as the orthogonal reference standard. captureMMRd correctly identified 107/113 MSI-H tumours and 508/515 MSS tumours (sensitivity = 94.69%, 95% CI 88.90-97.54%; specificity = 98.64%, 95% CI 97.22-99.34%; Figure 2). We next evaluated captureMMRd in tumour-normal WGS data. Across two independent cohorts, comprising 72 colorecta 58 and 100 oesophagogastric 59 tumournormal pairs, the locked model showed complete concordance with the reference assays (24/24 MMRd; 148/148 MMRp; sensitivity = 100%, 95% CI 86.20-100.00; specificity = 100%, 95% CI 97.47-100.00; Figure 2). Finally we assessed captureMMRd in MSK-IMPACT, a clinically implemented tumour-normal targeted gene panel deployed across 341-, 410-, 468-and 505-gene versions. We aggregated a combined set of 5,643 colorectal, endometrial and oesophagogastric samples sequenced with MSK-IMPACT from eight different cBioPortal 53-56 cohorts 30, [56] [57] [58] [59] [60] [61] [62] with available patient-level MMR IHC status. Without retraining or threshold adjustment, captureMMRd achieved sensitivities of 95.35% (472/495), 91.38% (392/429) and 93.88% (46/49) and specificities of 99.15% (3506/3536), 98.38% (790/803) and 100.00% (331/331) across these tumour types, outperforming MSIsensor in the same MSK-IMPACT datasets (Figure 2) Taken together, our single locked model achieved a pooled sensitivity of 94.38% (1175/1245; 95% CI 92.96-95.53) and specificity of 99.04% (5579/5633; 95% CI 98.75-99.26) across 6,878 samples from 7 tumour-normal validation cohort-assay groups spanning WES, WGS and targeted panel sequencing (Figure 2). Having established that captureMMRd generalises across assay classes when a matched normal is available, we next asked whether it maintains this performance in the setting that motivated its development: tumour-only sequencing.

Tumour-only panels and liquid biopsy support accurate MMRd diagnosis

Most routine clinical NGS is performed using tumour-only capture sequencing [44] [45] [46] [47] , the setting in which existing sequencing-based MSI tools require panel-specific baselines, pretrained models or dedicated microsatellite content. Tumour-only panels also vary widely in design, from standardised institutional assays to locally customised panels. We therefore evaluated captureMMRd in two independent tumour-only assays, an institutional panel deployed in three versions, a custom small panel, and in a plasma cfDNA assay.

In 453 colorectal adenocarcinomas sequenced with DFCI-OncoPanel 63 , a tumour-only institutional assay deployed in 275-, 300-and 447-gene versions, captureMMRd achieved a sensitivity of 92.86% (39/42; 95% CI 80.99-97.54) and specificity of 98.54% (405/411; 95% CI 96.85-99.33) against the MMR IHC reference standard (Figure 3a).

To extend evaluation beyond the tumour types most commonly used for MMRd benchmarking, we profiled an institutional cohort of 146 ovarian clear-cell and endometrioid carcinomas-histotypes in which the clinical relevance of MMRd detection is increasingly recognised 64 -using a custom small targeted panel with MMR IHC and MSI-PCR as orthogonal reference assays. captureMMRd maintained strong performance despite the reduced panel size (~0.5Mb), achieving a sensitivity of 94.44% (17/18; 95% CI 74.24-99.01) and specificity of 99.22% (127/128; 95% CI 95.71-99.86; Figure 3b).

Samples called MMRd by captureMMRd but not by the reference assays were enriched for loss-of-function alterations in MMR genes (Figure 3c). In the DFCI-OncoPanel cohort, three out of six such discordant samples harboured loss-of-function alterations in MSH6; in the ovarian cohort, the single discordant sample harboured loss-of-function alterations in MSH2 (Supplementary Table 4). These observations suggest that a subset of discordant predictions reflect biologically plausible MMRd events missed by IHC or PCR, not falsepositive calls, and support careful review of discordant cases before dismissal.

Finally, we tested captureMMRd in plasma cfDNA, a setting of increasing importance for patients with advanced or metastatic disease, where tissue availability may be limited, repeated biopsies are often impractical, and few approaches exist for MMRd detection. In 37 endometrial, colorectal and oesophagogastric adenocarcinomas with detectable ctDNA sequenced using MSK-ACCESS, a hybrid-capture cfDNA assay 65 (Methods), captureMMRd was fully concordant with tissue-based MMR IHC (6/6 MMRd; 31/31 MMRp; Figure 3d), extending MMRd detection to liquid biopsy workflows.

In contrast to the discordant MMRd calls, the four samples called MMRp by captureMMRd despite an MMRd reference diagnosis (three in DFCI-OncoPanel, one in OVAseq, none in MSK-ACCESS) carried sparse mutation catalogues within the panel footprint: 0-10 somatic indels (median 5.5), compared with a median of 14 among correctly classified MMRd samples (two-sided exact Wilcoxon rank-sum test, p = 0.016; Supplementary Table 8). Two sat marginally below the decision threshold, with summed MMR indel activity of 0.50 against an effective threshold of 0.56. The OVAseq false negative, MSH6-deficient by IHC, carried no somatic indels and four SBS within the panel, and was also classified as microsatellite stable by MSIsensor2 (score 9.1), indicating an absence of instability signal in the sequencing data itself. Two of the four false negatives were MSH6-deficient, a genotype associated with an attenuated instability phenotype 66 . The sensitivity limit of captureMMRd is therefore set by the number of informative somatic mutations captured by the panel, a constraint shared by sequencing-based approaches at low mutation burden.

Across all validation settings combined, a single locked model achieved a pooled sensitivity of 94.36% (1237/1311; 95% CI 92.97-95.48) and specificity of 99.02% (6142/6203; 95% CI 98.74-99.23) across 7,514 samples from 16 independent cohorts, four assay classes and five tumour types (Figures 2 and 3). captureMMRd thus extends accurate MMRd diagnosis to the tumour-only panels and liquid biopsy assays used in routine precision oncology, using only the somatic variant calls these assays already capture. captureMMRd matches tumour-only MSI callers without requiring microsatellite loci Existing approaches to tumour-only MSI/MMRd detection fall into two classes: microsatellitelocus readers, which depend on the panel's microsatellite content and platform-specific calibration, and single-feature mutation heuristics, exemplified by homopolymer indel counts calibrated against IHC in colorectal cancer 36 . These classes differ in what they require as input. Microsatellite-locus callers operate on aligned sequencing reads, available in this study only for the OVAseq cohort, whereas the indel-rate rule requires only the somatic variant calls available for every cohort. We therefore evaluated the indel-rate rule across all cohorts and performed a head-to-head comparison of all methods in OVAseq to test each class's dependence on assay design under controlled conditions.

Applied across cohorts using each panel's exonic footprint, the indel-rate rule showed similar sensitivity (100.0%, 94.4% and 100.0%; Figure 4a), but poorer specificity compared to captureMMRd (89.54%, 96.88% and 96.77%; Figure 4a). The sbs-rate rule instead showed much lower sensitivity compared to captureMMRd (73.8% and 33.3%, sbs-rate rule was not applied to MSK-ACCESS as no sample in the cohort had enough sbs; Figure 4a), but equivalent specificity in the cohorts tested (99.0% and 99.2%, sbs-rate rule was not applied to MSK-ACCESS as no sample in the cohort had enough sbs; Figure 4a).

In OVAseq, the cohort with raw sequencing data, we compared captureMMRd directly against MSIsensor2, a pre-trained microsatellite-locus caller 29 on identical samples. captureMMRd achieved a sensitivity of 94.44% (17/18) at a specificity of 99.22% (127/128), compared with 94.44% (17/18) and 100% (128/128) for MSIsensor2, the two methods separated by a single discordant MMRp sample (Figure 4b). As before, the indel-rate and sbs-rate rules fell below both methods (Figure 4b).

To determine whether this equivalence depends on the panel's microsatellite content, we progressively removed microsatellite-bearing targets from the OVAseq designs in silico and re-ran both methods on identical reductions. The OVAseq panels were designed to enrich microsatellite content, through dedicated microsatellite regions and a subset of exonic targets; in total, 63-65 targets per panel version, 2.3-2.5% of the panel footprint, contained every locus evaluable by MSIsensor2 (23-66 loci per sample passing its coverage filter). We removed these targets in ranked order to retain 75%, 50%, 25% and 0% of evaluable loci, across three random removal orderings per level (Methods), excluding the variants they contained from signature estimation.

MSIsensor2 performance degraded with panel design: mean sensitivity fell from 94.4% (17/18) on the full panel to 88.9% across the depletion levels (range 83.3-94.4% at 25% retained loci), specificity fell to a mean of 95.6%, and with every microsatellite-bearing target removed it returned no result for any MMRd sample (Figure 4c,d). captureMMRd diagnoses were unchanged throughout: all 146 samples received the same call as on the full panel at every depletion level, including complete removal, at a cost of less than 2.5% of the panel footprint (Figure 4c,d). The two methods therefore fail in different ways: a microsatellitelocus caller fails through panel design, whereas captureMMRd fails only when too few mutations remain to estimate signature activity, a condition we characterise below. captureMMRd is robust to preservation method and reduced tumour purity Clinical deployment requires stability against the variables that differ most across laboratories. Sample preservation is one of these variables that could impact the quality of samples the most.

Specifically, formalin fixation introduces characteristic artefactual substitutions that could in principle contaminate signature estimates. The MMRd-associated signature set does not include FFPE-artefact processes, and performance did not statistically differ between freshfrozen (sensitivity=100% and specificity=100%) and FFPE (sensitivity=93.33% and specificity=98.39%) samples in the independent WES cohort (Fisher's exact, p = 0.344 for sensitivity and p = 0.603 for specificity) (Figure 5).

Tumour purity varies widely in clinical specimens and lowers the variant allele fractions (VAFs) at which somatic mutations are observed. To emulate falling purity, we re-filtered the OVAseq cohort at minimum VAF thresholds from 0.05 (baseline) to 0.30 and repeated signature assignment and classification (Methods). Diagnoses were unchanged at a threshold of 0.10 (146/146 concordant with baseline), and the only changed calls at thresholds of 0.15 and 0.20 moved toward the reference standard, with the single baseline false positive reverting to MMRp (94.44% sensitivity at 100% specificity at 0.20). From a threshold of 0.25, equivalent to approximately 20% tumour purity at a 5% detection limit, sensitivity declined (14/18, and 11/18 at 0.30) as indel counts fell below the minimum mutation threshold. As with panel composition, reduced purity degrades captureMMRd only through the predictable minimum-mutation failure mode.

Minimal panel requirements for MMRd diagnosis

The robustness analyses identified a single predictable failure mode: too few mutations to estimate signature activity. Because captureMMRd relies on the SBS and indels captured within a panel's footprint, small panels may yield mutation counts below the reliable range, not through any property of the tumour but through assay design. We therefore characterised the minimum input required for a reliable diagnosis, and translated it into a prospective eligibility rule for any panel.

Using the TCGA training cohort, we fixed the number of SBS and ID mutations available per sample and evaluated prediction stability at each count. Three indels were sufficient to reach a 90% agreement with the full indel set in MMRp and MMRd samples (Figure 6a). For SBS signatures, because they were already highly specific, we just focused on MMRd samples. Here, 44 mutations were required to reach the 90% accuracy agreement (Figure 6b). These minima reflect the information content of each mutation class: indels at short repeats are the most direct consequence of MMR loss, so each observed indel carries substantially more diagnostic weight than a substitution. We next translated these minima into panel size. Combining the required mutation counts with the per-megabase ID rates (because indels alone carry most of the MMRd signal) of MMRd tumours in each cancer type, a panel footprint of 0.66Mb is sufficient for reliable MMRd diagnosis in colorectal, 0.91Mb in stomach cancer, and 1.54Mb in endometrial cancer (Figure 6c). The difference across tumour types reflects their distinct baseline mutation rates, and the estimates are deliberately conservative, derived from the lower range of mutation burdens observed among MMRd tumours.

To assess what these requirements mean in practice, we surveyed the exonic footprints of targeted panels which are widely used in the clinical setting, spanning commercial assays, institutional panels and the designs evaluated in this study (Figure 6d; Supplementary Table 5). Most panels exceed the minimum footprint for colorectal and stomach cancer, while endometrial cancer might require more regions covered. Overall, this indicates that most clinically deployed assays could support captureMMRd without modification for colorectal and stomach cancers. Discussion

In this study, we demonstrate that mismatch repair deficiency can be diagnosed from the somatic variant calls produced by routine tumour-only sequencing, using mutational signatures alone. A single locked model, trained once on tumour-normal WES, maintained a pooled sensitivity of 94.36% and specificity of 99.02% across 7,514 samples from 16 independent cohorts without retraining, recalibration or access to matched normal DNA. Previous sequencing-based approaches have anchored MMRd detection to the assay: microsatellite-locus callers require matched normal sequencing 25, 26 , panel-specific baselines 27, 28 , platform-specific pre-training 29, 30 or dedicated microsatellite content 19, [31] [32] [33] , and the only mutation-based alternative in clinical use is a single-feature indel-rate rule calibrated within one institutional colorectal workflow 36 . captureMMRd generalises the biology these approaches sample into a panel-agnostic classifier: the mutational consequences of MMR loss are read directly from the variant calls, so any panel that captures enough somatic mutations can support a diagnosis.

Framing detection around signatures changes where the method can fail. In the one cohort with raw sequencing data, captureMMRd and MSIsensor2 were separated by a single sample, yet the two methods degraded differently when microsatellite-bearing targets were removed in silico: MSIsensor2 lost sensitivity progressively and returned no result once its loci were gone, while captureMMRd calls were unchanged at every depletion level, at a cost of less than 2.5% of the panel footprint. A locus caller fails through panel design, sample by sample, as informative content shrinks. captureMMRd fails only when too few mutations remain to estimate signature activity, a condition that is explicit in the model, predictable from a panel's design file before any sequencing is performed, and reported as an eligibility criterion instead of a silent miscall. Calls were similarly stable to variant-filtering stringency, which we used to emulate reduced tumour purity, and to preservation method. Samples called MMRd by captureMMRd but not by the reference assays were enriched for loss-offunction alterations in MMR genes, consistent with the known failure modes of IHC and MSI-PCR [11] [12] [13] ; signature-based readouts may therefore recover a subset of MMRd tumours that current standards miss, although orthogonal confirmation would be required before such calls direct treatment.

We see captureMMRd as a complement to established testing, not a replacement for it. IHC and MSI-PCR remain the clinical standard where they are available and validated [8] [9] [10] . The setting captureMMRd addresses is the one existing methods leave uncovered: laboratories running tumour-only panels, particularly smaller or custom designs, that lack the matched normals, baseline cohorts and microsatellite content existing sequencing-based methods assume. For these laboratories, MMRd status becomes computable from data they already generate, at no additional assay cost, with eligibility determinable in advance from the panel design alone. The same property permits retrospective re-analysis: archived variant calls from historical cohorts can be screened for MMRd without returning to tissue, with implications for Lynch syndrome ascertainment 4, 5 and for identifying patients who may benefit from immune checkpoint blockade [1] [2] [3] . The full concordance observed in plasma cfDNA further suggests a route to MMRd assessment where tissue is unavailable, although this requires validation beyond the 37 samples tested here.

Our study has limitations. We treated IHC and MSI-PCR as ground truth, so where the reference errors, our reported sensitivity and specificity are conservative; we could not resolve the discordant calls with an independent assay. Training used colorectal and stomach adenocarcinoma with MSI-PCR labels, which are principally validated in gastrointestinal tumours 48 , and validation, although pan-cancer in aggregate, is dominated by the tumour types common in clinical sequencing cohorts; performance in rarer types, and in endometrial cancer on small panels, where the minimum footprint of 1.54 Mb exceeds several deployed designs, requires further evaluation. Raw sequencing data were available only for the OVAseq cohort, so the head-to-head comparison and the robustness analyses are confined to one assay, and the depletion and purity analyses were performed in silico on panel designs; prospective sequencing on panels without microsatellite content has not been performed. Finally, signature fitting near the minimum mutation counts is intrinsically unstable [67] [68] [69] . We handle this with explicit per-assay minima, assigning zero activity below them, which renders a subset of samples on very small panels ineligible instead of miscalled, and this indeterminate fraction is a cost of the approach that laboratories should report alongside performance. Further work will be necessary to establish performance prospectively across laboratories and bioinformatic pipelines, to extend validation to the tumour types and panel designs underrepresented here, and to define how signature-based calls should be combined with IHC and MSI-PCR in diagnostic algorithms. By operating on the output every sequencing workflow already generates, captureMMRd extends clinically actionable MMRd detection to assays that were never designed to measure it. The broader principle is that DNA repair deficiencies leave mutational scars that routine panel data already record; reading them directly may bring biomarker-directed therapy to every setting where targeted sequencing is performed.

Methods

Cohorts

We analysed samples from 18 independent cohorts comprising different sources, specimen types, sequencing assays, sample design and gold standard diagnosis.

Tumour-normal fresh frozen WES data was also retrieved from cBioPortal for three cohorts from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA): Colorectal Adenocarcinoma (coadread_tcga_pan_can_atlas_2018), Stomach Adenocarcinoma (stad_tcga_pan_can_atlas_2018), and Uterine Corpus Endometrial Carcinoma (ucec_tcga_pan_can_atlas_2018). MMR-D status in these cohorts was defined using MSI PCR. Additionally, three more tumour-normal WES non-TCGA cohorts containing fresh frozen or FFPE samples were included in which either MSI PCR alone or together with MMR IHC was used as MMR status gold standard diagnosis 51, 52, 70.

Tumour-normal fresh frozen WGS data was extracted for Colorectal 71 and Stomach Adenocarcinoma 72 from cBioPortal 53 54,55 . MRR-D diagnosis regarding Colorectal Adenocarcinoma was assessed using either MMR IHC or MSI PCR, while for Stomach Adenocarcinoma only MSI PCR was used.

Pancancer targeted tumour-normal sequencing data were obtained from MSK-IMPACT MiMSI cohort (pancan_mimsi_msk_2024), also available through cBioPortal 53 . MMR-D labels for these cohorts were defined based on available IHC or PCR results. Moreover, we also obtained MSK-IMPACT Colorectal Cancer data from the MSK-CHORD cohort (msk_chord_2024), from the Metastatic Colorectal Cancer cohort (crc_msk_2017) and from the Nigerian Colorectal Cancer cohort (crc_nigerian_2020). IHC and PCR were used as gold standard diagnosis respectively for these cohorts. Endometrial cancer data sequenced with MSK-IMPACT was also retrieved from the MSK-UCEC cohort (msk_ucec_2018), from the Endometrial Carcinoma MSI cohort (ucec_ccr_msk_2022) and from the Endometrial and Ovarian Cancer cohort (ucec_msk_2024). MMR IHC was used as the reference diagnosis for these samples.

Colorectal cancer tumour-only data was retrieved from samples sequenced with the DFCI-OncoPanel from the GENIE BioPharma Collaborative (BPC) colorectal cancer (CRC v2.0public) cohort (crc_public_genie_bpc) 55 from the publicly available AACR Project GENIE database 56 as well as from cBioPortal 73 . Additionally, colorectal adenocarcinoma tumouronly data sequenced with DFCI-OncoPanel, was also retrieved from cBioportal (crc_orion_2024). Somatic mutation data were used to derive mutational signatures, while mismatch repair immunohistochemistry (MMR IHC) and microsatellite instability PCR (MSI-PCR) results were used as reference standards for MMR deficiency classification.

Analyses of publicly available data from TCGA, AACR Project GENIE, MSK and the other published cohorts were performed under the terms of access of each resource and required no additional approval.

For circulating tumour DNA (ctDNA), we used the MSK ctDNA sequencing cohort (msk_ctdna_vte_2024) dataset that uses the MSK-ACCESS platform, selecting only those samples with matched tissue MMR IHC results reported in either MSK-CHORD (msk_chord_2024) or MiMSI cohort. Because total cfDNA input for MSK-ACCESS library preparation was not available, we used plasma cfDNA concentration as a surrogate for sample adequacy. MSK-ACCESS was validated using 20 ng of plasma cfDNA whenever possible, with analyses attempted down to 3 ng. We therefore applied a 2.5 ng/mL cutoff to select samples likely to provide near-optimal cfDNA input, assuming plasma volumes similar to those commonly obtained in clinical liquid biopsy workflows, where 8-10 mL of plasma would yield approximately 20-25 ng cfDNA at this concentration. Thus, only samples with cfDNA concentrations ≥2.5 ng/mL were included in this analysis. Because samples below this threshold were not analysed, we cannot assess the performance of the method in loweryield cfDNA samples. Additionally, we only considered samples with max VAF>0.005, as this was the VAF threshold at which MSK-ACCESS is reported to display 92% sensitivity and a 99% specificity for de novo SNVs and indels calling 65 Finally, we evaluated an institutional cohort of 146 ovarian tumour samples from a multicentric study, sequenced with a custom tumour-only panel. MMR-D status was determined by MMR IHC or MSI-PCR. Samples from this cohort were provided by the IdiPAZ Biobank (PT23-00028), integrated into the ISCIII Biobanks and Biomodels Platform; the Biobank of the Andalusian Public Health System (SSPA), which coordinated the collection, processing, management and distribution of the biological samples in accordance with its established standard procedures; and the BioBank of the Fundación MD Anderson Cancer Center España-Hospiten, integrated into the Spanish National Biobanks Network (B.0000745). All samples were processed following the standard operating procedures established by the respective biobanks, with the appropriate approval of their Ethics and Scientific Committees. This study was conducted in accordance with ethical standards of the Helsinki Declaration of the World Medical Association and ethical approval was obtained from the ISCIII Research Ethics Committee (CEI PI 96_2019-v2) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Of note, we removed all samples with indeterminate gold standard diagnosis for training, testing and validation of the model. Additionally, we followed the clinical guidelines for commercial targeted panels used in this study and restricted the analysis of MSK-IMPACT samples to: tumour purity > 10%, sample coverage > 200x, and matched somatic status. Whereas for DFCI-OncoPanel we just evaluated samples with: tumour purity > 20%. No purity or coverage filter was applied to WES, WGS, OVAseq or the cfDNA cohort. This included samples with single test indeterminate results and multiple but discordant gold standard diagnosis either being repeated discordant single IHC/PCR tests or both IHC-PCR discordancy. Ultramutated samples, either displaying TMB>100 or LoF mutations in POLE or POLD1, were also excluded for model training, testing and validation. These cancers have a clear path in the clinic, usually being referred to immunotherapy, independently of their MMR status. But because they share common features with MMRd cancers including them in the training might bias model performance. Additionally, patient-level deduplication is applied to the whole validation database after merging all cohorts data. Finally, MSI-L is folded into MSS before any modelling for any cohort reporting MSI-L in the MSI-PCR assay.

NGS data generation

Sequence panel design

We used a custom targeted sequencing panel comprising frequently altered cancer driver genes and 53 additional microsatellite regions. Two versions of the panel were used: version 1 targeted 145 genes (Supplementary Table 6), whereas version 2 targeted 155 genes (Supplementary Table 7). The total target regions covered approximately 0.57 Mb and 0.57 Mb (565,472 bp and 570,483 bp of target space), respectively. Capture probes were designed against the GRCh38 human reference genome.

Genomic DNA extraction and sample sequencing

For the institutional OVAseq cohort, genomic DNA was extracted from FFPE tumour samples using either the Maxwell RSC DNA FFPE Kit (Promega; catalog no. AS1450) or the truXTRAC FFPE Kit (Covaris; catalog no. 520136).

Library preparation differed between panel versions.

Libraries for version 1 were prepared using the SeqCap EZ HyperCap Kit (Roche; catalog no. 08278512001), whereas libraries for version 2 were prepared using the KAPA HyperCap Kit (Roche; catalog no. 07962428001), in both cases according to the manufacturer's instructions. Libraries generated using version 1 were sequenced on Illumina HiSeq 2500 or HiSeq 4000 instruments using 101-bp and 150-bp paired-end reads respectively. Libraries generated using version 2 were sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq 2000 instrument using 110-bp paired-end reads.

Bioinformatic Analysis

For the OVAseq cohort, paired-end FASTQ files were processed using nf-core/sarek v3.8.1 74, 75 , implemented in Nextflow 76 within the nf-core framework 77 . Reads were aligned to the GRCh38 human reference genome using BWA-MEM2 v2.2.1 78.

Sequencing quality-control metrics were calculated using Picard CollectHsMetrics v3.1.1. Samples were retained when at least 90% of targeted bases had a sequencing depth of ≥30× (PCT_TARGET_BASES_30X ≥ 0.90), median target coverage was ≥50× (MEDIAN_TARGET_COVERAGE ≥ 50), and the proportion of targets with zero coverage was ≤0.005 (ZERO_CVG_TARGETS_PCT ≤ 0.005). Patients with more than one sample were deduplicated selecting the first sample received from the ones that passed the QC. Overall, 146 OVAseq deduplicated samples passed these quality-control criteria and were evaluated.

Variant calling

For all publicly available cohorts, variant calls were obtained directly from their corresponding repositories. These variants were used with the annotations provided by each repository. For each cohort, information regarding the reference genome, variant-calling pipeline, and source repository is provided in (Supplementary Table 1).

For the OVAseq cohort, variants were called using nf-core/sarek pipeline v3.8.1 74, 75 . This pipeline uses Mutect2 from GATK v4.6.1.0 79, 80 , FreeBayes v1.3.10 81 , LoFreq v2.1.5 82 , and BCFtools v1.21 83 . Consensus variants were defined as variants detected by at least 2 callers. Variants were annotated with the Ensembl Variant Effect Predictor (VEP) 84.

Definition of predicted loss-of-function alterations

For publicly available cohorts, variants were classified as clinically relevant alterations if they were annotated as "Pathogenic" or "Likely pathogenic" in ClinVar 85 or "Oncogenic" or "Likely oncogenic" in OncoKB 86, 87.

For the OVAseq cohort, variants were filtered to identify predicted loss-of-function alterations. Variants were required to be rare in the general population (1000 Genomes 88 allele frequency <1% and not common in ExAC 89 ), have sufficient sequencing support (variant allele frequency >5%, ≥5 alternative-supporting reads, and total depth ≥10 reads), and affect a canonical transcript. SBS mutations were additionally restricted to variants called by at least 3 variant callers and to have a described reference SNP cluster ID whenever it was detected in more than 3 samples in our cohort. Predicted LoF alterations included frameshift, stop-gain, start-loss, and essential splice-site variants. Non-truncating exonic variants were also retained when classified as "Pathogenic" or "Likely pathogenic" in ClinVar.

Indel mutational signatures calling

For the COAD, STAD and UCEC TCGA cohorts we retained only variants that (1) passed filtering (FILTER ∈ {PASS, wga, native_wga_mix}) and (2) were annotated as canonical (CANONICAL = YES). For all the other publicly available cohorts, mutational matrices were computed using the raw mutations retrieved from each repository without any filtering. Regarding our institutional cohort, OVAseq, variants were included, expanding the criteria for the predicted LoF to also include synonymous mutations.

Mutational matrices were generated using SigProfilerMatrixGenerator v1.3.6 90 and translated into SBS and ID mutational signatures using SigProfilerAssignment v1.1.3 67 over the v3.5 of COSMIC signatures 37 91.

Microsatellite instability computation

For the TCGA and MSK-IMPACT cohorts, MSIsensor scores were obtained from cBioPortal. For TCGA samples, MSIsensor-pro scores were additionally obtained from the supplementary data associated with 28 . MSIsensor is called MSI-H at score ≥ 10, with scores between 3 and 10 treated as indeterminate and counted as a non-MSI-H cal and MSS at scores lower than 3l; MSIsensor-pro has no published cut-off, so the score maximising balanced accuracy at the training set was chosen, locked and applied unchanged.

For the OVAseq cohort, microsatellite instability was quantified from tumour-only sequencing data using MSIsensor2. As suggested by the authors, a score of 20% was used to classify samples as MSI-high when a categorical classification was required 29.

In silico microsatellite-target depletion

The OVAseq panel designs include targets selected for microsatellite content, in dedicated microsatellite regions and in a subset of exonic targets. For each panel version, every microsatellite locus evaluable by MSIsensor2 (≥20 tumour reads) was assigned to its covering target; 63-65 targets per version (2.3-2.5% of the panel footprint) contained all evaluable loci. Targets were removed in descending order of evaluable-locus count, with ties broken at random across three seeds, until 75%, 50%, 25% and 0% of in-panel evaluable loci were retained. Each resulting design was applied identically to both methods. For MSIsensor2, per-sample scores were recomputed from its per-site output restricted to retained loci (evaluable, ≥20 tumour reads; score, percentage of evaluable loci called somatic; MSI-H, score ≥20%); samples with no remaining evaluable locus were counted as returning no result. For captureMMRd, variants within removed targets were excluded, SBS96 and ID83 catalogues rebuilt, COSMIC v3.5 signatures refit with SigProfilerAssignment, and the locked model reapplied including the minimum mutation thresholds. Depletion performance is reported as concordance with each method's full-panel calls within the same pipeline.

VAF-dilution purity emulation

To emulate reduced tumour purity, the filtered OVAseq variant set was re-thresholded at minimum VAFs of 0.05 (the baseline filter), 0.10, 0.15, 0.20, 0.25 and 0.30. At each threshold, SBS96 and ID83 catalogues were rebuilt from the retained variants, COSMIC v3.5 signatures refit with SigProfilerMatrixGenerator and SigProfilerAssignment, and the locked model reapplied including the minimum mutation thresholds. A threshold t with a baseline detection limit of 0.05 emulates a tumour purity of approximately 0.05/t. Performance is reported as call concordance with the baseline threshold.

Tumour mutational burden measurement

For the publicly available cohorts, tumour mutational burden (TMB) values were obtained from cBioPortal. We used the "TMB (nonsynonymous)" attribute for TCGA cohorts and the "Impact TMB Score" attribute for MSK-IMPACT cohorts. Values were analysed as provided by cBioPortal and expressed as mutations per megabase (mut/Mb).

For the OVAseq cohort, TMB was calculated as the number of qualifying nonsynonymous coding variants divided by the panel-specific exome-targeted territory, expressed in megabases. Variants were required to match the same filters as for the predicted LoF mutations. Because two panel versions were used, separate TMB denominators were calculated. For each version, the panel target intervals were intersected with the Genecode protein coding sequences (gencode.v49.basic.protein_coding.CDS.merged.bed). Overlapping intervals were merged to avoid counting individual bases more than once. The resulting exome-targeted territories were 0.53 Mb for panel version 1 and 0.55 Mb for panel version 2.

Aneuploidy scores

Aneuploidy scores for the TCGA cohorts were extracted from cBioPortal.

Machine learning classifier

Data preprocessing TCGA stomach adenocarcinoma (STAD) and colorectal adenocarcinoma cohorts (COADREAD) were used for model learning. MSI-PCR was used as the gold standard label for the model. Because MSI-PCR is principally validated for gastrointestinal tumours and is not the recommended reference assay for endometrial cancer 48 , UCEC was used for model validation. Hence, STAD and colorectal cohorts were stratified by cohort and divided at the patient level into training (70%) and test (30%) sets using a stratified random split that preserved the proportion of MMRd and MMR-proficient samples. All preprocessing and feature-selection procedures were restricted to the training set.

We developed a binary classifier to predict mismatch repair deficiency (MMRd) from NGSderived biomarkers. The candidate features were LoF mutations in the MMR genes, MSIsensor score, TMB, Aneuploidy Score, SBS Signatures (Sum of SBS6, SBS14, SBS15, SBS20, SBS21, SBS26 and SBS44), Indel Signatures (Sum of ID1, ID2 and ID7) associated with MMR-D and the per-sample indel and SBS catalogue sizes. LoF mutations in the MMR genes was a binary variable and was therefore not scaled. SBS and ID signatures were not scaled either as their values already ranged from 0 to 1. Continuous features such as MSIsensor score, TMB, sbs counts, indel counts and Aneuploidy Score were transformed using min-max normalization exclusively using the training data. This prevented information leakage and ensured that features contributed comparably to coefficient-based elimination. Samples with missing features or reference-standard data were excluded.

Minimum number of SBS and ID to call mutational signatures per technology

Because of the compositional nature of mutational signatures 37, 92 , just a few random similarcontext variants can lead to high signature activities, even though they might not be truly associated with any mutational process, and no established per-sample minimum mutation count exists for signature assignment [67] [68] [69] . Hence, we performed exhaustive threshold optimization in the training set over the number of SBS and ID mutations per-sample to find the minimum number of mutations to reliably call SBS and ID signatures respectively. We selected the threshold that maximized balanced accuracy when predicting MSI-PCR labels, just using the per-sample number of SBS and ID. Samples below these thresholds were designated a 0 activity for the corresponding mutational signatures. For the tumour-normal WES training data, these minima were 563 SBS and 19 ID.

Because this threshold is a property of the assay, not the tumour, we recomputed it for each of the sequencing panels assessed in this study. To do so, we restricted the SBS and ID in the training set to ones captured by the concerned targeted sequencing panel and re-assess the threshold optimization. Therefore, a technology specific threshold was established (Supplementary Tables 2 and 3). These in turn were used as the so-called indel and sbs rate rules. A sample is called MMRd when its per-sample indel (or SBS) count reaches the threshold of its panel.

Although this method could be performed to select a SBS and ID threshold for all targeted sequencing panels independently of their size, in ultra small panels accuracy can drop significantly. To account for this, we evaluated which was the minimum number of SBS and ID that independently of the sequencing technology could result in an accurate MMRd diagnosis. Therefore, using the training set we iteratively selected a specific number of SBS and ID mutations and evaluated the diagnosis using a balanced-class weight, unregularized logistic regression classifier trained just with SBS or ID signatures. To perform this analysis 100 random subsamples of the catalogue at each mutation count were performed. Then, signatures were refitted on each subsample per sample and MMR prediction was evaluated.

Whenever the median of the percentage of agreement across samples reached 90%, the indel threshold was set. This showed that 3 indels was the lowest to reliably predict MMR status using a 90% agreement with the MMR status prediction using each sample full indels set. In contrast, 44 sbs were needed to reliably predict MMRd diagnosis using the 90% agreement criteria. For substitutions however, this procedure was performed only in the MMRd/MSI sample subset instead of the full set as for indels.

Using the indel ratio per exonic megabase in the TCGA, we assessed the minimum exonic capture length for a panel depending on the cancer type to have a reliable MMRd prediction using captureMMRd. To assess this, we obtained the number of indel and substitutions in the stomach and colorectal TCGA testing subsets as well as in the endometrial TCGA cohort and divided these number by 35.8Mb, which was taken as the exonic territory of the TCGA exome. Then we selected the 10th percentile of the MSI samples to obtain a conservative per-Mb indel rate.

This was applied to a subset of clinically widely deployed targeted panels to check captureMMRd suitability. Protein-coding intervals used as TMB denominators were taken from GENCODE v49 93 and intersected with the panel target intervals.

Feature selection

Model development and feature selection were performed in Python v3.12.13 using scikitlearn v1.6.1. Feature selection was conducted exclusively within the training set using recursive feature elimination with cross-validation (RFECV), with a logistic regression estimator using the default parameters. The candidate set comprised eight variables available for every training sample: the summed MMRd-associated ID activity, the summed MMRd-associated SBS activity, the MSIsensor score, tumour mutational burden, the presence of a loss-of-function alteration in an MMR gene, the aneuploidy score, and the persample indel and SBS catalogue sizes. Candidate feature subsets were evaluated using a custom Bayesian information criterion (BIC) Feature selection was repeated over 500 resamples of the training set (stratified 5-fold cross-validation repeated 100 times under a fixed random seed). At each iteration, features were eliminated sequentially, with a minimum of 1 retained feature. To quantify how reproducible the selected set was, recursive feature elimination was refitted to the optimal model size on every resample and the proportion of resamples retaining each candidate is reported in Fig. 1b; for this stability analysis the candidates were standardized within each training fold, so that the elimination order reflects standardized effect size rather than the units a feature happens to be expressed in. A model consisting of just the ID signatures (Sum of ID1, ID2 and ID7) associated with MMRd was the one maximizing the BIC-based score across cross-validation folds. Because both signature activities are obtained from the same signature-fitting step, the SBS activity was additionally assessed against the ID-only model over the same resamples: the two models returned identical calls in 477 of 500 resamples and differed by less than 0.1 percentage points in cross-validated balanced accuracy, and both features were therefore retained in the final classifier.

Logistic regression classifier

The final model was an unregularized logistic regression classifier implemented in scikitlearn v1.6.1 94 . Balanced class weight and default settings for other parameters were used. The classifier estimated the probability of MMRd from SBS and ID signatures. Hence, the final model resulted in a coefficient of 7.03 for the sum of ID signatures associated with MMRd, 15.44 for the sum of SBS signatures associated with MMRd and an intercept of -3.9214564. Of note, if a sample's indels or substitutions fall below its assay threshold, the concerned signature is assigned an activity of zero.

The final model was fitted to the complete training set and evaluated without further fitting or threshold optimization on the held-out 30% of the STAD and COADREAD cohorts. Generalizability to validation cohorts was evaluated separately on data totally excluded from the model training or feature selection.

Performance assessment

A prespecified probability threshold of 0.5 was used to classify samples as MMRd. Model performance was evaluated against the available orthogonal reference standard: MSI-PCR or MMR-IHC depending on the cohort (Supplementary Table 1). Sensitivity and specificity were calculated separately for each cohort. Corresponding 95% confidence intervals were estimated using the Wilson score method for binomial proportions.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed in Python using SciPy and statsmodels. Sensitivity and specificity were reported as binomial proportions with 95% Wilson score confidence intervals (statsmodels proportion_confint, method 'wilson'). Comparisons between independent groups of samples, such as FFPE against fresh-frozen specimens, were tested with twosided Fisher's exact test, and differences in sensitivity and specificity between groups were reported with 95% Newcombe confidence intervals (statsmodels confint_proportions_2indep, method 'newcomb'). Statistical significance was considered when p<0.05.
