How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. Abstract

Background Large language models (LLMs) are solely judged by their scores on biomedical benchmarks, yet these scores are rarely reproducible since the prompts, inference settings and model builds are seldom reported. We developed BenchmarkHub, an open-source platform for auditable evaluation of language and vision-language models for biomedical research.

Methods BenchmarkHub is a Python web application (Django 5.2, SQLite) released under the MIT licence and available at https://github.com/juanjuanignacio/benchmarkhub. The application can be accessed through a browser interface, a set of command-line tools and a REST API, so interactive exploration and scripted pipelines produce identical, comparable records. The platform samples questions with fixed seeds, runs models directly or as toolusing agents in a code sandbox, grades answers with deterministic rules and stores every prompt, reply and tool call. The interface provides a leaderboard to rank performance of the models, allowing model comparison without any preexisting programming knowledge. Findings We used BenchmarkHub to evaluate 12 open-weight models in five experiments on 15 biomedical benchmarks (11 text, two multimodal and two computational biology) and two general benchmarks. The pooled accuracy ranged from 46•1% (95% CI 43•0-49•3) to 80•9% (78•3-83•3). Re-grading the responses with simple answer-extraction rules lowered a model's accuracy by up to 33•1 percentage points leading to a reorder of the leaderboard. Published scores were found for 23 out of the 143 benchmark/model combinations tested. The accuracy measured by BenchmarkHub was lower than in the published results in 19 out of the 23.

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) currently need to pass medical licensing-style examinations. 1 Multiple-choice benchmarks such as MedQA, MedMCQA, PubMedQA and the medical subsets of MMLU have become the default evidence offered for their medical competence. [2] [3] [4] [5] A systematic review of 519 evaluation studies found that most assessed accuracy is on examstyle questions and only 5% used real patient data. 6 Benchmark accuracy also transfers poorly to real-world use: although LLMs identified at least one relevant medical condition in 94•9% of scenarios when assessed independently, members of the public using the same models . identified relevant conditions in fewer than 34•5% of cases, performing no better than participants who used other sources of information. ⁷ Diagnostic performance also degrades when models must interpret real patient information revealed sequentially. ⁸ Calls to retire exam benchmarks as headline evidence 9 and to validate generative models rigorously before clinical use 10 have been answered with broader frameworks, such as MedHELM, 11 and with new benchmarks, including RareArena and CARDBiomedBench, published in this journal. 12, 13 A less discussed problem is that a benchmark score depends not only on the model but on the whole evaluation pipeline: which questions were sampled, how the prompt was worded, how many tokens the model was allowed, how the answer was extracted from free text, which model build was served, and whether additional images or tools were available. Outside medicine, equivalent prompt formats, 14, 15 answer-extraction rules 16 and nominally deterministic decoding 17 each change accuracy substantially, and harness developers list these as the main obstacles to reproducibility. 18 Multimodal benchmarks can often be answered from the text alone, 19 and models that reach expert-level accuracy on medical image challenges often reason wrongly about the image. 20 These choices are rarely reported in medical evaluations, and developer-reported scores come from in-house protocols that cannot be rerun. 21 Existing frameworks address parts of the problem. HELM and the Language model evaluation harness provide standardised benchmarks, 18, 22 but they are code-first tools for machine learning engineers, and medical suites are typically run centrally by their developers. 11 Open-weight models are increasingly competitive on difficult diagnostic cases, 23 and running them locally keeps sensitive material in the institution, but it makes evaluation the institution's responsibility.

We developed BenchmarkHub, an open-source, self-hostable platform that makes every step of the evaluation pipeline an explicit, recorded and re-runnable variable, and that extends evaluation from single-turn questions to tool-using agents. Here we describe the platform and report five use cases, run entirely through its public API on locally served open-weight models, that quantify how answer extraction, prompting protocol, image availability and tool access change medical and biomedical results. BenchmarkHub is available at https://github.com/juanjuanignacio/benchmarkhub.

Methods

Platform design

BenchmarkHub is a Python web application (Django 5.2, SQLite) released under the MIT licence. The application can be accessed through a browser interface, a set of command-line tools and a REST API, so interactive exploration and scripted pipelines produce identical, comparable records (Figure 1). Models are reached through provider adapters for local inference servers (Ollama, vLLM and any OpenAI-compatible endpoint) and for cloud APIs (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Mistral, Groq and Cohere). When a local server is used, prompts, questions and outputs never leave the institution. Per-provider concurrency limits and retry policies protect shared inference servers.

The platform ships loaders for 65 benchmarks, including 16 medical, clinical or biomedical datasets and ten additional ones for bioinformatics questions. Users can add their own questions from CSV files, the Hugging Face Hub or document collections. A run is defined by a benchmark, a model, a sample size with an optional seed (so different models receive the same questions), sampling parameters, a prompting protocol (system instruction, chain-ofthought cue or reproducibly drawn k-shot examples) and, optionally, tools. Vision benchmarks can be run with images removed, as a text-only ablation on identical items.

Questions are answered by a single completion or inside an agent loop that uses the provider's native function calling or, failing that, a text protocol. Tools (a Python interpreter, a calculator, file reading and eight bioinformatics sequence utilities) run in a sandbox: a separate Python process with CPU-time, memory and file-size limits, no network access and a fresh working directory per question (appendix p 5-6). Every assistant turn, tool call and tool output is stored as an inspectable trajectory.

Answers are graded by deterministic rules; no LLM is used as a judge. Multiple-choice answers are extracted by an ordered set of rules that recognises explicit answer statements and conclusion lines before falling back to isolated option letters, and the procedurally generated benchmarks store a typed answer (a number, sequence, list or label) that is compared exactly or, for decimal values, within a stated tolerance. Reasoning traces are removed before grading; a reply truncated without an answer is retried once, and remaining empty replies are graded on the end of the reasoning trace, with both events flagged. For each question the platform stores the prompt, raw reply, reasoning trace, extracted answer, correctness, tokens and latency, so grading rules can be improved and all runs re-scored without new inference (appendix pp 3-5).

To evaluate computational biology tasks rather than recall, the platform includes two procedurally generated benchmarks: BioCompute-Bench (300 single-step computations in 15 families, eg, translation, open reading frames, variant effect, CIGAR span, alignment score) and BioAgent-Bench (80 multi-step analyses over generated FASTA, VCF, GFF3, FASTQ and expression files, eg, splicing and translating a multi-exon gene). Each item comes from its own seeded generator, so every installation produces identical items that do not exist on the web; ground truth is computed by the code that implements the sequence-analysis tools, and we recomputed all 380 answers independently with Biopython (appendix p 8).

Models and inference

We evaluated 12 open-weight models served by Ollama (version 0.32.14) on a shared institutional multi-GPU server: seven instruction models (Llama 3.1 8B, Mistral 7B, Qwen2.5 7B, Phi-4 14B, Gemma 3 12B, Gemma 3 27B and Llama 3.3 70B), four models that produce a reasoning trace before answering (DeepSeek-R1 7B, GLM-4.7-Flash, Gemma 4 31B and Qwen3.8 27B) and one additional vision-language model (Qwen2.5-VL 7B). Together they cover the main open model families, sizes from 7 to 70 billion parameters, instruction and reasoning models, and models with published scores on the benchmarks used. Each build is identified by its Ollama digest. All were 4-bit quantised builds (Q4_K_M) except Llama 3.1 8B (16-bit). The context window was 32 768 tokens, temperature 0 unless stated, and the reply limit 2048 tokens for instruction models and 8192 for reasoning models and agent runs (appendix p 1).

Statistical analysis

Accuracy is reported with Wilson 95% CIs. Pooled medical accuracy is question-weighted across the biomedical benchmarks. Because conditions were compared on identical items, paired differences between models, extraction rules, protocols, image conditions and tool conditions were tested with exact McNemar tests and reported with Newcombe hybrid score 95% CIs, with Holm correction within each experiment. In experiment 1, a published score was considered compatible with our measurement if it lay within the measured 95% CI; because the CIs of these comparisons had half-widths of 5•7-13•4 points (150 questions per benchmark, 50 per MMLU subset), compatibility does not establish equivalence, and results for individual MMLU subsets are descriptive. The number of questions needed to detect a paired protocol effect was estimated with the sample-size formula for McNemar's test, using the proportion of discordant questions observed in experiment 2 (appendix p 15). In a sensitivity analysis, answers recovered from reasoning traces or obtained after truncation retries were excluded or counted as incorrect. Analyses used Python (pandas, SciPy) in notebooks that read the platform's exports. Reporting follows the TRIPOD-LLM guideline where applicable; 27 CONSORT-AI, SPIRIT-AI and DECIDE-AI do not apply because no trial or clinical deployment was involved. The study used only public benchmarks and involved no patients or personal data, so ethical approval was not required.

Results

The five experiments comprised 205 runs (143 in experiment 1, 12 additional runs in experiment 2, 12 in experiment 3, 20 in experiment 4, and 18 in experiment 5) and 21 490 graded answers. All runs, including their prompts, model outputs, extracted answers and evaluation records, are accessible through the BenchmarkHub interface (Figure 1).

Across the 950 medical questions included in the pooled analysis, model accuracy ranged from 46•1% (95% CI 43•0-49•3) for Mistral 7B to 80•9% (78•3-83•3) for Gemma 4 31B (Figure 2). A sensitivity analysis excluding the 233 answers that were recovered from reasoning traces or obtained after truncation retries increased GLM-4.7-Flash's pooled accuracy by 7•4 percentage points, moving it to second place. The remaining models changed by no more than 1•2 percentage points. Counting these answers as incorrect instead changed only the relative ranking of the two lowest-performing models (appendix p 14).

Evaluation choices changed measured accuracy by amounts comparable to the differences between models. The OpenAI simple-evals pattern, which searches for an explicit "Answer: X" statement, identified only 1-48% of replies as answerable, depending on the model. Discussion

BenchmarkHub makes model evaluation a recorded, repeatable and locally executable procedure. Using it on 12 open-weight models, we found that the answer-extraction rule alone changed medical accuracy by more than the differences between many models and reordered the leaderboard, that published scores were usually higher than those measured under a documented protocol, and that image availability and tool access changed results substantially, whereas prompting-protocol effects of up to 11 points could not be estimated precisely with 150 questions. A single number on a model card therefore says little unless the procedure that produced it is reported and can be rerun.

Answer extraction is usually treated as an implementation detail, but it was the largest source of variation among the evaluation settings we examined. Sensitivity to prompt format and answer extraction has been documented on general tasks, [14] [15] [16] and harness developers name extraction and normalisation as recurrent sources of irreproducibility; 18 we show that on medical benchmarks simple rules found in evaluation scripts change accuracy by up to 33 points for the same replies, penalising models for their reply style rather than their knowledge, and that even careful revisions of a rule move scores and ranks. Because the platform keeps every raw reply, extraction rules can be compared and audited after the fact, and all runs rescored without new inference. Medical evaluations should report their extraction rules and, ideally, release raw replies.

The gap with published scores, largest on MMLU-Pro, is consistent with those scores having been obtained with different prompting protocols and internal frameworks; quantisation is an unlikely sole explanation, because the largest gap was for the only model served with 16-bit weights. It illustrates why published and locally measured numbers should not be compared without their protocols. Typical medical evaluation sizes are also too small to estimate protocol effects: with 150 questions, differences of about 10 points between prompts were compatible with chance, and several hundred questions per condition are needed. Temperature 0 gave identical repeats in our setting, in which one local server processed requests without batching, but it does not guarantee reproducibility in general: when models are run through APIs or with batched inference, outputs at temperature 0 still vary between runs, 17, 28 so serving conditions belong in the methods of any evaluation and repeated runs are advisable.

The image ablation shows that multimodal accuracy can overstate visual understanding. Textonly shortcuts have been documented for general multimodal benchmarks 19 and flawed visual reasoning behind correct answers for medical image challenges. 20 MedXpertQA MM was filtered for difficulty rather than for image necessity, 24 and its original report did not include a text-only baseline; the two strongest models answered half of its items correctly without the image, and no model gained significantly from it. On MMMU 26 medical subjects, by contrast, the strongest models clearly used the image. Running every vision benchmark with and without images is a cheap control that the platform performs on identical items.

The agentic experiment adds a dimension that static question answering misses. Biology benchmarks without tools show that LLMs handle sequence manipulation poorly, 29 and agentic benchmarks over real analyses remain far from solved. 30 On our procedurally generated tasks, a sandboxed interpreter converted near-zero closed-book accuracy on character-level computations into near-perfect accuracy for the larger models, and multi-step analyses depended on writing code rather than on the predefined sequence tools, whereas small models stopped early or looped through failing tool calls. Procedural generation with computed, independently verified ground truth makes these benchmarks exactly gradable and resistant to memorisation, and the stored trajectories make agent failures diagnosable.

For health systems, running open-weight models on premises is attractive for data protection but shifts the burden of evaluation to the institution. BenchmarkHub lets a hospital or research group evaluate models on public benchmarks and its own questions without data leaving its infrastructure, compare them under identical documented conditions with paired statistics, and export complete records for audit, as recommended by reporting guidance for LLM studies. 27 Such evaluations are a screening step: benchmark accuracy is not clinical utility, [7] [8] [9] and our results add that even benchmark accuracy is conditional on the evaluation procedure. The platform can also host clinician-defined task suites 11 and new clinical benchmarks. 12, 13 This study has limitations. Benchmarks were sampled (150 questions per benchmark, 50 per MMLU subset), so individual accuracies carry CIs of about ±6-8 points (±10-14 for MMLU subsets) and protocol effects were underpowered; the platform can run complete benchmarks.

The final extraction rules were refined on replies from the same models and may be less accurate for other models. All but one model were 4-bit builds, which may explain part of the differences from published scores, and the published scores available were few and heterogeneous in protocol. The generated benchmarks cover well defined computational tasks rather than open research questions. The sandbox limits resources and blocks network access but does not isolate the filesystem or filter system calls, so untrusted models require a container or virtual machine (appendix p 6). Finally, all benchmarks were in English and none used real patient records.

In conclusion, medical benchmark results depend on how they are produced. Open, selfhosted evaluation infrastructure that records each step allows institutions to reproduce, audit and extend claims about LLMs before considering them for clinical use.
