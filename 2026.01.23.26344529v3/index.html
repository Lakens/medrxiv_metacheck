Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

This is a methods paper on the physiological interpretation of independent component analysis (ICA) applied to scalp EEG, but its scope extends to historical review and hypothesis-driven research. First, we trace how the small-patch model, which equates each brain independent component (IC) with a cortical patch smaller than 1 cm², became the central assumption of this interpretation. Second, we review a clinically and biophysically grounded alternative, the large-patch model (≥ 6 cm²). The two models make opposing predictions: the small-patch model predicts that about two-thirds of ICs lie on sulcal walls with tangential dipoles, whereas the large-patch model predicts radial dipoles displaced deeper than the cortex because fitting a single dipole to an extended source produces depth bias. Third, we test them: in simulation, depth bias grew with patch radius and was modulated, but not eliminated, by skull-conductivity mismatch. In resting-state EEG from two open datasets (820 recordings; 14,578 qualified brain ICs), 77.6% of fitted dipoles lay more than 5 mm inward from the template boundary and 57.9% lay more than 14 mm inward. The pooled signed-distance distribution showed two prominent peaks, near -1 mm and -19 mm, separated by a pronounced minimum near -5 mm (2-mm bins). Fitted dipole orientations showed no tangential dominance among deep ICs, which also exhibited higher activation power and lower residual variance than superficial ICs. These results favor the large-patch model. ICA remains valid for separating EEG sources, but the depths of fitted dipoles may reflect the spatial extent of the underlying current sources.

Introduction

This is a methods paper on the physiological interpretation of independent component analysis (ICA) (Bell & Sejnowski, 1995; Comon, 1994; Hyvärinen et al., 2001) applied to scalp EEG (hereafter, ICA means ICA application to EEG). However, to elucidate the historical and conceptual origins of this physiological interpretation, the scope of this paper extends beyond methodological research to encompass historical analysis, a literature review, and hypothesis-driven original research, with equal weight given to each. Accordingly, in the Introduction, before motivating the testable hypothesis, we first explain how the concept of the small-patch model, motivated by the microanatomy of intracortical lateral connections, became the central biophysical assumption underlying the conventional physiological interpretation of ICA. We then review the clinically and biophysically grounded alternative, the large-patch model, which has long existed outside the community of ICA users, and finally state the hypothesis and study design. A more detailed philological critique, quoting the original sources verbatim, is presented in the Discussion.

Motivation of the current study

ICA is widely used to remove artifacts from EEG (Castellanos & Makarov, 2006; Chaumon et al., 2015; Delorme, Sejnowski, et al., 2007; Jung, Makeig, Humphries, et al., 2000; Jung, Makeig, Westerfield, et al., 2000; Mognon et al., 2011; Nolan et al., 2010; Winkler et al., 2011). Beyond artifact removal, ICA-derived scalp topographies are fitted with equivalent current dipoles to localize ICs to brain regions (Debener et al., 2005; Delorme, Westerfield, et al., 2007; Jung, Makeig, McKeown, et al., 2001; Jung, Makeig, Westerfield, et al., 2001; Makeig & Onton, 2011; Makeig et al., 2002; Makeig, Debener, et al., 2004; Makeig, Delorme, et al., 2004; Onton & Makeig, 2006; Onton et al., 2005 Onton et al., , 2006)). Although this use of ICA is less common, it has become an established practice and can be found in recent studies (Huang et al., 2026; Lahijanian et al., 2024; Phillips et al., 2023; Symeonidou & Ferris, 2023). This practice rests on an auxiliary assumption that "there are strong biological reasons to believe that under favorable circumstances ICA should separate signals arising from local field activities in physically distinct, compact cortical source areas" (Delorme et al., 2012) (p.1) in which "expected effective size is relatively small (circa 1 cm or less)" (Akalin Acar et al., 2016) (p. 171). However, this assumption is seldom explicitly confirmed and, to our knowledge, has not been directly tested. If ICs arise from synchronous patches spanning several gyral crowns, single dipole model fits to their scalp projections should be displaced systematically beneath the cortex (Fuchs et al., 2017; Scherg et al., 2019). Hereafter, we refer to this as depth bias. Consequently, IC selection based on fitted dipole locations relative to the gray matter (Jungnickel & Gramann, 2016; Lin et al., 2018) may reject ICs with apparently implausible source depth, even though such depths are what extended generators predict.

Justification of ICA on EEG: independence-dipolarity identity (IDID)

ICA often yields near-dipolar scalp projections consistent with anatomically plausible cortical sources, despite using no spatial or biophysical constraints (Delorme et al., 2012; Onton & Makeig, 2006). In ERP studies, successful ICA decomposition has repeatedly been accompanied by scalp projections attributable to anatomically plausible sources (Debener et al., 2005; Delorme, Westerfield, et al., 2007; Makeig et al., 2002; Makeig, Delorme, et al., 2004; Makeig, Westerfield, Jung, et al., 1999; Makeig, Westerfield, Townsend, et al., 1999; Mouraux & Iannetti, 2009). Delorme et al. (2012) quantified this relation: across 18 ICA and related algorithms, the greater the reduction in mutual information between components, the larger the number of near-dipolar components. This correspondence between temporal independence and spatial dipolarity has been taken as evidence for the physiological validity of ICA (Delorme et al., 2012); we refer to it as the independence-dipolarity identity (IDID).

This almost unreasonable effectiveness of ICA in EEG analysis may be explained by three properties of EEG generation. First, volume conduction mixes source activities linearly and instantaneously, and this process is modeled by a single matrix multiplication. However, this condition is shared by all linear decompositions and not unique to ICA. Second, volume conduction performs spatial averaging through linear mixing, which, by the central limit theorem, makes scalp observations closer to Gaussian than their sources. Mixing therefore leaves a margin that ICA can exploit (Hyvärinen et al., 2001), which is "rather beautiful motivation behind ICA" (Friston, 1998). Third, empirical evidence suggests that functionally distinct EEG sources exhibit approximately independent temporal dynamics and spatially distinct distributions, consistent with the assumptions underlying ICA. However, it is important to note that ICA makes no assumption about the size or shape of the sources. We next review the origin of the small-patch model.

A historical review of the small-patch model

The application of ICA to EEG was first reported in 1996 (Makeig et al., 1996) and was developed further through the mid-2000s, including high-profile journal publications such as PNAS (Makeig et al., 1997) and Science (Makeig et al., 2002). The size of the cortical sources was initially loosely described, as "compact" (Makeig et al., 2002) or "cm²-scale" (Makeig, Debener, et al., 2004). Onton and Makeig (2006) attributed the small size of the sources to short-range neuronal connections and, to our knowledge for the first time, gave a spatial scale for them (500 μm). In 2012, Delorme and colleagues opened their paper by stating that "there are strong biological reasons" to expect ICA to separate "compact cortical source areas", and argued that "short-range (< 100 μm) lateral connections between cortical neurons are vastly more dense than longer-range connections" (Delorme et al., 2012). In the same year, an animation illustrating the small-patch model was made publicly available (https://youtu.be/6HTaRSiFFBg; Figure 1A). Akalin Acar et al. (2016) developed Simultaneous tissue Conductivity And source Location Estimation (SCALE), adopting source "compactness" as an optimization objective alongside goodness of fit. They specified the expected size as "circa 1 cm or less" which, taken as a diameter, corresponds to an area of less than 1 cm 2 (Akalin Acar et al., 2016). Thus, the small-patch model is motivated by the microanatomy of lateral neuronal connections and assumes "fully or partially coherent across a single small cortical domain or patch" (Akalin Acar et al., 2016). However, to our knowledge, the source-size assumption has not been directly tested.

An alternative: the large-patch model

Outside the ICA community, the question of how large a synchronously active cortical area must be to produce a scalp-recordable EEG signal has been studied since the 1960s. A long-standing estimate places the minimum cortical area resolvable by scalp EEG at approximately 6 cm 2 (i.e., 1 inch 2 ) (Cooper et al., 1965). This minimum has been repeatedly replicated by multiple groups of epileptologists using simultaneous EEG-ECoG recordings (CosandierRimélé et al., 2007; Ebersole, 1997 Ebersole, , 2000;; Hashiguchi et al., 2007; Ikeda et al., 1999; Kobayashi et al., 2005), and simulation studies have also supported its validity (CosandierRimélé et al., 2007; Nunez & Srinivasan, 2006). Importantly, 6 cm 2 represents a lower bound: examining inter-ictal spikes, Tao and colleagues found that 90% of cortical spikes arising from > 10 cm 2 of activated cortex produced observable scalp potentials, whereas those arising from < 6 cm 2 never did. They concluded that synchronous activation of 10-20 cm 2 of gyral cortex is common (Tao et al., 2005 (Tao et al., , 2007)), confirming the prediction from the simulation (Nunez & Srinivasan, 2006). We refer to the resulting view that scalp-recordable sources span at least 6-10 cm² of cortex as the large-patch model (Figure 1C). Throughout this paper, the two models are distinguished by area alone, and we read "compact" in the quoted literature as referring to size, not to spatial contiguity.

Why, then, do ICA-derived sources appear localized? Nunez and Srinivasan classified cortical sources of scalp EEG by spatial localization and temporal stationarity into four types: Type I, localized at fixed locations, such as lemniscal-mediated (Somervail et al., 2026) somatosensory evoked potentials (SEP) within 50 ms (Nunez & Srinivasan, 2006); Type II, localized at moving locations; Type III, distributed with a spatial pattern that is stable over time; and Type IV, distributed with a spatial pattern that changes over time. They argued that Type IV sources are "the rule rather than the exception" (Nunez & Srinivasan, 2006) (p. 195), and that localized sources are special cases. Ahlfors et al. (2010) similarly noted that "with the possible exception of early sensory evoked responses, patterns of activation are often sufficiently widespread that the effects of cancellation are relevant to interpretation of EEG and MEG data" (p. 145) (Ahlfors et al., 2010). The contrast can be quantified: the ratio of EEG to MEG peak-to-peak amplitude (μV to fT/cm) is about 0.04 for the median-nerve response at 20 ms, presumed to arise from a single focal dipole, but about 2.0 for sleep spindles, a 50-fold difference that was attributed to a distributed generator of EEG spindles (Dehghani et al., 2011). Although combining ICA with a single dipole model is well justified (Delorme et al., 2012), it may give the misleading impression that all ICA-derived EEG sources are Type I. This impression arises because the combination of ICA and single-dipole fitting reduces every source type to Type I in two steps. First, ICA removes spatial nonstationarity by fitting a time-invariant mixing matrix (Type II to Type I, Type IV to Type III). Second, the single equivalent dipole removes spatial extent by representing each IC scalp map by a point source (Type III to Type I). The central hypothesis of the current study is that the spatial extent removed in the second step does not vanish but reappears as depth bias.

Applied to cortical anatomy, the small-patch model makes a specific prediction. An average gyral crown is only 0.45-0.55 cm wide throughout adulthood (DíazCaneja et al., 2021), so a patch about 1 cm across cannot be accommodated on most gyral crowns. In contrast, cortical sulci provide a substantially larger surface: with an average depth of 1.3-1.4 cm and a wall-to-wall width of 0.3-0.4 cm, a single sulcus provides roughly 3 cm of continuous cortical surface from shoulder to shoulder, and the average sulcal length is nearly 700 cm (DíazCaneja et al., 2021). Multiple 1 cm² patches could fit within this geometry. Consequently, the small-patch model predicts a strong dominance of sources arising from sulcal walls rather than gyral crowns.

Competing predictions and study design

In this study, we test the two models against each other using ICA-derived equivalent current dipoles and show that the results support the large-patch model, thereby revising the assumptions about cortical source size underlying the physiological interpretation of ICA. As illustrated in Figure 1, the two models make distinct predictions regarding the depth and orientation of these dipoles. The predictions rest on three anatomical facts: only about one-third of the cortex lies on gyral crowns (DíazCaneja et al., 2021; Van Essen & Drury, 1997; Zilles et al., 1988), the average cortical thickness is approximately 2.5 mm, and the average sulcal depth is approximately 14 mm (DíazCaneja et al., 2021; Pakkenberg & Gundersen, 1997; Stark et al., 2007). If ICs sampled the cortex in proportion to its surface area, the small-patch model would predict that about one-third of brain ICs are localized to gyral crowns with small dipole angles relative to the radial direction, and about two-thirds to sulcal walls with large angles. Because a patch about 1 cm across cannot be accommodated on most gyral crowns, the sulcal fraction should be even larger. In either case, the fitted dipoles should remain within the cortical mantle, where the average sulcal depth is approximately 14 mm. The large-patch model instead predicts predominantly small dipole angles together with a general depth bias. Within a patch spanning several gyri, the opposing walls of each sulcus carry dipole layers of opposite orientation whose contributions largely cancel, leaving a net far field approximately radial to the cortical envelope (Ahlfors et al., 2010; Fuchs et al., 2017; Nunez & Srinivasan, 2006). When this broad field is fitted with a single equivalent dipole, volume conductor theory predicts that the fitted dipole is displaced deeper than the cortical layer, increasingly so as the patch area grows. Because this depth bias is known in principle (Ahlfors et al., 2010; Fuchs et al., 2017), we conducted a simple simulation with a four-layer spherical head model, parametrically varying dipole-layer area and depth, to calibrate its magnitude.

The two models also differ in the spatial structure of dipole orientations across the cortex and in how IC activation power should vary with depth (Table 1). Depth bias can also arise from an inaccurate head model, notably from misestimated skull conductivity (Akalin Acar et al., 2016). We therefore also test for a positive relation between IC activation power and dipole depth, which a global head-model error would not produce. To determine which model the data support, we analyzed a total of 820 EEG recordings (62 and 64 channels) from healthy adults during eyes-open and eyesclosed rest, drawn from two open datasets (Babayan et al., 2019; Getzmann et al., 2024). Table 1 summarizes the predictions of the two models.

EEG preprocessing

Figure 2 provides an overview of the preprocessing pipeline. EEG preprocessing was performed using Matlab R2023a (The MathWorks Inc., Natick, MA) and EEGLAB library (Delorme & Makeig, 2004) with custom code (https://github.com/MakotoMiyakoshi/DS1). Raw EEG data were downsampled to 250 Hz. Canonical electrode locations on the MNI head template were assigned (Collins et al., 1994; Evans et al., 1993). A high-pass FIR filter (Hamming window; cutoff 1.5 Hz at -6dB; 1 Hz transition bandwidth) was applied. Artifact subspace reconstruction (ASR) was performed using the EEGLAB plugin clean_rawdata, with a cutoff threshold of SD = 20 (Anders et al., 2020; Chang et al., 2018 Chang et al., , 2020;; Kim et al., 2025; Kothe & Jung, 2016; Kothe & Makeig, 2013; Miyakoshi, 2023). No other options were used in applying clean_rawdata. EEG signals were then re-referenced to the average of all scalp electrodes plus the initial reference (continuous zero channel), thereby recovering FCz as the reference electrode (Kim et al., 2023). Adaptive mixture independent component analysis (AMICA) (Palmer et al., 2016) was applied, discarding data points exceeding 3 SD during the first 15 iterations (maximum 2000 iterations) only for the purpose of AMICA per developer's recommendation (Jason Palmer, private communication). Independent components (ICs) were classified probabilistically using ICLabel (PionTonachini et al., 2019) into the categories of brain, eye, muscle, heart, line noise, channel noise, and other. Finally, equivalent current dipole models were fitted to each IC scalp topography (columns of the ICA mixing matrix mapped to electrode locations) using FieldTrip (Oostenveld et al., 2011) and EEGLAB plugin fitTwoDipoles to fit symmetric bilateral dipoles where appropriate (Piazza et al., 2016).

Head model preparation

For EEG source-model analysis, we used the ICBM152 template head model included with EEGLAB (avg152t1.mat). This anatomical template represents the averaged T1weighted MRI of 152 healthy young adults (ages 18.5-43.5 years) acquired at the Montreal Neurological Institute as part of the International Consortium for Brain Mapping (ICBM) project (Fonov et al., 2011; Mazziotta et al., 1995) Anatomical labeling was performed using the Automated Anatomical Labeling (AAL) atlas (TzourioMazoyer et al., 2002) as implemented in the ROI_MNI_V4.nii file distributed with FieldTrip (Oostenveld et al., 2011). Because our analysis did not require internal compartment boundaries (e.g., ventricular walls or cerebellar interfaces), we manually edited lower axial slices (z = -26 to -12 mm) to close deep sulcal clefts and ventricular/cerebellar cavities, yielding a topologically spherical and approximately convex cortical volume. This customized cortical model was then expanded to match the spatial extent of the EEGLAB's template head model ICBM152 on a slice-by-slice basis (see Figure S1 in Supplement). The resulting atlas was thus coregistered to the EEGLAB's head model used for dipole fitting. The modified AAL cortical mask was used for anatomical classification. Dipole depth and orientation were calculated relative to a separate boundary mesh derived from the innermost (brain-compartment) surface of the three-layer EEGLAB DIPFIT template BEM head model (vol.bnd(3)). This surface was voxelized and processed to generate a closed mesh, which served as the common geometric reference for signed-distance and dipole-angle calculations. The resulting boundary is not equivalent to the individual cortical gray-matter surface.

For the primary angle analysis, we calculated polarity-invariant axial angles relative to the local outward normal at the nearest point on the template braincompartment boundary. As a sensitivity analysis, we repeated the calculation using the radial direction from the MNI origin to each fitted dipole. To examine the dependence of the angle-depth relationship on the reference direction, we constructed joint histograms for both definitions using identical dipole populations and signed depths. Histograms were computed using fixed bin widths of 3° × 1 mm and smoothed with a twodimensional Gaussian kernel (σ = 1.5 bins). For Figures 4 5, signed distance was defined as negative inward and positive outward. For Figure 8, dipole depth was defined as the negative of this signed distance, so that positive values indicate inward displacement. Axial angles were defined as arccos| |, where and are unit vectors, yielding values between 0° and 90°. A separate set of 43 AAL labels was used as one criterion for identifying dipoles in the operationally defined Subcortex tier. These labels included the olfactory cortex, insula, hippocampus, parahippocampal gyrus, amygdala, caudate, putamen, pallidum, thalamus, and cerebellar regions. The tier name does not imply that all these structures are anatomically subcortical.

EEG analysis

Power spectral density (PSD) for IC activations was computed from 2 to 45 Hz in 0.1-Hz steps using MATLAB's stft function with a 1-s Hamming window and 50% overlap. For robustness against outliers, the median across time windows was used instead of the mean. The spectral exponent coefficient of the 1/ spectrum was estimated using FOOOF (Donoghue et al., 2020) with the following parameters: aperiodic_mode = 'fixed', max_peaks = 1, min_peak_height = 0, peak_width_limits = [0.5 4], proximity_threshold = 0, peak_type = 'best', guess_weight = 'weak', hOT = 1, and thresh_after = true. The resulting exponent from the 1/ fit was later used for quality control. For each IC, the activation time series was divided into 100 nonoverlapping segments of equal length. The variance was calculated within each segment, and the median of these values was used as a robust estimate of IC activation variance. The ICA scalp projection maps were normalized to unit root-mean-square (RMS) amplitude across channels, ensuring a consistent scaling convention for comparing IC activation variances. Finally, residual variance from dipole fitting was also obtained for both quality control and evaluation. ICs labeled as "brain" by ICLabel were further screened according to the following criteria: (1) residual variance of dipole fitting < 0.15 (2) IC activation power falls between the 0.1 and 99.9 percentiles (3) spectral exponent coefficient α > 0.4.

The remaining 14,578 qualified Brain ICs were assigned to three operationally defined spatial tiers. Interpretation of the AAL-defined cortex.

It is important to note that the AAL-defined cortex is substantially thicker than actual human gray matter. Empirically, mean cortical thickness in the human brain is approximately 2.5 mm (DíazCaneja et al., 2021). The AAL cortical labels describe broad anatomical volumes and do not delineate cortical gray matter. In the modified mask, the mean thickness at z = 20 mm was 23.6 mm (SD = 6.2; range: 8.9-44.0 mm; Figure 4D), substantially exceeding cortical gray-matter thickness. These labels alone cannot identify dipoles fitted near cortical gray matter. We introduced an independent 5-mm inward-distance threshold to distinguish dipoles fitted near the template boundary from those fitted farther inward. This threshold is an operational criterion, not an anatomical estimate of cortical thickness. The resulting categories describe fitted equivalent-dipole locations, not the locations of their underlying current sources.

Simulation Study of Serial Forward-Inverse Source Modeling

The forward-inverse simulation demonstrated depth bias in single-dipole fitting (Figure 3). As expected, this simple simulation revealed the area-depth dependency when using a singledipole model. The overall residual variance was < 2% (for detail, see Supplement). We concluded that the large-patch model explains the depth bias when a single current dipole model is fitted.

To assess the effect of skull-conductivity mismatch, we varied the BSCR independently in the forward and inverse models (Figure 3D). Because depth shifts are expressed relative to the matched fit, the zero lines for the matched conditions are definitional and do not indicate zero absolute localization error. Relative to the matched fits, a higher inverse BSCR shifted the dipoles toward the scalp, and a lower inverse BSCR shifted them deeper. With a forward BSCR of 80, inverse BSCRs of 40 and 20 increased the estimated depth by approximately 4-6 and 9-11 mm, respectively. With a forward BSCR of 20, inverse BSCRs of 40 and 80 decreased it by approximately 3-5 and 7-11 mm, respectively. A forward BSCR of 40 showed the same pattern. These shifts were nearly constant across UDL radii, whereas the matched depth bias grew with radius, reaching approximately 35-38 mm at 64 mm (Figure 3C). Fits outside the brain, and fits that depended on the initial dipole position, occurred only when the inverse BSCR exceeded the forward BSCR, and only for small radii (≤ 16 mm). Thus, skullconductivity errors can amplify or partially offset the depth bias of extended sources, but within the tested range they did not eliminate its radius dependence.

Extracting preselected brain components

Across the 820 datasets, we obtained 52,056 ICs, of which ICLabel identified 19,985 as Brain class candidates (Figure 4A). Spatial classification and characterization of fitted dipoles

The retained Brain ICs were assigned to three operationally defined spatial tiers: Surface cortex (19.7%), Deep cortex (52.5%), and Subcortex (27.8%) (Figure 4C, G-I). The pooled signeddistance distribution showed two prominent peaks, near -1 mm and -19 mm, separated by a pronounced minimum near -5 mm (2-mm bins; Figure S7). The independently chosen 5-mm inward threshold therefore coincided approximately with a minimum in the empirical distribution. Overall, 77.6% (11,315 of 14,578) of qualified Brain ICs were fitted more than 5 mm inward from the template boundary. This proportion ranged from 74.9% to 79.6% when the threshold was varied from 3 to 7 mm.

Because sulcal walls extend well below gyral crowns, dipole depth alone cannot reliably distinguish the two models. Nevertheless, the depth distribution was strongly shifted inward: 57.9% (8,437 of 14,578) of qualified Brain ICs were fitted more than 14 mm inward from the template boundary. This distance is not directly equivalent to anatomical sulcal depth. Sulcalwall generators should predominantly produce tangential dipoles with bipolar, figure-eight scalp topographies. Under both geometric reference definitions, the joint angle-depth distributions showed broad ranges of dipole axial angles among deeply fitted ICs, without the concentration near 90° expected from predominantly tangential sulcal-wall generators (Figure 8). Angle-difference tomography, reference sensitivity, and angle-depth relations

The small-patch model predicts predominantly radial orientations at gyral crowns and tangential orientations along sulcal walls. However, a fitted dipole's distance from the template braincompartment boundary does not directly identify whether its underlying generator occupies a gyral crown or a sulcal wall. The empirical angle tomography in Figure 7A reveals broad spatial structure rather than an abrupt transition between radial and tangential orientations.

A band of relatively larger axial angles was observed across approximately z = 16-56 mm (Figure 7A). Although this pattern may overlap peri-Rolandic regions, its precise anatomical origin cannot be determined from the present template-based tomography. Time-frequency signature in these regions is often characterized as the μ rhythm to which ICA is notably sensitive. This observation suggests that regional anatomical features may contribute to dipoleangle variability, although the relative contributions of anatomical curvature, fitted-dipole depth, and the choice of geometric reference direction cannot be determined from the tomography alone.

In contrast to the tangential dominance expected for sulcal-wall generators under the small-patch model, the local-reference distributions showed median axial angles of 36.4°, 40.6°, and 47.9° for Surface cortex, Deep cortex, and Subcortex, respectively (Figure 7B). None of the three distributions was concentrated near the tangential limit of 90°. This consistent dominance of moderately oblique orientations suggests the influence of additional, unresolved sources of angle variation at mesoscopic-macroscopic scales which were not captured by either the smallpatch or large-patch idealizations.

To assess the sensitivity of dipole angles to the choice of reference direction, we compared two definitions: the local outward normal of the template brain-compartment boundary and the radial direction from the MNI origin to each fitted dipole (Figure 7B-C). To eliminate the arbitrary polarity of ICA-derived dipoles, angles were expressed as polarity-invariant axial angles ranging from 0° to 90°. The two definitions yielded nearly identical distributions for Surface cortex (median 36.4° vs. 35.9°), but increasingly different distributions for Deep cortex (40.6° vs. 33.5°) and Subcortex (47.9° vs. 37.1°). Thus, the estimated radiality of deep equivalent dipoles is sensitive to the geometric reference direction, whereas superficial dipoles are relatively insensitive to this choice.

To examine how the choice of geometric reference direction affects the apparent angledepth relationship, we generated joint histograms using both the local boundary normal and the MNI-origin radial direction (Figure 8). Under the local-normal reference, peak angles increased from 22.5° for Surface cortex to 34.5° for Deep cortex and 40.5° for Subcortex (Figure 8A-C). In contrast, under the MNI-origin reference, the corresponding peaks were 25.5°, 22.5°, and 22.5° (Figure 8E-G), which replicated our previous report (Miyakoshi et al., 2023). Thus, the apparent increase in peak angle across depth tiers was not preserved when the reference direction was changed. Nevertheless, under both definitions, Surface-cortex ICs were concentrated near the template boundary, whereas Deep-cortex and Subcortex ICs occupied broadly overlapping regions of angle-depth space. These findings demonstrate the sensitivity of the apparent angle-depth relationship to the geometric reference direction. The reported peaks represent modes of smoothed joint histograms and do not directly quantify the conditional dependence of angle on depth. Taken together, the empirical distributions show no clear tangential dominance among deeply fitted dipoles under either reference definition. Although the local-normal histograms show increasing peak angles across depth tiers, this pattern is absent under the MNI-origin reference. Thus, the apparent angle-depth relationship is referencedependent and cannot be interpreted as direct evidence of a transition from gyral to sulcal generators. These findings underscore the limitations of anatomical interpretations based solely on fitted equivalent-dipole geometry.

In most comparisons, the observed distributions departed from the predictions of the smallpatch model in dipole depth, orientation, or angle-depth structure. A narrow peak of fitted dipoles was observed near the template boundary (Figure 4G), separated from the broader inward peak by a pronounced minimum near -5 mm (Figure S7). Thus, ICs with stronger activation and better dipolar fits were frequently associated with unexpectedly deep equivalent-dipole locations. To our knowledge, this tension has not been discussed in the ICA literature.

Discussion

In this study, we attempted to determine the physiological interpretation of ICA applied to scalp-recorded EEG. To this goal, we compared the small-patch source model proposed by early ICA proponents with the large-patch model supported by neurologists and theorists. Using two European resting-state EEG databases, we analyzed the distributions of ICA-derived dipole depth and their relationships to IC activation power, residual variance, and dipole orientation. Across these measurements, empirical results consistently favored the large-patch model over the small-patch model. Below, we discuss the implications of these findings point by point.

The significance of the current study

The original physiological interpretation of ICA rests on the small-patch source model (Akalin Acar et al., 2016; Cao et al., 2012; Delorme et al., 2012; Makeig et al., 2002; Makeig, Debener, et al., 2004; Onton & Makeig, 2006; Tsai et al., 2014), an assumption that has not been directly tested, although simultaneous EEG-ECoG studies point to larger sources (CosandierRimélé et al., 2007; Ebersole, 1997 Ebersole, , 2000;; Hashiguchi et al., 2007; Ikeda et al., 1999; Kobayashi et al., 2005; Nunez & Srinivasan, 2006; Tao et al., 2005 Tao et al., , 2007)). To our knowledge, the present study is the first to explicitly interrogate this core premise and test its predictions against large-scale empirical EEG data. Notably, despite the widespread adoption of ICA for artifact rejection, its use for extracting putative brain sources has remained limited. One plausible reason is that the physiological interpretation of ICA critically relies on dipolarity and the associated IDID (Delorme et al., 2012), yet ICA-derived dipole fits frequently yield physiologically implausible depths, which is a pattern supported by the predominance of inwardly fitted dipoles in the present data. Specifically, 77.6% of qualified Brain ICs were fitted more than 5 mm inward from the template boundary, and 57.9% more than 14 mm inward. Although these distances are not directly equivalent to anatomical sulcal depths, the observed depth distribution, together with dipole orientation, challenges the small-patch interpretation. Such results may have discouraged anatomical interpretation, in addition to the more technical challenge of post-ICA inter-subject inconsistency. A second reason concerns distributed source models. Dipolarity is not a physical quantity but a model-relative one: it is the goodness of fit of a single dipole. It therefore makes sense only within dipole fitting. Distributed source models applied to ICs provide no equivalent measure, so the validation performed by Delorme et al. (2012) cannot be carried out in that framework. Distributed source modeling typically imposes a gray-matter constraint on source locations. In contrast, our dipole fitting does not impose any constraint on the location of fitted dipoles. Although the gray-matter constraint is physiologically motivated and computationally useful for solving the inverse problem, it carries the risk of prematurely discarding informative structure present in minimally constrained model outputs. The present study demonstrates the value of a minimally constrained model by examining outputs that may initially appear physiologically implausible. As shown in Figure 6 and 7, dipoles localized at implausible depths nevertheless exhibited systematic, spatially organized low-frequency patterns. This observation argues against dismissing deep dipoles. In fact, these deep dipole sources showed higher signal power and lower residual variance, indicating greater dominance and better model fit than superficially localized, physiologically plausible dipole sources. These observations caution against a bias toward premature physiological interpretation. Rather, judgment should be suspended (i.e., Husserlian epoché) until the full set of observations can be accounted for in light of basic anatomy and electrophysiology established in the literature. Finally, the presence of a systematic bias should not be regarded as bad as it may appear because it points to regularity in the underlying generative mechanism producing the error, like the case of the dipole's depth bias we clarified using simulation.

Finally, the present study tested the small-patch model within the methodological framework of the original ICA studies. We used the same analysis tools and criteria as prior ICA-based work but examined two aspects that have received little attention: dipole depth and orientation. By applying the original approach to previously unexamined dimensions of the data, we test the small-patch model within its own methodological framework.

Can head-model error explain the observed depths?

Skull-conductivity mismatch in the direction expected for our inverse model would shift fitted dipoles outward and therefore cannot explain the predominance of deep fitted locations. Our inverse model assumed a BSCR of 80, substantially higher than the value of 27.6 adopted in a recent account of EEG biophysics (Halnes et al., 2024). Although the actual BSCRs of our participants are unknown, this comparison suggests that the inverse model may have overestimated the ratio. As shown in Figure 3D, using an inverse BSCR higher than the forward BSCR shifts fitted dipoles toward the scalp. If the actual BSCR was below 80, this mismatch would oppose the inward bias associated with extended cortical generators. Our simulations also produced dipole estimates outside the brain boundary when the inverse BSCR exceeded the forward BSCR, particularly for small patches. In the empirical data, 1,195 of 2,873 Surface-cortex ICs (41.6%) were fitted outside the template boundary, with a median outward distance of 0.66 mm. This observation is consistent with the direction of the simulated conductivitymismatch bias, although template anatomy, electrode-position errors, and other modeling uncertainties may also contribute.

A canonical statement of the small-patch model We selected a paragraph from Akalin Acar and colleagues (Akalin Acar et al., 2016) that we believe most clearly articulates the physiological concept underlying the small-patch model. It is a lengthy passage, but because it is central to the argument, we reproduce it in full below without modification.

More formally, we assume that each IC source represents a far-field projection to the electrodes of local cortical field potentials that are fully or partially coherent across a single small cortical domain or patch of unknown size and shape. In large part because of the extreme preponderance of short-range corticocortical connections (≤100 μm), wholly so for inhibitory neurons and glia, and the predominance of tight radial thalamocortical loops, both supporting oscillatory local field dynamics, models of cortical field dynamics (Deco et al., 2008) demonstrate the emergence of such patches or islands of cortical local field synchrony likened to "pond ripples" by Freeman (2003) and to recurring (point spread) avalanches by Beggs and Plenz (2004). Thus, brain connectivity both favors the emergence of temporal synchrony within small, connected cortical domains and minimizes temporal synchrony between such domains other than in exceptional locations and circumstances. Because of the common alignment of cortical pyramidal cells perpendicular to the cortical surface, spatially coherent local field activity across such a patch will have a non-negligible far field projection to the scalp (Baillet et al., 2001; Nunez and Srinivasan, 2006), forming an effective EEG source whose time course may typically be near statistically independent of the time courses of concurrent far field potentials generated by other, spatially separated cortical source patches.

Within this passage, the second sentence is of particular importance. At the same time, it is syntactically dense and conceptually layered. Focusing on this key sentence, we can simplify the argument as follows:

Because of the preponderance of short-range corticocortical connections (≤100 μm) and the predominance of radial thalamocortical loops, models of cortical field dynamics (Deco et al., 2008) demonstrate the emergence of small cortical patches.

In the following sections, we refer to this passage as the canonical statement of the small-patch model, and examine its validity.

Where did the small-patch model come from, and what does it miss?

The small-patch model was motivated by electrophysiological studies conducted at microscopic spatial scales (≈100-1000 μm), including recordings from inhibitory (Budd & Kisvárday, 2001) and excitatory neurons (Stepanyants et al., 2009; Stettler et al., 2002) in the visual cortex of cats and macaques. These studies characterized the distribution of cortico-cortical lateral connection distances and emphasized the predominance of short-range connectivity such as ≤100 μm (Delorme et al., 2012) and < 500 μm (Onton & Makeig, 2006). From this microcircuit perspective, the original ICA studies inferred that EEG-measurable sources should arise from small cortical patches.

While this inference is intuitively appealing, it overlooks a critical property of population-level field generation that relatively small fractions of synchronized neurons can dominate macroscopic electrophysiological signals. Empirical and theoretical studies have shown that as little as 1% of pyramidal neurons within a cortical area of 1 cm² can account for approximately 96.5% of the observed signal amplitude (Hari, 1990; Hari et al., 1997). This counterintuitive effect arises from the nonlinear scaling of synchronous versus asynchronous contributions in large neuronal populations. To illustrate this point quantitatively, consider that the human cerebral cortex contains 22.9 billion neurons distributed across approximately 1,883 cm² of surface area in average man (Pakkenberg & Gundersen, 1997; Stark et al., 2007), with roughly 85% being pyramidal neurons (Kanari et al., 2019). This yields an estimate of approximately 10 million neurons per cm², which is remarkably similar to corresponding estimates in macaque visual cortex (O'Kusky & Colonnier, 1982). A synchronized subset comprising just 1% of this population corresponds to 100,000 neurons, whereas the expected amplitude of random, uncorrelated activity scales with the square root of the population size corresponds to 3162 neurons. Thus, 1% of synchronous neurons can produce 31.6 times larger signal than the background noise. The underlying simple mathematics shows that the contribution of a synchronized fraction always exceeds that of the entire population acting asynchronously once the number of neurons go beyond a critical mass (0.01 √ , 10000), which is only modest in the spatial scale of EEG (Elul, 1971).

Independent empirical support for this sparse-synchrony, large-area view comes from the classic work of Walter (Walter et al., 1964). Using human intracranial EEG recordings, Walter observed that sensory-evoked responses rarely exceeded 30 µV, whereas direct electrical stimulation of the same cortical regions could elicit highly localized after-discharges of 1-3 mV, confined to only a few cubic millimeters of cortex. Interpreting these after-discharges as an analogue of "fully coherent" activation (as in the canonical statement) of the local neuronal population, Walter inferred that sensoryevoked responses engage on the order of 1% of neurons at any given cortical locus. The comparison on brain potentials between spontaneous field oscillation and that caused by electrical stimulation was replicated immediately (Cooper et al., 1965). Walter concluded that:

The conclusion is that a signal from any receptor, even if due to maximal sensory stimulation, can activate only about one neuron in a hundred in frontal cortex, but will have this effect over a vast surface containing several thousand million neurons. All significant sensory stimuli therefore […] must be considered as activating some tens of millions of neurons over a very extensive area, without necessarily intruding on any other afferent signals or background activity.

The scaling relationship reviewed above implies that scalp-recorded EEG is extremely sensitive to fluctuations in neural synchrony, and that this sensitivity grows rapidly with the spatial extent of the contributing cortical population. In contrast, the absolute synchrony rate becomes progressively less important as population size grows. By focusing on micro-scale connectivity and absolute synchrony rates, the small-patch model does not take into account this population-level property of field summation, thereby underestimating significance of the effective spatial extent of EEG-generating sources. Instead, scalp-recorded EEG is more plausibly dominated by weak neural synchrony distributed across large cortical patches (> 10 cm 2 (Tao et al., 2005 (Tao et al., , 2007)), corresponding to neural populations of > 100 million neurons (Kanari et al., 2019; O'Kusky & Colonnier, 1982; Pakkenberg & Gundersen, 1997; Stark et al., 2007)), with synchrony rate less than a few percent (Hari, 1990; Hari et al., 1997). This scaling law is both unique and critical to scalp EEG research, and fundamentally distinguishes it from invasive electrophysiological modalities.

Finally, we found that two anatomical claims commonly cited in support of the small-patch model do not match their sources. For example, in the canonical statement, work by Deco and colleagues is cited as evidence that an extreme preponderance of short-range corticocortical connections (≤100 μm) supports a small source model.

However, Deco and colleagues explicitly stated that "Intracortical fibers are mostly unmyelinated and extend laterally up to 1 cm (in the human brain) with excitatory and inhibitory connections." (Deco et al., 2008) Their work contains no analysis or claim linking the prevalence of ≤100-μm connections to the emergence of small patches of EEG sources. A second representative example appears in Delorme and colleagues (Delorme et al., 2012), who argued:

There are strong biological reasons to believe that under favorable circumstances ICA should separate signals arising from local field activities in physically distinct, compact cortical source areas: First, short-range (<100 μm) lateral connections between cortical neurons are vastly more dense than longerrange connections [3, 4]… Reference [4] in this passage is the work of Stepanyants and colleagues (Stepanyants et al., 2009). However, Stepanyants and colleagues summarized their findings in the opposite direction: "excitatory connectivity in cat area 17 is highly nonlocal; 74% of excitatory synapses near the axis of a 1,000-μm-diameter cortical column originate from neurons located outside the column." Thus, explicitly nonlocal connectivity (> 500 μm from the column axis) was cited as evidence for local connectivity (<100 μm). In both cases, the cited source does not support the claim attributed to it, yet these claims have been repeated for more than a decade and continue to reinforce the small-patch model. We suspect the reason lies in who reads which literature. The anatomical studies are read mainly by microcircuit physiologists, who have little occasion to open a methods paper on scalp-EEG ICA, and ICA users seldom go back to the primary anatomy. We have not surveyed the small-patch literature systematically and do not claim that these two cases are the only ones. Both discrepancies, however, are evident from the cited sources themselves, and readers should consult those sources before relying on anatomical claims passed along through this literature.

Implications for the small-patch model How much does challenging the small-patch model affect the physiological interpretation of ICA? We argue that the impact is limited because IDID (Delorme et al., 2012) is independent of the assumption of the source model and remains intact. That said, our findings have direct implications for Simultaneous Tissue Conductivity and Source Location Estimation (SCALE) (Akalin Acar et al., 2016). In this framework, skull conductivity is parametrically varied to identify a solution that minimizes the estimated cortical source patch size. While the methodological idea is innovative, the justification of the central optimization criterion for minimizing cortical patch size directly depends on the validity of the small-patch model. If the small-patch assumption does not hold, the physiological meaning of the SCALE solution becomes unclear, and the rationale for favoring minimal patch size over alternative models is no longer supported.

Examples of a large-patch model

Whitmer and colleagues stated that "synchronous activity across a multi-cm cortical region has not been reported in the normal awake brain" (Whitmer et al., 2010). However, there are at least two counterexamples for this statement, which exemplifies the large-patch model across a multi-cm cortical region.

The first counterexample is the macroscopic traveling wave: simulation demonstrated that local coupling with neighbors supports globally extended spatiotemporal order (Jeong et al., 2002; Udeigwe & Ermentrout, 2015). Large spatial scales can be phase-organized without requiring long-range, millisecond-precise zerolag synchrony. Synchrony is not a unitary phenomenon, and zero-lag synchrony across area is one extreme. The broad area can be coherent but not simultaneously. There are various phase gradients and travelling waves. The traveling wave phenomena in neural field has been known since early days of scalp EEG research (Hughes, 1995). There is a recent surge of publications on traveling waves from high-impact journals, with the emphasis on computational modeling (Dugué & Chavane, 2025) including (Halgren et al., 2019; Muller et al., 2018). In grey-matter recordings, Alexander and Dugué found that the spatial spectrum of cortical phase peaked at the longest measurable wavelengths (8-16 cm) from delta to high gamma, arguing against local generators blurred by volume conduction as the origin of scalp-recorded waves (Alexander & Dugué, 2026). These observations are difficult to reconcile with the small-patch premise that synchrony between cortical domains about 1 cm across is minimal (Akalin Acar et al., 2016): at the dominant wavelengths of 8-16 cm, sites 1 cm apart along the cortical sheet differ in phase by only about 20-45°.

Another counterexample shows more direct evidence of large-scale, simultaneous coherence mediated by extralemniscal thalamic pathway. It has been well-demonstrated that sudden and isolated sensory stimuli (SISS) trigger some of the largest and most widespread electrocortical responses in the awake mammalian brain mediated by the extralemniscal thalamic system: for review, see (AlbeFessard & Besson, 1973; Miyakoshi et al., 2025; Somervail et al., 2026; Walter, 1964). It has some prominent characteristics including supramodality, sensitivity to inter-stimulus interval, vertex-maximum topography, and state of consciousness. In the current context, the most relevant and notable property of the extralemniscal thalamic system is that its projection is pan-cortical. The supramodality and pan-corticality was demonstrated using depth recording of mouse sensory cortex, in which auditory stimuli robustly triggered sensory cortex activation when inter-stimulus interval was 8 s (Benusiglio & Asari, 2024). Walter called non-modality-specific cortices and responses as non-specific cortex and response, respectively (Walter, 1964), the latter of which corresponds to responses of the extralemniscal thalamic system, and described it as follows:

Since the fields of the evoked potentials in nonspecific areas are so widespread that no part of the head is quite unaffected by them.

With this arrangement evoked responses to auditory, visual, and tactile stimuli have been recorded from the ears, chin, neck, and, in fact, all parts of the head.

The most satisfactory principle is to assume that, in experiments with human subjects, all evoked responses are mainly due to nonspecific activation unless the contrary can be proved.

To provide quantitative estimates, the ratio of thalamic neurons in the lateral geniculate nucleus to the cortical neurons they innervate is 0.1-1% (Connolly & Van Essen, 1984; O'Kusky & Colonnier, 1982). However, each individual axon is not restricted to a vertical column of these neurons, but spreads out tangentially and overlaps with its neighbors. As a result, this rate increases in the synaptic counts: The rate of cortex receiving thalamic input was classically estimated to be 5% in cat visual cortex (Peters & Payne, 1993), 6.9-8.7 % in macaque visual cortex (Latawiec et al., 2000). More recent estimate using a combination of 3D-electron-microscopy and 3Dconfocal imaging of thalamic boutons on macaque visual cortex, 15 and 20% for magnocellular and parvocellular pathways, respectively, which is 2 to 3 times larger than the earlier estimates. (GarciaMarin et al., 2019). In the rodent barrel cortex, the number has been around 15 % (Benshalom & White, 1986; Bruno & Sakmann, 2006; Schoonover et al., 2014). These quantitative estimates suggest that extralemniscal thalamo-pancortical projection contributing to form a large-patch cortical EEG source is anatomically plausible, contrary to the microanatomical argument of the small-patch model.

Simultaneous EEG-MEG recordings point the same way. Dehghani et al. (2011) proposed that EEG is most sensitive to sleep spindles generated diffusely and synchronously by the nonspecific (matrix) thalamocortical system, whereas MEG is most sensitive to multiple focal, asynchronous spindles generated by the specific (core) system (Dehghani et al., 2011; Somervail et al., 2026). This distinction parallels Walter's separation of specific and nonspecific responses and suggests that scalp EEG preferentially records the extended activity driven by nonspecific thalamic projections. In Nunez and Srinivasan's terms, the traveling wave is a Type IV source and the extralemniscal projection a Type III source. ICA reduces Type IV to Type III, and single-dipole fitting then converts the extent of either into depth bias; the present data therefore cannot distinguish them.

Why do sulcal sources contribute little to dominant ICs?

How much do sulcal sources contribute to the dominant ICs of scalp EEG? About twothirds of the cortical surface lies in sulci (DíazCaneja et al., 2021; Van Essen & Drury, 1997; Zilles et al., 1988), yet bipolar, 8-shaped topographies generated by tangentially oriented dipoles were rare among high-quality brain ICs. In our previous analysis of the same Leipzig data, positive-dominant ICs with low residual variance peaked at 10-30° from the radial axis, with no secondary peak near 90° (Miyakoshi et al., 2023). The geometry of an extended source explains this. When a patch spans a sulcus, the opposing walls carry dipole layers that face each other and point in opposite directions. Their far fields cancel to leading order, leaving a quadrupolar residual whose magnitude scales roughly with the ratio of the wall separation to the electrode distance. With the two grey-matter layers 5-7 mm apart and the electrodes 2-3 cm away, this residual is roughly 20-30% of the contribution of a single wall. The net field of a folded patch is therefore dominated by the radial contributions of the gyral crowns, which sum like a smooth dipole layer on the cortical envelope. The fundi are also radially oriented but contribute less because they occupy a smaller area and lie about 14 mm deeper, farther from the electrodes. (Ahlfors et al., 2010; Nunez & Srinivasan, 2006). Sulcal cortex is thus unlikely to form a dominant IC unless only one wall of a sulcus is active, in which case it appears as a tangential source. Such ICs are rare: we estimated them to account for at most about 9% of qualified brain ICs (Miyakoshi et al., 2023). The sensitivity analyses (Figures 7B-C and 8) further demonstrate that both the distribution of dipole angles and their apparent relationship with depth depend on the geometric reference direction. In particular, the increase in peak angle across spatial tiers observed under the local-normal reference was not reproduced under the MNI-origin reference. Neither reference necessarily corresponds to the orientation of local cortical columns.

Conclusion

The present study showed that most ICA-derived brain components yielded deep fitted dipoles (77.6% more than 5 mm inward and 57.9% more than 14 mm inward from the template brain-compartment boundary), without the tangential dominance expected from the small-patch model. Moreover, the apparent relationship between dipole orientation and depth depended substantially on the geometric reference direction. Together, these findings challenge the conventional small-patch interpretation and favor the large-patch model as a more plausible alternative. A lesson from this work is that macroscopic EEG analysis requires a correct understanding of scaling laws: for example, how synchrony in as little as 1% of neurons can outweigh the square root of total but random activity. ICA itself remains valid for separating EEG sources; what changes is how the depths of fitted dipoles should be read. We hope that this study motivates future investigations into whether depth bias reflects a general property of EEG source modeling and how such biases may be explained by established and emerging biophysical theories.
