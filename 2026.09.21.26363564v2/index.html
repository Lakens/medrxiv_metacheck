How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Although numerous clinical prediction models (CPMs) have been developed to identify pregnancies at risk of serious adverse outcomes, most lack robust external validation, which restricts their reliability in clinical practice. This study aims to systematically review and externally validate existing clinical prediction models for Gestational Diabetes Mellitus (GDM), Pre-eclampsia (PE), Stillbirth, and Small-for-Gestational-Age (SGA), focusing on models for use in preconception or early pregnancy. The models were evaluated using a large, representative primary care cohort to assess their performance within a UK population.

Four two-stage systematic reviews identified CPMs for GDM, PE, Stillbirth, and SGA. Eligible models used routinely collected maternal characteristics available before conception or in early pregnancy and fully reported the model equation. Validation used the Clinical Practice Research Datalink (CPRD) Aurum, including over 1.58 million pregnancies among women aged 14-49 years between 2000 and 2019. Performance was assessed through discrimination (C-statistic), calibration-in-the-large (CITL), calibration slope and calibration plot, with pooled estimates across 20 imputations.

Forty-eight models from 30 studies were included (47 binary and one continuous outcome), and 23 were developed in cohorts that included a UK population. Model development sample sizes ranged from 151 to 113,415 (median 5,013). Although 29 models had been externally validated, only one had been validated in a cohort including a UK population that met the required sample size and number of events. Across all outcomes, most models demonstrated limited generalisability in the UK population. C-statistics ranged from 0.57 to 0.77 for GDM, 0.50 to 0.72 for PE, 0.54 to 0.58 for stillbirth and 0.58 to 0.67 for SGA. Miscalibration was substantial across all outcomes, with CITL ranging from -6.14 to 9.64 (for binary-outcome models) and calibration slopes from 0.19 to 17.78.

Most existing CPMs that use pre-conception and early-pregnancy data to predict adverse pregnancy outcomes showed suboptimal discrimination and miscalibration in a large UK population, likely reflecting differences in population characteristics and outcome prevalence. Existing models are not suitable for direct implementation in UK practice and require further recalibration or updating using large, representative data before achieving clinical utility.

Background

Pregnancy is a particularly vulnerable period for women and their babies, with approximately 15-25% of pregnancies reportedly ending in miscarriage [1]. In the UK, pregnancy complications such as pre-eclampsia (PE), gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), and fetal growth restriction (FGR), affect approximately 5-8% of pregnancies, with a further 0.4% resulting in stillbirth [2] [3] [4] [5]. Clinical prediction models (CPMs) have been increasingly developed for identifying pregnant women at higher risk of developing a complication or adverse outcome [6, 7]. For instance, the Fetal Medicine Foundation (FMF) developed a first trimester screening algorithm to identify pregnancies at high risk of preterm pre-eclampsia, which showed improved detection rates of PE compared to standard clinical screening and enhanced aspirin prescribing, improved risk stratification and saw a significant reduction in preterm and early preeclampsia rates [8, 9]. Although many CPMs to predict the risk of developing a complication or adverse pregnancy outcome exist, small sample size, short follow-up periods, as well as lack of robust external validation, diminish the performance in estimates, especially for prediction of rare outcomes [10] [11] [12] [13] [14]. Additionally, the majority of existing models were developed using data from selective populations in secondary or tertiary specialised care settings, which restricts their generalisability to routine antenatal populations. Many existing models have been developed for use during pregnancy, rather than at the pre-conception or early pregnancy stage, which limits their utility for early risk stratification and preventive intervention [7, 15].

External validation is essential before the implementation of a prediction model in clinical practice [16] [17] [18]. When the target-use population differs from the original development cohort, a reduction in model performance is common.

Rigorous external validation therefore serves as a critical first step to determine if a model is ready for clinical implementation or if the model requires further refinement, such as re-calibration or subsequent updating, to ensure reliable performance in the intended population. To date, external validation studies of existing models have been restricted by small sample sizes, insufficient numbers of events, or non-representative cohorts, thereby limiting statistical performance and generalisability specifically for a national population [10] [11] [12] [13] 19]. Furthermore, current CPMs for some outcomes have not been adequately validated in UK, non-specialised populations, limiting confidence in use for risk assessment within the National Health Service (NHS). Therefore, utilising large routinely collected and more accessible primary care data from early pregnancy is crucial for developing and validating timely and effective screening tools.

The UK offers a unique opportunity through its large-scale, routinely collected primary care datasets, containing electronic health records of clinical characteristics such as body mass index (BMI), blood pressure, smoking status, ethnicity, and comorbidity history, linked to secondary care data about the birth of babies. Furthermore, evidence from a recent systematic review demonstrated that pre-conception health data derived from these sources is strongly associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes [20]. This highlights that such data may be valuable for validating existing predictive models, which could then inform strategies to optimise pre-conception health, support early pregnancy risk stratification, and guide targeted interventions at a population level.

This study aimed to conduct a rigorous systematic review and external validation study of existing CPMs for the most common and serious adverse outcomes of high clinical and patient health importance in the UK: pre-eclampsia, gestational diabetes, fetal growth restriction, and stillbirth. During this validation, we focused on prediction models that use predictors available before conception and during early pregnancy, thus evaluating whether such routinely collected data in UK primary care records can be used for early prediction. Our focus was on maternal demographic and clinical history, rather than biomarkers, ultrasound findings, or screening results typically obtained later in pregnancy. To achieve this aim, we used the Clinical Practice Research Datalink (CPRD) Aurum, which is a large, nationally representative dataset of primary care records and linked secondary care data including birth outcomes.

Literature search and selection

We conducted four outcome specific two-stage systematic reviews on PubMed to identify existing clinical prediction models (CPMs) for: gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), stillbirth, pre-eclampsia (PE), and fetal growth restriction (FGR). Each review used the same broad approach but applied outcome-specific search terms and eligibility criteria.

Eligible models were those developed to predict one of the specific pregnancy outcomes using routinely collected maternal characteristics available before conception or during early pregnancy. Routinely collected characteristics were defined as demographic factors, physical measurements, medical comorbidities, and maternal history. Existing literature used significant ambiguity in the definition and application of Small-for-Gestational-Age (SGA) and Fetal Growth Restriction (FGR), and often used interchangeably. Most definitions also failed to adhere to the international Delphi consensus definition, which incorporated expert agreement on fetal biometric, Doppler, and clinical predictors [23]. Consequently, we searched for all relevant publications that employed the terms FGR or SGA and used their original definition as outcome in subsequent model validation. Given that most model development studies utilised an SGA-based outcome (birthweight < 10 th centile), we henceforth use "SGA" to denote a growth restricted outcome in this study. Full details on the search strategies and screening criteria are illustrated in Additional file 1: Table S1.

Stage one identified existing systematic reviews, meta-analyses, or external validation studies for each outcome. Titles and abstracts were screened first, followed by full-text review of potentially eligible articles. Where reviews or validation studies reported potentially relevant prediction models, the original model development studies were retrieved and assessed to confirm eligibility, reproducibility, and applicability. In stage two, we conducted updated outcome-specific searches from the search end date of the most recent relevant review for each outcome to 1 June 2025. Titles and abstracts from the updated searches were screened before full texts were assessed for eligibility.

The original development studies were examined to ensure strict model reproducibility and applicability. Models were eligible for inclusion if they predicted a specific binary outcome, or a continuous outcome that could be dichotomised into the outcome of interest, e.g., as SGA is defined based on a fetal weight centile threshold, models predicting birthweight were also eligible for inclusion. In cases where a model was developed based on routinely collected maternal characteristics (prior-risk model), which was then updated with other biomarker or genetic data, the version of prior-risk model was included. Models were excluded if they used a survival or competing-risks framework, because the outcome was defined as time-to-event (which can be unreliable in routine maternity data where care is delivered across care sites) rather than the occurrence of the complication itself. In addition, models including fewer than three predictors were excluded to distinguish comprehensive risk algorithms from simple association or biomarker-screening tests. Models were also excluded if their individual predictors could not be replicated within our validation database or required predictors that are not routinely collected data such as biomarkers, ultrasound measures, or genetic information. Furthermore, models were excluded if they did not report sufficient details for full reproduction of the prediction equation, such as the intercept and/or predictor coefficients for each predictor in a logistic regression model. Models that reported all predictor coefficients but omitted the intercept were excluded from the primary analysis (as calibration cannot be evaluated without absolute risk predictions) and were instead retained for a sensitivity analysis evaluating discrimination alone.

Data Source and Study cohort

This study utilised the Clinical Practice Research Datalink Aurum (CPRD Aurum, June 2021 release) and its linked datasets, including the Pregnancy Register and Hospital Episode Statistics Admitted Patient Care (HES-APC) [24].

CPRD Aurum is a representative database of anonymised patient electronic health records from primary care settings in the UK, which includes comprehensive longitudinal records, covering 1,373 contributing primary care practices and approximately 20% of the population in the UK [25]. The study cohort included women aged 14 to 49 who were pregnant between 1 January 2000 and 31 December 2019. Follow-up for each woman began at the latest of the study start, or the patient registration date. Follow-up ended at the earliest of study end date, or date of deregistration (where available), or date of death (if applicable). Only singleton pregnancies occurring entirely within the follow-up period were eligible for inclusion. To ensure clinical plausibility and outcome relevance, pregnancies were included in the validation cohort only if their gestational length was 20-42 weeks. Separate validation datasets were constructed for each outcome due to the variation in definitions among outcomes and predictors. Additional file 2: Figure S1 shows the process of constructing the cohort for primary analysis.

Definition of outcomes and predictors

Outcomes for GDM, PE, and stillbirth, as well as predictors were defined using medical codes from CPRD Aurum and ICD-10 codes from HES-APC. Relevant medical codes were identified using keyword searches in the CPRD Code Browser and reviewed for comprehensiveness. For HES-APC, ICD-10 codes were selected from the relevant diagnostic categories of the ICD-10 dictionary, retaining only the first five characters to align with HES-APC coding conventions and ensure consistency. In addition, published code lists, including SNOMED CT and Read codes, were reviewed and mapped to relevant formats where appropriate, before our final review. For outcomes of SGA, birthweight data were extracted from HES-APC and then were standardised into Z-scores using the World Health Organization (WHO) calculation algorithm [26, 27]. These Z-scores were subsequently mapped to percentiles based on the standard normal distribution. The binary outcome was then defined by applying the specific percentile threshold as defined in each original development study.

To account for potential delays in the recording of events across different care settings, outcome time windows were uniformly extended by two weeks. For example, stillbirth, originally defined as occurring from 24 weeks in gestation until the end of pregnancy, was redefined to include any record from 24 weeks in gestation up to two weeks after the documented pregnancy end date. In terms of predictors, due to the inherent limitations of retrospective data, information on maternal characteristics and health conditions needed to be retrieved from historical patients records. This included physical measures (e.g., height, weight, and blood pressure), medical history, and chronic conditions (e.g., chronic hypertension, diabetes, autoimmune diseases). Therefore, the observation window for these predictors was extended up to five years before pregnancy start for long-term conditions, or up to one year prior for more recent clinical indicators, depending on the nature of the predictor. If a patient had multiple medical records during the time window, the latest was used. To ensure clinical plausibility and minimise bias from data entry errors, values were treated as missing if they fell outside the following ranges: maternal weight (40 -200 kg), height (100 -200 cm), BMI (10 -100 kg /m²), systolic blood pressure (70 -250 mmHg), diastolic blood pressure (40 -150 mmHg). See Availability of Data and Materials section for code lists and definitions of outcomes and predictors.

Required Sample Size Calculation

Sample size requirements were calculated following the recommendations of Riley et al. [28]. Specifically, sample sizes were determined to achieve prespecified precision across three criteria: the observed-to-expected (O/E) ratio (target 95% confidence interval [CI] width = 0.2), calibration slope (target 95% CI width = 0.2), and C-statistic (target 95% CI width = 0.1), taking the largest requirement across evaluable criteria [28]. For each eligible model, we used the outcome prevalence in our validation cohort and the reported C-statistic from the development study, assuming normally distributed linear predictors with common variance in event and non-event groups (Additional file 1: Table S2). Using the same approach, we also calculated the required sample size for each existing external validation study based on its own cohort outcome prevalence (evaluating the largest cohort in multi-cohort studies) to assess whether its sample size was sufficient.

Missing data and multiple imputation

To address missingness in predictors (height, weight, systolic and diastolic blood pressure) we performed multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE), generating 20 imputed datasets, separately for each outcome of interest [29]. Missing body mass index (BMI) and mean arterial pressure (MAP) were derived from imputed weight, height, and systolic/diastolic blood pressure values. Missing ethnicity was retained as an "Unknown" category. For the imputation process of each outcome-specific validation dataset, all predictors from the models associated with that outcome, along with their outcomes, were included. As all predictors subject to imputation were continuous, linear regression imputation with added normally distributed residuals was employed. For each imputed predictor, values were constrained within clinically and statistically plausible ranges as observed in the original data, and convergence was assessed using diagnostic plots to ensure stability and consistency of the imputed distributions.

Validation of existing models

The models selected for validation included linear regression, logistic regression, and Poisson regression models. To validate each model, the linear predictor (LP, i.e., the sum of each covariate multiplied by the corresponding model coefficient as reported in the original development study of each CPM) was calculated for every pregnancy episode using the published model equation. For logistic models, the predicted risk probability of the outcome was then obtained by applying the inverse logit transformation [30]. The predicted probability of Poisson models was calculated by applying an exponential transformation to the linear predictor [31].

Calibration was assessed using three measures: calibration slope, calibration-in-the-large (CITL), and calibration plots.

The calibration slope assesses the overall calibration by fitting a logistic regression model (for binary outcome) or linear regression model (for continuous outcome) of the outcomes on the linear predictor, where a slope of one indicates perfect calibration. CITL evaluated whether the mean predicted risk across all individuals matched the overall observed event rate in the population, with an ideal value of zero indicating perfect agreement on average. A calibration plot visually assessed model calibration by plotting observed event frequencies against predicted risk probabilities. Perfect calibration occurred when the plotted curve aligns with the 45-degree diagonal line.

Discrimination was assessed using the C-statistic (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve, AUC), where a value of one denotes perfect discrimination [16, 30].

All performance metrics (calibration slope, CITL, and C-statistic) were estimated for each imputation cohort and pooled across the 20 datasets using Rubin's rules along with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). For calibration plots, a single pooled plot was generated for each model by first averaging the LP across all imputed datasets and then transforming the pooled LP into predicted probabilities.

Sensitivity Analysis

As CPRD pregnancy episodes are algorithm derived, some start and end dates of pregnancy episodes for individuals may overlap. To mitigate potential bias, a validation sub-cohort retaining only the first chronological pregnancy for overlapping episodes was used to evaluate performances using the same discrimination and calibration metrics. A complete-case sensitivity analysis was also conducted to assess the robustness of the external validation regarding the approach to missing data. For this, the validation cohort was restricted to pregnancy episodes with complete records for both outcomes and predictors to re-estimate discrimination and calibration metrics. In addition, for models reporting complete predictors and coefficients but lacking the intercept, we further assessed discrimination (C-statistic) alone using the linear predictor excluding the intercept. By applying the same validation cohorts, 20 imputed datasets, and Rubin's rules as in the primary analysis, pooled C-statistics and 95% CIs were estimated for each model.

Fairness

Assessment of fairness was not a primary objective of this study. The primary aim was to externally validate previously developed prediction models. The original model development studies were conducted in populations with substantially different demographic and ethnic compositions. Therefore, fairness-related analyses (e.g., evaluation of model performance across ethnic subgroups) were not applicable within the scope of this work.

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting, or dissemination plans of this research.

Results

Eligible Models for External Validation

Stage one of the four two-stage systematic reviews identified a total of 664 articles from systematic reviews, metaanalyses, or external validation studies across the four specified outcomes, following full text screening of 88 eligible studies and subsequent review of original development studies, 43 clinical prediction models (CPMs) were included meeting the criteria for external validation. Within this first stage, the literature search dates for the most recent systematic reviews concluded on 06 June 2024 for GDM, PE, and SGA [7], and on 18 October 2022 for stillbirth [32].

The second stage, which focused on models published after the most recent systematic review for each outcome yielded an additional 1,269 articles. From these, 172 articles were eligible for full text screening, resulting in five additional models from three articles meeting the inclusion criteria (Figure 1). In total, 48 eligible models from 30 original development studies were included (47 binary-outcome models and one continuous-outcome model). For a comprehensive list of all models that were reviewed in full text but subsequently excluded, along with exclusion reasons, see Additional file 3: Table S11.

Table 1 summarises the characteristics of the 48 included models by outcome. Outcome definitions varied across models. For stillbirth, one model defined the outcome as occurring at ≥24 weeks of gestation, whereas the other used ≥32 weeks. Among the 35 PE models, 12 predicted any PE regardless of onset, eight each predicted early-onset PE (EOPE, <34 gestational weeks) and late-onset PE (LOPE, ≥34 gestational weeks), four predicted preterm PE (<37 gestational weeks), one predicted term PE (≥37 gestational weeks), one predicted intermediate PE (34-37 gestational weeks), and one predicted late PE (≥38 gestational weeks). For SGA, three models predicted binary SGA and one linear regression model predicted log-transformed birthweight. Across all 48 models, 23 were developed using cohorts that included a UK population. The number of predictors per model ranged from 3 to 17 (median 6). Substantial variation was observed in original development sample sizes, ranging from 151 to 113,415 (median 5,013), as well as in outcome prevalence across the development cohorts (GDM: 2.41%-55.35%; Stillbirth: 0.35%-0.58%; Any PE: 1.78%-66.89%; EOPE: 0.50%-11.97%; LOPE: 1.53%-16.53%; Preterm PE: 1.65%-20.00%; Term PE: 4.64%; Intermediate PE: 0.76%; Late PE: 2.22%; SGA: 9.50%-19.53%). Details of the model developed with the largest sample size for each outcome are presented in Table 2, with full specifications of all 48 models provided in Additional file 1: Table S2.

Regarding previous external validations of the 48 included models, 29 models (28 binary-outcome models and one continuous-outcome model) had been externally validated across 13 published studies, including 21 models validated in cohorts containing a UK population (Additional file 1: Table S3). Cohort sample sizes across these validation studies ranged from 230 to 406,286. All existing validation studies of binary-outcome models reported discrimination (C-statistics), whereas three model validations (across three studies) did not report calibration metrics.

Sample Size Calculation

For our external validation in the CPRD cohort, the minimum required sample size could be calculated for 42 of the 48 included models (excluding five PE models that did not report a C-statistic in their original development studies and one continuous birthweight model). Based on the outcome prevalence in our validation cohort, the estimated minimum sample size required for our external validation ranged from 5,905 pregnancies (795 events) for SGA to 307,424 pregnancies (1,138 events) for stillbirth ≥32 weeks (with required events ranging from 369 to 2,711 across all evaluable models; Additional file 1: Table S2). All required sample sizes and event counts were substantially smaller than our available CPRD validation cohorts. When evaluated against the sample size requirements calculated for each existing validation cohort, only one model (Yerlikaya 2016 for stillbirth ≥24 weeks [33]) had previously been validated in a cohort including a UK population that satisfied both the required sample size and number of events (Additional file 1: Table S3).

Study Cohort Characteristics

In the primary analysis, the validation dataset for GDM, PE, and stillbirth included 1,063,974 women with 1,581,161 unique pregnancy episodes. Routine maternal characteristics such as maternal age, parity, ethnicity, and body mass index (BMI) were available, although some predictors had notable levels of missingness (weight, height, systolic and diastolic blood pressure, see Table 3 and Additional file 1: Table S4), consistent with the nature of routinely collected clinical data. The mean maternal age was 29.86 years, and 43.96% of pregnancies were nulliparous. In terms of ethnicity, 79.55% of the cohort were White, followed by Asian (10.21%) and Black (4.85%) women. Filtering to observations with a recorded birthweight to validate models predicting SGA reduced the validation cohort to 957,496 pregnancies, resulting in minor changes in the prevalence of predictors (Table 3). The prevalence of the outcomes of interest, including GDM, SGA, PE, and stillbirth, was consistent with national statistics [2] [3] [4], ensuring the representativeness and external validity of the study cohort. External Validation of Models

The performance metrics of all models displayed significant heterogeneity, with a summary of distribution for each outcome shown in Figure 2. Detailed performance metrics and calibration plots for individual models are presented in Additional file 1: Table S5 and Additional file 2: Figure S2. The validation results for the model possessing the largest development dataset within each outcome are presented in Table 2.

Compared with models for the other outcomes, models for gestational diabetes (GDM) demonstrated higher discriminative ability and less extreme calibration instability. Across all GDM models, C-statistics ranged from 0.57 to 0.77, calibration-in-the-large (CITL) from -1.88 to 0.20, and calibration slopes from 0.48 to 1.26. Six of the seven models achieved C-statistics above 0.70 (0.71 -0.77), with only one model [34] tailored to high-risk parous women exhibiting a lower C-statistic (0.57). Most models yielded negative CITL (-1.88 to -0.44), indicating a consistent tendency to systematically overestimate baseline risk in the UK population. Regarding calibration slopes, four models showed slight underfitting (1.04 to 1.26), whereas the remaining three models suffered from overfitting with slopes (0.48 to 0.69).

Across all 35 pre-eclampsia (PE) models, C-statistics ranged from 0.50 to 0.72, CITL from -6.14 to 0.92, and calibration slopes from 0.19 to 17.78. Discrimination varied considerably across outcome definitions, ranging from 0.55 to 0.72 for any PE, 0.50 to 0.69 for early-onset PE (EOPE), 0.54 to 0.71 for late-onset PE (LOPE), and 0.62 to 0.68 for preterm, term, intermediate, and late PE. Most models (29 of 35) yielded negative CITL (-6.14 to -0.01), indicating a consistent tendency to overestimate baseline risk, including five models with severe overestimation beyond -5.00 (-6.14 to -5.14) and 24 models ranging from -4.10 to -0.01. Regarding calibration slopes, the majority of models (26 of 35) suffered from overfitting with slopes below 1.0 (0.19 to 0.92), whereas four models showed calibration slopes from 1.04 to 2.94, and five models showing extreme underfitting with slopes above 5.00 (5.54 to 17.78).

The two stillbirth models showed relatively poor discrimination in this validation cohort, with C-statistics of 0.58 and 0.54. In terms of calibration, both models yielded positive CITL (0.49 and 0.89), indicating systematic underestimation of risk. Regarding calibration slopes, both models suffered from overfitting with slopes below 1.0 (0.42 and 0.87).

Calibration plots showed little separation of risks and no clinically useful stratification (Additional file 2: Figure S2).

Across the three binary outcome models for SGA, C-statistics ranged from 0.58 to 0.67. CITL varied widely from -0.41 to 9.64, reflecting slight baseline risk overestimation in two models (-0.41 and -0.16) but severe underestimation in one model (9.64). Regarding calibration slopes, all three binary models suffered from overfitting with slopes below 1.0 (0.69 to 0.73). In addition, the continuous linear regression model [35] predicting birthweight had a calibration slope close to ideal (0.94) and a CITL of 104.23 g.

Sensitivity analysis

Excluding overlapping pregnancies reduced the cohorts to 1,527,385 (GDM, stillbirth and PE) and 936,994 (SGA), while complete-case analysis further restricted the sample sizes to 793,031 (GDM), 792,401 (PE), 1,178,044 (stillbirth) and 502,967 (SGA). Both sensitivity analyses showed minimal changes in characteristic distributions and yielded validation results consistent with the primary analysis (Additional file 1: Tables S6-S9; Additional file 2: Figure S3). For models without reported intercepts, 12 models from seven studies were evaluated for discrimination alone (eight for GDM and four for PE). Validated C-statistics ranged from 0.67 to 0.77 for GDM, and from 0.69 to 0.73 for PE (any PE: 0.69; early-onset PE: 0.73; intermediate PE: 0.71; late PE: 0.69). Discussion

Main findings and Interpretation

This study represents a comprehensive external validation of clinical prediction models (CPMs) for adverse pregnancy outcomes using a large, nationally representative UK linked primary and hospital care dataset. Most existing models, when applied to the UK population data, demonstrated suboptimal generalisability. Specifically, most models showed suboptimal discrimination and/or calibration. For low prevalence outcomes, such as stillbirth, only a limited number of models were available, and their performance was consistently poor. The observed decline in performance is likely attributable to several factors. Firstly, significant heterogeneity exists between our validation cohort and the original model development populations. Many of the validated models were developed in small, hospital-based, or prospectively recruited cohorts with different demographic features, outcome prevalences, and ethnic compositions.

Secondly, some models used a limited number of predictors, with as few as three predictors. This may be insufficient to capture all useful information leading to adverse outcomes, thus reducing predictive power when applied to a new, broader population.

Strengths and Limitations

This study used a large, nationally representative electronic health record dataset of the UK population, providing a realistic sample and ample statistical power for certainty in our performance estimates. This enhances the generalisability of the findings to UK clinical settings and supports their relevance (or lack of for some models) for clinical application. The systematic approach to identifying relevant models and the comprehensive sensitivity analysis conducted to explore potential reasons for performance degradation add to the overall robustness of the study. However, some limitations inherent to the use of retrospective, routinely collected data are evident. The inaccuracies in clinical coding and misclassification bias cannot be entirely ruled out. Although missing data was addressed with multiple imputation, high levels of missingness for certain predictors (such as height and weight) remain a concern, and imputation cannot fully compensate for the information lost due to missingness. The results from sensitivity analyses were, however, largely consistent with the primary analyses, suggesting the strategies for handling missing observations and algorithm derived overlapping pregnancies did not introduce significant bias.

Finally, whilst our findings are highly relevant to the UK population, their generalisability to populations in other countries may be limited.

Implications for Clinical Practice and Future Research

This extensive external validation work has several implications for clinical prediction models if they are to be used to improve antenatal risk stratification. Many existing models were identified with several having acceptable discrimination in our validation, indicating that routinely collected, early maternal information contains meaningful predictive signal associated with developing complications or poor outcomes. This aligns with previous evidence showing that the routinely collected primary care data has strong association with pregnancy complications [20], and models based solely on clinical characteristics can achieve performance comparable to those incorporating biomarkers or ultrasound [11]. However, despite their discriminative potential, most models showed limited generalisability, failing to achieve adequate discrimination and calibration simultaneously, with pronounced overestimation of risk. Similar limitations have been reported across existing validation using international cohorts [11, 12, 19, 36], suggesting overfitting in original development studies and highlighting that most models cannot be directly applied to a new, larger population without adjustment. Applying such poorly performing tools in the preconception or early pregnancy window could lead to unnecessary investigation and intervention for low-risk pregnancies and misunderstanding of the true at-risk group.

Model performance inherently varies across different time periods, geographical locations, and demographic structures. When model performance is optimal and implemented in practice, monitoring of model performance is required to detect any future shifts in these factors, ensuring models remain clinically relevant and reliable in evolving healthcare settings [37]. Given the suboptimal generalisability of models within the UK population, our future work will explore the potential for performance improvement by prioritising re-calibration or where necessary, model updating, to prevent research waste. The development of entirely new models should only be considered if updating yields insufficient improvements or if substantial structural modifications are required [38]. Furthermore, many pregnancy complications and adverse outcomes are complex and interrelated. For example, both gestational diabetes and hypertensive disorders of pregnancy can increase the risk of FGR, and FGR is strongly associated with an elevated risk of stillbirth [39]. Therefore, methods to predict the risk of individuals developing multiple outcomes by capturing these associations may enhance the predictive abilities and provide a more holistic assessment of pre-or early pregnancy risks. Importantly, studies should also examine whether these models retain predictive accuracy across diverse populations, healthcare settings, and time periods, whilst also assessing the accuracy of key outcome components and identifying potential inconsistencies among predictors [40]. Such work will be essential to ensure that prediction models are both clinically applicable and reliable for improving maternal and perinatal outcomes.

Conclusions

In this large-scale external validation of clinical prediction models for adverse pregnancy outcomes using preconception or early pregnancy routine health data, most existing models demonstrated suboptimal discrimination and/or calibration, particularly for rare outcomes such as stillbirth. These findings indicate that currently available models lack generalisability to real-world clinical practice in the UK and should not be implemented without consideration of model re-calibration, or model updating, before consideration of re-development.
