How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). Abstract

Public health organizations can benefit greatly from infectious disease epidemic forecasting to guide and support interventions and policies that reduce morbidity. However, accurate and reliable epidemic forecasting remains notoriously challenging and is an evolving field in which new scientific advances continue to support forecasting efforts. Computational models that underpin artificial intelligence (AI) models are promising developments that may improve seasonal epidemic forecasting. This study aims to compare the performance of a selection of AI models previously identified as well adapted to epidemic forecasting, including machine learning (XGBoost), deep learning models (Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM), Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU), Transformer), time-series foundation model (Chronos), and a hybrid model: physics-informed neural networks (PINNs), against statistical models (Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA), Prophet) and a compartmental epidemiological model (CEM) in seasonal epidemic forecasting across Canada.

The study uses weekly positivity-rate incidence of seasonal influenza A across eight Canadian provinces during the 2025/2026 season as a test case. Each model generated short-term (up to 5 weeks ahead) forecasts independently for each province from October 11, 2025, to March 28, 2026. We evaluated performance using mean absolute error (MAE) for each province.

Our results show that AI models, specifically GRU, Transformer, and Chronos models, achieve lower MAE than CEM in four of eight provinces (Alberta, British Columbia, Prince Edward Island, and Saskatchewan). Meanwhile, CEM and ARIMA outperform all AI models in three provinces (Ontario, Quebec, Manitoba) and one province (Nova Scotia), respectively. Furthermore, data pooling strategies that leverage multi-province data for the target virus or multi-virus data within individual provinces greatly improve deep learning models, and Chronos performs well under zero-shot forecasting in British Columbia. In contrast, Prophet always has the highest MAE, and PINN is worse than CEMs. Overall, AI models performed comparably to, or slightly better than, CEMs. However, these modest gains may not offset their lack of interpretability, high computational costs, and reliance on the specialized expertise required for implementation. We therefore conclude that AI models are best as a complement, rather than a replacement, to CEMs, which remain crucial for interpretability and informing decision-making. 1.

Seasonal acute respiratory infections impose a recurrent burden on healthcare systems and contribute substantially to annual morbidity [1]. At the onset of each season, surveillance systems track the emergence of epidemic activity, signaling the early phase of an outbreak. The progression of an epidemic is typically summarized using an epidemic curve, which represents the temporal evolution of indicators or targets such as incident cases, test positivity rates, hospital admissions, or infection-associated deaths. Whether strictly seasonal or driven by other transmission dynamics, such epidemics often follow a broadly predictable trajectory. In its simplest form, this trajectory consists of an initial phase of approximately exponential growth in incidence, followed by a peak as the pool of susceptible individuals becomes depleted, and subsequently a decline toward near-zero incidence. Despite this typical shape, it is usually challenging to predict the epidemic trajectory accurately. Therefore, the development of epidemic forecasting models is an active research area aiming to robustly forecast epidemic trajectories with satisfactory accuracy and confidence. Forecasting models are commonly designed to generate predictions ranging from a few weeks to several months ahead (depending on the disease and/or the goal of the forecasts), as well as to estimate peak magnitude and peak timing. As shown during multiple severe outbreaks, accurate forecasting models are essential for informing public health policy and operational planning [2]. Indeed, advance knowledge of epidemic trends enables policymakers to implement timely interventions, including situational awareness, vaccination campaign planning, expansion of healthcare capacity, and optimized resource allocation when peak timing and magnitude can be anticipated. In the absence of reliable forecasting models, decision-making and interventions can be suboptimal, leading to substantial human and financial costs.

Several methodological approaches have been proposed for epidemic forecasting, among which compartmental epidemiological models (CEMs) provide a mechanistic framework for describing disease transmission dynamics. CEMs are the most popular formulation, partitioning the population into relevant epidemiological states. The progression of the epidemic occurs through transitions of individuals between those epidemiological states, for example using systems of differential equations (the canonical Susceptible-Infectious-Recovered model [3]) or computational rules (agent based models). Machine learning algorithms underpinning "artificial intelligence" (AI) have had enormous interest from the scientific community over the past few years. Indeed, they have rapidly achieved capabilities that open new possibilities in terms of support and implementation of scientific projects across various fields. 2.1 Forecasting model

An epidemic curve can be represented as a time series, where observed incidence is periodically reported (e.g., daily, weekly, monthly, etc.). We denote the observed incidence sequence by {y 1 , y 2 , . . . , y T }. The forecasting task consists of predicting the next H future observed incidences, namely {y T +1 , . . . , y T +H }, where H and T represent the forecast horizon and total number of observations, respectively. Formally, the forecasting task can be expressed as fitting, or "learning", a function f such that {y T +1 , . . . , y T +H } = f ({y 1 , . . . , . . . , y T }), where f is called a forecasting model.

Forecasts for a given horizon may be either deterministic or probabilistic. Deterministic forecasts produce a single predicted value and are commonly referred to as point forecasts. In contrast, probabilistic forecasts provide a predictive distribution or a set of quantiles, thereby characterizing uncertainty in future outcomes (Figure 1). Ideally, forecasts should be accurate, stable, and, in the probabilistic setting, accompanied by well-calibrated and sufficiently narrow uncertainty intervals.

In this paper, we consider a diverse set of forecasting models. Specifically, we consider compartmental mechanistic models (CEMs), statistical models, machine learning (ML) models, deep learning (DL) models, physics-informed neural networks (PINNs), and time-series foundation models (TSFMs). Furthermore, in order to compare all evaluated models, our primary focus is centered strictly on point forecasts as some models cannot natively generate probabilistic forecasts.

Before describing the individual models, we first explain how we transform a time series into training samples for models that require an explicit supervised-learning formulation. Standard ML and DL models are inherently static; they expect tabular data with distinct feature vectors (X) and target vectors (y) and cannot process raw, continuous sequence data. To bridge this gap, we use lag embedding (the sliding-window approach). This approach converts a single long time series into training supervised samples (input-target pairs), where the feature vector consists of a window of p past observations and the prediction target is the next h future observations. By framing temporal dependencies as static tabular features, lag embedding lets us cast sequence forecasting as a standard regression task and leverage powerful ML and DL algorithms.

2.2 Lag embedding

Lag embedding, also called training sample creation, transforms a univariate time series into a supervised learning problem by using a sliding window of past observations as predictors and subsequent observations as targets. For a given time series, a specified number of past observations form each input sample, while the following observation(s) form the corresponding forecasting target. Moving the window through the time series generates a sequence of inputtarget pairs for model training.

This representation enables ML and DL models to learn temporal dependencies and patterns from historical observations. The resulting samples are subsequently used to train the forecasting models.

Recursive forecasting strategy

Recursive forecasting produces multi-step-ahead forecasts sequentially, one step at a time. The model predicts the next observation based on the latest available observations. The model then re-injects the predicted value into the input sequence and uses it, along with the remaining recent observations, to generate the next prediction. This is repeated recursively until the desired forecasting horizon is reached. Thus, predictions from previous steps are used as input for subsequent forecasting steps.

Consider a univariate time series with 10 observations: y = [y 1 , y 2 , y 3 , y 4 , y 5 , y 6 , y 7 , y 8 , y 9 , y 10 ].

We construct supervised learning data using a lag order (window size or context length) p = 3, where each input x i consists of the previous three values of the series, and the target y i is the next value. Thus, the time series is transformed into a supervised training dataset: 1 y 1 y 2 y 3 y 2 y 3 y 4 y 3 y 4 y 5 y 4 y 5 y 6 y 5 y 6 y 7 y 6 y 7 y 8 y 7 y 8 y 9

For models requiring lag embedding, the number of training samples generated from the original time series is T -p. In the example above, with T = 10 and p = 3, this yields 10 -3 = 7 training samples. This formulation provides a unified framework for predicting future incidence values from historical epidemic data.

Some forecasting strategies instead train a separate model for each prediction horizon; this approach is known as direct forecasting. In contrast, multi-output forecasting uses a single model to generate predictions for multiple future horizons simultaneously. In this study, we consider p ∈ {2, 4, 8, 16} for models requiring sample creation through lag embedding. AI and statistical models use a recursive strategy and generate one-step-ahead forecasts.

2.4 Compartmental mechanistic models

Compartmental epidemiological models (CEMs) are the standard mechanistic models that are widely used for infectious disease modelling, and have their origin in the 1920s with the work of Kermack and McKendrick [6]. The fundamental features of these models are they i) compartment individuals of the population into epidemiological states, and ii) describe mathematically the migration dynamics of individuals between the compartments as the infections spread in the population. The temporal evolution of compartment sizes is typically modelled using systems of ordinary differential equations (ODEs) that describe transition rates between compartments, or equivalently, renewal equations [7]. One of the simplest compartmental models to simulate epidemics is the Susceptible-Infectious-Recovered ("SIR") model. Individuals who have never been infected (i.e., still susceptible to infections) are grouped in the "susceptible" compartment, and the size of this compartment is noted S. Infectious individuals, their number is noted I, are grouped in the "infectious" compartment. Finally, the "recovered" compartment (R) is filled with individuals who are no longer infectious and immune for life. The CEMs can be designed to represent very complex epidemiological processes. The practical limit to their complexity and size being the available data to fit the model parameters to and computational power (most CEMs are not analytically tractable and require numerical solvers).

CEMs are scientifically grounded as they mathematically represent biological and epidemiological processes underlying disease transmission and natural history, behaviors, and interventions. These models are inherently interpretable and allow the estimation of key epidemiological parameters (e.g., basic reproduction number R 0 ). They can also be used to assess the potential impact of public health interventions or behavioral changes on epidemic dynamics.

CEMs are often used to forecast infectious disease epidemics because they provide an attractive balance between modeling tractability and representativeness of the real world. Forecasting is conceptually straightforward: once the model is fitted to observations, epidemic trajectories are simulated forward in time (beyond the last observation date) up to a predefined time horizon, constituting the forecasted epidemic.

The CEM used in this study is a simple SIR model implemented with a renewal equation, presented in Subsection 3.3.

2.5 Statistical models

Many statistical models [8, 9] have been developed for time-series forecasting, and reviewing them is beyond the scope of this study. Statistical models are data-driven models that learn patterns and relationships from historical data to predict future outcomes. These models are often used for forecasting because they can capture complex temporal dependencies and trends without requiring explicit mechanistic assumptions about the underlying processes. Researchers have applied statistical models to forecast various targets in infectious disease epidemics [10, 11, 12, 13, 14].

Many statistical models have been developed for time series forecasting, including autoregressive integrated moving average models (ARIMA), seasonal autoregressive integrated moving average (SARIMA), exponential smoothing (Holt-Winters), and Prophet. In this study, we selected ARIMA [15] and Prophet [16] as representative statistical forecasting models. ARIMA has been shown to be effective for short-term forecasting when temporal patterns are approximately linear and stationary after differencing. Its interpretability and relatively low computational complexity have contributed to its extensive use in epidemiological forecasting and disease surveillance.

Many studies have proved the effectiveness of ARIMA in infectious disease forecasting.

ARIMA has been used to forecast the spread of COVID-19 in Italy [17], estimate the number of confirmed and recovered COVID-19 cases [18], and predict trends in COVID-19 prevalence in Italy, France, and Spain [19]. ARIMA has also been applied to forecast influenza in China beyond COVID-19 [20]. The authors found that ARIMA performed well, especially for shortterm epidemic forecasting in modeling temporal dynamics in public health data.

The second statistical forecasting model selected for our study Prophet [16], as previous studies report competitive forecasting accuracy, especially for time series with long training histories [21]. It has also been used in epidemiological forecasting, e.g., for influenza prediction [20]. Its inclusion therefore provides a complementary statistical approach to ARIMA for evaluating forecast performance across different temporal patterns and forecast horizons.

More broadly, the distinction between traditional statistical forecasting models and CEMs is not always definitive in terms of predictive performance. Previous studies have shown that these approaches may outperform one another depending on the forecasting scenario and disease dynamics [22]. For example, collaborative forecasting studies on influenza-like illness demonstrated that statistical forecasting models can achieve competitive accuracy in epidemic prediction tasks [23].

2.6 AI models

Given that the primary audience for this report is public health practitioners, many of whom may have limited familiarity with AI models, this section offers a concise, high-level description of how they work.

AI models is a loose, umbrella term that generally refers to any computational model used to perform tasks associated with artificial intelligence, such as prediction, classification, generation, or decision-making. Here, leaning on recent literature, we focus on modern models relevant to forecasting infectious disease epidemics.

In this paper, we focus on two categories of AI models: machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL). Machine learning is a class of statistical and computational methods that learn patterns or relationships from data to make predictions, classifications, or decisions, without being explicitly programmed with fixed rules [24]. Learning types are typically categorized by the amount of human/expert guidance provided. In traditional machine learning, the three main types of learning are supervised, unsupervised, and reinforcement learning. Supervised learning is a form of machine learning in which a model is trained on labeled data, that is, pairs of inputs and known outputs, to learn a mapping that can predict the output for new, unseen inputs. In unsupervised learning, the model receives only unlabelled inputs and discovers patterns or structures, such as clustering similar data points, detecting anomalies, or reducing the dimensionality of complex datasets while preserving key information. In this paper, we focus on supervised learning, specifically the regression task, as our goal is to forecast continuous variables (e.g., influenza incidence) rather than predict categorical labels.

2.1 Machine learning models

Machine learning models have been widely applied to epidemic forecasting [25], with various models used for different forecasting tasks. Linear regression and its regularized versions (Ridge, LASSO, and Elastic Net), decision trees, support vector machines (SVMs), and tree-based ensemble methods [26, 27] such as random forest and extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) are commonly used in epidemic forecasting. In this paper, we use XGBoost as a representative decision-tree model, given its widespread use and strong performance in various forecasting tasks [20, 28, 29].

Decision tree regression is a non-parametric supervised learning method that models nonlinear relationships between input features and a continuous target variable. The model recursively partitions the feature space into disjoint regions by selecting split points that minimize prediction error, typically measured using Mean Squared Error (MSE). At each terminal node (leaf), the predicted output is the mean value of the training samples in that region. Figure 2 illustrates how a decision tree works. Decision tree models are easy to understand and interpret, but have high variance and tend to overfit the training data, especially in complex datasets [30]. To overcome this limitation, tree-based ensemble methods such as Random Forest and XGBoost combine multiple decision tree models to improve the predictive performance and robustness.

XGBoost

XGBoost is a widely used tree-based ensemble method for classification and regression on tabular data, known for its strong predictive performance and ability to model complex relationships in structured datasets [29]. It efficiently implements gradient-boosted decision trees (GBDT) by combining multiple weak decision trees into a strong predictive model. Given a set of training observations {(x i , y i )} m i=1 , XGBoost builds an ensemble of n decision trees and gives the prediction ŷi = n k=1 f k (x i ).

For example, Tree 1 may encode a rule such as: "if the number of cases in the previous week exceeds 100, then predict an increase in cases".

To make a forecast, the current observations, say 110 cases last week, 80 two weeks ago, and 60 three weeks ago, are passed through all the trees. Each tree contributes a small numerical value (for example, Tree 1 predicts, say, +100, Tree 2 predicts +20, Tree 3 predicts +10), and the final prediction for the cases next week is obtained by summing contributions from all the trees (e.g., 130 cases for the next week in this simplistic example). In this way, the model combines multiple simple rules derived from past epidemic behavior to produce an overall forecast.

Note that for real-world applications, more than hundreds of trees are generated, making the interpretation of the "discovered" rules by humans practically impossible (that is, outputs results are treated as a "black box").

To summarize simply, XGBoost forecasts an epidemic by learning how past case trajectories evolve, using many small decision trees that capture key patterns (like growth and acceleration).

XGBoost has been chosen on the basis of evidence from earlier forecasting studies. As shown in [31], XGBoost outperformed deep learning models, such as Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks, for tabular COVID-19 forecasting at 1 to 5 weeks ahead. Similarly, [32] found that Gradient Boosting Regression Trees (GBRTs) can outperform state-of-the-art deep neural network (DNN) models by efficiently engineering input and output structures. This means XGBoost should serve as the tree-based machine learning baseline in our study.

2.2 Deep learning models

Deep learning (DL) is a type of machine learning that uses artificial neural networks with multiple layers to learn hierarchical data representations, allowing complex relationships and patterns to be modeled. The Deep Neural Network (DNN) models [8, 12, 33, 34, 35, 36] employed in epidemic (short-term) forecasting are simple Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs), Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM), Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU), BiLSTM, BiGRU, and transformers.

Artificial Neural Networks An Artificial Neural Network (ANN) is a deep learning technique based on biological neural systems and comprises neurons connected and arranged in layers; it learns the relationship between inputs and outputs by modifying its weights and biases during training. The neurons are the fundamental units of computation in an ANN. Each one calculates a weighted sum of its inputs, adds a bias term, and then applies a nonlinear activation function. Activation functions let the network model complex relationships by introducing non-linearity; without them, the network would function as a linear one no matter how deep it is. An ANN consists of input, hidden (optional), and output layers: the input layer takes in the data, the hidden layers learn intermediate representations, and the output layer generates predictions.

Feedforward.

A feedforward neural network is a network in which information flows in one direction through fully connected layers.

Multi-layer perceptron.

A multi-layer perceptron (MLP) is a common example of a feedforward neural network with one or more hidden layers and non-linear activations.

Deep neural network.

A deep neural network (DNN) is an MLP with many hidden layers to learn progressively more abstract patterns. DNNs can learn complex nonlinear relationships and hierarchical representations from high-dimensional data. Depending on the architecture, they can also capture temporal dependencies over different time scales.

In time series forecasting, these models act as static regressors, i.e., using a fixed window of past observations to predict future values, which means they lack memory and struggle to capture long-term temporal dependencies. As a result, they are less suited to sequential data; hence, recurrent neural networks (RNNs) and their variants were introduced to incorporate memory by allowing time-dependent hidden states to accumulate information over time.

Long Short-Term Memory.

Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks [37] are a type of RNN designed to overcome the "vanishing gradient problem" (i.e., when the network forgets how to learn from earlier information, because the updates become too tiny to have any effect) and capture long-range dependencies in sequential data. They maintain two forms of memory, a long-term and a short-term, and use gating mechanisms to control what information is kept, updated, or discarded over time.

Gated Recurrent Units. Gated Recurrent Units (GRUs) [38] are a simpler version of LSTMs designed to handle the vanishing gradient problem in RNNs. They use fewer gates (mainly an update gate and a reset gate) to control memory, making them faster and simpler while still capturing long-term patterns in sequential data. Appendix A provides additional architectural details.

Transformers. The transformer architecture, introduced in [39], is a neural network architecture designed to handle sequential data efficiently that relies on "self-attention" mechanisms.

Transformers have revolutionized natural language processing and have been adapted for time series forecasting because they can model long-range dependencies and parallelize computations effectively. The core of a Transformer is self-attention, which lets the model decide how important each time step is when making a prediction. It does this by considering all the data at once, capturing the most important parts of the information, assigning weights to all inputs, and combining them into a contextual representation. An encoder processes the input sequence, and a decoder uses this information to produce the forecast, while multi-head attention captures different types of relationships in the data simultaneously, making it particularly effective for complex time series forecasting tasks where multiple factors may influence the target variable.

2.7 Time Series Foundation Models

When neural networks are used for forecasting infectious diseases, they usually require large amounts of data, can be prone to spurious correlations, and may not perform well when applied beyond the conditions found in the training data [40]. In this context, so-called Time Series Foundation Models (TSFMs) [41, 42] have emerged as a promising solution, shifting from task-specific forecasting models to unified models that can generalize across fields such as epidemiology, finance, energy, and climate. Like Large Language Models (LLMs) in natural language processing, TSFMs are pre-trained on large, diverse sets of time series data, which may reduce the need for extensive task-specific training.

TSFMs are trained on datasets that differ greatly in sequence length and temporal resolution, covering observations on a quarterly, monthly, weekly, daily, and hourly basis. The datasets can contain real-world, synthetic, or hybrid time series drawn from a variety of domains as mentioned above. Through pretraining, TSFMs learn general temporal patterns, such as trends, seasonality, and long-range dependencies, which can then be applied to downstream tasks or previously unseen datasets.

As illustrated in Figure 4, TSFMs generally operate through two stages: pretraining and adaptation. During pretraining, researchers define a model architecture and train it on diverse time series using supervised or self-supervised learning. Most TSFMs use transformer-based architectures, including encoder-only, decoder-only, and encoder-decoder models, although non-Transformer architectures. During adaptation, the pretrained model is tailored to a specific forecasting task or dataset to leverage the temporal representations learned during pretraining.

Common adaptation strategies include zero-shot inference, fine-tuning, prompt engineering, and tokenization-based [42].

We examine two adaptation methods: zero-shot and fine-tuning. With zero-shot inference, we use the pre-trained model directly on the target dataset without further training, enabling us to assess its ability to generalize to previously unseen datasets. This method is especially appropriate when historical data are scarce and offers a ready-made forecasting approach for various diseases, regions, and outcomes. In fine-tuning, the pre-trained model is modified to suit the specific forecasting task by updating its parameters with task-specific data. This finetuning process can involve updating all of the model's parameters or only some, leaving the rest unchanged. Chronos model

For this study, we choose the Chronos model [46] as an example of a TSFM because of its comprehensive documentation and accessible off-the-shelf Python implementation. This makes Chronos usable for users without specialist expertise in TSFMs. Moreover, Chronos and TimesFM [47] have shown higher accuracy than other TSFMs [44]. Chronos treats numerical time-series forecasting as a sequence prediction task, similar to language modeling. Each time series is normalized and then binned into a sequence of discrete tokens. This allows diverse time series to be represented in a unified token space. These token sequences are input to a pretrained model trained to predict future tokens based on past ones. Finally, we assess Chronos using zero-shot and fine-tuning.

2.8 Physics-informed neural networks

Physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) are neural networks that are trained not only on data, but also guided by known physical laws (like equations describing how a system behaves).

In simple terms, they learn from data while being constrained within the known rules of physics, so their predictions stay realistic (at least in the realm of what is physically possible). In the context of epidemic models, using standard SIR model, the constraints can be driven by the dynamics of the subgroup population sizes (e.g., susceptible, infectious, recovered, etc.) and initial conditions. For a more detailed discussion of PINNs and their applications to epidemic modeling and forecasting, we refer the reader to [48, 49, 50, 51, 52, 53, 54].

2.9 Literature scan on AI models to forecast epidemics

To provide context, we conducted a broad, non-systematic literature review to highlight published studies that used AI models to forecast epidemics. Several recent reviews and broader perspectives have examined the use of artificial intelligence and machine learning in infectious disease modeling, epidemic forecasting, disease surveillance, and pandemic preparedness [4, 55, 56, 57, 58, 59, 60, 61, 62]. Recent work has also specifically reviewed the application of AI to influenza forecasting [40].

We focus on respiratory diseases in this review because our study uses influenza A as a working example. We do not review the studies using "traditional" statistical and compartmental mechanistic models, as they are too numerous and such a review would be beyond the scope of this study.

ML models can identify relationships between input variables and desired outcomes and have become increasingly used in epidemic forecasting. For instance, in [20], XGBoost, seasonal autoregressive integrated moving average (SARIMA), Prophet, and Holt-Winters were compared for forecasting monthly influenza cases in Fuzhou. XGBoost outperformed the statistical models in both fitting and forecasting, and Prophet also showed competitive predictive performance. Although the Holt-Winters additive model fitted the data less well than SARIMA, its forecasts were more accurate. Similarly, [63] found that a support vector regressor (SVR) performed better than ARIMA in forecasting cumulative COVID-19 cases six days ahead for 10 Brazilian states. These studies show that ML models can provide competitive epidemic forecasts by relying on data-driven relationships between predictors and outcomes.

DL models have likewise shown powerful predictive abilities by learning the complex relationships between input variables and the target outcomes. After having been trained, they are able to use observed data to predict future values, which has made them ever more useful for forecasting epidemics. A number of DNN architectures have been used in short-term epidemic forecasting, such as RNNs, LSTM, GRUs, bidirectional LSTMs (BiLSTMs), bidirectional GRUs (BiGRUs), and Transformer models [8, 12, 33, 34, 35, 36].

LSTM models have been extensively studied for epidemic forecasting because they can model temporal dependencies. They have been used for forecasting the transmission of COVID-19 in Canada [64] as well as for short-term influenza forecasting [65]. Recently, [66] showed that LSTM-based models are effective for short-term influenza forecasting and mentioned the importance of including meteorological variables and national public holidays in the predictive framework.

Researchers have also investigated the use of transformer-based models for forecasting epidemics, since their attention mechanism can model complex temporal relationships and dependencies over time. For instance, Transformer models have been applied to predict influenza prevalence [67]. In a comparative study involving short-term forecasting of Influenza A, [68] found that the Transformer model performed better than LSTM, GRU, BiLSTM, and ARIMA.

Likewise, [69] compared various models for short-term Influenza A forecasting and found that deep learning models outperformed traditional statistical models such as ARIMA and exponential smoothing (ETS), with the Transformer model achieving the best results. Further comparative studies have also examined LSTM, GRU, and BiLSTM models against ARIMA, and in those studies LSTM showed competitive predictive performance [70].

More recently, TSFMs are presented as a promising model family, leveraging massive pretraining on diverse sequential data to achieve zero-shot forecasting. Recent assessments of TSFM architectures have revealed various operational trade-offs [21]. Models like TabPFN [71], Chronos, TimesFM, and TimeGPT [72] tend to perform well not only for short-and longterm point forecasting, but also for peak-period forecasting, where TimesFM performs well.

Notably, [44] showed that Chronos and TimesFM deliver superior overall accuracy compared to other TSFMs, directly motivating our interest in evaluating Chronos's performance dynamics for public health forecasting.

The strong performance of deep learning models also raises the question of whether complex DL architectures are always necessary for time series forecasting. Ensemble learning methods, particularly tree-based boosting models, provide an alternative data-driven approach. For example, Gradient Boosting Regression Trees (GBRTs) can compete with, and in some cases outperform, state-of-the-art DNN models through careful engineering of the input and output structures [32]. These findings suggest that effective feature engineering combined with ensemble learning can provide competitive forecasting performance without the complexity of deep neural networks.

Recently, researchers have explored physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) as an extension of DNN models. PINNs use epidemiological knowledge or governing equations into the learning process, for instance, an SIR model, so their predictions are guided by known disease dynamics rather than relying solely on data. According to [52], PINNs outperformed conventional DNN models for epidemic forecasting, highlighting the advantage of combining data-driven learning with domain knowledge.

[61] reviews and compares traditional statistical and deep learning methods for epidemic forecasting.

This literature scan suggests that AI models, including ML models, DL models, and PINNs, can have competitive forecasting accuracy. For these reasons, we include all these models.

First, performance varies. First, performance varies by disease, forecasting horizon, and region.

Second, complex models do not always increase forecasting accuracy; for instance, XGBoost can perform similarly to more complex deep neural network architectures. Finally, PINNs are hybrid methods that combine DNNs and CEMs; they sometimes outperform DNNs.

2.10 Limitations of AI models

Although AI and machine learning models have been successfully applied to infectious disease forecasting, significant limitations continue to constrain their practical implementation.

First, interpretability remains a major challenge. These models can produce accurate forecasts of disease dynamics but often provide limited insight into the mechanisms underlying their predictions, as they lack explicit mechanisms to depict how disease progression actually occurs. This lack of transparency matters in epidemiological applications, where forecasts must be interpretable and actionable for policymakers and epidemiologists. Moreover, purely datadriven models are not inherently grounded in epidemiological principles and therefore cannot directly provide scientifically meaningful quantities, such as the transmission rate or the basic reproduction number.

Second, their predictive performance and robustness depend heavily on data quality and availability. This drawback is relevant for disease data, which are often sparse, under-reported, delayed, noisy, and over-dispersed. Consequently, their performance may deteriorate, as is often the case for emerging or localized outbreaks. AI models can learn spurious correlations rather than meaningful relationships, which may limit their ability to generalize to new outbreaks.

Finally, practical implementation presents significant operational hurdles. Training and deploying these traditional AI models can be computationally expensive, requiring extensive hyperparameter tuning across high-dimensional parameter spaces. Furthermore, successful implementation demands specialized technical expertise in machine learning engineering, and these architectures can struggle with long-term forecasting.

3 Study design

This study aims to assess the both the practicality and performance of selected AI models to forecast influenza A in the most populous provinces in Canada. The choice for influenza A is motivated by the fact that this is a seasonal infection which has been part of the PHAC's respiratory viruses surveillance system for more than about two decades, hence there is a lot of data that can be leveraged by the models. Although influenza A has, nearly every season, the expected typical shape of an epidemic -exponential growth, a single peak, followed by a exponential decrease -the start of seasonal epidemics as well as their peak are variable from season to season, making it a challenging epidemic to forecast. Moreover, seasonal influenza A is epidemiologically important because of its burden on health systems and global morbidity. To summarize, seasonal epidemics of influenza A in Canada provide a good balance (for our study) between data quality, data quantity, season-to-season variability and public health relevance.

3.1 Data preparation and splitting

For each province, we independently normalize the influenza A time series using Min-Max scaling and transform it into a supervised learning dataset through lag embedding. The lagged observations serve as predictor variables, while the subsequent observation serves as the target variable. We then train a separate local model for each province using only the corresponding influenza A lag-embedded matrix.

For global modeling, individual time series are scaled (Min-Max or standardized), transformed into lagged feature matrices, and vertically stacked into a single supervised regression dataset [73].

We train the models on the training set, tune their hyperparameters on the validation set, and evaluate their forecasting performance on the test set. The dataset, comprising data from the 2007/2008 season through the 2025/2026 season, is evaluated using a rolling-window procedure. The forecasting period begins on October 11, 2025, and extends through March 28, 2026. Thus, October 4, 2025, is used as the as-of date to ensure that no observations from the forecasting period are used for model training or hyperparameter tuning.

For each as-of date, only observations available before and on that date are used to construct the training and validation sets. The initial training period ends on October 2, 2024, and the training end date is subsequently advanced by one week at each iteration. Consequently, the training period expands progressively throughout the evaluation period. At each iteration, observations from dates after the training end date through and including the as-of date are used as the validation set. Forecast performance is evaluated on observations in the test period, from October 11, 2025, through March 28, 2026. This is applied to all models.

4.3 Discussion

The exceptional growth of machine learning models underpinning "artificial intelligence" has driven a revolution in what science thought possible: large language models, protein-folding algorithms, and computer coding agents are just a few examples. Hence, it is natural to apply these innovations in epidemiology, particularly to support efforts to lessen the impact of seasonal acute respiratory infections, which significantly strain health systems. Hence, our study aimed to answer a simple question: as of 2026, can "AI" models forecast relatively standard seasonal epidemics, and can they outperform canonical compartmental epidemiological models (CEMs)?

As a concrete example, we chose to forecast influenza A in selected Canadian provinces.

To do this, we reviewed the literature and selected machine learning models that already yielded promising results in other studies. After the literature review, we included one tree-based ensemble model (XGBoost), three deep learning models (LSTM, GRU, and Transformer), a physics-informed neural network (PINN) model, and one time-series foundation model (Chronos).

To compare these AI models with more traditional approaches, we chose an ARIMA model, a simple yet popular statistical approach; a more sophisticated statistical model, Prophet; and a SIR-like compartmental mechanistic model (CEM) used internally within PHAC to forecast seasonal respiratory infections over the past 5 years.

Short-term forecast accuracy for AI models was comparable to, and in select provinces marginally better than, traditional approaches; similarly, accuracy declined as the forecast horizon increased. However, when accuracy was better, it was only marginally so, which ultimately does not really compensate for their limitations presented in subsection 2.10. As a result, the expectation for the level of forecast accuracy should be much higher for AI models than for mechanistic models; in other words, to be practically useful, AI models should be much more accurate to compensate for their limitations (the main one being their intractability). Additionally, we found that documentation for implementing AI models for non-expert users was minimal, if not lacking, for most AI models, consequently hampering their use by public health practitioners. Furthermore, we note that the expertise required to run most AI models is currently relatively high, hampering adoption in most public health settings because they generally lack highly technical staff.

An important finding from our study is that while AI models could reach a similar or better accuracy than the CEM in half of the provinces (e.g., GRU, Transformer, and Chronos), the improvement was only marginal and, from a practical viewpoint, did not compensate for the opaqueness of AI models. Indeed, public health practitioners need to understand the basic mechanisms behind a given forecast to explain the underlying epidemiology and, when forecasts are pessimistic, provide potential countermeasures to decision-makers. Given their mechanistic nature, CEMs can disentangle all epidemiological aspects of a forecast, whereas AI models are largely inscrutable.

Although PINN models did not perform best in our study, they may be a promising approach in some cases. This lower performance may stem from the simple SIR model it uses, or from the difficulty of tuning the loss weights. Future work should thoroughly analyze how these loss terms affect PINN accuracy.

While TSFMs had a promising performance, Chronos achieved the best performance in British Columbia under zero-shot settings, demonstrating its utility without task-specific finetuning. Nevertheless, we do not see Chronos systematically outperforming the best-performing models across all jurisdictions. Future work should explore more TSFMs based on architectures (including transformer variants, i.e., encoder-decoder, encoder-only, and decoder-only, non-transformer models, and diffusion models), learning strategies (fully supervised vs. selfsupervised learning), and input representations (e.g., point-based vs. patch-based tokenization), as these factors may affect performance. Synthesizing those factors will establish benchmark practices for epidemic forecasting.

In this work, we explored cross-domain generalization by pooling time-series data from multiple provinces and viruses (Influenza B, RSV, and COVID-19). This data pooling significantly improved deep learning model performance, enabling globally and partially pooled architectures (such as GRU and Transformer) to outperform localized variants and achieve top ranks in multiple provinces. Expanding cross-domain data pooling remains a critical avenue for future research in epidemic forecasting.

Despite the advantages of CEMs in providing insights into disease progression and their inherent interpretability, several limitations remain. First, CEMs often rely on simplifying assumptions, such as homogeneous population mixing (even when the population is stratified), simplified contact patterns, or simplified pathogen evolution. Second, model misspecification and partial observability may occur when the chosen CEM does not fully reflect real-world dynamics or when some compartments are not directly observable. Third, epidemiological data are often sparse and uncertain because of under-reporting, testing limitations, and reporting delays, which complicates estimation of key model parameters. These parameters are typically inferred from historical data that may change over time due to evolving public health policies, pathogen evolution, behavior change, etc.

Our study focused narrowly on the 2025/2026 influenza A season in selected Canadian provinces. Hence, our results may not be generalizable to other settings or viruses. Although we chose the Canadian setting out of interest, seasonal respiratory virus epidemic waves in Canada are similar to those in other temperate western countries. Because AI models are datadriven, we expect similar findings if we applied our study to other similar geographical locations.

Forecasting influenza A, rather than other seasonal respiratory viruses, was chosen as we thought this virus provided a good balance of complexity: on the one hand, access to historical data of about 15 past seasons likely facilitated the fitting and training of the forecasting models; on the other hand, there is a marked heterogeneity between influenza A seasons (e.g., season start and peak timing, growth rate, etc.), likely caused mainly by the rapid evolution of this virus.

We attempted to leverage the AI models well. However, our use may not have been optimal and may have impacted forecasting performance.

In future work should explore other supervised methods for generating samples, such as direct, multi-output, or hybrid models.

This study emphasized a practical approach: using AI models in a typical public health setting by professionals with a fair understanding of machine-learning theory and implementation, but who are not experts in this field.

Evaluating the 2025/2026 influenza A season across Canadian provinces shows that short-term forecast accuracy for AI models (such as GRU, Transformer, and Chronos) was comparable to or better than traditional models, and each model's accuracy declined as the forecast horizon increased. The empirical findings yield key insights for AI deployment: cross-provincial and cross-virus data pooling help AI models to mitigate overfitting on small local sample sizes; AI models are slightly better than CEM, and zero-shot TSFMs like Chronos are also promising. Conversely, models like Prophet or PINNs performed poorly. An important finding from our study is that while AI models could reach similar or better accuracy than the CEM, the improvement was only marginal and, from a practical viewpoint, did not compensate for AI models' limitations. Because public health practitioners require transparent, epidemiologically grounded forecasts to explain disease dynamics and justify interventions to decision-makers, mechanistic CEMs remain the most practical and reliable primary forecasting tool.

As AI models improve and become more widely used, they could play an important role in epidemic forecasting. Further directions for investigation are numerous. One approach is to develop hybrid methods that combine AI models with traditional models (statistical models and CEMs) to exploit their complementary strengths. Alternatively, AI models can enhance CEMs by inferring their parameters from the observed data. Such approaches could combine AI's predictive power with the interpretability and epidemiological structure of mechanistic models. The GRU architecture is computationally more efficient than LSTMs due to its reduced complexity, while still maintaining the ability to capture long-term dependencies in sequential data. The update gate allows the model to adaptively control the flow of information from previous time steps, while the reset gate enables the model to forget irrelevant past information when generating the candidate activation. Although LSTMs and GRUs are effective at modeling long-term dependencies, they still rely on sequential processing of data, which can limit their efficiency when dealing with very long sequences. This has led to the development of transformer architectures that leverage self-attention mechanisms to capture dependencies without relying on recurrent connections.

Another limitation of RNNs, including LSTMs and GRUs, is that they process sequences sequentially, which can lead to longer training times compared to feedforward models that can be parallelized. This is particularly problematic for long sequences, where the computational cost can become prohibitive. Transformers address this issue by allowing for parallel processing of sequence data, enabling more efficient training and better handling of long-range dependencies.

Transformer

The transformer architecture, introduced in [39], is a neural network architecture designed to handle sequential data efficiently that relies on "self-attention" mechanisms. Transformers have revolutionized natural language processing and have been adapted for time series forecasting tasks due to their ability to model long-range dependencies and parallelize computations effectively. The core of a Transformer is self-attention, which lets the model decide how important each time step is when making a prediction. It does this by considering all the data at once and aim to capture the most important parts of the information, assigning weights to all inputs and combining them into a contextual representation. An encoder processes the input sequence, and a decoder uses this information to produce the forecast, while multi-head attention helps capture different types of relationships in the data at the same time, making it particularly effective for complex time series forecasting tasks where multiple factors may influence the target variable.

C Global Forecasting Methodology

The global forecasting methodology follows the cross-learning (or data pooling) paradigm, in which a single forecasting model is trained on multiple related time series. In this study, the global models leverage information from surveillance data collected across 8 Canadian provinces and three respiratory viruses (influenza A, Covid-19, and RSV). Unlike local forecasting, where each model is trained independently for a single province and target virus, global models exploit shared temporal patterns and cross-series dependencies among different provinces and viruses to improve predictive performance.

Data preparation

Each time series is independently normalized using either Min-Max scaling or standardization.

The normalized series is then transformed into a supervised learning dataset via lag embedding, in which lagged observations serve as predictor variables and the subsequent observation is the target variable. The lag-embedded matrices from all selected time series are vertically concatenated to form a single large training matrix. Consequently, model training reduces to a standard supervised regression problem in which the predictor matrix consists of the lagged observations and the response vector contains the corresponding target values (see [73]).

Data splitting, model architecture, hyperparameter search space, and hyperparameter tuning follow the same procedures as in the local paradigm.

Recurrent Units (GRUs) are a simplified variant of Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks that also aim to address the vanishing gradient problem in standard Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs). GRUs combine the forget and input gates of LSTMs into a single update gate and use a reset gate to control the influence of previous hidden states on the current candidate activation. This results in a more streamlined architecture with fewer parameters while still effectively modeling long-range dependencies in sequential data.
