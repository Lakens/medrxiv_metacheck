Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 5 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background and Objectives: Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a chronic neurological disorder characterized by demyelination and neuroaxonal injury. Genetics of MS susceptibility and long term disability is well characterized, but the genetic contribution to disability recorded earlier in the disease course is uncertain. We performed common-variant associations with the early period MS severity to identify potential causal loci, effector genes and associated pathways, and understand the relationships of early MS severity and other traits. Methods: The primary outcome was the first age-related MS severity score (ARMSS), an ageadjusted severity measure, in 7,209 individuals of Swedish discovery cohorts and independent Italian and multi-national European replication cohorts. Replicated loci underwent conditional analysis, finemapping, time-to-event MS phenotype association analyses, and multi-omics-integrated gene prioritization. Using the genome-wide summary statistics, we also conducted heritability analyses, polygenic risk score association with MS susceptibility and late MS severity, gene-level analyses, cross-trait analyses, Mendelian randomization, and drug-repurposing screening. Results: Two suggestive loci were replicated: rs1234317 on chromosome 1 (effect allele T: beta = 0.100, SE = 0.0189, P = 1.08 × 10 -7 ) and rs929769 on chromosome 5 (effect allele T: beta = -0.0956, SE = 0.0184, P = 1.95 × 10 -7 ). Fine-mapping supported rs929769 strongly in chromosome 5, and rs1234317 and rs6657136 in chromosome 1 with lower confidence. The SNPs did not show a significant change in effect sizes when adjusted for heritable traits and exhibited attenuated associations with long-term MS disability progression. TNFSF4 was the top prioritized effector for rs1234317, TNFSF18 for rs6657136, and ADAMTS2 for rs929769. Early MS severity showed partial genetic overlap with MS susceptibility and late MS severity, but no significant correlation with a range of neurological, psychiatric, autoimmune, cognitive, and aging traits. Pathway analyses identified ion-channel regulation and folic acid metabolism. Mendelian randomization suggested nominal bidirectional causal associations with educational attainment. In silico drug screening nominated several candidate drugs. Discussion: This study identifies candidate causal variants of early MS severity and their effector genes: TNFSF4, TNFSF18, and ADAMTS2. It provides insights into the genetic architecture and therapeutic measures for the early phase of MS severity. NOTE: This preprint reports new research that has not been certified by peer review and should not be used to guide clinical practice.

Introduction

Multiple sclerosis (MS) involves immune infiltration of the central nervous system (CNS), leading to neurological disability. Interplay between genetic and lifestyle factors shapes susceptibility to MS. The Human Leukocyte Antigen (HLA) region has the strongest genetic effect 1 on disease susceptibility, whereas >200 additional non-HLA loci contribute modest to small effects. 2 Although many genetic risk factors for MS susceptibility have been identified, disease severity remains largely underexplored. Recently, the first genetic loci (rs10191329, P<5 × 10 -8 ; rs149097173, P<5 × 10 -6 ) were reported to be associated with MS severity, highlighting the role of CNS resilience in long-term disability progression. A higher cortical lesion rate and brain atrophy was also associated with rs10191329 in people with MS (pwMS). 3 Despite this evidence, other studies have failed to replicate the rs10191329 association, 4, 5 which is likely due to the low statistical power. 6 Notably, a study suggested that the effect of severity variants might differ over the disease period, reporting that rs10191329 had the greatest effect between 14 and 20 years after MS onset, 4 implicating that severity loci other than rs10191329 and rs149097173 could have effects in the earlier period of MS. Since the existing genome-wide association study (GWAS) on MS severity focused on the late disease phase, we aimed to elucidate the genetic architecture of the disease severity of the early phase of MS. In addition to GWAS, we conducted fine-mapping for causal variants identification, association analyses with time-to-event MS phenotypes of the replicated SNPs (eFigure 1), integration of multi-omics gene prioritization, and tissue-specific gene expression and gene-set enrichment analyses. We further examined genetic correlations with various traits, assessed causal traits in relation to early MS severity, and potential therapeutic compounds. Given that treatment development supported by genetics significantly increases the potential of success, 7 our findings offer mechanistic insights and help in patient care in the early period of MS disability progression.

Methods

Standard Protocol Approvals, Registrations, and Patient Consents

The study setting, participant characteristics, eligibility to participate are presented in Table 1 and eSupplementary. The overview of this study is given in eFigure 1. Swedish patients have supplied written informed consent for the data collection and the study was approved by the local ethics committee. The MultipleMS prospective cohort study was approved by ethical review boards at nine participating sites. The Italian study was approved by the ethical committee of the IRCCS San Raffaele Hospital.

Genome-wide association and meta-analyses

The genotype quality control (QC) and imputation resulting in three discovery Swedish chip cohorts (GSA, OE, GSA2) and two replication multi-national European chip cohorts (OSR, MMS) are provided in the eSupplementary and eFigure 2-4. To identify genetic variants associated with early severity of MS, we used a linear regression model implemented via PLINK 2.0. 8 To ensure a normal distribution of the residuals, we applied a rank-based inverse normal transformation (RINT) on the first measured ARMSS. ARMSS is an age-adjusted MS severity score that ranks EDSS relative to pwMS in the same age bin (±2 years). EDSS is a neurological disability scale ranging from 0 to 10 to quantify MS disability. 9 Year of birth, sex, and principal components (PCs) were included for adjustment, but disease-modifying therapy and smoking were not adjusted due to the possibility of collider biases. 10 To evaluate potential residual confounding arising from population stratification or cryptic relatedness, we computed the genomic inflation factor and LDSC intercept using the HapMap3 variants and linkage disequilibrium (LD) scores from the 1000 Genomes Project phase 3. 11 Lead variants were identified by iteratively extending ±500KB around the most significant variant and merging the overlapping regions to the point where no variant below the P < 1 × 10 -5 threshold were identified within the set window. 12 Conditional analyses were performed to identify secondary signals. The lead variants were then tested for the replication analysis. Replication analysis was performed in the same regression model and the single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) were considered successfully replicated if the significance level of the one-sided P-value was below 0.05 with concordant direction of effect. The association statistics from the discovery and replication cohorts of the primary GWAS were meta-analyzed using a fixed-effects approach with METAL software. 13

Heritability estimation

To estimate SNP-based heritability, we used pooled genotype data of the discovery and replication populations and calculated a genomic relationship matrix (GRM) after cohortlevel quality control and harmonization to a common autosomal biallelic SNP set. Variants were trimmed with minor allele frequency (MAF) >= 0.01, genotype missingness <= 0.02 from the autosome. The related individuals were removed with the coefficients of relationship cutoff 0.025. We employed the resulting GRM to estimate SNP heritability using the restricted maximum likelihood method in GCTA (v 1.94.1). 14, 15 Given that SNP heritability is affected by LD and allele frequency, 16 we also implemented a GREML-LDMS model incorporating ten GRMs constructed from variants categorized into five MAF bins (0.01-0.1, 0.1-0.2, 0.2-0.3, 0.3-0.4, and 0.4-0.5), with each bin further subdivided based on the median LD score. Heritability analyses were adjusted for sex, year of birth, cohort, and ten PCs.

Heritability enrichment analyses

We implemented stratified LDSC (version 1.0.1) to estimate SNP-based heritability enrichment in 53 baseline model functional annotations (version 1.2). 17 To capture the tissue-specific contributions, we conducted stratified LDSC using a reference gene expression dataset of 205 tissues and cell types as given in LDSC. 17 Multiple testing correction was carried out with false discovery rate (FDR)-adjusted P < 0.05. Prior to analysis, summary statistics were restricted to HapMap3 variants with imputation quality R² ≥ 0.9 and excluded variants within the major histocompatibility complex (MHC) region.

Fine mapping

We fine-mapped the two replicated loci using 1Mb windows around the lead variants. Summary statistics from the meta-analysis platforms were intersected with genotype data from all the cohorts. Platform-specific LD matrices were calculated after residualizing the genotypes for study-specific covariates and combined using sample-size weighting. Kriging diagnostics and λs_rss were calculated to support the use of the weighted sum LD matrix. We utilized two methods for robustness: SuSiE-RSS 18 and FINEMAP v1.4 19 allowing up to 10 causal variants. We compared the SuSiE and FINEMAP results by estimating Pearson and Spearman correlation of posterior inclusion probabilities.

rs1234317, rs6657136, and rs929769 associations with MS time-to-event phenotypes

The time-to-event disability analyses examined three traits: rate of Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS) progression over disease duration using a generalized linear mixed model (GLMM), time to clinically meaningful worsening (24-week confirmed disability worsening, Cox proportional hazards), and time to EDSS 6.0 with sensitivity analysis as time to sustained EDSS 6.0, both using interval-censored Cox model. Hazard ratios were calculated adjusted for sex, cohort, and PCs, with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons (P < 0.0083). Full model specifications are in eSupplementary.

Sensitivity analyses

We first performed linear regression focusing on the SNPs of interest, without any transformation on the first ARMSS score. In addition, we utilized a linear mixed-model on RINT first ARMSS score using LDAK-KVIK, 20 a genome-wide mixed-model tool with more realistic SNP effect size variation modeling than others. 21 Both models were performed on discovery cohorts and meta-analyzed with METAL. 13 We then respectively adjusted for age at onset, disease duration, and ever-smoking history in linear regression with RINT first ARMSS score, in a pooled discovery cohort and compared the estimates without the mentioned traits. Imputation chip and wave were accounted in each model. Collider bias was tested by examining the associations between each SNP of interest and the adjusted trait in linear regression framework.

Multi-omics integrated gene prioritization and annotation

Candidate effector genes were prioritized using an integrative SNP-to-gene framework applied to the SNPs of interest and their LD proxies from the 1000 Genomes phase 3 European reference panel (r2 ≥ 0.6). Included strategies are 1) ANNOVAR/refGene positional variant-to-gene annotation, 2) molecular quantitative trait locus (QTL) evidence from expression QTL (eQTL)/splicing QTL (sQTL) resources, 3) chromatin interaction evidence from Hi-C, promoter-capture Hi-C (pcHi-C), and ABC resources, 4) Open Targets L2G predictions (L2G >= 0.5), 5) precomputed cS2G SNP-to-gene scores, 6) post-hoc transcriptome-wide association study (TWAS), and 7) colocalization evidence. Each strategy was weighted from the literature-prior evidence hierarchy. The gene that had the highest weighted sum of score for each SNP was prioritized as the candidate effector genes. For the annotations of the prioritized genes, tissue and cell-type expression profiles were examined using GTEx v10 bulk RNA-seq, the Human Protein Atlas single-cells and single-nucleus RNA-seq dataset. Full details of the resource references and the weighting scheme are in eSupplementary.

Genome-wide gene association scan and pathway analyses

Two complementary genome-wide gene association scan (GWGAS) methods were employed: MAGMA v1.10 (linear, multi-model mode) 22 and LDAK-KVIK-GBAT (mixed model). 20 Pathway enrichment was assessed via MAGMA competitive gene-set analysis, Ingenuity canonical pathway and upstream regulator analyses, and FUMA tissue enrichment using GTEx v8 differentially expressed gene sets. Multiple testing correction used the Benjamini-Hochberg method. Full details are in eSupplementary.

Genetic overlap of early MS severity with MS susceptibility, late severity, and non-MS traits

We tested 208 MS susceptibility variants 2 for association with early MS severity using Bonferroni correction (P = 2.40 × 10 ⁴). LD between early MS severity (rs1234317, ⁻ rs6657136, and rs929769) and susceptibility SNPs were calculated using PLINK 2.0. 8 The concordance in effect directions was evaluated with an exact binomial test. Polygenic scores (PGS) were constructed with respect to the MS susceptibility variants and early MS severity were regressed on the PGS in GSA2 chip cohort two ways, one with PGS, year of birth, sex, and the PCs, and another without PGS and only the covariates. GSA2 chip cohort was utilized since the participants do not overlap with MS susceptibility GWAS. 2 Incremental R² was calculated from the R² difference of the two. For the genetic comparison between early and long-term MS severity, we tested rs10191329 and rs149097173 from the late MS severity GWAS 3 in association with early MS severity. LD with early severity SNPs was calculated using PLINK 2.0.8 PGS with respect to suggestively significant late severity SNPs (P < 5 × 10 ⁶) were constructed ⁻ after LD clumping (r 2 < 0.01) and incremental R² was calculated in the same manner in the GSA2 chip cohort. GSA2 chip cohort was selected due to non-overlap of the participants.

For genetic comparison with non-MS traits, we evaluated phenome-wide associations for our SNPs of interest across the GWAS Catalog, UK Biobank, and FinnGen. Genomewide cross-trait analyses were performed using LDSC for 16 neurological, psychiatric, autoimmune, cognitive, and aging traits. Full details and references for the implementations are in the eSupplementary.

Mendelian Randomization

Two-sample MR was performed with four exposures: 25-hydroxyvitamin D, body mass index (BMI), lifetime smoking index, and educational attainment. Instruments were selected at two P-value thresholds (P < 5 × 10 ⁸ and ⁻ P < 1 × 10 ⁵) after LD clumping (r² ⁻ < 0.001). Primary analysis used inverse-variance weighted (IVW) with random effects; sensitivity analyses included MR-RAPS, weighted median, MR-PRESSO, and MR-Egger. Reverse MR was also performed. Full details are in the eSupplementary.

Genomics-based drug discovery

In silico drug prioritization leveraged two complementary strategies: 1) compound-gene matching via the Scalable Precision Medicine Open Knowledge Engine (SPOKE) graph database, querying ChEMBL, BindingDB, PharmGKB, DrugBank, and Therapeutic Target Database for compounds targeting the top candidate effector genes and the LDAK-KVIK-GBAT FDR-significant genes; and 2) TransPhar analysis to identify compounds with inverse transcriptional correlation between the predicted genetically regulated expression profiles of early MS severity and the compound-regulated gene expression in LINCS Cmap L1000 data. Full details are in the eSupplementary.

Results

Heritability analyses of early MS severity

We analyzed 7,209 pwMS European descent, with the discovery cohort (N = 5,593) having a mean age of 38.9 years at first EDSS assessment and a mean disease duration of 5.25 years, while the replication cohort (N = 1,616) had corresponding values of 34.2 and 3.57 years (Table 1). For the single-component heritability analysis, the estimates were imprecise (h2 = 0.0388, SE = 0.0658), yet in multi-component model, the analysis gave estimates in better precision (h2 = 0.185, SE = 0.0947, 95% CI = [-0.0006, 0.3706]; eTable 1). The heritability partitioning across the 53 functional genomic annotation categories (eTable 2) nor for the gene sets across 205 tissues and cell types (eTable 3) did not find any enrichment.

Genome-wide association study and fine-mapping

To understand the genetic components of the disability severity in the early period of MS, we conducted GWAS using rank-inverse normalized first ARMSS scores, 23 in a linear regression framework in each chip cohort, followed by meta-analyses. The genomic inflation was modest ( GC = 1.01) and the linkage disequilibrium score regression (LDSC) intercept was not significantly different from 1 (estimate = 1.00, SE = 0.00632). We identified 33 independent SNPs with suggestive significance (P < 1.0 × 10 -5 , Fig. 1A), and of these SNPs, rs1234317 (1:173187775:C:T) and rs929769 (5:178727997:T:C), were replicated (Table 2, eTable 4). Conditional analyses did not reveal additional signals in each locus (Fig. 1B and 1C). Fine-mapping with SuSiE-RSS and FINEMAP prioritized rs929769 at chromosome 5 as a single-variant 95% credible set (SuSiE-RSS posterior inclusion probability (PIP) = 0.964, FINEMAP posterior probability (PP) = 0.643). At chromosome 1, the primary 95% SuSiE credible set had two SNPs with the probabilities of 0.561 (rs6657136) and 0.399 (rs1234317), while the secondary set contained 1,399 variants including rs6657136 and rs1234317. FINEMAP assigned two-causal-variant model as the most probable in this locus (PP = 0.620), yet the highest individual two-variant configuration had PP=0.009. Detailed LD-matrix diagnostics, model comparisons, and SNP-level posterior probabilities are provided in eFigure 5 and eTables 5-6. In the meta-analyses of the three SNPs across the discovery and replication cohorts, rs929769_T allele decreased the early MS severity (β= -0.0956, SE= 0.0184, P=1.95 × 10 -7 ), rs1234317_T allele increased it (β= 0.100, SE= 0.0189, P=1.08 × 10 -7 ), and rs6657136_A allele increased the severity (β= 0.0812, SE= 0.0175, P=1.35 × 10 -7 ; Table 2).

Sensitivity analyses of rs929769, rs1234317, and rs6657136

We then tested each SNP in linear mixed models and did not find notable difference in effect size or statistical significance compared to main analysis. We also conducted linear regression without RINT for clinical interpretability, which exhibited significance in effects (eTable 7). In addition, for these three SNPs we adjusted for several traits: age at onset, disease duration at first EDSS, and ever-smoking history to detect any confounding effects. No evidence of collider biases was observed and all three SNPs remained significant and similar in effect sizes after adjustments (eTable 8, eFigure 6).

rs1234317, rs6657136, and rs929769 associations with MS time-to-event phenotypes

We next examined whether the prioritized SNPs affect MS progression using three disability accumulation MS phenotypes: rate of EDSS progression, time to 24-week confirmed worsening, and time to EDSS 6 with interval censoring. Time to sustained EDSS 6 was a sensitivity analysis of time to EDSS 6. Overall, associations with MS disability accumulations were weaker and less consistent. The rs1234317_T allele was associated with shorter time to EDSS ≥ 6 (HR = 1.15, SE = 0.0504, P = 0.00688), and rs929769_C was associated with higher EDSS scores over disease duration (β = 0.159, SE = 0.0472, P = 0.0007). No variant showed consistent evidence of altered EDSS progression rate or 24week confirmed disability worsening. Full per-variant analyses of the tested MS progression phenotypes are provided in eFigures 7-9 and eSuppplementary.

Multi-omics integrated gene prioritization

Integrative SNP-to-gene prioritization of 23 LD proxies for the three candidate causal variants are shown in eTable 9 and were used for prioritizing the potential effector genes. TNFSF4 was prioritized for rs1234317 based on positional, cS2G, and L2G evidence. The bulk and single-cell RNA-seq annotations for TNFSF4 supported its expression in immune and CNS-relevant cell types. TNFSF18 was prioritized for rs6657136 in positional, eQTL, and chromatin-interaction mapping. The TNFSF18 expression was found across brain tissues and neuronal, macrophage/microglial, and glial populations. ADAMTS2 was prioritized for rs929769 based on positional and cS2G evidence. Its expression was seen in fibroblasts, macrophages, oligodendroglial cells, and neuronal clusters. Detailed geneprioritization scores, expression profiles, eQTL results, and chromatin-interaction annotations are in eSupplementary text, eTable 9, and eFigures 10-12.

Genome-wide gene association scan and pathway analyses

Genome-wide gene-association scan and pathway analyses provided suggestive support for biological pathways relevant to early MS severity. Although MAGMA did not identify FDR-significant genes, LDAK-KVIK-GBAT identified 189 FDR-significant genes associated with early MS severity (eFigure 13, eTables 10-11). Tissue-specific expression analyses demonstrated enrichment of the FDR-significant genes among downregulated differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in brain regions, upregulated DEGs in EBVtransformed lymphocytes, and bidirectional DEGs in the left ventricle (eFigures 14-15, eTables 12-13). Pathway analyses highlighted sinoatrial node cell development and differentiation, cardiac pacemaker cell differentiation, folic acid transport, and folic acid binding (eTables 14-18, eFigure 16). The full results are in eSupplementary.

Genetic relationships of early MS severity with MS susceptibility, late MS severity, and non-MS traits

Genetic overlap analyses suggested partial but limited sharing between early MS severity, MS susceptibility, and late MS severity. Compared with published MS susceptibility 2 and late-severity GWAS results 3 , early MS severity showed no significant immune-or CNScell heritability enrichment pattern matching those traits (Figure 2; eTable 3). One MS susceptibility variant, rs4409785 (eTable 19), was associated with early MS severity after Bonferroni correction (P=0.0302). The proportion of susceptibility variants in concordant direction of effect with the early severity variants significantly differed from randomness (P = 0.0266, eTable 20). The late-severity variant rs10191329 was associated with early MS severity in the same direction of effect (β =0.0623, P = 0.00588), although in cohorts that did not overlap with the late MS severity GWAS, 3 the association was insignificant (β =0.0327, P=0.546). The LDs between the early MS severity loci and the published susceptibility or late-severity variants were low (susceptibility: r2 ≤ 0.00133; late-severity: r2 ≤ 2.99 × 10 -4 ; eTables 21, 22; eFigure s17A, 17B), and PGS for MS susceptibility and late severity explained small variance in early MS severity (susceptibility: ∆R² = 0.00343; late-severity ∆R² = 0.00211; eTables 23, 24). Phenome-wide lookup of rs929769, rs6657136, and rs1234317 found no significant evidence of pleiotropic associations and genome-wide cross-trait analyses reported limited shared genetic contributions of early MS severity with broader neurological, psychiatric, cognitive, autoimmune, or aging-related traits (eTable 26; eFigure 18). The detailed results are in eSupplementary.

Mendelian randomization of modifiable traits

In two-sample MR analyses, we found no evidence of causal or reverse-causal associations for 25-hydroxyvitamin D, body mass index, or lifetime smoking index, although the reverse analysis for lifetime smoking showed heterogeneity (P = 5.3 × 10 -4 ). Higher educational attainment suggested a nominal inverse causal association with early MS severity across the MR estimators, with no evidence of directional pleiotropy by Egger intercept (P = 0.732 and 0.346) or MR-PRESSO global tests (P = 0.201 and 0.091). Reverse MR for educational attainment also suggested a nominal causal association (β = -0.0755, SE = 0.0327, P = 0.0209), with acceptable heterogeneity (P = 0.253; Figure 3; eTables 27-28).

In silico drug screening

In silico drug-prioritization analyses, compound-gene matching nominated candidate compounds targeting TNFSF4, TNFSF18, ADAMTS2, and genes identified in gene-based analyses. However, Inverse correlation analysis between the predicted genetically regulated expression (GREx) and LINCS L1000 gene expression signature did not identify any compounds (eTables 29-31; eFigure 19). The detailed description of the results are in eSupplementary.

Discussion

We conducted a GWAS of early MS severity using the first ARMSS measurement to characterize disability at an early stage when long-term progression and cumulative treatment exposure have not dominated the phenotype. Disability accumulation in MS involves two partially separable processes: relapse-associated worsening (RAW) that predominates early, and progression independent of relapse activity (PIRA) that contributes increasingly as the disease evolves. 24 Our study design leveraged early ARMSS and excluded measurements proximal to relapses, to reduce the relapse-driven disability dominance but retain sensitivity to early PIRA-related disability accrual. We identified two replicated loci, at chromosomes 1 and 5, and fine-mapping prioritized rs1234317, rs6657136, and rs929769 as candidate causal variants. Integrative gene prioritization nominated TNFSF4, TNFSF18, and ADAMTS2 . We also observed partial overlap of early MS severity genetics with MS susceptibility and less with late severity, but limited overlap with broader disease-related traits. Potential bidirectional causal relationship was indicated between early MS severity and the education years, and several pathways and drug candidates were suggested. Although SNP-heritability estimates was barely crossing the borderline null value in 95% CI, the results imply common-variant contribution to early MS severity, which could be detected with larger participant size. Fine-mapping provided the strongest support for rs929769 at chromosome 5, whereas chromosome 1 locus demonstrated rs6657136 and rs1234317 as the most probable causal SNPs yet with lower confidence shown by large number of variants in the second credible set. Sensitivity analyses confirmed the effect of all three SNPs. The attenuated associations of these variants with later time-to-event disability outcomes suggest that their effects may be more relevant to early severity than to long-term disability accumulation. Integrative multi-omics-based candidate effector gene investigation prioritized TNFSF4, TNFSF18, and ADAMTS2. TNFSF4 encodes OX40L, a regulator of T-cell activation. Astrocytic OX40L can stimulate OX40-positive T cells in CNS tissue, promoting glial activation, blood-brain barrier (BBB) breakdown, and axonal damage. [25] [26] [27] TNFSF18 encodes glucocorticoid-induced TNFR-related protein (GITR) ligand (GITRL) expressed in immune and CNS-resident cell types and participates in regulatory T-cell expansion, CNS neuroinflammatory signaling. 28, 29 ADAMTS2 encodes a secreted metalloprotease involved in extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling, with expression localized to excitatory neurons and perivascular fibroblast-like cells. The dysregulation in ADAMTS family members are associated with ECM degradation and MS progression, [30] [31] [32] suggesting the possible role of ADAMTS2 in similar mechanistic context. Genetic comparison with MS susceptibility and late MS severity suggested partial but limited overlap. Although the selected susceptibility and late-severity variants were associated with early MS severity and non-random concordance in effect direction between early severity and MS susceptibility was detected, early severity did not show the immunecell or CNS-cell heritability enrichment patterns observed for MS susceptibility and late severity, respectively. This could be due to early severity not being driven by either mechanism or power issue. 2, 3 However, early severity regressed on PGS constructed by the two phenotypes explained little additional variance. Collectively, these findings suggest that early MS severity may share selected genetic components with MS susceptibility and later disability severity, but to limited extent. In examining the genetic relationships of early MS severity with various phenotypes, neither phenome-wide analyses for rs929769, rs6657136, and rs1234317, nor genomewide genetic correlations with neurological, psychiatric, aging, and autoimmune traits revealed significant associations. The results could be due to early severity having unique genetic architecture or limitation in heritability estimates due to available sample size. 33 Secondary overrepresentation analyses of early MS severity GWGAS genes in tissues and cell-specific DEGs may reflect shared transcriptional characteristics with reduced expression in brain tissues, active expression in immune cells, and cellular energy metabolism or stress response signatures across excitable cell types. Pathway analyses highlighted non-canonical MS pathways that may reflect shared regulatory modules across excitable cell types, particularly ion channel regulation and metabolic support. 50, 51 The findings are exploratory but hints on linked biology of early period of MS severity. The MR analyses did not support causal associations of 25-hydroxyvitamin D, BMI, consistent with prior evidence that these factors failing to exert modulatory effects on disability progression. 37, 38 The lifetime smoking index also did not exhibit any causal relationship. Smoking has been associated with faster progression of the disease, 39 but the effect is reported to diminish after quitting smoking. 40 Since this study measured the first recorded EDSS scores after diagnosis, it is plausible that pwMS had significant motivation to change their smoking behavior following diagnosis, as a result weakening the causal relationship. Educational attainment suggested a nominal bidirectional causal association with early MS severity. This bidirectional outputs align with the broader concept of cognitive reserve and resilience, in which higher educational attainment may buffer against cognitive damage. 41, 42 Conversely, genetic variants that accelerate early disease severity may influence neural integrity in prodromal and early phase of MS during adolescence and early adulthood, leading to cognitive difficulties and having fewer years of education. 43 In silico drug-prioritization analyses nominated compounds targeting the GWASprioritized genes TNFSF4, TNFSF18, and ADAMTS2. Despite the drug-gene link are more of hypothesis-generating, the findings narrow down the candidate drug and therapeutic components that could be informative in further validation experiments to establishing disease modifying treatment in MS. This study has limitations. First, all participants were of European genetic ancestry, limiting generalizability to other ancestries. Second, relapse status at the time of EDSS assessment was unavailable in the Italian cohort during the data analyses phase. However, EDSS measurements were clinically guided to be conducted outside periods of recent relapse. Approximately 1.7% of the EDSS measurements (discovery: 116/5593, replication: 4/1616) may have been used in the late MS severity GWAS, since the individuals (N = 120) had only one measurement recorded in the registries. However, the minimal overlap suggests a negligible impact on our findings. Finally, in MR analyses, instrumental variable assumption violation cannot be fully excluded, despite our sensitivity MR analyses displayed no sign of horizontal pleiotropy. In conclusion, our study provides insight into the genetic architecture of early MS severity and nominates effector biology for functional follow-ups. Further experimental work is needed to clarify the mechanism and evaluate therapeutic relevance for the early stages of MS.
