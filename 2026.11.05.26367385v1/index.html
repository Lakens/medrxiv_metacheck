Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. 1. Introduction

Sexual minority people experience persistent inequalities in health and health-related behaviours compared with heterosexual people (King et al., 2008; Plöderl & Tremblay, 2015). They are also disproportionately exposed to experiences of discrimination, harassment and victimisation related to their sexual identity (Katz-Wise & Hyde, 2012). The minority stress framework proposes that these experiences, alongside expectations of rejection, concealment of sexual identity and internalised stigma, can generate additional and chronic stress among sexual minority people (Meyer, 2003). Such stressors may contribute to poorer social and health outcomes, and drug use has been proposed as one potential coping response (Parent et al., 2019).

Drug use, in this analysis, refers to the use of a substance for a purpose not consistent with legal or medical guidelines, for example the non-medical use of prescription medications or the recreational use of illicit drugs. Such use of drugs is associated with a wide range of health and social harms, including premature mortality. In 2024, 5,565 deaths related to drug poisoning were registered in England and Wales, the highest number recorded since records began in 1993 (ONS 2025). These risks may be heightened by increases in the potency of some drugs. Cocaine sold to users in Europe in 2024 was 44% purer than in 2014 (EUDA, 2026), while high-strength cannabis has increasingly displaced weaker varieties in Britain (Freeman et al., 2021; Potter et al., 2018). Nitazenes, synthetic opioids generally far more potent than heroin, have entered the heroin and counterfeit medicine markets (Holland et al., 2024), with implications for drug-related mortality (Chen et al., 2026).

Drug use is consistently reported to be more prevalent among sexual minority than heterosexual people. A recent meta-analysis of 304 studies found that sexual minority youth had consistently higher odds of using a range of illicit drugs than their heterosexual peers. Across substances, odds ranged from 71% higher for cannabis use (OR 1.71) to 363% higher for heroin use (OR 4.63), with wider inequities among plurisexual youth, sexual minority girls and young women, and those aged 18 or under (Mereish et al., 2026). Recent evidence from the UK Millennium Cohort Study similarly found higher odds of drug and cannabis use among sexual minority adolescents, with inequalities varying by ethnicity (Khanolkar et al., 2023). Among adults, analyses of national probability samples find similar patterns. In the National Survey on Drug Use and Health (NSDUH), substantially higher past-year illicit drug use was reported by gay, lesbian and especially bisexual adults in the United States (SAMHSA, 2016 (SAMHSA, , 2023)). Crime Survey for England and Wales (CSEW) data for 2011/12-2013/14 found past-year drug use of 28.4% among lesbian, gay and bisexual adults compared with 8.1% among heterosexual adults aged 16-59. This difference was evident for most drug types and the relative difference was greater in women (22.9% vs 5.1%) than men (33.0% vs 11.1%) (Home Office, 2014). Analyses of the combined 2007 and 2014 Adult Psychiatric Morbidity Survey (APMS) samples found higher rates of self-reported past year drug use among 16 to 64 year olds identifying as bisexual (37%) and lesbian or gay (25.3%) compared with those identifying as heterosexual (10.5%) or reporting another sexual identity (8.5%) (Pitman et al., 2022). These differences remained despite adjustment for reported experiences of bullying and discrimination (Pitman et al., 2022).

There are significant gaps in the evidence base. Much existing research has used convenience samples or lacked a heterosexual comparison group. Initial research on substance use among sexual minority adults often recruited via social and community groups (Hughes et al., 2020). Community-based LGBT+ surveys face related issues; the magnitude of inequities and variation by drug types cannot be reliably established from convenience data (Drabble et al., 2018). National social and health surveys have also not consistently collected information on sexual identity, and data on gender identity have been even more limited (ONS, 2019). Where sexual identity is measured in probability surveys, achieving sufficiently large samples can be challenging, given sexual minority participants tend to comprise less than 5% of general population samples (Hayes et al., 2012). Small samples also limit disaggregation by demographic and identity subgroups, despite evidence that bisexual people, and bisexual women in particular, may experience particularly pronounced inequalities (Schuler & Collins, 2020).

Another limitation is that much of the evidence base is dated and does not tell us about change over time or (2002) (2003) (2004) (2005) (2006) (2007) (2008) (2009) (2010) (2011) (2012) (2013) (2014) (2015) suggest narrowing sexual-orientation inequities in substance use among males but persistence or widening among females (Paschen-Wolff et al., 2019). Previous APMS analyses found no significant interaction between 2007 and 2014 (Pitman et al., 2022). Comparable evidence from the UK and Europe remains limited. A UK analysis covering 2014-2021 found little evidence of sustained widening, but only examined nicotine vaping, tobacco smoking and alcohol rather than illicit drug use (Bai et al., 2026).

Official UK estimates of drug use tend to be drawn from the CSEW, a survey of crime rather than of health.

The CSEW is a victimisation survey administered alongside questions on being the victim of crime. This framing may position drug use as offending behaviour, potentially reducing the likelihood of disclosure. Its drug use module has historically been restricted to respondents aged 16-59 (ONS, 2024). A recent review of UK secondary data sources concluded that no existing health survey currently supports robust analysis of sexual identity differences in drug use (Davies et al., 2022).

Most of the existing literature on drug use and sexual identity, including the main meta-analytic synthesis, focuses on young people (Mereish et al., 2026) or school and college samples (Mereish, 2019; Grigsby et al., 2026). Despite evidence that inequities vary across the life course and may follow different age patterns for men and women (Schuler et al., 2018; Parent et al., 2019), substance use among sexual minority adults in mid and later life has rarely been examined in population-based samples. Because APMS 2014 only asked sexual identity of participants aged 16 to 64, Pitman and colleagues pooled analysis focused on this age group. Across the full adult age range, population-based evidence on sexual identity inequities in drug use is lacking.

The present study aims to address these limitations. We analysed the APMS, a probability sample health survey of the household population of England aged 16 and over, in which sexual identity and illicit drug use were measured in a comparable way at three points in time: 2007, 2014 and 2024 (Morris et al., 2025). We examined self-reported past-year illicit drug use among sexual minority and heterosexual participants, addressing whether drug use inequities changed over time and if trends differed by sex and age.

Specifically, we asked: (i) how large were sexual identity inequalities in past-year drug use in England in 2024, overall and by drug type; (ii) have inequalities narrowed, persisted or widened between 2007, 2014 and 2024; and (iii) whether trends differ by sex and age group.

2. Methods

>Methods

2.1 Study design and participants

We conducted a secondary analysis of the cross-sectional 2007, 2014 and 2024 APMS, which covered the household population of England aged 16 years and older. Sampling used a stratified, multistage random sampling design, based on the Small User Postcode Address File. This involved the selection of primary sampling units, addresses within selected primary sampling units, and one adult from each address.

Participants had to be able to speak English sufficiently well to be interviewed, and provided verbal informed consent. Interviews were conducted in participants' homes (or elsewhere, if preferred) by trained research interviewers, and averaged 1.5 hours. Interviews comprised computer-assisted personal interviewing, with some sensitive information collected through computer-assisted self-completion interview in which participants used the interviewer's laptop. Further methodological detail is published elsewhere (McManus et al., 2020; Morris et al., 2025).

2.2 Measures

Drug use: questions were asked in the self-completion part of the interview (Roberts et al., 2016). They covered lifetime and past-year use of over twenty types of named drugs. Alongside the main drug name, a variety of informal terms were provided to help with recognition, for example '(marijuana, grass, hash, ganja, draw, skunk, weed, spliff)' was listed in brackets after cannabis. The drugs asked about largely corresponded to those included in recent waves of the CSEW, with some additional drug types such as volatile substances (described on the survey as 'glues, solvents, gas or aerosols (to sniff)') and amyl nitrite.

New psychoactive substances (NPS) as a distinct category was not included, although some of the drugs asked about (such as mephedrone), could historically be classified as NPS. Two combined drug use in the past year variables were derived: one comprised drug types asked about in every survey, including cannabis, amphetamines, cocaine powder, crack cocaine, ecstasy, heroin, methadone, tranquillisers, alkyl nitrites, anabolic steroids, volatile substances, LSD and magic mushrooms. The other captured the expanded list of drug types asked about in 2024, which additionally included nitrous oxide, methamphetamine, synthetic cannabinoids, khat and prescription opioids. In addition, trends in past-year use of seven specific drug typescannabis, cocaine, ketamine, ecstasy/MDMA, nitrites/poppers, mephedrone, and methamphetamine -are also presented.

Sexual identity: was derived using an approach consistent with that of Pitman and colleagues (2022). In 2007 participants were randomised (50:50) to one of two versions of a question, derived from American (Remafedi et al., 1992) or New Zealand (Fergusson et al., 2005) instruments. The first version asked participants 'Which statement best describes your current sexual orientation? This means sexual feelings, whether or not you have had any sexual partners.' It presented the following six options: entirely heterosexual (attracted to persons of the opposite of sex); mostly heterosexual, some homosexual feelings; bisexual (equally attracted to men and women); mostly homosexual, some heterosexual feelings; entirely homosexual (attracted to persons of the same sex); other sexual identities. The second version asked participants 'Please choose the answer below that best describes how you think of yourself.' It presented these six options: completely heterosexual; mainly heterosexual; bisexual; mainly gay or lesbian; completely gay or lesbian; other sexual identities. The question using 'gay or lesbian' elicited higher reporting of minority orientation than the question using 'homosexual', confirming that reporting is sensitive to question wording (Hayes et al., 2012). The wording in 2014 and 2024, however, aligned with the Office for National Statistics (ONS) national harmonised standard: heterosexual or straight; gay or lesbian; bisexual; other sexual identities (ONS, 2019). Sexual identity was asked of all participants (aged ≥16 years) in the 2007 and 2024 surveys, but only of participants aged 16-64 years in the 2014 survey. Data were collected using computer-assisted self-interviewing (CASI).

To derive broadly comparable groups, we first combined data from 2007 across the two question versions to form six categories: heterosexual; mainly heterosexual; bisexual; mainly homosexual; homosexual; other sexual identities. Following Pitman and colleagues (2022), we then combined 'mainly heterosexual' with 'heterosexual' and 'mainly homosexual' with 'lesbian/gay'. This mapped the additional 'mainly' categories available in 2007 onto the closest corresponding categories available in later surveys and avoided disproportionately enlarging the sexual minority group by including the relatively large 'mainly heterosexual' category. While there are differences in practice, this approach was used to approximate comparable groupings over time. Due to the small numbers in some categories, a binary sexual identity variable (heterosexual vs sexual minority) was also generated. Sociodemographic factors: These included age (16-24, 25-34, 35-44, 45-54, 55-64, 65+ years), sex (male, female), and area-level deprivation, measured using Index of Multiple Deprivation (IMD) quintiles assigned according to participants' residential postcode. Although gender identity was asked on the most recent (2024) survey, a binary coded men/women was the only classification available across all the surveys.

2.3 Statistical analysis

Analyses were weighted and accounted for complex design, selection probabilities (likelihood that someone would be selected to take part), and non-response. Population control totals drew on the UK Office for National Statistics (ONS) population estimates for age by sex and region. Analyses were conducted in Stata 19 MP. Participants with missing data on sexual identity or drug use (topics covered in the self-completion section of the interview) were excluded from the relevant analyses; unweighted bases in each table therefore vary and reflect complete-case analysis for each measure.

Using the 2024 survey, we described the distribution of sexual identity across four categories and the prevalence of any past-year illicit drug use within each category. The distributions of any drug use and seven specific drug types across survey years were also examined. We then assessed patterns and trends in pastyear drug use by binary sexual identity, for both any drug use and the seven specific drug types, with estimates additionally stratified by sex and age group.

For all descriptive analyses, unweighted bases and numbers of cases were reported alongside weighted prevalence estimates with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), where applicable. Analyses involving sexual identity across all three survey years were restricted to participants aged 16-64 years because sexual identity was assessed only in this age range in APMS 2014. Such analyses across the full adult age range (including those aged ≥65 years) were therefore available only for 2007 and 2024.

Logistic regression was used to examine the association between sexual identity and any past-year drug use.

Models were fitted separately for participants aged 16-64 years in 2007, 2014 and 2024, with both unadjusted models and models adjusted for age, sex and IMD reported.

3. Results

>Results

3.1 Past-year drug use by sexual identity in 2024

The APMS samples comprised 7403 participants in 2007, 7546 in 2014, and 6912 in 2024.

In 2024, 81.9% of participants identified as heterosexual or straight, around 5% identified as bisexual, gay or lesbian, or other sexual identities (3.1%, 1.9%, and 0.4%, respectively); and sexual identity was missing or unavailable for 12.8% (Supplementary Table S1). In 2024, one in ten (10.2%; 95% CI 9.0, 11.4) adults aged 16 and over reported illicit drug use in the past year, on a measure comparable across survey years (Supplementary Table S2). Past-year illicit drug use was lowest among heterosexual adults (9.1%; 95% CI 7.9, 10.4), around double among gay or lesbian adults (18.3%; 6.6, 30.0), and almost triple among bisexual adults (26.8%; 17.0, 36.5) and those reporting other sexual identities (28.3%; 3.3, 53.3) (Figure 1 and Supplementary Table S1). Note that few participants reported other sexual identities (N=20), there are wide confidence intervals and the results for this group should be interpreted with caution. Estimates on an expanded measure that included drug types added to the survey in 2024 were slightly higher, but the difference between the groups was similar. Results using the binary sexual identity measure were consistent (Supplementary Table S3).

Cannabis was the most commonly used drug among both heterosexual (7.8%; 6.7, 9.0) and sexual minority adults (21.1%; 14.5, 27.7) (Supplementary Table S3). The prevalence of use was higher among sexual minority than heterosexual adults for all individual drug types examined, with particularly pronounced inequities for some drugs. Prevalence was more than twice as high for cocaine (5.3% vs 2.1%), compared with around five times as high for ecstasy/MDMA (5.8% vs 1.1%) and four to five times as high for ketamine (3.6% vs 0.8%). The inequity was especially large for nitrites/poppers (3.6% vs 0.1%). For these less prevalent drug types, the number of sexual minority participants reporting use was small and confidence intervals were wide.

3.2 Trends in past-year drug use by sexual identity

Trends across the three surveys can be examined for adults aged 16 to 64 (Table 1 and Figure 2). In this age group, past-year drug use among heterosexual adults changed little over the period (10.3% in 2007, 9.9% in 2014, 11.6% in 2024). Among sexual minority adults it was also broadly stable: 22.9% (18.3, 27.5) in 2007, 27.5% (20.2, 34.8) in 2014, and 24.9% (18.1, 31.8) in 2024. At each time point, the rate in the sexual minority group was at least twice that in the heterosexual group, alongside non-overlapping confidence intervals there was no indication that this inequity narrowed or widened over time.

Trends by drug type were also broadly similar in the two groups (Table 1). Around one in five sexual minority adults used cannabis at each wave (18.0%, 21.2%, 22.0% in 2007, 2014 and 2024, respectively), as did around one in twelve heterosexual adults (8.4%, 8.2%, 9.9%), and cocaine use changed little in either group. Two exceptions were evident. First, use of nitrites (poppers) declined among heterosexual adults, from 0.7% (0.4, 0.9) in 2007 to 0.1% (0.0, 0.2) in 2024, while remaining between 3% and 6% among sexual minority adults, widening the relative difference between the groups. Second, ketamine use (first asked about in 2014) increased in both groups: from 0.5% to 1.1% among heterosexual adults, and from 2.0% to 3.8% among sexual minority adults, although the numbers of sexual minority participants reporting ketamine use were small and the confidence intervals wide.

3.3 Association between sexual identity and past-year drug use

In unadjusted models, sexual minority adults had higher odds of past-year illicit drug use than heterosexual adults in each survey year: 2007 (OR 2.6; 95% CI 2.0-3.5), 2014 (OR 3.5; 95% CI 2.4-5.1), and 2024 (OR 2.5; 95% CI 1.7-3.8) (Supplementary Table S4), consistent with the patterns observed in the descriptive analyses. These associations remained after adjustment for age, sex and area-level deprivation: 2007 (OR 2.4; 95% CI 1.7-3.2), 2014 (OR 3.2; 2.2-4.8), and 2024 (OR 2.2; 1.4-3.4) (Figure 3; Supplementary Table S4).

3.4 Trends in past-year drug use by sexual identity, stratified by sex

Among adults aged 16 and over, men were nearly twice as likely as women to have used drugs in the past year across all three surveys, with rates stable over time in both sexes (12.0%, 11.3% and 13.4% for men; 6.7%, 6.0% and 7.0% for women in 2007, 2014 and 2024, respectively) (Supplementary Table S5).

Trends by sexual identity, however, appeared to move in different directions for men and women (Figure 4; Table 2). Among heterosexual men aged 16 and over, past-year drug use was 11.2% (9.7, 12.7) in 2007 and 13.2% (11.0, 15.3) in 2024. Among sexual minority men, the point estimate fell from 23.1% (17.2, 29.0) in 2007 to 16.2% (7.8, 24.5) in 2024 (based on 115 participants). As a result, the difference between sexual minority and heterosexual men evident in 2007 was no longer evident in 2024, although it should be noted that the confidence interval around the 2024 estimate for sexual minority men was wide.

The pattern for women was the reverse. Drug use among heterosexual women was stable: 5.8% (5.0, 6.7) in 2007 and 5.2% (4.0, 6.4) in 2024, while among sexual minority women the point estimate appeared to rise from 18.0% (12.5, 23.5) in 2007 to 29.6% (19.3, 40.0) in 2024. This was largely accounted for by cannabis, reported by 27.4% (17.0, 37.7) of sexual minority women compared with 4.5% (3.4, 5.5) of heterosexual women; ecstasy/MDMA (5.5% vs 0.4%) and ketamine (6.1% vs 0.5%) use also contributed. 3.5 Trends in past-year drug use by sexual identity, stratified by age

Drug use varied by age in every survey year, but the shape of this association has changed (Table 3). Among heterosexual adults, past-year drug use in the youngest age group (16 to 24 years) fell from 23.6% (19.5, 27.6) in 2007 to 20.6% (16.6, 24.6) in 2014 and 15.0% (9.4, 20.6) in 2024. In contrast, drug use rose in midlife: between 2007 and 2024 it increased from 6.9% (5.5, 8.4) to 12.4% (9.7, 15.1) among those aged 35 to 44, tripled among those aged 45 to 54 (from 2.9% to 8.8%), and more than doubled among those aged 55 to 64 (from 1.8% to 4.6%).

The profile was different among sexual minority adults. Around a third of sexual minority individuals aged 16 to 24 years reported past-year drug use at every wave (31.2% in 2007, 34.9% in 2014, 34.1% in 2024), while drug use fell among young heterosexual adults (Table 3). In 2024 the rate in sexual minority 16 to 24 year olds (34.1%; 19.1, 49.2) was more than double that of their heterosexual peers (15.0%; 9.4, 20.6), whereas the two groups had been closer in 2007 (31.2% vs 23.6%). Drug use among sexual minority adults aged 45 to 54 also increased, from 10.6% (2.9, 18.3) in 2007 to 30.6% (14.5, 46.7) in 2024. However, a similar midlife rise was also observed in the heterosexual group. The divergence appears to be driven by changing use of cannabis (Table S6; Figure 5). Sexual minority estimates for individual age groups were based on fewer than 50 participants in most cells in 2014 and 2024 and should be interpreted with caution.

4. Discussion

Past-year drug use was at least twice as common among sexual minority adults as heterosexual adults at every point (2007, 2014, 2024), with no evidence that this inequity has narrowed. In 2024, around one in four (23.9%) sexual minority adults aged 16 and over reported illicit drug use in the past year, compared with about one in eleven (9.1%) heterosexual adults, and every drug type examined was used by a higher proportion of the sexual minority group. Beneath this overall stability, trends appeared to have moved in different directions for men and women. Among men, the difference observed in 2007 was no longer apparent by 2024. Among women the pattern was reversed: drug use among sexual minority women rose from 18.0% in 2007 to 29.6% in 2024, a rate more than five times that in heterosexual women, largely accounted for by cannabis. The age profile of drug use also appears to have shifted: drug use fell among young heterosexual adults and rose in midlife in both groups, while around a third of sexual minority 16 to 24 year olds reported past-year drug use at every wave.

These findings indicate that the higher levels of drug use previously observed among sexual minority people in England have persisted through a period of substantial legislative and attitudinal change. This mirrors the persistence of inequalities across other health outcomes despite improvements in legal rights and social acceptance, which may reflect continued exposure to stigma and discrimination related to sexual identity and other minority stressors (Khanolkar, 2024). While confidence intervals were wide, the observed results suggest that drug use may be rising among sexual minority women, with no comparable increase among sexual minority men. A possible divergence in trends by sex is consistent with US data suggesting narrowing inequities among males but persistence or widening among females (Paschen-Wolff et al., 2019), and with evidence that inequalities may be particularly pronounced for bisexual women (Schuler & Collins, 2020), although the samples here were too small to disaggregate sexual identity subgroups. Sexual victimisation has been proposed as one contributing pathway, given higher reported rates among sexual minority women, particularly bisexual women (Canan et al., 2021), and evidence of greater substance use to cope with such experiences among sexual minority women (López & Yeater, 2021). Poor mental health and substance use frequently co-occur among sexual minority populations, and minority stress theory frames drug use as a means of coping with stigma, discrimination and victimisation within heteronormative social contexts (Bränström & Pachankis, 2018; Meyer, 2003). While previous APMS analyses found that these inequities were attenuated after adjustment for reported bullying and amplified after adjustment for discrimination, remaining significant in both cases (Pitman et al., 2022), social and peer norms surrounding substance use, and living in heteronormative environments may represent additional pathways contributing to these inequalities (Mereish et al., 2017). The persistence of nitrites (poppers) use among sexual minority adults, and among sexual minority men in particular, while use declined to very low levels among heterosexual adults, is consistent with evidence that poppers are particularly used by gay and bisexual men in sexual contexts (Vaccher et al., 2020; Schwartz et al., 2020). This illustrates how patterns of use can be identity specific, with implications for the substances that harm reduction services should anticipate. With drugrelated deaths at record levels (ONS, 2025) and use rising in midlife, these results underline the need for drug treatment and wider health services to ask about and respond to drug use in sexual minority populations (Hillyard et al., 2026a), and among sexual minority women in particular (Hillyard et al., 2026b).

The finding that inequalities have persisted through a period in which formal legal equality was substantially extended has implications for how these inequalities are understood. If legislative change alone were sufficient, the inequities documented may be expected to have narrowed. That they have not is consistent with accounts locating drivers in everyday and structural stigma, which formal legal change addresses only indirectly. For population health policy this points away from framing sexual minority drug use as an individual behavioural risk, and towards responses that see it as a socially patterned inequality with structural determinants. The signs of diverging trends between men and women are also notable, and may reflect changing population norms around cannabis use, the growth and changing composition of the bisexual population among younger women, and different and changing exposures to minority stress.

Our study offers particular strengths over the existing evidence base. This study provides a heterosexual comparison group within a single sampling frame and mode, and spans the full adult age range from 16, including older adults who are excluded from the CSEW drug module and from many studies of sexual minority groups. This enabled analysis of trends in sexual identity inequities in drug use, and in use of specific drug types, in England over an extended period spanning major social change. Because APMS is a health survey -the government's key official source of data on mental health in England's adult populationdrug use was measured within a health-behaviour framing, and both drug use and sexual identity were collected by computer-assisted self-completion rather than face to face, which is likely to support better disclosure of sensitive information. Trend estimates drew on a derived variable restricted to the drug types asked about in every survey, so trends are not an artefact of the expanding drug list.

However, important limitations remain. The small size of key subgroups led to imprecise estimates and prevented investigation of sexual identity subgroups, adjustment for a wider range of potential confounders, and examination of intersections between age, sex, ethnicity and sexual identity. The repeated cross-sectional design is ideal for examining change at the population level but is not suitable for causal questions.

Differences were assessed by inspecting the overlap of 95% confidence intervals, a conservative approach, and formal tests of trend or of interactions (for example sex by identity by survey year) were not conducted due to the survey years being separate datasets. Drug use was self-reported, so under-reporting is possible, although the self-completion mode and health survey framing may mitigate this. The self-completion was not completed by all participants, accounting for the substantial proportion not providing a sexual identity response, increasing overall missing data and introducing potential non-response bias. Willingness to report both illicit drug use and a non-heterosexual sexual identity may also vary with their perceived social acceptability and may have changed over time (Harrison, 1995). The sexual identity question also changed between waves: participants in 2007 were randomised to one of two question versions while 2014 and 2024 used the ONS harmonised standard, and reporting of minority identity is sensitive to question wording (Hayes et al., 2012), so trends by sexual identity could partly reflect measurement change as well as changing willingness to disclose. The proportion and composition of adults identifying with a sexual minority status may itself have shifted, so within-group trends may reflect changing membership of the group as well as changing behaviour. Because sexual identity was only established for 16 to 64 year olds in 2014, all-age comparisons rest on two time points. Sex was binary coded as this is the classification available across all three surveys, and gender identity was asked only in 2024, so trans and non-binary participants could not be identified consistently. Finally, as a household survey, key groups such as people who are in prison or homeless were out of scope; these are groups likely to experience higher than average rates of drug use (Bebbington et al., 2021; Khanolkar et al., 2026).

5. Conclusion

Drawing on three national probability-sample health surveys spanning 17 years, this study shows that sexual minority adults in England remain more than twice as likely as heterosexual adults to have used drugs in the past year, and that this inequity is now particularly evident among sexual minority women and young people.

Efforts to reduce these inequalities should consider the social and structural factors that may contribute to elevated drug use among sexual minority people, including social norms around drug use and discrimination.

Drug prevention and treatment services should also be inclusive and responsive to the needs of sexual minority populations. Routine drug use statistics should collect and publish estimates by sexual identity, which the CSEW last did for 2011 to 2014, and future surveys should be designed with samples large enough to disaggregate bisexual, gay and lesbian, and other identities, and their intersections with age and sex.
