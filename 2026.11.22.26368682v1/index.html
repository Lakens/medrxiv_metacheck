Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

How Replication Check works

This module checks references and warns for citations of original studies for which replication or reproduction studies exist in the FLoRA database.

The Replication Check module compares the reference list against studies in the FLoRA (FORRT Library of Replication Attempts) database based on the DOI. Abstract

Advances in ultrasensitive proteomics offer opportunities to characterize molecular pathways underlying brain aging. We measured 220 plasma proteins using NULISAseq Neuro220 in 250 Chinese, South Asian, and non-Hispanic White older adults (mean age=68.2 years; 70.4% female). Proteins implicated in neurodegenerative diseases (including brain-derived phosphorylated Tau; BDpTau), synaptic function, oxidative stress, and inflammation were associated with poorer cognition. Phosphorylated Tau205 was associated with reduced entorhinal thickness, whereas inflammatory proteins were associated with imaging markers of cerebral small vessel disease.

Several findings were replicated in 1,381 participants in the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative, including associations of BDpTau species, NPTX2, BACE1, TREM1, CCL17, PRDX6, and IGF1R with cognitive decline and clinical progression.

Cardiometabolic risk factors were associated with widespread proteomic alterations, many of which were also linked to poorer brain health. These results identify promising molecular markers of brain aging and extend proteomic research to ethnically diverse populations historically underrepresented in Alzheimer's disease research.

1. INTRODUCTION

Cognitive and brain aging are shaped by multiple, co-occurring biological processes, including neurodegenerative pathology, cerebrovascular dysfunction, inflammation, metabolic dysregulation, and synaptic loss. [1] [2] [3] [4] Identifying the molecular signatures of these processes may help identify individuals at increased risk of cognitive impairment and reveal potential targets for intervention. [5] [6] [7] [8] The plasma proteome offers a minimally invasive means of investigating these processes. 9 Despite major advances in blood biomarkers for Alzheimer's disease (AD), 10, 11 our understanding of the broader plasma proteomic signatures associated with cognitive and brain aging remains limited, particularly in ethnically diverse populations.

Recent advances in high-sensitivity multiplex proteomics now enable the simultaneous quantification of hundreds of circulating proteins relevant to brain aging and neurological disease. 12 Studies using the NUcleic acid Linked Immuno-Sandwich Assay (NULISAseq) CNS 120 platform have identified molecular signatures associated with cognition, neuroimaging measures, and AD pathology, spanning inflammatory, vascular, synaptic, and neurodegenerative pathways. [13] [14] [15] [16] The expanded NULISAseq Neuro 220 panel includes approximately 100 additional brain-relevant proteins, including phosphorylated tau species beyond pTau217 (e.g., pTau205 and pTau212 17- 21 ), brain-derived pTau species, and additional inflammatory and synaptic targets.

Whether these expanded targets provide further insight into cognitive and brain aging remains largely unexplored.

Another important limitation of dementia biomarker research is the underrepresentation of ethnically diverse populations. This raises questions about whether previously identified proteomic associations generalize across populations. 22, 23 This is particularly relevant given population differences in cardiometabolic health, including hypertension and diabetes, which may influence circulating protein profiles and contribute to cognitive impairment and cerebrovascular disease. [24] [25] [26] Social and structural determinants of health may further affect these relationships. 27 Examining whether cardiometabolic risk factors and brain aging share circulating proteomic signatures may provide insight into the biological processes connecting systemic and . brain health. However, these relationships remain poorly characterized in ethnically diverse aging populations.

Here, we used the NULISAseq Neuro 220 panel to characterize circulating proteomic signatures of cognitive, brain, and cardiometabolic health in the Canadian Multi-Ethnic Research on Aging (CAMERA) study. This deeply phenotyped cohort includes adults aged 55 to 85, primarily from Chinese, South Asian, and non-Hispanic White backgrounds. Our primary objective was to characterize these associations across the full cohort, extending the investigation of plasma proteomic signatures of brain aging to populations historically underrepresented in dementia biomarker research. We identified proteins associated with cognitive performance and MRI markers of neurodegeneration and cerebral small vessel disease, spanning processes related to AD pathobiology, synaptic function, inflammation, and oxidative stress.

Several proteomic associations were replicated in the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI), an independent observational cohort with substantially different demographic and clinical characteristics. We further identified overlapping proteomic signatures of cardiometabolic risk and adverse cognitive and neuroimaging outcomes, particularly involving inflammatory proteins. Together, these findings broaden our understanding of the circulating proteome of cognitive and brain aging and identify biological processes shared with cardiometabolic health >RESULTS

2.1. Participant characteristics

We included cross-sectional data from 250 adults in the CAMERA study. 23 Participants included 82 Chinese, 74 South Asian, 83 non-Hispanic White, and 11 individuals of other ethnicities (Table 1; Figure 1A). The mean (SD) age was 68.2 (6.78) years and 29.6 % (N=74) were male. Age, sex, and education did not differ significantly across ethnic groups (Figs. 1B-1C). Fifty-nine participants (23.6%) were APOE ε4 carriers, with a higher prevalence among non-Hispanic White than Chinese participants (33.3% vs. 14.5%; Fig 1D). Most participants were cognitively unimpaired (Clinical Dementia Rating [CDR] global score = 0), with only six participants (2.4%) scoring 0.5.

2.2. Age, sex, and APOE ε4 associations with plasma proteomic profiles

Given the established roles of age, sex, and APOE ε4 carriage in brain aging and dementia, 3, 28, 29 we first examined their associations with 200 NULISAseq proteins and three protein ratios reflecting AD-related amyloid and tau pathology (i.e., pTau217/Aβ42, pTau217/MAPT [pTau217%], brain derived [BD] pTau217/MAPT [BDpTau217%]). 20 In multivariable linear models including terms for age, sex and ethnicity, age was positively associated with 67 proteins (50 FDR, 17 nominal) and negatively associated with 4 proteins (2 FDR-significant, 2 nominally significant; Table S1; Figure 2A). The strongest positive associations included proteins linked to neurodegenerative disease and neurodegeneration (NEFL, NEFH, BDpTau212), biological aging (GDF15, Figure 2B), inflammation (GFAP, TREM1, TREM2 CHI3L1), and cardiac stress (NTproBNP, Figure 2C). By contrast, extracellular matrix glycoprotein EFEMP and growth factor receptor EFGR were negatively associated with age.

In the same models, 23 proteins were higher in men (12 FDR, 11 nominal) and 39 were higher in women (26 FDR, 13 nominal; Table S1; Figure 2D). Top proteins elevated in men included the alpha-globin subunit of hemoglobin HBA1 (Figure 2E) and the synaptic and partially testes-secreted protein NPTX2, whereas those elevated in women included the synaptic protein NPTX1, cardiac stress marker NTproBNP, and chemokine CCL22 (Figure 2F).

In models adjusted for age, sex, and ethnicity, APOE ε4 carriers exhibited higher levels of 16 proteins (11 FDR, 5 nominal; Table S2; Figure 2G), including phosphorylated tau species BDpTau231 (Figure 2H), BDpTau217 (Figure 2I), pTau217, and pTau212, as well as the synaptic marker GAP43 and BACE1, which is involved in β-amyloid precursor protein processing. Conversely, APOE ε4 carriers exhibited nominally lower levels of Aβ42 (reflecting less favorable amyloid levels), APOE, and the metabolic protein FAM3.

Given evidence that associations of APOE ε4 with AD risk and biomarkers may differ across ancestral populations, 30 we conducted exploratory analyses stratified by self-reported ethnicity (South Asian, Chinese, and non-Hispanic White; Table S2; Extended data Figure 1). APOE ε4 carriers generally had higher levels of AD-related . 6 proteins across all three groups, including several p-tau species, BACE1, and GAP43.

Effect estimates for pTau species tended to be larger among non-Hispanic White participants, whereas associations with BACE1 and GAP43 were more similar across groups. Exploratory analyses testing the interaction between ethnicity and APOE ε4 carriage showed nominally significant interactions on several p-tau species (i.e., BDpTau181, pTau181, pTau212, among others), with stronger associations in White than South Asian and Chinese participants. By contrast, associations of APOE ε4 with p-tau levels did not significantly differ between South Asian and Chinese individuals (Table S2; Extended data Figure 1).

2.3. Plasma proteins associated with cognition

We next examined associations of circulating proteins with cognitive performance and three MRI-derived measures of brain aging (Figure 3A). Cognition was assessed using a composite adapted from the Preclinical Alzheimer Cognitive Composite (PACC), 31 comprising predominantly nonverbal tests to reduce language-related influences across ethnic groups. In multivariable linear models adjusted for age, sex, ethnicity, years of education, and English as a first language, 58 proteins were negatively associated with cognition (15 FDR, 43 nominal; Table S3; Figure 3B). The strongest associations with poorer cognitive performance included oxidative stressrelated protein PRDX6, SNARE protein STX4, NPTX2, and several phosphorylated tau species including BDpTau181 and pTau217 (Figure 3C).

2.4. Plasma proteins associated with entorhinal thickness

We next examined whether circulating proteins were associated with entorhinal cortical thickness, a region particularly vulnerable to aging and early AD-related neurodegeneration. 32 Among 233 CAMERA participants with available brain MRI, 12 proteins were associated with lower entorhinal thickness (1 FDR, 11 nominal; Table S3; Figure 3D), adjusting for age, sex, and ethnicity. The strongest association was observed for pTau205 (Figure 3E), an emerging marker of tau neurofibrillary tangle pathology. 33, 34 Other notable nominally significant proteins included BDpTau231 and proteins implicated in aging and inflammation, including CCL11, SLIT2, and SFTPD. To . assess the regional specificity of these findings, we also examined cortical thickness in the precentral gyrus, a brain region typically spared in aging and early AD. 32 No proteins survived FDR correction, although 13 showed nominal associations (6 positive and 7 negative, Table S3; Supplementary Figure S1). Notably, no pTau species associated with precentral gyrus thickness. NGF was the only protein nominally associated with thinner cortex in both regions.

2.5. Proteins associated with cerebrovascular disease markers

We next examined associations between circulating proteins and two established MRI markers of small vessel disease: white matter hyperintensity (WMH) volume and volume of MRI-visible perivascular spaces in the basal ganglia (BG-PVS) quantified using specialized deep learning models. Models were adjusted for age, sex, ethnicity, and intracranial volume. A total of 27 proteins were positively associated with WMH volume (1 FDR, 26 nominal; Table S3; Figure 3F), with inflammatory proteins prominently represented. TNF showed the strongest association and was the only protein to survive FDR correction (Figure 3G). Other nominally significant inflammatory proteins included CXCL13, IL6R, LGALS3, and ICAM1. By contrast, higher levels of synaptic protein NPTXR were nominally associated with lower WMH burden.

No proteins were associated with BG-PVS at the FDR threshold, although 7 proteins showed nominally significant positive associations (Table S3; Figure 3H). Proinflammatory cytokine CXCL10 exhibited the strongest association (Figure 3I), with additional associations involving proteins implicated in inflammation (S100A12, PAFAH1B3) and metabolic or oxidative stress-related processes (HMOX1, HBA1, PRDX6, FAM3B).

2.6. Convergent proteomic associations across cognitive and brain measures

To identify proteins associated with multiple aspects of brain aging, we compared associations across all four cognitive and brain MRI markers (Figure 3J). A heatmap of the top 30 proteins, ranked by the number of nominally significant associations and effect sizes, revealed several proteins associated with multiple outcomes. Notably, higher pTau205 was associated with poorer cognition, reduced entorhinal thickness, and greater WMH burden. Higher PAFAH1B3 (involved in synaptic signaling and neurodevelopmental processes) was associated with poorer cognition, greater WMH burden, and greater PVS volume, while higher PRDX6 was associated with poorer cognition and greater PVS volume. Higher NPTX2 was associated with poorer cognition and reduced entorhinal thickness. An exploratory network analysis based on pairwise protein correlations at a threshold of r=0.35 identified three broad clusters (Extended data Figure 2). One cluster was enriched for AD proteins (including multiple pTau species); a second for predominantly cytokine and chemokine cluster, and a third heterogeneous cluster including inflammatory and cellular stress-related proteins.

Finally, we examined whether plasma proteins explained additional variance in cognition beyond demographic, neuroimaging, and genetic factors. Among the 58 nominally associated with cognition, the 25 proteins with the greatest incremental explanatory power individually explained an additional 2.0-3.6% of variance (Table S4, Extended data Figure 3). When included simultaneously, these 25 proteins collectively explained an additional 14.3% of variance compared with 0.9% for brain MRI markers, 1.0% for APOE ε4 carriage, 25.9% for demographic factors. These exploratory findings suggest that circulating proteins may capture aspects of cognitive variation not accounted for by conventional demographic and neuroimaging measures.

2.6. Plasma protein associations with cognition and brain outcomes are supported in an independent cohort

To evaluate the reproducibility of proteins identified in CAMERA, we analyzed data from 1,381 participants in the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI; mean [SD] age = 72.7 [7.24], 726 [52.6%] male; 658 [47.7%] mild cognitive impairment; 213 [15.4%] dementia; 1,311 [94.9%] White; Table S5; Figure 4A). Baseline plasma samples were assayed for 131 proteins on the NULISAseq CNS 120 panel. After quality control (see Methods), 116 proteins and two protein ratios (pTau217/Aβ42; pTau217/MAPT [pTau217%] overlapped with those measured in CAMERA. We examined longitudinal change in two cognitive outcomes: the modified PACC (mPACC) 31 and CDR sum of boxes (CDR-SB) over a mean follow-up of 4.8 (3.5) years.

We also examined two cross-sectional neuroimaging outcomes: cortical thickness and . WMH burden. Given the higher prevalence of cognitive impairment in ADNI, we focused on an AD signature region reflecting both early and more advanced AD-related neurodegeneration. Entorhinal cortical thickness was examined as a secondary outcome.

For longitudinal cognitive outcomes, in linear mixed-effects models adjusted for age, sex, and education, 36 proteins (27 FDR, 9 nominal) were associated with faster mPACC decline, including several brain-derived pTau forms: BDpTau217, BDpTau181, and BDpTau231 (Table S6; Figure 4B). Conversely, four proteins (2 FDR, 2 nominal) were associated with slower mPACC decline, including Aβ42 and NPTXR. A similar pattern emerged for CDR-SB, with 59 proteins (42 FDR, 17 nominal) associated with faster progression (Table S6; Figure 4C) including several brain-derived pTau isoforms.

By contrast, higher Aβ42 and CRP, were associated with slower CDR-SB progression (both FDR-significant).

For cross-sectional neuroimaging measures, linear models adjusted for age, sex, imaging site, and time between blood collection and MRI identified 35 proteins (26 FDR, 9 nominal) associated with reduced AD-signature cortical thickness, including pTau isoforms, GFAP, NEFL, ACHE, MAPT, and CHI3L1. In contrast, 3 proteins (1 FDR, 2 nominal) were associated with greater cortical thickness, with NPTXR surviving FDR correction (Table S6; Figure 4D). Analyses of entorhinal cortical thickness yielded similar findings (Table S6, Supplementary Figure S2). Thirty-nine proteins (22 FDR, 17 nominal) were associated with greater WMH volume (log-transformed and adjusted for intracranial volume), including BDpTau231, TREM2, VEGFA, and SQSTM1 (Table S6; Figure 4E). By contrast, 2 proteins, NPTX1 and NPTXR (both FDR), were associated with lower WMH burden.

To assess the consistency of protein associations across cohorts, we compared protein effect estimates for cognitive and neuroimaging outcomes in CAMERA and ADNI. Correlation analyses revealed effect size concordance (r = 0.21 -0.44, Extended data Figure 4). A heatmap highlighting the 30 proteins with the most consistent associations across outcomes and cohorts (Figure 4F) identified several recurring proteins, including BDpTau231 (Extended data Figure 5), NPTX2 (Extended data Figure 6), BDpTau181, BDpTau217, BACE1, TREM1, ENO2, PRDX6, CCL17, and IGF1R.

2.7. Cardiometabolic risk factors share proteomic signatures with cognitive and brain aging

Given the established contribution of cardiometabolic risk factors to AD and related dementias, 35, 36 their influence on the circulating proteome, 27, 37, 38 and their potential contribution to ethnic group differences in dementia risk, 39, 40 we examined whether cardiometabolic risk and cognitive and brain aging share common proteomic signatures in CAMERA. Identifying these shared signatures may provide insight into the molecular pathways linking cardiometabolic health to cognitive and brain aging. We examined six cardiometabolic factors that together reflect adiposity (i.e., BMI), vascular health (i.e., systolic blood pressure), lipid profiles (i.e., triglycerides, HDL and LDL cholesterol), and glycemic regulation (i.e., HbA1c). Separate linear models adjusted for age, sex, and ethnicity examined associations between each cardiometabolic measure and circulating protein levels.

Cardiometabolic measures showed widespread associations with the circulating proteome, particularly those involved in inflammation and metabolism (Table S7). BMI was associated with 67 proteins (50 FDR, 17 nominal), including positive associations with inflammatory proteins (TNFSF14, CRP, and CCL22), and negative associations with AD biomarkers, including BACE1 and BDpTau217% (Figure 5A). HbA1c was associated with 69 proteins (20 FDR, 49 nominal), including positive associations with the aging marker GDF15 and metabolic proteins NPY and MDH1 (Figure 5B). HDL cholesterol was associated with 65 proteins (40 FDR, 25 nominal). Lower levels of HDL were associated with higher levels of lipid-related proteins such as APOA2 and LDLR and higher levels of inflammatory markers TNFSF14, TNF, CXCL13 (Figure 5C).

Triglycerides showed the most extensive associations (88 total, 66 FDR, 22 nominal), including positive associations with LDLR, APOE and several inflammatory proteins, and a negative association with HDL component protein APOA1 (Figure 5D).

Associations with systolic blood pressure were comparatively weaker, with no proteins surviving FDR correction, although 42 proteins showed nominal associations including immune targets S100A12, CCL22, CRP and CHI3L1 (Figure 5E). Finally, LDL cholesterol was associated with 39 proteins (8 FDR, 31 nominal), with higher LDL associated with higher levels of APOE, cell adhesion protein ITGAV, and inflammatory protein TNFSF14. Counterintuitively, lower LDL was associated with higher levels of GDF15, BDpTau181, and BDpTau231 (Figure 5F).

To identify shared proteomic patterns across cardiometabolic measures, we examined the 30 proteins showing the most consistent associations (Figure 5G). These proteins predominantly involved immune and inflammatory processes, including CRP, TNF, TNFSF14, CCL22, CCL18, MMP9, and IL6. Many showed positive associations with BMI, HbA1c, and triglycerides, and negative associations with HDL cholesterol, consistent with a common inflammatory signature of adverse cardiometabolic health.

Several brain-relevant proteins, including TREM1, NTRK3, BACE1, and SAA1, were among the top 30, suggesting shared biological pathways underlying cardiometabolic and brain health.

We next examined whether proteins associated with cardiometabolic risk overlapped with those associated with cognition and MRI measures of brain aging in CAMERA. To assess this overlap, we correlated protein effect estimates from the cardiometabolic and cognitive/neuroimaging models (Figure 5H). Proteins associated with greater WMH burden tended to show associations with cardiometabolic measures in directions consistent with greater cardiometabolic risk, except LDL cholesterol.

Similarly, proteins associated with greater BG-PVS volume showed concordant positive associations with higher HbA1c and systolic BP, whereas proteins associated with better cognitive performance tended to be associated with lower HbA1c and lower systolic BP. However, not all patterns followed expected risk directions. Protein effect estimates for entorhinal cortical thickness were positively correlated with those for systolic blood pressure, while estimates for LDL cholesterol were positively correlated with better cognition and negatively correlated with BG-PVS volume.

At the individual protein level, several proteins showed overlapping associations with cardiometabolic and brain aging outcomes. Higher pTau205, TREM1, CCL11, and BDpTau231 were associated with both higher HbA1c and poorer cognition (Figure 5I), whereas higher TNF, TREM2, AGRN, and CRP were associated with both higher triglycerides and greater WMH burden (Figure 5J). Together, these findings highlight shared protein signatures of cardiometabolic risk and adverse cognitive and neuroimaging outcomes, with inflammatory pathways prominently represented.

2.8. Sensitivity analyses

In CAMERA, associations between plasma proteins with cognitive, brain and cardiometabolic measures remained largely unchanged after additional adjustment for APOE ε4 carrier status (Table S8; Supplementary Figures S3 and S4). Associations with cardiometabolic factors were also largely unchanged after adjustment for relevant medication use (i.e., lipid-modifying medications, glucose-lowering medications, blood pressure-lowering medications; Table S9; Supplementary Figure S5) and BMI (Table S10; Supplementary Figure S6). In ADNI, excluding participants with dementia at baseline (N=213) yielded results largely consistent with the primary analyses for cognitive and neuroimaging outcomes (Table S11, Supplementary Figure S7).

3. DISCUSSION

In an ethnically diverse cohort with substantial representation of Chinese and South Asian adults, populations historically underrepresented in aging and AD biomarker research, we used the expanded NULISAseq 220 panel to characterize circulating proteomic signatures of cognitive performance, cortical thickness, cerebral small vessel disease, and cardiometabolic health. We identified established and emerging markers of AD pathology, synaptic function, inflammation, and oxidative stress, including brain-derived p-tau species, pTau205 and pTau212, [17] [18] [19] [20] [21] plus targets such as BACE1, NPTX2, TREM1, PAFAH1B3, and PRDX6. Several proteins showed associations across multiple cognitive and neuroimaging outcomes, with findings also supported in an independent cohort with substantially different demographic and clinical characteristics. We further identified considerable overlap between proteomic signatures of poorer cognitive performance, greater cerebral small vessel disease burden and cardiometabolic risk, particularly involving inflammatory proteins Together, these findings expand our understanding of circulating proteomic signatures of cognitive and brain aging to ethnically diverse populations and identify biological processes shared with cardiometabolic health.

Using the expanded NULISAseq Neuro 220 panel, we identified widespread associations of circulating proteins with age, sex, and APOE ε4 status, extending previously reported and emerging proteomic patterns to a broader range of brainrelevant proteins in an ethnically diverse cohort. [41] [42] [43] Older age was associated with higher levels of proteins implicated in neurodegenerative disease and neurodegeneration (pTau, GFAP, NEFL, NEFH), inflammation (TREM1, TREM2, CHI3L1), cardiac stress (NTproBNP), and systemic aging (GDF15). 2 We also observed sex differences in several brain-relevant proteins, including the synaptic proteins NPTX1 and NPTX2, which were higher in women and men, respectively. A similar pattern was reported by Trelle and colleagues in cohort of cognitively unimpaired older adults. 13 APOE ε4 carriers had higher levels of several AD-related proteins, including brain derived pTau species, the amyloid processing protein BACE1, and the synaptic protein GAP43. Exploratory analyses suggested that the magnitude of APOE ε4-related differences in some pTau species may vary across ethnic groups, with generally stronger associations among non-Hispanic White than among Chinese and South Asian participants. In contrast, APOE ε4 associations with BACE1 and GAP43 appeared more similar across ethnic groups. These findings are consistent with a recent study reporting smaller APOE ε4-associated increases in plasma pTau217% among Asian compared to White older adults. 44 Although limited by small subgroup sizes and exploratory interaction analyses, our findings raise the possibility that APOE ε4-related proteomic signatures differ across populations. Larger studies are needed to establish whether these differences are reproducible and relevant to the interpretation of blood-based AD biomarkers.

Several circulating proteins were associated with poorer cognitive performance, including established AD biomarkers as well as proteins involved in synaptic function and oxidative stress. Associations involving pTau217 and brain-derived pTau species are consistent with previous studies linking circulating tau biomarkers to cognitive impairment and decline. 45, 46 Beyond these AD markers, we identified associations involving NPTX2, 47 STX4, 48 and PRDX6. 49 Notably, higher plasma NPTX2 was associated with poorer cognition, contrasting with previous studies linking lower CSF NPTX2 to faster cognitive decline and progression to MCI. 50, 51 Since CSF and plasma . levels of NPTX2 do not correlate, 52 associations of peripheral NPTX2 with cognitive outcomes may partly reflect processes linked to non-central nervous system expression of NPTX2 in tissues such as the pancreas, adrenal glands, and testes (in men). Higher plasma STX4, a protein involved in vesicular trafficking, was also associated with poorer cognition. PRDX6, an antioxidant enzyme involved in cellular membrane homeostasis, has been implicated in AD dementia, 53 with increased expression reported in experimental models of AD 49 and in hippocampal tissue from individuals with AD. 54, 55 Our findings extend these observations to plasma, identifying PRDX6 as a correlate of cognitive performance in a predominantly cognitively unimpaired, ethnically diverse cohort.

Among the neuroimaging outcomes, plasma pTau205 showed the strongest association with lower entorhinal cortical thickness and was the only protein to survive FDR correction. The entorhinal cortex is among the earliest regions affected by AD, 56, 57 but is also vulnerable to age-related tauopathy. 58 CSF studies suggest that pTau205 becomes abnormal later in the AD continuum than other phosphorylated tau species and may more closely reflect neurofibrillary tangle pathology. 21, 33, 34 Our findings extend this work to plasma, identifying an association between pTau205 and entorhinal thinning in a predominantly cognitively unimpaired cohort, although whether this reflects ADrelated or age-related tau pathology remains unclear.

In contrast, proteomic associations with cerebral small vessel disease were characterized predominantly by inflammatory proteins. TNF showed the strongest association with WMH burden and was the only protein to survive FDR correction. Other nominal associations included proteins involved in immune signaling (CXCL13 and IL6R), vascular inflammation (ICAM1), and inflammatory and tissue-remodeling processes (LGALS3). 59 These findings are consistent with evidence implicating inflammatory signaling, endothelial dysfunction, and immune activation in cerebral small vessel disease. 60, 61 Associations with MRI-visible basal ganglia PVS were weaker, with no proteins surviving correction for multiple comparisons, although nominal associations involving inflammatory and oxidative stress-related proteins were observed. These findings may reflect processes involved in perivascular and vascular homeostasis, 62 although the evidence remains preliminary. The different proteomic patterns associated with entorhinal thickness, WMH, and PVS suggest that these measures capture partly distinct aspects of structural brain aging. However, the limited number of associations surviving multiple-comparison correction warrants caution.

An important finding was that several proteins were associated with more than one cognitive or neuroimaging outcome, including pTau205, NPTX2, PRDX6, and PAFAH1B3, a protein involved in neurodevelopmental and cellular signaling. 63 These findings suggest that some circulating proteins may reflect biological processes shared across different aspects of brain aging. We also explored whether plasma proteins provided information about cognitive performance beyond demographic characteristics, APOE ε4 status, and neuroimaging measures. In exploratory analyses, 25 selected proteins collectively explained an additional 14.3% of the variance in cognition.

However, because these proteins were selected and evaluated in the same cohort, the estimate may be inflated and requires independent validation. Analyses in ADNI using the NULISAseq CNS 120 panel provided independent support for several of the protein associations identified in CAMERA, despite substantial differences in ethnic composition, cognitive status, and outcome measures. Consistent patterns were observed for established AD-related proteins, including brain-derived pTau species and pTau217, as well as proteins involved in synaptic function, inflammation, and cellular processes, including NPTX2, TREM1, CCL17, PRDX6, and IGF1R. Notably, higher plasma NPTX2 was associated with poorer cognitive performance in CAMERA and worse longitudinal cognitive outcomes in ADNI, providing further support for the direction of the plasma association despite its contrast with previous CSF findings. Although correlations between protein effect estimates across cohorts were moderate, differences in cohort characteristics and outcome measures may have contributed to this variability. The consistency of several individual associations is nevertheless notable given these differences. Comparisons were limited to proteins shared between the CNS 120 and Neuro 220 panels, leaving additional targets, particularly pTau205, without independent validation. Beyond cognitive and neuroimaging measures, we identified substantial overlap between signatures of cardiometabolic risk and brain aging. Proteins associated with greater WMH burden generally showed associations with poorer cardiometabolic health . whereas those associated with worse cognition tended to be associated with higher HbA1c and systolic blood pressure. At the individual protein level, higher pTau205, TREM1, CCL11, and BDpTau231 were associated with both higher HbA1c and poorer cognitive performance, whereas higher TNF, TREM2, AGRN, and CRP were associated with higher triglycerides and greater WMH burden. The prominence of inflammatory proteins across these associations is consistent with growing evidence linking cardiometabolic dysfunction and inflammation to neurodegenerative and cerebrovascular disease. 64, 65 Higher BMI was associated with lower levels of plasma BACE1 and BDpTau217%. The latter finding is consistent with previous reports of inverse associations between later-life BMI and AD pathology or progression. 66, 67 Such associations may reflect reverse causation, whereby weight loss accompanies preclinical neurodegeneration, rather than a protective effect of higher adiposity. 68 This study has several strengths, including the use of the expanded NULISAseq Neuro 220 panel in a deeply phenotyped, ethnically diverse cohort with detailed neuropsychological, neuroimaging, and cardiometabolic assessments. Importantly, the substantial representation of Chinese and South Asian participants extends plasma proteomic research to populations historically underrepresented in dementia biomarker studies. Independent analyses in ADNI further allowed us to assess the consistency of several protein associations in a cohort with different demographic and clinical characteristics.

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, although CAMERA is an ongoing longitudinal study, the present analyses were restricted to baseline data, precluding assessment of whether the identified proteomic signatures predict subsequent cognitive decline, atrophy, or cerebral small vessel disease progression. Second, although CAMERA included substantial representation of Chinese and South Asian participants, the sample size limited statistical power to robustly investigate ethnic differences in proteomic associations. Third, without direct measures of cerebral amyloid and tau pathology, we cannot determine whether associations involving AD-related proteins reflect underlying AD pathology. Fourth, circulating proteins may originate from multiple tissue sources and their plasma concentrations do not necessarily reflect changes within the brain. Finally, external validation was restricted to 118 proteins and ratios shared between CAMERA and ADNI, leaving several novel targets without independent validation. Longitudinal follow-up in CAMERA and replication in larger, ethnically diverse cohorts will be important for establishing the reproducibility and clinical relevance of these findings.

In conclusion, application of the expanded NULISAseq Neuro 220 platform identified novel circulating proteomic signatures of cognitive and brain aging in an ethnically diverse cohort with substantial representation of Chinese and South Asian adults. Beyond established AD biomarkers, we identified proteins associated with cognitive performance and structural brain changes, spanning synaptic function, oxidative stress, and inflammation, with several associations independently supported in ADNI. The substantial overlap between brain-related and cardiometabolic proteomic signatures further highlights shared inflammatory and metabolic processes associated with brain aging. Together, these findings expand our understanding of the circulating proteome in brain aging and underscore the potential of multiplex plasma proteomics to advance biomarker discovery and more inclusive approaches to dementia risk assessment.

4. METHODS

>METHODS

4.1 The CAMERA study

The Canadian Multi-Ethnic Research on Aging (CAMERA) study is a Torontobased cohort comprising adults aged 55-85 who self-identify as South Asian, Chinese, or non-Hispanic White. The study design eligibility criteria have been described previously. 23 CAMERA recruited community-dwelling adults with the aim of enrolling cognitively unimpaired individuals (Clinical Dementia Rating [CDR] global score of 0); however, participants subsequently found to have a CDR global score of 0.5 following enrollment were retained. Exclusion criteria included major neurological or psychiatric disorders, unstable medical conditions, and insufficient English proficiency to provide informed consent and complete study assessments. Participants underwent blood collection, neuropsychological and clinical assessments, and a brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scan. The study was approved by the Research Ethics Board of Sunnybrook Health Sciences Centre, and all participants provided written informed consent.

4.2 Plasma NULISAseq proteomics

Plasma was collected following an overnight fast into ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) tubes and stored at -80°C until analyses. Plasma proteomics were quantified using the NULISAseq Neuro 220 Panel (Alamar Biosciences, Fremont, California, USA), which measures 220 proteins relevant to brain health and neurological disease. [13] [14] [15] [16] Samples were thawed and centrifuged at 2,200g for 10 minutes. Using the automated Alamar Argo HT platform, immunocomplexes were generated and labeled with sample-and target-specific DNA barcodes, pooled into a single library and quantified by next-generation DNA sequencing. Samples were randomized across plates, stratified by ethnicity, and analyzed in singlicate, according to previously established NULISA procedures. 15, 16 All quality control (QC) metrics met predefined thresholds, including median intraplate internal control coefficient of variation (CV) and median inter-plate control CV of 5.94% (threshold of 10%) and 6.99% (threshold of 15%), respectively. In CAMERA, 11 . proteins (Aβ38, CALB2, CGRP, FCGR2B, GSDMD, PARP1, pLRRK2. S1292, PTN, SNCB, UCHL1, and YWHAZ) with median detectability below 50% were excluded, consistent with previous NULISAseq studies. 16, 69 Seven proteins associated with rare genetic disorders were also excluded (pQ.HTT, mHTT.exon1, pPRKN.S65, pQ.ATXN3, pRAB10. T73, pRAB12. S106, pRAB29. T71).

APOE4 and CHIT1 were excluded from continuous protein association analyses due to their bimodal distributions. However, APOE4 was used to infer APOE ε4 allele carrier status. To minimize the influence of extreme values, protein measurements were winsorized at three times the interquartile range. We used the weighted gene coexpression network analysis (WGCNA) sample network connectivity outlier algorithm to identify sample outliers (defined as >3 SD below mean sample connectivity), resulting in the removal of two samples from the analytic proteomics dataset. 70, 71 Following these exclusions, analyses included 200 individual NULISAseq proteins and three protein ratios reflecting AD pathology (pTau217/AB42, pTau217%, and BDpTau217%), calculated as the difference between log-transformed NULISA Protein Quantification (NPQ) values, as previously described. 20 To facilitate interpretation and effect size comparison, all 203 proteomic measures were ztransformed prior to analysis.

4.3 APOE ε4 carrier status

In CAMERA, APOE4 protein levels were used to infer APOE ε4 carrier status, with participants classified as carriers (APOE4 > 4 NPQ) or noncarriers (APOE4 ≤ 4 NPQ; Supplementary Figure S8).

4.4 Cognitive assessments

CAMERA participants completed a battery of cognitive tests assessing attention, executive function, processing speed, episodic memory, language, and visuospatial abilities. Cognitive performance was quantified using a composite informed by the Preclinical Alzheimer Cognitive Composite (PACC), 31 emphasizing measures sensitive to early AD-related cognitive changes 72, 73 and incorporating predominantly nonverbal tasks to minimize language-related influences across ethnic groups. The composite included the Free and Cued Selective Reminding Test total score (FCSRT96; sum of free and cued recall across three learning trials), 74 the Doors Subtest total score (Part A and B) from the Doors and People Test, 75 category fluency (Animals), 76 the Digit Symbol Coding subtest (WAIS-III), 77 and the Mnemonic Similarity Test Set C (lure discrimination index, LDI). 78 Scores on each test were z-transformed and averaged to compute the composite, with higher scores reflecting better performance.

Global cognitive and functional status was assessed using the CDR based on interviews with participants and their study partners. All assessments were administered by trained research staff under the supervision of a clinical neuropsychologist. images were acquired at 0.8 mm isotropic resolution with a 256x256x180 mm field of view (FOV). T1-weighted images were skull-stripped using a deep learning tool (ICVMapp3r 79 ) and processed with FreeSurfer v7, 80 including intensity normalization, white and pial tissue segmentation, and cortical parcellation using the Desikan-Killiany atlas. 81 Sagittal 3D T2-weighted (TR=5000 ms, TE=388 ms) fluid attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) images were acquired at 1.0 mm isotropic resolution and with a 256x256x192 mm FOV. FLAIR images were rigidly co-registered to the T1-weighted images followed by automated segmentation of white matter hyperintensity (WMH) and enlarged perivascular space (PVS) using a deep-learning based tool, segcsvd. [82] [83] [84] Basal ganglia (BG) PVS volumes were generated by intersecting the PVS segmentation with a BG mask generated from FreeSurfer subcortical segmentations (caudate, putamen, pallidum, and accumbens nucleus).

We focused on three MRI-derived measures to capturing complementary aspects of brain aging. Mean bilateral entorhinal cortical thickness was selected as a marker of early neurodegeneration, given its vulnerability to age and AD-related atrophy. 56, 57 Mean bilateral precentral gyrus thickness was examined as an AD-spared control region. We also examined two markers of cerebral small vessel disease: WMH volume and BG-PVS volume, both log-transformed to reduce skewness and corrected for intracranial volume in analyses. To facilitate interpretation, all three measures were ztransformed prior to analyses.

4.6 Cardiometabolic measures

Cardiometabolic health was assessed using standardized clinical measurements, including body mass index (BMI), calculated from measured height and weight, systolic blood pressure (averaged across three seated readings taken one minute apart using an automated sphygmomanometer), plus serum levels of triglycerides, high-density lipoprotein (HDL) and low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol, and HbA1c. Medication use was self-reported, and lipid-modifying, glucose-lowering, and antihypertensive medications were included as covariates in sensitivity analyses.

4.7 External validation cohort

To externally validate associations between plasma proteins with cognitive and brain outcomes identified in CAMERA, we analyzed NULISAseq data from the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI; adni.loni.usc.edu). The primary goal of ADNI has been to test whether serial neuroimaging, biological markers, and clinical and neuropsychological assessment can be combined to measure the progression of mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and early Alzheimer's disease (AD). Given differences in cohort composition and outcome measures, validation focused on the consistency of protein associations with corresponding cognitive and neuroimaging outcomes rather than exact replication of the CAMERA analyses.

Plasma samples from 1,428 ADNI participants were assayed using the NULISAseq CNS 120 panel, comprising 131 protein targets, following standard processing protocols. Consistent with CAMERA, protein concentrations were winsorized at three times the interquartile range. Nine targets were excluded because plate-level detectability was below 50% on more than 20% of plates (SNCB, UCHL1, PTN, YWHAZ, YWHAG, pTDP43409, IL1B, BDMAPT, SNAP25) and APOE4 was excluded because of its bimodal distribution. An additional 14 participants were excluded as sample outliers detected with the WGCNA sample network connectivity outlier algorithm. 70, 71 The resulting dataset included 116 individual proteins, and two derived ratios (pTau217/Aβ42, pTau217%), with 118 proteomic measures overlapping between ADNI and CAMERA. All proteomic measures were z-transformed prior to analysis. ADNI participants underwent repeated cognitive and clinical assessments every 6-12 months. Longitudinal cognitive performance was assessed using the modified PACC (mPACC), 31, 85 For neuroimaging analyses, we used publicly available processed MRI measures. Given the greater proportion of cognitively impaired participants in ADNI compared with CAMERA (Table S4), the primary neurodegeneration outcome was mean bilateral cortical thickness across AD-vulnerable regions, including the entorhinal, parahippocampal, inferior and middle temporal, and fusiform cortices. Mean bilateral entorhinal thickness, corresponding directly to the primary CAMERA measure, was also examined in secondary analyses.

Total WMH burden was derived from an automated segmentation pipeline. In brief, 3D T1 and FLAIR images were co-registered using an affine transformation, corrected for intensity inhomogeneity, and transformed to a common template. WMH were segmented using a modified Bayesian approach using a likelihood estimation from histogram and threshold-based segmentation, a prior probability mask generated from semi-automated WMH segmentations from a training dataset, and tissue constraints.

The final masks were binarized using a 3.5 standard deviation threshold and transformed back into native space.

Given that only cross-sectional FreeSurfer v7 measures were available, we restricted MRI analyses to cross-sectional data, using the first-available brain MRI obtained at or after the time of the NULISA proteomics blood draw, with analyses adjusted for this time difference (0-13.2 years, median = 0.5 years). All cognitive and neuroimaging measures were standardized to facilitate interpretation. The final validation sample included 1,381 participants with available cognitive or MRI data, with a mean (SD) cognitive and clinical follow-up of 4.8 (3.5) years.

4.8 Statistical analyses

Analyses were conducted in R v4.6.1 and Python v3.11.12. Statistical significance was assessed using a false discovery rate (FDR) threshold of 5%, with multiple comparisons corrected using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure. Associations meeting nominal (i.e., unadjusted) significance were also reported. Demographic and clinical characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics and compared using analysis of variance (ANOVA), t-tests, or χ² tests, as appropriate. Correlation analyses and scatterplots were used to assess concordance of effect estimates where specified.

In CAMERA, we first characterized associations of demographic factors with the NULISAseq Neuro 220 proteome. Separate multivariable linear models examined associations of age and sex with individual protein levels, adjusting for ethnicity. We also tested associations of APOE ε4 carrier status (carrier vs. non-carrier) with protein levels, adjusting for age, sex, and ethnicity. Given evidence that associations of APOE ε4 with AD risk and biomarkers may differ across ancestral populations, 30 we conducted exploratory post hoc stratified and interaction analyses testing whether associations of APOE ε4 carriage and protein levels differed by self-reported ethnicity (South Asian, Chinese, and non-Hispanic White).

We next examined associations of plasma proteins with cognitive performance and MRI-derived measures of brain aging. Separate multivariable linear models were fitted for each protein-outcome pair, adjusting for age, sex, and ethnicity. Cognitive models additionally adjusted for years of education and English as a first language (EFL), whereas volumetric MRI models (i.e., WMH and BG-PVS) additionally adjusted for total intracranial volume. Exploratory variance partitioning analysis examined whether the proteins nominally associated with cognition in main models explained additional variance in cognitive scores beyond demographic variables (i.e., age, sex, years of education, ethnicity, English-as-a-first-language status), APOE ε4 carriage, and neuroimaging measures (i.e., entorhinal thickness, intracranial volume-adjusted WMH and BG-PVS volumes).

To externally validate CAMERA findings, we examined associations of overlapping plasma proteins with corresponding cognitive and MRI outcomes in ADNI.

Longitudinal associations with mPACC and CDR-SB were assessed using linear mixedeffects models with participant-specific random intercepts and slopes. Models included protein levels, time, and their interaction, adjusting for baseline age, sex, years of education, and their interactions with time. The protein-by-time interaction was the primary term of interest, reflecting the association between baseline protein levels and longitudinal cognitive or clinical change. Cross-sectional associations with cortical thickness and WMH were assessed using linear models to test associations between protein levels and brain outcomes, adjusting for age, sex, imaging site, time between blood draw and MRI, and intracranial volume (for WMH volume only).

To probe the potential influence of cardiometabolic health on the proteomic signatures of cognitive and brain aging in CAMERA, we also characterized NULISAseq Neuro 220 protein associations with cardiometabolic measures. Separate multiple linear models examined associations of standardized BMI, HbA1c, HDL cholesterol, LDL cholesterol, triglycerides, and systolic blood pressure with individual protein levels, adjusting for age, sex, and ethnicity. Sensitivity analyses examined the robustness of CAMERA protein associations with cognitive and brain outcomes as well as cardiometabolic factors following additional adjustment for APOE ε4 carrier status. Cardiometabolic models were adjusted for relevant medication use (glucose-lowering medications for HbA1c, lipidmodifying medications for HDL and LDL cholesterol and triglycerides, and antihypertensives for systolic blood pressure) and BMI for all non-BMI predictors. In ADNI, sensitivity analyses re-estimated associations of proteins with cognitive and brain outcomes excluding participants with dementia at baseline.
