Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. Abstract

We asked what topological structure adds to a clinical prediction model and how that addition should be measured. Drug-resistant tuberculosis (DR-TB) treatment outcome prediction is the testbed. The contribution is an evaluation design and a bounded null result. Methods: Cross-sectional records for 9210 patients from the NIAID TB Portals programme were collated to one row per patient by worst-case aggregation, giving a 99-feature clinical schema. We derived two topological representations. A Mapper graph, built on a UMAP lens over a Gower dissimilarity, supplied four neighbourhood-position features. Local Vietoris-Rips persistent homology on each patient's 26-point neighbourhood supplied degree-0 and degree-1 summaries, vectorised by the best of thirteen catalogued methods. We compared five feature configurations across four classifiers in a pipeline in which every topological step is fitted inside the training partition. A sealed test lockbox of 1776 patients, 463 of them with adverse outcomes, was opened once. We compared discrimination by the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) using paired DeLong tests with Bonferroni correction (corrected threshold 0.0125).

.

1 Background

Drug-resistant tuberculosis (DR-TB) continues to cause significant morbidity and mortality worldwide. The 2025 WHO Global Tuberculosis Report estimates that around 400,000 people develop multidrug-resistant or rifampicin-resistant tuberculosis annually, with global treatment success rates remaining below 60% [1]. Identifying patients at high risk of treatment failure from their clinical record would allow earlier escalation of care and more efficient allocation of resources, but prediction from baseline clinical data is constrained. Models built on demographic, clinical and radiological variables typically report AUROC values in the range of 0.71 to 0.74 for treatment failure and loss to follow-up [2]. Multimodal analyses incorporating genomic and imaging data have improved discrimination only for narrow endpoints such as culture conversion at six months [3]. Prediction of TB treatment failure from any single data modality plateaus at a level that is promising but insufficient, and meaningful gains in discrimination require integrating clinical, radiological, and pathogen genomic features into a unified multimodal model [4].

2 Topological data analysis (TDA) offers a different perspective on patient data.

Rather than summarising each patient as a flat feature vector, TDA constructs representations of the geometric shape of the data distribution. TDA investigates which patients cluster together, where the boundaries between groups lie, and whether the population exhibits structural patterns that a coordinate-based representation would not capture. The premise is that this geometric information is not already contained in standard predictors and that recovering it could improve prediction accuracy. If topological features carry discriminative signal beyond a well-specified clinical baseline, augmenting that baseline should improve AUROC on an independent test set.

That is the specific claim this paper evaluates.

Applied studies of TDA in clinical prediction have rarely tested this claim carefully. Three problems recur. First, few studies specify the clinical baseline rigorously, so the comparison is between a topological model and a weakly configured reference rather than between topological augmentation and a well-tuned incumbent [5]. Second, topological features such as Mapper nodes and persistence diagrams are computed from pairwise dissimilarities or embeddings fitted on the full cohort, so they can carry information from held-out patients into the training representation, a form of leakage documented as a recurring failure across machine learning research [6]. Third, differences in AUROC are typically reported as point estimates without a paired test or a multiplicity correction, so a positive result cannot be distinguished from sampling variation and a null result cannot be bounded. Together these problems mean that neither positive nor negative results in this literature are as informative as they appear. This paper makes three contributions, each bearing on how TDA augmentation should be evaluated. First, we describe a leakage-controlled evaluation design in which every topological step -the dissimilarity matrix, the UMAP embedding, the Mapper cover and the local persistence computation -is fitted exclusively on the training partition and applied to a sealed test set without further modification. Second, we 3 . report a bounded null result: no topologically augmented configuration meaningfully improved discrimination over a 99-feature clinical baseline on a held-out lockbox of 1,776 patients, with all upper confidence limits below +0.014 AUROC across sixteen paired DeLong tests with Bonferroni correction. Third, we show that this null is compatible with genuine structural information in the topological features, information that a cohort-level rank statistic is not designed to detect, and we propose a minimal set of evaluations capable of detecting it. The DR-TB setting makes this a demanding testbed: the baseline is already well-specified, and any incremental contribution must be measured against it.

1.1 Related work

1.1.1 Topological data analysis in clinical prediction

Topological data analysis (TDA) has been applied across biomedicine for over a decade and a recent review of that work has been done by Skaf and Laubenbacher [7]. The claims in it are of two kinds and separating them matters. The first is a discovery claim. Nicolau and colleagues [8] analysed breast cancer gene expression topologically and isolated a subgroup with an unusual survival profile that conventional stratification had missed. Li and colleagues [9] built a patient similarity network from electronic health records and genotype data and reported three subtypes of type 2 diabetes with distinct comorbidity profiles. Since 2015, TDA has identified discriminative structure across multiple biomedical domains. In neurotrauma, TDA identified perioperative hypertension as a superior predictor of recovery [10]. In neuroscience, clique topology revealed that hippocampal neural correlations encode intrinsic circuit geometry beyond spatial position [11]. For epilepsy, a persistent-entropy classifier distinguished seizure from healthy electroencephalography with 97.2% AUROC [12]. In histology, persistent entropy differentiated epithelial tissue types [13], while in oncology, TDA characterised dynamic tumour vascular network structure in relation to treatment 4 response [14]. None of these studies asserts that a topological feature improves the discrimination of a tuned prediction model, so none is put at risk by a study testing only that narrower proposition. The second is a performance claim. Kazeminia et al. [15] showed that topological descriptors embedded within Multiple Instance Learning architectures yielded AUROC gains of 3.3% to 5.9% over standard pooling strategies.

Incorporating persistent homology features into convolutional and visiontransformer architectures for breast ultrasound screening similarly yielded consistent improvements in both accuracy and AUC across multiple backbone architectures, even under limited data conditions [16]. In another context, a topology-guided network achieved a 4.6% improvement in AUROC for predicting neoadjuvant chemotherapy response [17]. These results indicate that topological features can improve predictive performance when applied to imaging data, where topology is computed directly from pixel-level structure or from learned embeddings. The dominant condition appears to be that topological information contributes most when the underlying feature representation is noisy or weakly discriminative and adds marginal benefit once embeddings are already well-structured [15]. This raises the question of whether such gains generalise to settings where the baseline feature space is already highly informative, such as curated clinical tabular data.

1.1.2 Mapper and persistent homology as feature generators

Two constructions dominate the TDA space. Mapper takes a dissimilarity on the observations and a filter function, covers the filter range with overlapping intervals, clusters the observations in each preimage and forms a graph with one node per cluster and an edge wherever two clusters share an observation [18]. Persistent homology takes a filtration, a nested family of simplicial complexes indexed by a scale parameter, and records the scale at which each connected component, loop or higher void appears and disappears. Its output is a persistence diagram, a multiset of birth and death pairs [19]. Both outputs are objects, not vectors, and most clinical applications simply 5 . inspect them as is. However, using them as predictors requires vectorisation, a map to a fixed-length real vector. For persistence diagrams, this is done with landscapes [20], persistence images [21], kernels [22] and a wider family catalogued elsewhere [23]. For Mapper, the usual route is graph descriptors attached to each observation through the nodes containing it.

Vectorising a topological object produces a fixed-length feature block subject to the same requirements as any other predictor set: its dimension must be proportional to the available data, it may correlate with existing features, and fitting the vectorisation on the full cohort before splitting introduces leakage. Stability results establish that persistence diagrams change by a bounded amount under small perturbations of the input [24]. Neither result establishes whether a vectorised topological summary adds information beyond what the original feature set already contains. That is an empirical question, and one this paper addresses directly.

1.1.3 Mixed-type clinical data and the dissimilarity problem

Every topological pipeline sits downstream of a dissimilarity. For imaging and omics data this is rarely contentious. Tabular clinical data are different. A patient record mixes continuous measurements, ordered severity grades, unordered categories, binary indicators, and no canonical dissimilarity exists for that mixture.

Gower's coefficient is the standard answer, averaging a per-variable dissimilarity across the record [25]. It also fixes, by construction rather than by evidence, the weight of a single category disagreement against a full-range difference in a continuous variable. Van de Velden and colleagues review the wider family of mixed-data distances and show the choice changes recovered cluster structure materially [26]. The consequence for the topology is direct. Stability theorems control the output under small perturbations of a fixed metric, so they say nothing about two metrics that are not close to each other. A Mapper graph on mixed clinical data is therefore partly a property of the encoding, as is any persistence computation built on the same dissimilarity 6 or on an embedding derived from it [27]. Clinical applications typically name the metric in one clause and report no sensitivity analysis.

1.1.4 Gaps in current evaluation practice

The weakness of this literature is not in the topology but in the comparison. We identify four problems. The first is the baseline. An incremental claim needs a reference model tuned as carefully as the augmented one. Many topological studies report the topological model alone, or compare against a default configuration of a conventional model, so the claim that topology improves prediction has no denominator. The general form of the point is settled: a systematic review found no benefit of machine learning over logistic regression once baselines were properly specified [5]. The second is leakage. Topological features are computed from the geometry of a set of observations, which makes them structurally prone to it. A dissimilarity matrix, a Mapper cover or an embedding fitted on the full cohort before splitting carries information about held-out observations into the training representation. Kapoor and Narayanan document leakage as a recurring failure across seventeen fields and 294 papers [6]. The topological case is a clean instance of it and is rarely addressed explicitly. The third is inference. Differences in AUROC are usually reported as point estimates. A paired test for correlated ROC curves has been available since 1988 [28], and comparing several feature sets across several classifiers also requires a multiplicity correction. Without both, a difference of 0.01 supports no conclusion in either direction. The fourth is the choice of metric. Discrimination is almost always the only quantity reported. Calibration is neglected across prediction modelling generally [29], decision-analytic measures such as net benefit are rarer still [30], and TRIPOD+AI asks that discrimination, calibration and intended use be reported together [31]. A feature block can shift predicted risks usefully or improve the ordering of patients within a subgroup without moving a cohort-level AUROC. A study measuring only AUROC cannot separate no added information from added information the metric cannot register. Together, these leave 7.

the field with almost no bounded negative results. Nulls are usually unpublished, or stated without an interval, so there is no published estimate of how large a plausible topological effect might be. A well-powered null with an interval attached is therefore more informative than a further positive point estimate.

2 Methods

2.1 Data source and cohort construction

We obtained data from the National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases (NIAID) TB Portals programme, a multi-country DR-TB surveillance resource. 2.3 Leakage-controlled pipeline order

We used 10-fold stratified cross-validation, with folds stratified on the ternary treatment-outcome label. The same processing sequence was applied within every cross-validation fold and identically at the training-to-lockbox transition. The sequence is as follows. We first split the data into training and test partitions. We then fit a median imputer on the training patients and apply it to fill missing values in both partitions. Next, we compute pairwise Gower distances over all training patients to form 9 a square distance matrix, and project test patients into this space using only the feature ranges from the training fold. We fit a UMAP embedding on the training Gower matrix and use it as the Mapper lens. We build the Mapper graph on this training embedding, and derive the D + topological features from training-fold outcome labels only, so node purity estimates carry no test information. Simplicial SMOTE was then applied to the training fold to address class imbalance, after which a StandardScaler was fitted on the real, non-synthetic training patients and used to scale both matrices.

We then fit the classifier on the scaled, balanced training data. We evaluate on the original test fold, which is not resampled and retains the natural class distribution. Two ordering choices define the design. First, balancing follows topological feature extraction. In an earlier pipeline variant, we applied balancing before Mapper construction and found that synthetic points inflated local density, shifted node membership and altered node purity, distorting the topology the features are meant to measure. Synthetic points are interpolations inside the convex hull of real neighbourhoods, so they change the geometry on which the graph is built. In the current design the graph is built on real training patients only and synthetic patients inherit the D + values of their nearest real training neighbour in the 99-dimensional imputed clinical space by Euclidean 1-NN. Second, the lockbox was written to disk at the split and was not opened, inspected or used in any engineering, selection, tuning or diagnostic step until the single final evaluation.

Our balancing approach used mixed-type Simplicial SMOTE [32], which samples uniformly inside simplices of the clique complex of a symmetric k-NN graph rather than along edges. That graph is built on Gower distance, so binary and categorical variables are matched exactly rather than treated as continuous. 2.4 Dissimilarity

The feature matrix mixes continuous scores, binary radiological flags and ordinal severity ranks. Euclidean distance treats all of these as continuous and is scale sensitive, so we used the Gower coefficient [25]: where δ ijk = 1 if both x ik and x jk are observed (and R k > 0) and δ ijk = 0 otherwise, and R k is the range of feature k in the training fold [25].

Continuous features thus contribute a range-normalised absolute difference, binary and nominal features contribute 0 on agreement and 1 on disagreement, and pairs with a missing value are excluded from both numerator and denominator, so the average is taken over jointly observed variables only. The resulting matrix is symmetric with d G (i, i) = 0 and, for values within the training range, lies in [0, 1]. Gower distance serves two roles. First, it defines the k-NN graph for Simplicial SMOTE. Second, it is the input to UMAP and hence to the Mapper pipeline. Persistent homology was not computed on the Gower dissimilarity but on Euclidean distances in a scaled principal-component space. This distinction matters. The persistence diagrams describe the geometry of that Euclidean embedding, not the mixed-type dissimilarity space, and the two agree only insofar as the embedding preserves Gower structure, which is not guaranteed for binary and ordinal variables. Gower-based persistence, using the dissimilarity matrix as a precomputed filtration, is a stated limitation and a direct extension. 12

2.5 Persistent homology

Persistent homology tracks how topological features of a point cloud appear and disappear as a scale parameter grows [33]. In a Vietoris-Rips filtration, two points are joined when their distance is at most ϵ, and a simplex enters when all its vertices are pairwise joined. As ϵ grows, components merge and cycles form and fill. Each feature is recorded as a birth-death pair, and the collection of pairs in dimension q is the degreeq persistence diagram. H 0 records connected components, H 1 records one-dimensional cycles.

We designed the analysis to be local and per-patient. We standardised features with a RobustScaler and reduced dimensionality with PCA, keeping the smallest number of components reaching 85% cumulative explained variance, capped at 25.

For each patient, we formed a local cloud from that patient plus its 25 Euclidean nearest neighbours in the training PCA space, giving 26 points. For lockbox patients, the 25 neighbours were drawn exclusively from the training set. We computed Rips persistence on each cloud with ripser [34, 35] at maxdim = 1, yielding H 0 and H 1 only.

We opted for the local construction approach for this problem instead of a global Three limitations follow from this design. First, we computed homology only up to dimension one, capturing connected components (H 0 ) and loops (H 1 ); two-dimensional voids (H 2 ) were not computed and are not represented in any model feature. Second, we used no global filtration over the full cohort for modelling. Third, distance to measure (DTM) weighted filtrations and Gower-based persistence were not evaluated, as neither would have materially altered the evaluation design or the primary conclusion.

2.6 Mapper and the D + features

Mapper [18] summarises a point cloud as a graph. A lens maps the data to a lowdimensional space, that space is covered by overlapping sets, points in each pre-image are clustered, each cluster becomes a node and nodes sharing a point are joined.

Overlap is what preserves connectivity a hard partition would destroy. The lens is the first two dimensions of a UMAP embedding [27] with n neighbors = 15 and min dist = 0.1, fitted on the training-fold Gower matrix under a fixed seed. Unseen patients are projected by distance-weighted interpolation of the UMAP coordinates of their k = 5 nearest training patients under Gower distance; this approximation is used in place of the standard UMAP transform because Mapper requires 2-D coordinates rather than a parametric embedding. The cover uses 10 hypercubes per axis at 50% overlap. Pre-images are clustered with DBSCAN [36] at ε = 0.3 and min samples = 3.

We built graphs with KeplerMapper [37].

2.6.1 Hyperparameter stability ablation

Mapper parameters were fixed by a stability criterion rather than by a downstream classification metric. This choice is necessary because the Mapper graph feeds directly into the classifier. Selecting parameters by AUROC or F1 would allow the downstream performance measure to influence the input features, which is circular.

We evaluated a 3 × 3 grid of nine configurations: n cubes ∈ {8, 10, 12} crossed with ε ∈ {0.2, 0.3, 0.4}, with overlap fixed at ρ = 0.5 and min samples = 3. For each configuration, B = 20 stratified bootstrap resamples were drawn from 80% of the fold-1 training UMAP embedding. On each resample, a Mapper graph was constructed and two stability metrics were recorded: Because all nine configurations passed, the ablation does not identify a single optimal setting. The topological structure recovered by Mapper is stable across the full parameter range and is not an artefact of any particular configuration.

2.6.2 Variant D + topological feature extraction

For patient i, let N (i) denote the set of Mapper nodes to which that patient belongs, where is the number of such nodes.

A patient may belong to more than one node because the cover elements overlap. For each node v ∈ N (i), π -(v) denotes negative purity, d fail (v) the hop distance to the nearest high-risk node, d success (v) the 15 hop distance to the nearest high-success node, and m v = |M(v)| the node size, where M(v) is the set of patients assigned to node v. Four patient-level features were derived from the Mapper graph and together form the Variant D + block: i. topo risk score: the mean of π -(v)/(1 + d fail (v)) over N (i).

ii. topo success score: the mean of 1/(1 + d success (v)) over N (i).

iii. topo stability index: the mean node size, discounted by multiplicity and branch proximity: , where d branch (v) is the hop distance from v to the nearest branch node (degree ≥ 3), set to 0 when the graph contains no branch node.

The factor 1/n i in the denominator is a multiplicity discount: patients assigned to many overlapping nodes receive proportionally lower values, down-weighting patients assigned to densely overlapping cover elements.

iv. topo fiedler: the mean of the Fiedler value ψ(v) over N (i), where ψ(v) is the entry of the Fiedler vector at node v, defined below.

All purities and thresholds were computed from training labels only. Node purity values used to construct each training patient's D + features were computed from the outcomes of all other training patients assigned to that node, excluding the patient itself, so each patient's own outcome label does not contribute to its own feature values.

Persistent homology features for synthetic SMOTE patients were assigned by the same For training patients, all assigned nodes lie within one component, because Mapper places an edge between any two nodes that share a patient, so patients sharing a node are always in the same component. A held-out patient is assigned to the single nearest node by centroid distance in the UMAP projection; if the implementation assigns more than one node, the mean is taken over the corresponding component-local entries. Because each ψ c has unit norm on C c and sums to zero on C c , entries are comparable within a component but not across components: a component with many nodes has smaller individual entries by normalisation than a component with few nodes. For patients whose nodes span different connected components, topo fiedler summarises 17 local spectral position within each component rather than a coordinate in a single global spectral embedding. It is therefore used here as a patient-level descriptor for prediction, and values should not be compared as positions in a shared embedding space.

2.7 Vectorisation ablation

A persistence diagram is a multiset, not a vector, so it must be mapped to fixed-length coordinates before a classifier can use it. The map is not neutral and the literature offers no default, so we treated the choice as a designed ablation over the taxonomy of [23] rather than a search for a winning number. We implemented all 13 catalogued methods: persistence statistics, entropy summary, algebraic functions, tropical coordinates, complex polynomial, Betti curve, lifespan curve, persistence landscape, persistence silhouette, persistence image, template functions, adaptive templates and ATOL. These fall into statistical, algebraic, curve, functional and ensemble families, which between them span the ways a diagram can be summarised: by moments, by algebraic invariants, by functions of the filtration parameter, by kernel density on the birth-death plane, and by learned landmarks.

We applied each method separately to H 0 and H 1 and concatenated the outputs.

Three points govern leakage. The data-driven methods, adaptive templates and ATOL, learn template centres, covariances and landmarks, and were refitted inside each training fold. Curve-type methods need a fixed evaluation grid, so t-ranges were fixed from training barcodes and applied unchanged to test barcodes. The ablation used 5-fold cross-validation across the four-classifier panel. Persistence statistics performed best and we carried it forward to all downstream analysis.

2.8 Model configurations and classifiers

Five configurations were compared, all sharing the 99-feature clinical baseline (Table 1). The A against B Mapper comparison used 10-fold stratified cross-validation, chosen for paired-test power, since the effect under test was expected to be small and paired fold-level contrasts need enough folds to be informative. 2.9 Evaluation protocol

In the primary lockbox analysis, we performed final binary evaluation on the 1,776 non-pending lockbox patients. We partitioned the non-pending training data into three disjoint subsets: a fit set (80%), a calibration set (10%), and a threshold-tuning set 19 (10%). We trained base classifiers on the fit subset, then calibrated probabilities on the calibration subset using Platt scaling [42]: where A and B are fitted by maximum likelihood on the calibration subset, with A typically negative for margin-based classifiers.

We selected the operating threshold on the threshold-tuning subset by maximising the Youden index [43]. No subset was used for more than one purpose.

We compared discrimination with paired DeLong tests [28] on the calibrated lockbox probabilities, per classifier. We declared two correction families in advance. The A-referenced family holds four comparisons (A against B, C, D and D-lite), Bonferroni corrected at α corr = 0.05/4 = 0.0125. Bonferroni correction was applied within each classifier's four comparisons rather than globally across all sixteen, on the grounds that each classifier constitutes an independent evaluation of the same topological augmentation question. A global correction across sixteen tests would have been α corr = 0.05/16 = 0.003125, which is more conservative than the design warrants. Equivalently, adjusted p-values are p adj = min(1, 4p) and a comparison is declared significant when p adj < 0.05.

Bonferroni correction was used rather than false discovery rate (FDR) control. FDR is suited to screening studies where a small fraction of false positives is acceptable [44].

Here, four prespecified comparisons each ask whether a named augmentation improves on the clinical baseline. A single false positive would incorrectly support the central claim of the paper. Bonferroni controls the probability that any one comparison is wrongly declared significant. It is conservative, and that conservatism is appropriate when one false positive is one too many. As a sensitivity check, applying a global Bonferroni correction across all sixteen tests would give α corr = 0.05/16 = 0.003125. Discrimination alone is insufficient under 74/26 imbalance. AUROC is invariant to prevalence and can stay flat while the ranking of the adverse minority shifts, and it says nothing about whether predicted probabilities are usable as risk estimates [45].

We therefore report AUROC with 95% confidence intervals alongside adverse-class PR-AUC, the Brier score against the base-rate Brier of 0.1927, and sensitivity and specificity at the Youden threshold.

4 Discussion

We asked what topological structure adds to a clinical prediction model and how that addition should be measured. The discriminative answer is negative, and the structural one is not.

4.1 Interpreting the null result

Our AUROC values ranged from 0.69 to 0.75, in line with the 0.71 to 0.74 reported for models using baseline clinical data [2, 47]. Higher pooled estimates exist, but they come predominantly from studies with limited external validation and a high risk of bias [48]. DR-TB outcomes are shaped by comorbidities such as HIV and alcohol misuse [49] and by treatment adherence [50]. Three limits bound what the design can establish. The design is powered for moderate AUROC differences on 1,776 lockbox patients, of whom 463 have adverse outcomes. Differences of a few thousandths of AUROC lie within the noise of a test set this size, so the study can rule out a useful discrimination gain but cannot resolve a marginal one. The analysis is single-cohort.

Splitting one TB Portals extract gives an internal held-out estimate, not external validity. Site composition, imaging platforms and outcome ascertainment are shared across the split, so transportability to another programme is untested. A null DeLong result does not establish equivalence. Failing to reject equal AUROC is not evidence that AUROCs are equal. Equivalence requires a prespecified margin and an equivalence test, neither of which we undertook. Where we report that topological augmentation gives no discrimination gain, the claim is that none was detected under a protocol built to detect one, not that none exists.

No topologically augmented configuration meaningfully improved lockbox AUROC over the clinical-only baseline for any of the four classifiers (Section 3.4). Across all sixteen DeLong comparisons the upper limit of the 95% confidence interval never exceeded +0.014 AUROC (Random Forest, Model B), and every other upper limit was at or below +0.008. That is a real constraint: an improvement of the size usually claimed for a new feature block in clinical prediction would have been detected. Under the support vector machine the constraint is tighter still, with all four intervals at or below 0.008 AUROC in width and all four point estimates within 0.0005 of zero. One 29 of these, SVM with Model B, was nominally significant at the adjusted p-value level (p adj = 0.0478) but without any practical meaning.

The constraint is asymmetric. The widest intervals, random forest against Model C and against Model D, each spanned 0.034 AUROC units. For the Model C comparison the data exclude improvement beyond +0.001 but remain compatible with a decrement of moderate size. The study therefore supports a bounded negative claim, not a symmetric one: for this cohort, this baseline and these classifiers, topological augmentation does not improve rank discrimination by more than 0.014 AUROC units (the largest upper limit of all sixteen 95% confidence intervals), and in several configurations it reduces it. Four comparisons, three under logistic regression and one under XGBoost, showed a significant reduction.

Two qualifications limit this conclusion. The study was not designed as a formal equivalence trial; no margin was prespecified and the lockbox size was fixed by the available data. Second, the null is conditional on this baseline: the 99-feature clinical schema reached an AUROC of 0.7548 (XGBoost; 0.7523 with logistic regression) and the best adverse-class PR-AUC and Brier score of any configuration tested, and a well-specified baseline is the hardest condition under which to show incremental value.

4.2 Discriminative metrics as the wrong instrument

AUROC is a rank statistic computed over the entire cohort. Two structural properties matter for evaluating any new feature block. First, AUROC is dominated by the majority of patient pairs. A representation that sharpens risk estimates for a small subgroup leaves most pairwise rankings unchanged, and its contribution to the cohort-wide statistic is diluted proportionally. If topological position carries information mainly about patients in unusual regions of the data manifold, those patients are by definition few, and their effect on AUROC will be correspondingly small. Second, AUROC is insensitive to where on the risk scale a change occurs. A model that reorders 30.

patients near a decision threshold is more useful than one that reorders patients who are already clearly low risk, yet AUROC assigns equal weight to both [30].

A pattern in the results is consistent with this argument. Model C with Logis- tic Regression achieves the highest macro-F1 of all 20 model-classifier combinations (0.6614) and a sensitivity of 0.7113, against 0.5636 for the same classifier on the clinical baseline, while its AUROC (0.7519) is statistically indistinguishable from the baseline (0.7523).

The Youden threshold was fixed on the held-out tuning partition, not on the lockbox, so this is not threshold selection on test data. Two interpretations are consistent with the evidence. The sensitivity shift may reflect genuine enrichment of the classifier's probability estimates near the decision boundary by the persistent homology feature block. Equally, the same pattern arises if the Youden threshold simply lands at a different operating point on an otherwise unchanged ROC curve. The data collected here cannot separate these interpretations, because neither calibration within risk strata nor decision-analytic comparisons were prespecified as primary endpoints. Separating the two interpretations requires measurements designed for that purpose. Calibration should be assessed within risk strata rather than only through an aggregate Brier score. Discrimination should be examined conditional on baseline model uncertainty. Comparisons should be decision-analytic, reporting net benefit across a range of plausible threshold probabilities [30]. Work that claims structural value for a topological representation should prespecify measurements capable of detecting it. Testing only a global rank statistic when the hypothesised benefit is local and subgroup-specific is a mismatch between the instrument and the claim. Future evaluations of TDA-augmented clinical prediction models should include calibration-stratified and decision-analytic endpoints as primary outcomes alongside AUROC, specified before outcome data are accessed. Beyond the discrimination question, two structural findings from the topological representations survive scrutiny, each with a qualification. 31 Neighbourhood-based risk stratification. The four Mapper D + features place each patient at a position in the graph built on the training cohort, and they are not redundant with one another. The largest pairwise correlation was |r| = 0.600 and the largest variance inflation factor was 3.318, both inside the prespecified thresholds. A patient can therefore be placed in a high-risk or high-success region of a precomputed atlas before any prediction is made. This supports a descriptive use, not a predictive one: three of the five lockbox configurations carry the D + block and none improved discrimination by a meaningful margin. One qualification is essential: the D + scores are derived from outcome-labelled node purities and are supervised summaries of the training cohort. Fold-wise construction prevented leakage, but their behaviour should not be read as topology discovering risk independently of the labels. Variation in adverse-outcome composition across patient neighbourhoods. The Mapper graph comprised 99 nodes and 183 edges, with 11 nodes (11%) above the high-risk purity threshold and 72 nodes (73%) above the high-success threshold. Among the fifteen largest nodes, adverse-outcome purity ranged from 5.9% to 30.5%, a fivefold spread within a set of nodes that a single dominant-class label would treat as equivalent.

Whether this is clinically meaningful heterogeneity is a separate question. The stability ablation shows that the structure is reproducible under resampling and across a 3 × 3 parameter grid, with node-count CVs between 3.8% and 7.9% and purity standard deviations between 0.0098 and 0.0230, all inside prespecified thresholds.

Reproducibility is necessary but not sufficient. The geometry is also metric-relative:

Gower's coefficient weights each variable equally [25], so the neighbourhood structure is a consequence of that encoding as much as of the underlying biology. Persistence summaries are provably stable to small perturbations of the point cloud [24], but that guarantee says nothing about the metric that defines the cloud.

4.3 Relation to prior work and evaluation recommendations

The evaluation problem generalises beyond this application. Studies applying topological methods to clinical prediction should report the following, alongside the items required by TRIPOD+AI [31]: 1.

The baseline, fully specified. Report feature count, selection procedure and baseline performance before any comparison.

2. Effect sizes with intervals, and a prespecified margin. A null is informative only if the reader can see what it excludes. 3. Stability of the representation as a primary result. Sensitivity to lens, cover and clustering choices belongs in the main text, with thresholds fixed in advance.

4.

Outcome-informed and outcome-blind topological features reported separately. They carry different evidential weight.

5.

One prespecified decision-analytic or calibration-based comparison, such as decision curve analysis [30]. This distinguishes added structural information from movement along an unchanged ROC curve.

6. The dissimilarity metric and the encoding of mixed-type variables.

Topological output is not invariant to either.

Our structural findings agree with the established use of Mapper for subgroup discovery. Nicolau et al. identified a breast cancer subgroup with a distinct survival profile that conventional stratification missed [8], and later work has recovered homogeneous patient subgroups in clinical data by similar means [51]. Those studies claim subgroup discovery, not incremental discrimination over a tuned baseline, so our null addresses a different claim and does not contradict them.

Our result diverges from studies reporting discriminative gains. The most plausible explanation is the input representation. Xin et al. [52] achieve an AUC of 0.767 and 0.875 from two clinical classification tasks for Multiple Sclerosis by applying persistent homology directly to inter-lesion spatial structure in MRI, a setting where topology is 33 an intrinsic property of the image rather than a derived summary. Somasundaram et al. [53] show that cubical persistent homology features from lung CT scans associate with survival independently of tumour size, again extracting signal from unstructured pixellevel data. Our input was a tabular schema in which geometrically relevant structure had already been extracted by feature engineering.

These comparisons suggest that topological augmentation may contribute most when the input representation is unstructured and least when it has already been curated by feature engineering. This is a hypothesis for future work rather than an established general principle. The broader pattern is familiar. A systematic review found no performance benefit of machine learning over logistic regression for clinical prediction [5]. Our result is the same finding one level down, at the representation rather than the algorithm. Multimodal and radiological data carry real prognostic signal in tuberculosis [54]; our result suggests that a well-specified tabular schema already captures much of it.

4.4 Limitations

The cohort came from a single surveillance programme. It spans multiple countries, but referral, imaging and reporting practices within one programme are correlated, and no external cohort was available. Generalisability to routine programmatic settings is untested. We collapsed longitudinal records to one row per patient by worst-case aggregation, so every binary indicator became an "ever recorded" flag. In particular,

Lost to follow-up was coded as an adverse (Negative) outcome alongside Died and Failure. Loss to follow-up is often a surveillance failure rather than a direct proxy for poor clinical outcome, and sensitivity to this coding choice was not tested. More broadly, the worst-case aggregation uses findings recorded across the full follow-up period rather than information available at a defined baseline time point. The resulting 34 model reflects the complete clinical trajectory and cannot be directly applied to early treatment decisions without a prospective baseline cohort. Gower's coefficient, the UMAP lens and DBSCAN clustering were each reasonable choices not compared against alternatives. The null is bounded by the intervals reported in Section 3.4 rather than by a designed detectable effect. A larger or more heterogeneous cohort, or a weaker baseline, could yield a different answer. The stability of the derived D + feature values themselves under Mapper parameter variation is a separate, open question not addressed by the graph-level ablation reported in Section 2.6.1.

5 Conclusions

Topological structure contributed descriptively rather than discriminatively to this clinical prediction task. Mapper and local persistent homology provided a view of the patient manifold not captured by the clinical feature schema, but neither augmented AUROC over a well-specified 99-feature baseline -four of sixteen paired comparisons significantly favoured the baseline, and no upper confidence limit exceeded +0.014 AUROC units. The methodological contribution is an evaluation design in which every topological step is fitted inside the training partition and applied to sealed data, making the comparison interpretable, together with evidence that AUROC parity is compatible with a feature block carrying feature variation that is not linearly redundant with the clinical predictors under standard correlation diagnostics. The direct next step is to apply this design to cubical persistent homology on CT and chest radiograph images, where the input is unstructured and its geometric content has not already been extracted by feature engineering. Let A be the binary adjacency matrix of the training-fold Mapper graph, with A uv = 1 if nodes u and v share at least one patient and A uv = 0 otherwise, and let D be the diagonal matrix of node degrees, D vv = u A vu . The graph Laplacian is L = D-A. Its second-smallest eigenvalue λ 2 is the algebraic connectivity of the graph[38]: λ2 = 0 if and only if the graph is disconnected, and the associated eigenvector is the Fiedler vector. Because L1 = 0, the all-ones vector 1 is always an eigenvector for eigenvalue zero, so the Fiedler vector is orthogonal to 1 and therefore sums to zero over the graph. When the Mapper graph has k ≥ 2 connected components, L is block-diagonal: after permuting rows and columns by component membership, it takes the form diag(L 1 , L 2 , . . . , L k ), where L c is the graph Laplacian of the subgraph induced by component C c . Consequently λ 2 (L) = 0 and a global Fiedler vector is not uniquely defined. We therefore compute the Fiedler vector separately within each component. Nodes in singleton components (|C c | = 1) take ψ(v) = 0. The computation uses the training-fold graph only and no outcome labels.
