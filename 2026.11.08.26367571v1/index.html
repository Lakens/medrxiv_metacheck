What we found

We found 3 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Maternal ICU admission provides advanced, lifesaving medical care for pregnant or postpartum women who are critically ill. The burden of obstetric critical illness requiring ICU admission is higher in low-and middle-income countries (LMICs) than in high-income countries. Most critically ill obstetric patients admitted to the ICU have undergone major surgical interventions such as caesarean sections, hysterectomies and explorative laparotomies.

Common indications for maternal ICU admission following surgery include postpartum hemorrhage (PPH), hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, and sepsis. Maternal ICU admission following obstetric surgery is associated with high maternal mortality, prolonged ICU stay, and poor neonatal outcomes. Identifying factors associated with poor maternal outcomes after ICU admission following obstetrics surgeries is essential for reducing preventable severe morbidity and mortality. This study aimed to assess the factors associated with the clinical outcomes of maternal ICU admissions following obstetric surgery at Muhimbili National Hospital (MNH), Dar es Salaam, Tanzania. This was a prospective cross-sectional study, conducted at the MNH, Obstetrics ICU, involving 115 women admitted following obstetric surgery. Data were collected using a modified WHO maternal near-miss checklist. Data were analyzed using SPSS version 27. Categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables were summarized using median and interquartile range (IQR). The associations between variables were assessed using Pearson's chi-square and Fisher's exact tests. Modified Poisson regression analysis was used to determine the strength of associations, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05 and 95% confidence interval. PPH was the most common indication for maternal ICU admission following surgery, accounting for 46.1% of admissions. The overall maternal ICU mortality was 16.5%, whereas 40.9% of patients experienced prolonged ICU stays. In bivariate analysis, mechanical ventilation (p = 0.04) and use of inotropes and/or vasopressors (p = 0.022) were significantly associated with maternal mortality, while mechanical ventilation (p = 0.025), PPH (p = 0.031), and blood and/or blood products transfusion (p = 0.003) were significantly associated with prolonged ICU stay. In the modified Poisson regression analysis, mechanical ventilation (adjusted prevalence ratio [aPR] = 2.3; 95% CI: 1.11-4.64; p = 0.025) and blood and/or blood product transfusion (aPR = 1.9; 95% CI: 1.23-2.80; p = 0.003) were independently and significantly associated with prolonged ICU stay.

This study demonstrates that PPH was the most common indication for maternal ICU admission following obstetric surgery. The overall maternal mortality was 16.5% with 40.9% of patients experience prolonged ICU stay. The need for mechanical ventilation, vasoactive support and blood transfusion were significantly associated with poor maternal outcomes.

Healthcare providers should strengthen antenatal identification and correction of risk factors for PPH, including anaemia, previous PPH, multiple pregnancy, and hypertensive disorders of pregnancy during antenatal care visits (ANC); ensure appropriate prophylactic uterotonics administration during delivery; and promote early detection and timely, multidisciplinary management of PPH to reduce severe maternal morbidity, ICU admission, and maternal mortality. In addition, hospital management should strengthen early recognition and timely management of critically ill obstetric patients to reduce maternal mortality and prolonged ICU stay.

INTRODUCTION

Maternal ICU admission provides advanced, lifesaving medical care for pregnant or postpartum women facing severe, life-threatening complications or who are critically ill (1).

Maternal ICU admission is a critical sign used to measure maternal morbidity and is applicable in local surveillance and quality assurance activities related to pregnancy and puerperium (1) (2). The burden of obstetric critical illness requiring ICU admission is higher in LMICs than in high-income countries. The higher burden of critically ill obstetric patients requiring ICU admission in LMICs compared with HICs may be attributed to a higher burden of severe obstetric complications, delayed healthcare-seeking and referral, limited access to timely emergency obstetric care, inadequate critical-care infrastructure and resources, and limited availability of intermediate-care facilities (3). Most critically ill obstetric patients admitted to the ICU have undergone major surgical interventions such as cesarean sections, hysterectomies and explorative laparotomies (4).

Maternal ICU admission after obstetric surgery can occur due to multiple indications, which are pregnancy and non-pregnancy-related. According to the WHO, 10% of women who undergo CS will need exceptional care in a high-dependency unit (HDU) during the postoperative period, and ICU transfers account for 3.5% of them (5). Common reasons for maternal ICU admission following obstetric surgeries include, PPH, hypertensive disorders of pregnancy and sepsis (6). In Sub-Saharan Africa, PPH, eclampsia, sepsis and septic shock are the most common indications for ICU admission following obstetric surgeries (7) (8) (9). General anesthesia-related complications, such as difficult or failed intubation and anaphylaxis during obstetric surgery, may result in maternal ICU admission (10). High spinal and total spinal anaesthesia are other anesthesia-related complications that may occur during obstetric surgery and may cause maternal ICU admission (11).

Following maternal ICU admission after obstetric surgeries, different outcomes may occur for both the mother and the baby. Globally, maternal mortality among women admitted to ICU following obstetric surgeries varies substantially across settings, with considerably higher mortality reported in LMICs than in HICs. A recent systematic review reported pooled ICU mortality of 12.4% in LMICs compared with 1.4% in HICs (12) (13) (14). Maternal mortality following obstetric surgeries and ICU admission in Sub-Saharan Africa remains an urgent public health crisis. Data indicates that maternal mortality rates among obstetric patients admitted to ICUs after different surgical interventions ranges from 26% to 49% (15) (16).

Prolonged ICU stay and adverse neonatal outcomes are among the reported outcomes associated with maternal ICU admission following obstetric surgery (17) (18).

In Tanzania, different studies have shown multiple factors associated with Maternal ICU admission after obstetric surgeries and the complications encountered. According to a study conducted at Tumbi Regional Referral Hospital, PPH was the most common indication for maternal ICU admission after cesarean section (19). From the study done at KCMC, preoperative anemia, grand multiparity, and longer surgical times were linked to more complications, which result in maternal ICU admission post-cesarean section (20).

Although MNH has a separate maternal ICU to treat critically ill obstetric patients admitted from the hospital's maternal wards, theatres, and referrals from other hospitals, little is known about the factors that influence maternal ICU admission and the clinical outcomes following obstetric surgeries.

METHODS

Study design and settings

This study employed a prospective cross-sectional study design that aimed at assessing factors associated with the clinical outcomes of maternal intensive care unit admission following obstetric surgeries at MNH, Tanzania. This study was conducted in the Obstetric ICU of MNH-Upanga in Dar es Salaam, Tanzania. MNH serves as a National Referral Hospital, Research Center, and University teaching Hospital with a 1,500-bed capacity and 2,000 outpatients per day. MNH provides comprehensive maternal healthcare services and has a dedicated ICU for obstetric patients, making it an ideal site for evaluating maternal ICU admissions following obstetric surgeries. The maternal ICU has eight beds, each with a bedside monitor and a working mechanical ventilator. Twenty-three Nurses are stationed in the unit with different levels of education (Master's degree -1, Degree 6, Diploma -10, Attendants-6). Data were collected by the principal investigator and a trained research assistant. This reflects the critical nature of severe obstetric bleeding, which can rapidly lead to hypovolemic shock, coagulopathy, and multiorgan failure, requiring ICU admission for close management and monitoring. This finding aligns with the study conducted in India by Ashakiran et al. (22), who found similar results, with PPH as the leading cause of maternal ICU admission (44.05%). However, these results contrast with those of a study from Nigeria by Tobi et al. (7), where PPH contributed to 6.3% of maternal ICU admission. Studies conducted in tertiary referral hospitals may report higher proportions due to the concentration of severe cases, while variations in the availability of blood transfusion services, timely obstetric interventions, and ICU admission criteria also play significant roles. PPH remains a major contributor to maternal ICU admissions following obstetric surgery, indicating gaps in pre-operative screening during ANC visits, early recognition and timely management of the condition. Strengthening ANC visits screening and detection of women at risk of PPH and early referral for care, intra and post-operative vigilance, early use of uterotonics and tranexamic acid, and improving access to blood transfusion services are essential to reduce the progression to severe hemorrhage and ICU admission. Preeclampsia: Preeclampsia was the second most common indication for maternal ICU admission in our study, contributing to 16.5% of all cases admitted. This finding aligns with the study conducted in Nigeria by Asudo et al.(23), which reported similar results, where preeclampsia contributed to 20.1% of cases admitted to the ICU. This finding is contrary to the findings of Keizer et al. (24) in the Netherlands, where preeclampsia contributed to 62.0% (higher proportion compared to our findings) of all maternal cases admitted to the ICU. The discrepancy in findings could be accounted for by differences in study design, as the study by Keizer was a 12 years retrospective study, which used a larger sample size than our study. Clinically, these findings highlight the need to strengthen the early identification and optimal perioperative management of hypertensive disorders during pregnancy. Ensuring effective antenatal screening, timely blood pressure control, appropriate use of magnesium sulfate for seizure prophylaxis, and close intraoperative and postoperative monitoring are critical in preventing the progression to severe complications requiring ICU care. Eclampsia: Eclampsia accounted for 14.8% of ICU admissions in this study, highlighting the continued burden of poorly controlled hypertensive disorders during pregnancy. This proportion is higher than that reported in Iran (5.7%) by Farzi et al. (25) but considerably lower than the findings from Nigeria (54.3%) by Tobi et al. (7), suggesting that differences in antenatal care coverage, timeliness of intervention, and ICU admission practices may influence these variations.

Clinically, these findings underscore the need to strengthen the early detection and effective management of hypertensive disorders before progression to eclampsia. Ensuring routine antenatal surveillance, timely initiation of antihypertensive therapy, and appropriate use of magnesium sulfate for seizure prevention are essential. Furthermore, improved perioperative monitoring, rapid stabilization of patients with severe preeclampsia, and timely surgical decision-making may reduce the progression to eclampsia and subsequent ICU admission.

Sepsis:

Sepsis was another major indication for ICU admission in this study, accounting for 10.4% of all admitted patients, indicating its important contribution to postoperative maternal morbidity.

This is somewhat consistent with a study conducted in the United Kingdom by Acosta et al.

(26), where sepsis accounted for 14.4% of maternal ICU admissions. However, the findings of the current study differ from those of a study conducted in Portugal by Oliveira et al. (27), where a higher percentage (20.4%) of maternal ICU admissions due to sepsis was observed. Another study in South Africa by Lafon et al. (28) found that sepsis post-CS accounted for 50% of all patients admitted to the ICU. These discrepancies found in these studies could be attributed to the fact that Oliveira et al. involved both antepartum and postpartum patients admitted to the ICU, while Lafon et al. involved only patients with sepsis after CS, admitted to the ICU. Clinically, these findings emphasize the need for strict aseptic techniques, timely use of antibiotics, and close postoperative monitoring to prevent progression to severe sepsis, which requires ICU admission. Anesthesia-related complications: Anesthesia-related complications were identified as a collective indication for ICU admission, encompassing complications from general anesthesia, total spinal anesthesia, and high spinal anesthesia, which contributed to 7.8% of maternal ICU admissions. This is contrary to the study conducted in Australia by Stephen et al. (29), where 12.6% of maternal ICU admissions were due to anesthesia-related complications. However, a study from Columbia by Carrasquilla et al. (30) reported a higher percentage (29.9%), similar to the study conducted in Nigeria by Amarengiaye et al. (31), where 50% of ICU admissions were due to general anesthesia-related complications. Discrepancies found in these studies could be attributed to the facts that, a study by Stephen et al was a 10 years retrospective study, with 126 cases involved, but Carrasquilla et al study involves only preeclampsia patient with severe criteria, however Amarengiaye et al study focused on anaesthesia related complications following CS necessitating ICU admission.

Clinically, these findings highlight the importance of strengthening safe obstetric anaesthesia through adequate training, appropriate monitoring, and preference for safer techniques, such as regional anaesthesia, where feasible. Improving airway management skills, ensuring the availability of essential equipment, and enhancing perioperative vigilance are critical for reducing anesthesia-related complications and subsequent ICU admissions.

Diabetic Ketoacidosis (DKA):

Although relatively uncommon, diabetic ketoacidosis represents a serious metabolic indication for maternal ICU admission. It is frequently associated with precipitating factors such as infection and the physiological stress of delivery, including CS, and often requires intensive monitoring and critical-care support. In this study, DKA accounted for 1.7% of maternal ICU admissions. Similarly, a study conducted in California by Hersh et al. (32) found that 2.0% of patients with type 1 diabetes mellitus and DKA during pregnancy were admitted to the ICU.

This study by Hersh et al. was an 11 years retrospective-cohort that involved 786 patients with type 1 diabetes mellitus and DKA, whereby DKA was reported to be one of the causes of maternal ICU admission during pregnancy. Clinically, these findings highlight the importance of optimal glycemic control, early identification of high-risk patients, and close perioperative monitoring of these patients. Prompt recognition and timely management of metabolic derangements are essential to prevent the progression to severe illness requiring ICU admission.

HELLP Syndrome:

HELLP syndrome was a rare indication for ICU admission in this study, contributing to 0.9% of cases admitted to the maternal ICU. This finding aligns with the study conducted in Iran by Although relatively uncommon, HELLP syndrome is a life-threatening condition associated with significant maternal complications and ICU admissions. Clinically, these findings highlight the importance of early recognition of severe preeclampsia, timely intervention, and close perioperative monitoring to prevent the progression to HELLP syndrome and reduce ICU admissions.

Pulmonary embolism:

Pulmonary embolism was an infrequent indication, contributed to 0.9% of maternal ICU admission following obstetric surgery. This finding aligns with the study conducted in Korea by Yi et al. (34), whereby 2.2% of maternal ICU admissions were due to pulmonary embolism.

However, the findings of the current study differ from those of a study conducted in Austria by Farr et al. (35)in which 4.2% of maternal ICU admissions were due to pulmonary embolism, and from those of another study in Italy by Vargas et al. (36), in which pulmonary embolism accounted for 7% of maternal ICU admissions. These discrepancies could be attributed to the fact that, Yi et al involved 176-sample size, while Farr et al involved 238-sample size, which with 84.5% of cases underwent CS, but Vargas et al involved 66 cases of which 95% were post CS. Clinically, these findings emphasize the importance of routine risk stratification, use of thromboprophylaxis in high-risk patients, early mobilization, and prompt recognition and management to reduce ICU admissions and improve maternal outcomes Cardiac disease: Cardiac disease was also a rare indication for maternal ICU admission in this study, contributing to 0.9% of all cases admitted to the maternal ICU. This proportion is lower than that reported by a study conducted in South Africa by Motiang et al. (37), in which 44% of ICU admissions were due to pre-existing cardiac disease. Similarly, a study conducted in North Carolina by Small et al. (38) reported that 36% of 86 women admitted to the ICU were due to cardiac disease. In addition, a study conducted in China by Zhang et al. (39) reported that 12% of maternal ICU admissions were due to cardiac disorders. Discrepancies observed in these studies could be attributed to the fact that Motiang et al. involved 210 women, delivered by CS (88.6%) and by normal vaginal delivery; Small et al. used a retrospective study design, while Zhang et al. involved 133 cases, of which 73.7% were post-CS. This wide variation likely reflects differences in the underlying prevalence of maternal heart disease, referral patterns, and ICU admission criteria across settings. Clinically, these findings highlight the importance of early identification of high-risk women with cardiac disease, multidisciplinary management, and careful perioperative monitoring to prevent decompensation and reduce the need for ICU admission. Clinical outcomes; Mortality: The maternal ICU mortality observed in this study was 16.5%, reflecting the significant burden of severe maternal complications among women admitted to the ICU after obstetric surgery. This finding is similar to other studies from LMICs; studies from Nigeria by Tobi et al. (7), demonstrated an overall ICU maternal mortality of 23.2%, from Malawi, by Prin et al. 41% (40) and from Ghana, by Anane-Fenin et al. 26% (41). However, studies from HICs, reported lower maternal mortality compared with these findings from LMICs. For instance, a study from Netherlands by Zwart el al. (42) reported maternal mortality of 3.5% and a study from United Kingdom by Harrison et al. (43)reported maternal mortality of 4.2%. The higher maternal mortality in LMICs compared with HICs is multifactorial, reflecting inadequate and delayed antenatal care and identification of high-risk pregnancies, delays in accessing and receiving skilled intrapartum and emergency obstetric care, and, following delivery or obstetric surgery, delayed ICU admission (for those who are critically ill) and limited availability of critical-care resources, including blood products, mechanical ventilation, vasoactive support and adequately trained multidisciplinary teams. Clinically, this mortality proportion underscores the need to strengthen the continuum of maternal care through improved ANC attendance and early identification of high-risk pregnancies, early recognition and timely management of obstetric complications, particularly at peripheral facilities, improving referral systems, ensuring rapid stabilization prior to transfer, and expanding access to critical care services are essential strategies for reducing maternal mortality. Prolonged ICU stay: The median duration of ICU stay in this study was 5 days, with 40.9% of patients experiencing prolonged ICU stay (> 5 days), indicating a considerable burden on critical care resources. Evidence in the literature regarding duration of ICU stay post surgery is conflicting, a study from South Africa by Motiang et al. (37) reported that the median length of ICU stay was 24 hours. While a study conducted in Bangladesh by Khanum et al. (44)reported that most patients stayed in the ICU for up to two days. Discrepancies from these studies could be attributed to the fact that, in Motiang et al. study, most of the patients (87.1%) were hemodynamically stable and needed minimal intervention in the ICU, but Khanum et al. used a retrospective study design and only 105 patients were involved. The longer ICU stay observed in this study may be attributed to several factors, including delayed presentation, severity of obstetric complications such as hemorrhage and sepsis, and the need for prolonged organ support, such as mechanical ventilation and transfusion. However, a study from Turkey by Agacayak et al.

(45)reported that the average length of stay in the ICU was 7 days due to transfusions requirements, which is higher than our study findings.

In this study, 40.9% of patient experienced prolonged ICU stay, similar to Wang et al. (46) reported that 39.5% of critically ill obstetric patients had a prolonged ICU stay. Clinically, prolonged ICU stay has important implications, including an increased risk of hospitalacquired complications, higher healthcare costs, and greater demand for critical care resources.

These findings highlight the need to strengthen the early management of obstetric emergencies, optimize perioperative care, and enhance timely stabilization to reduce the severity of illness at ICU admission and subsequently shorten the ICU stay.

i. The study was conducted at one tertiary hospital, which may limit generalizability to lower-level health facilities ii. Another limitation is since the study is cross-sectional, makes it impossible to definitively determine causal-effect relationships between the variables studied iii.

CONCLUSION

The findings of this study revealed that PPH was the most common indication for maternal ICU admission following obstetric surgery, accounting for 46.1% of admissions. Furthermore, Prolonged ICU stay was observed in 40.9% of patients, while the overall maternal mortality was 16.5%. Mechanical ventilation and blood and/or blood product transfusion were significantly associated with prolonged ICU stay. In addition, mechanical ventilation and the use of inotropes and/or vasopressors were significantly associated with maternal mortality.

Overall, these findings indicate a substantial burden of severe maternal morbidity and mortality among women requiring ICU admission after obstetric surgeries. Clinically, the predominance of hemorrhagic complications and the association of advanced life support interventions with poor outcomes highlight the need for early recognition of risk factors of PPH during ANC visits, timely recognition and management of obstetric emergencies, optimized perioperative monitoring, and strengthened critical care services to improve maternal outcomes.
