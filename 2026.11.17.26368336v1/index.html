To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 10 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Abstract

Importance: Accelerated protocols have emerged over the past two decades as a promising method of transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) administration that allows for shorter treatment duration and faster time to response. Although recent research has suggested accelerated TMS may be noninferior to daily TMS on clinical outcome measures, no studies have directly compared the neural effects of daily and accelerated TMS within the same participant group.

Objective: Compare the neural effects of daily vs accelerated TMS applied to a personalized default mode network (DMN) target on bilateral parietal DMN connectivity in a transdiagnostic sample (n=36) before and after TMS.

Design: Within-subjects crossover design.

[div-01]

Trial Registration: NCT07155096 (https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT07155096), NCT06389266 (https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06389266)

Introduction

Over the past forty years, transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) has emerged as a revolutionary treatment option for a range of psychiatric disorders, notably treatment refractory major depressive disorder (MDD) and obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD). Up to 58% of patients who receive TMS treatment for MDD show clinical improvement (1), and TMS for OCD consistently elicits higher response rates than sham stimulation (2). Thus, TMS provides a valuable treatment alternative for patients who have had limited success with prior medications and psychotherapies.

Despite the established clinical benefits of TMS, traditional protocols require patients to attend daily sessions of TMS over the course of many weeks, placing considerable logistic and financial demands on patients and families (3, 4). In 2008, the first Food and Drug Administration (FDA)cleared protocol for MDD required patients to attend one session of TMS per day, five days per week, for 4-6 weeks (5). Over a decade later, TMS gained FDA clearance for the treatment of OCD with a 6-week treatment course of Monday-Friday daily TMS (6). Amidst these FDA approvals, the need for shorter treatment durations resulted in several clinical trials investigating "accelerated" protocols, which have transformed the delivery of TMS (7, 8). Rather than administering TMS daily for weeks at a time, accelerated protocols administer multiple TMS sessions each day, which allows for shorter treatment courses and faster time to response.

Although accelerated TMS protocols date back to 2001 (9), they became increasingly common in the mid 2010s in novel trials testing TMS applied to the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) for individuals with treatment-resistant depression (10, 11).

The first randomized, head-to-head comparison of accelerated versus standard TMS was published in 2018 by Fitzgerald et al. (12). In this comparison, outpatients with MDD were randomized to accelerated TMS (3 sessions/day tapered over 3 weeks, 63,000 total pulses) or standard TMS (once daily x 4 weeks, 63,000 total pulses). Authors observed no significant differences in response rates, remission rates, or depression score reduction between groups, indicating that accelerated TMS may be clinically comparable to traditional daily schedules.

The publication of the Stanford Accelerated Intelligent Neuromodulation Accelerated TMS (SNT) protocol further advanced accelerated TMS, with 10 intermittent theta burst stimulation (iTBS) sessions per day delivered over 5 consecutive days for the treatment of MDD (7, 8). This finding demonstrated the tolerability and effectiveness of TMS at high dosages over shorter periods of time compared to standard, daily TMS.

To further examine the clinical outcomes of accelerated versus daily TMS, Hanlon et al performed a multisite, randomized, blinded non-inferiority trial (n=104) comparing accelerated iTBS (5 sessions/day x 6 days with H1 coil, 1800 pulses/session) to standard once/daily 18 Hz TMS (1980 pulses/day x 4 weeks with H1 coil) (13). They observed accelerated iTBS was noninferior to daily 18 Hz TMS based on change in depression scores and Week 6 response and remission rates. Moreover, accelerated treatment achieved a faster time to remission (21 vs. 28 days, p=.03). Analysis of patient-reported outcome measures from the same trial also found comparable improvements in depression, anxiety, and quality of life (14). Most recently, Apostol and colleagues published a retrospective comparison of accelerated TMS (5 sessions/day x 5 days, n=40) compared to standard once-daily TMS for at least 30 days (n=135) and found no differences in PHQ-9 scores between the two groups (15). Importantly, the accelerated iTBS group had a shorter time to response (11 vs. 16 days, p=.04), demonstrating further clinical benefit of accelerated TMS.

In summary, a growing body of evidence suggests that accelerated TMS administration is noninferior to standard daily administration for clinical outcome measures. However, a major gap in the field exists: no studies have directly compared the neural effects of accelerated and standard daily TMS protocols in the same trial, a gap highlighted by experts in the field (16).

Although accelerated TMS protocols have shown transdiagnostic efficacy across psychiatric and substance use disorders (17, 18), the extant literature has focused on TMS applied to the left DLPFC for depression (19). To be explicit, no studies have compared accelerated vs. daily TMS protocols in psychiatric disorders outside of depression or to alternative TMS targets. Therefore, we compared the effects of accelerated versus daily TMS applied to a personalized, parietal TMS target in a transdiagnostic sample of individuals with a variety of psychiatric disorders and substance use (n=36, Figure 1) in a secondary analysis of data from two clinical trials.

Although there are many ways to compare the neural effects of accelerated and daily TMS protocols, we selected a neural outcome (bilateral parietal connectivity) that we have shown in multiple prior studies is modulated by single and multiple sessions of parietal TMS and is associated with behavior change (20, 21). This analysis fills a critical gap in the TMS literature and clinical practice. If the neural effects of accelerated and daily TMS are comparable, this would provide the first neurobiological evidence suggesting it is the cumulative dose of TMS, rather than treatment schedule, that determines the neural effects of TMS.

Methods

Participants:

A transdiagnostic sample of 36 individuals aged 18-65 were enrolled (Table 1, Supplemental Table 1). Participants were recruited in two diagnostic groups: 1) Psychosis and 2) Non-psychosis (NCT06389266, NCT07155096). Individuals in the psychosis group had a DSM-5 diagnosis of schizophrenia or schizoaffective disorder, while the non-psychosis group had no lifetime history of psychosis but had other psychiatric disorders (e.g., depression, anxiety, attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, see Supplemental Table 2 for TMS applied using a MagPro X100 stimulator and active figure-of-8 coil (Cool B65, MagVenture, Denmark) held tangentially to the scalp with the handle at 45 degrees. TMS was applied in the standard theta-burst pattern (3 pulses at 50-Hz repeated at a rate of 5-Hz for 600 pulses at 100% AMT, details in Supplement) (22). Both daily and accelerated DMN-Targeted cTBS were applied to an individualized left parietal DMN target (see Individualized DMN Target). Accelerated TMS was administered in one-day with a 30-minute inter-session interval.

Daily and accelerated TMS occurred at least 3 months apart to avoid carryover effects (mean =379.26 days, range: min = 121 days, max = 972 days), consistent with prior literature (23).

Individualized DMN Targeting: To identify an individualized DMN map for TMS targeting, a standard DMN template (24) was warped into native space and applied to the participant's pre-TMS scan. In each participant, the resultant connectivity maps yielded a correlation cluster in the left posterior inferior parietal lobule (IPL). A target was placed in the averaged center of the left posterior IPL correlation cluster (formed from the overlay of the left posterior IPL clusters derived from connectivity maps) on the cortical surface using Brainsight neuronavigation software (Rogue Research, Inc.). This individualized left parietal DMN region served as the TMS target for both daily and accelerated TMS.

Region-of-Interest (ROI) Neuroimaging Analysis:

To compare neural effects of accelerated and daily TMS protocols, we selected a neural outcome (bilateral parietal connectivity) that we have shown in multiple prior studies is modulated by single and multiple sessions of personalized, parietal TMS and is associated with behavior change (20, 21). To calculate bilateral parietal connectivity, we extracted time courses from a 6mm sphere placed at our left lateral parietal DMN TMS target (x = -46, y = -66, z = +30) (Raichle 2011) and the right parietal DMN region we have previously linked to TMS-induced neural change and behavior change (25) The BOLD signal time courses from ROIs were correlated with each other and z-transformed to generate ROI-to-ROI connectivity values for analysis. See Supplemental Methods for Details.

Statistical Analysis

To compare DMN connectivity before and after TMS, linear mixed models were used to predict bilateral parietal DMN connectivity based on time, group, TMS schedule (accelerated vs daily), and age with a random effect for subject. All analyses were conducted in RStudio version 2025.09.2. See Supplemental Tables 3-7 for details.

Results

Thirty-four participants completed both daily and accelerated DMN-targeted cTBS and were included in analysis. Nineteen participants were diagnosed with psychotic disorders, and fifteen were diagnosed with non-psychotic psychiatric disorders (Supplemental Table 2). No serious adverse events were reported (see Supplemental Tables 8 9for minor adverse events).

DMN-Targeted cTBS Reduces Bilateral Parietal DMN Connectivity

In a model predicting bilateral parietal DMN connectivity based on visit (pre-or post-TMS) and a random effect for subject, bilateral parietal DMN connectivity was significantly reduced following DMN-targeted cTBS administration across all participants and both treatment schedules (t(90.16)=-2.07, p=0.0416) (Figure 2A, Supplemental Table 3). In a separate linear mixed effects model examining bilateral parietal DMN connectivity change based on group (psychosis vs non-psychosis), there was no effect of group (t(28.34)=-0.53, p=0.601) on DMN connectivity change (Figure 2B, Supplemental Table 4).

Neural Effects of TMS Do Not Differ by TMS Administration Schedule

To test the effects of TMS schedule (daily vs accelerated) on bilateral parietal DMN connectivity change, a linear mixed effects model was used which modeled bilateral parietal DMN connectivity change based on the pre-TMS bilateral parietal DMN connectivity and TMS schedule while including a random effect for subject. TMS schedule (t(31.27)= -0.44, p=0.67, Cohen's d = 0.12, 95% CI [-0.45, 0.69]) was not a significant predictor of bilateral parietal DMN connectivity change, indicating that no significant differences in connectivity change were observed between accelerated and daily TMS schedules (Figure 3A). Only pre-TMS bilateral parietal DMN connectivity predicted post-TMS bilateral parietal DMN connectivity change (t(55.94)=-7.82, p<0.001) (Supplemental Table 5).

Diagnosis did not interact with TMS schedule (accelerated TMS vs daily TMS) to affect bilateral parietal DMN connectivity change. In a model predicting bilateral parietal DMN connectivity change based on pre-TMS bilateral parietal DMN connectivity and the TMS schedule*group interaction, TMS schedule (t(28.10)=-1.09, p=0.284), group (t(44.52)=-1.01, p=0.318), and the TMS schedule*group (t(26.73)=1.07, p=0.292), interaction were not significant predictors of connectivity change; only pre-TMS bilateral parietal DMN connectivity predicted post-TMS bilateral parietal DMN connectivity change (t(53.97)=-7.75, p<0.001) (Figure 3B, Supplemental Table 6).

Neural Effects of TMS May Differ by Age

In an exploratory analysis to investigate the effects of age on neural response to TMS, bilateral parietal DMN connectivity change was modeled based on pre-TMS bilateral parietal DMN connectivity, TMS schedule, group, and age while including a random effect for subject. Age (t(26.91)=-2.21, p=0.036) and pre-TMS bilateral parietal DMN connectivity (t(53.28)=-7.97, p<0.001) were both significant predictors of bilateral parietal DMN connectivity change following TMS. Older age was associated with greater reductions in bilateral parietal DMN connectivity across all participants and both treatment schedules (Figure 4A & 4B, Supplemental Table 7).

Discussion

Accelerated TMS has been heralded since the publication of Stanford Neuromodulation Therapy in 2020 (7, 8). Growing evidence suggests that accelerated TMS protocols are noninferior to daily administration for clinical outcomes. However, no studies have compared the neural effects of accelerated versus daily TMS administration. This is the first study comparing neural effects of accelerated versus daily TMS administration.

We used a within-subjects crossover design in a transdiagnostic psychiatric sample and observed that TMS schedule was not associated with connectivity change, suggesting that accelerated and daily TMS may have similar neural effects.

Our findings are consistent with the growing body of literature suggesting that accelerated and daily TMS protocols may have comparable effects. Several studies have shown that accelerated and daily TMS have comparable anti-depressant effects. Our study now suggests that the comparable clinical effects may be due to comparable neural effects of accelerated and daily TMS schedules. This also supports a broader principle in TMS treatment: the total dose of stimulation determines response, rather than the schedule on which it is administered. If this principle is true, it would follow that spaced TMS (i.e., less than daily) would also have the same effects as daily TMS. This was tested by Galletly et al, who compared spaced TMS (3 days/week x 6 weeks) to daily TMS (5 days/week x 4 weeks) for major depression (26). At the end of treatment, they observed no differences in antidepressant response between groups, suggesting response was determined by was total number of treatments, rather than treatment schedule. However, they found that at 4 weeks, the daily group (which had already received 20 treatments compared to the spaced group, which had only received only 12) had a faster response. This suggests that response to TMS may be predicted by number of sessions rather than administration schedule, whether daily, accelerated, or spaced.

Across all diagnoses and both treatment schedules, older age was associated with greater reductions in bilateral parietal DMN connectivity following TMS. While exploratory, this finding is consistent with previous literature which associates higher age with greater improvement in depression severity following TMS (27) (28) (29). Given evidence for age related changes in DMN connectivity (30), it is possible that TMS treatment response may differ between older and younger adults. However, future studies are needed to directly examine the role of age on treatment efficacy and to identify the neural mechanism underlying these changes. This study has several major strengths. The primary strength of this study was its within-subjects crossover design that compares the neural effects of accelerated versus daily TMS administration within the same participant, thereby minimizing confounding from between-subjects factors.

Retention of highly symptomatic individuals across two interventions with repeat neuroimaging and an extended washout period (mean =379.26 days) was another major strength. Each participant received four MRI scans, providing dense within-subject sampling. Furthermore, individualized TMS targets were created from each participant's resting state functional MRI scans, ensuring that stimulation was personalized to each participant's DMN. This approach represents a unique methodological strength, as recent evidence suggests that personalized, connectivity-based targeting may lead to greater clinical benefit than scalp landmark-based targeting (17). Another strength of this study was the use of a transdiagnostic psychiatric sample that included individuals with psychotic, mood, anxiety, and substance use disorders, an approach used in other TMS trials (31) (32) (33) (34) (35). Given evidence that accelerated TMS has transdiagnostic effects (18), our analysis provides evidence that the neural effects of accelerated and daily TMS administration do not differ regardless of diagnosis. However, our sample was not powered to investigate diagnostic differences, so this should be explored in future studies.

Our study also has several limitations. First, we investigated the effects of five sessions of TMS, and it is possible that neural effects between accelerated and daily TMS may differ after more sessions. However, testing a smaller number of sessions, where the duration of TMS effect is shorter allowed us to conduct this unique crossover design trial. It is important to note that this study was conducted using a fixed-order design where each participant received daily TMS before accelerated TMS. Although we cannot fully exclude the possibility that treatment order contributed to the results, the three-month minimum washout period (mean 379.26 days) separating the two arms well exceeds the timeframe that TMS-induced functional connectivity changes are expected to persist. Previous work has demonstrated that neural effects are expected to normalize on a scale of hours to days following single sessions of cTBS (36). There is little evidence to suggest that 5 single sessions of TMS would persist beyond 3 months (37). However, to fully address this limitation, future studies using a counterbalanced crossover design are needed. An additional limitation is that this study was not powered as a formal noninferiority trial. Although no significant differences in neural outcomes were observed between accelerated and daily TMS, the relatively small sample size resulted in wide confidence intervals that do not exclude clinically meaningful differences in connectivity change. Larger, adequately powered noninferiority studies are needed to determine whether accelerated and daily TMS produce formally comparable neural outcomes within prespecified noninferiority margins.

Another limitation of our findings was the use of a personalized, network-based target. While our finding that accelerated and daily TMS may have similar neural effects is consistent with studies investigating the clinical outcomes from DLPFC-targeted TMS, it is possible that our findings are specific to TMS applied to the parietal cortex. Future studies should compare accelerated versus daily TMS administration using different TMS targets. Finally, while our transdiagnostic sample enhances the generalizability of our findings, it is also a limitation. It is possible that there are differences in accelerated versus daily TMS administration by diagnostic groups. It is also possible that differences in neural response differ person-to-person rather than by diagnostic group.

Due to the time and transportation barriers that many patients face with tedious, daily TMS treatment (3,4), accelerated TMS protocols offer the opportunity for increased convenience and access to care by reducing the number of treatment visits. In addition to shortening the treatment course, accelerated TMS protocols have been shown to expedite treatment response compared to traditional daily TMS protocols (12). Given the clear practical and clinical benefits of accelerated TMS, there has been increased interest in the therapeutic benefit of daily TMS schedules compared to accelerated TMS schedules. However, major questions remain about administration parameters for accelerated TMS, including the optimal number of pulses per session, number of sessions per day, and inter-session interval that should be explored in future studies (17, 19).

In conclusion, this is the first study to compare the neural effects of accelerated and daily TMS administration. MRI Acquisition & Processing: Imaging data were collected on 3.0-T Philips Intera Achieva MRI scanner (Philips Healthcare, Andover, MA). Participants completed MRIs the week before and after TMS. Post-TMS scans were scheduled within 8 days of TMS (daily mean 4.39 (SD 1.61) days; accelerated mean 4.91 (SD 1.38) days, p=0.153). Briefly, 1-mm 3 T1-weighted anatomical scans and multiple 10-minute functional runs were acquired (TR 2000ms, TE 28.0ms, flip angle 90 degrees, field of view = 240mm, 38 slices, 3-mm 3 voxels, anterior to posterior phase-encoded). Scans were processed using the same procedure described above. Details in supplement. After quality control, there were 126 pre-/post-TMS scans for 34 participants. TMS Protocol: In this crossover trial, individuals received five daily sessions of DMN-Targeted continuous theta burst stimulation (cTBS) and five accelerated sessions of DMN-targeted cTBS.

Figure 1. Daily vs Accelerated TMS Study Design. In this crossover trial, 36 individuals received five daily sessions of DMN-Targeted cTBS and five accelerated sessions of DMNtargeted cTBS with neuroimaging before and after each TMS intervention. Both daily and accelerated DMN-Targeted cTBS (600 pulses, 100% AMT) were applied to an individualized left parietal DMN target (see Individualized DMN Target in Supplement). Accelerated TMS was administered in one-day with a 30-minute inter-session interval. Daily and accelerated TMS occurred at least 3 months apart to avoid carryover effects (mean =379.26 days, range: min = 121 days, max = 972 days). Details in supplement.
