Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. Abstract

Adolescent psychopathology is commonly assessed using high-dimensional questionnaire data. These data often consist of ordinal, sparse, and overlapping symptom items, creating challenges for deriving latent phenotypes that are both interpretable and generalisable. This study addressed the need to derive lowdimensional transdiagnostic phenotypes from adolescent mental health data by evaluating denoising autoencoders as a flexible non-linear representation-learning approach.

Using baseline data from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development Study, we analysed mental health questionnaire data from a large community sample of adolescents (N = 9,799). A denoising autoencoder was trained to learn lowdimensional latent representations and was benchmarked against principal component analysis, exploratory factor analysis, and radial basis function kernel principal component analysis using held-out reconstruction explained variance. Interpretability was assessed using item-level canonical correlation analyses and alignment with a framework of well-established psychopathology dimensions. External validity was examined using gradient-boosted tree models predicting clinical and functional validators.

Across three to five factors solutions, the autoencoder consistently achieved the highest reconstruction explained variance compared with all benchmark methods. The five-factor autoencoder solution showed interpretable correspondence with established dimensional structures of psychopathology, rather than mapping onto a.

Introduction

Most mental disorders begin before adulthood, making preadolescence and early adolescence a significant period for identifying at-risk phenotypes (Solmi et al., 2022). At the same time, youth psychopathological disorders are usually comorbid, thus any attempts to link one diagnostic label to aetiology or neurobiology have been challenging (Caspi et al., 2014; Kieling et al., 2011; Lahey et al., 2021). A central challenge is therefore how to characterise emotional and behavioural problems, which are typically measured using many overlapping symptoms rather than a single summary score, in a lower-dimensional and analytically useful form.

Current frameworks increasingly emphasise dimensional and transdiagnostic descriptions of mental health disorders. These frameworks integrate symptoms into broader domains to better match the structure observed in data and account for comorbidity and overlap among symptoms. In doing so, these approaches aim to facilitate research by avoiding a focus on diagnostic categories which are likely not underpinned by clearly distinct pathology (Insel et al., 2010; Kotov et al., 2017). At a methodological level, dimensionality reduction methods such as exploratory factor analysis (EFA) have been used to find transdiagnostic factors (Michelini et al., 2019). EFA can transform high dimensional symptoms into lower dimensional latent phenotypes that allows to capture shared features, reduce redundancy, and support downstream mechanistic interpretability (Conway et al., 2019). Prior work has shown that childhood psychopathology can be organised into higher-order dimensions with meaningful external correlates using linear methods (Holmes et al., 2021; Michelini et al., 2019; Modabbernia et al., 2022). For example, Michelini et al. (2019) used hierarchical EFA to identify a hierarchy of dimensions in the ABCD study sample, showing meaningful associations with clinically relevant validators at different levels of this hierarchy.

Despite the value of dimensionality reduction methods, measures of youth psychopathology based on questionnaires pose several challenges for dimensionality reduction. First, item responses are often sparse, and skewed in many samples, which can mislead downstream factor models (Rhemtulla et al., 2012). Second, item sets often contain substantial redundancy, including highly similar or tightly coupled content that can complicate the recovery of a stable latent structure (Fried, 2017). Although redundancy is commonly addressed by dropping or collapsing items, such action can make latent solutions sensitive to preprocessing decisions. Third, dimensionality reduction also raises a core evaluation problem: the reduced dimension space should be stable, reproducible, or useful outside the sample from which they were derived. Nested cross-validation has long been recommended, because it separates hyperparameter search from held-out evaluation and reduces optimism in error estimates (Varma & Simon, 2006). This highlights the need to assess robustness across repeated runs, rather than relying on a single train-test split or a single crossvalidation run (Bouthillier et al., 2021; D'Amour et al., 2022).

Linear approaches have the limitations that they impose structural assumptions that may be restrictive when symptom relationships are shaped by thresholds, interactions, or other non-linear dependencies (Hinton & Salakhutdinov, 2006). Non-linear approaches therefore warrant explicit evaluation in youth mental health data, where questionnaire responses are often sparse, ordinal, and potentially heterogeneous in structure. Non-linear approaches such as kernel principal component analysis (kernel PCA) aim to capture curved or manifold structure by implicitly mapping inputs into a high dimensional feature space before performing PCA. It offers a flexible alternative when relationships are not well approximated by linear subspaces (Schölkopf et al., 1998). These approaches have different inductive biases, and in practice they may trade interpretability, robustness, and sensitivity to preprocessing decisions in different ways, providing essential benchmarking for mental health data dimension reduction.

Autoencoders provide a flexible representation learning approach to derive lowdimensional latent phenotypes from high-dimensional symptom data, with the aim of capturing potentially non-linear symptom relationships while reducing redundancy and preserving clinically meaningful variation (Hinton & Salakhutdinov, 2006; Milano et al., 2024; Ostojic et al., 2024). They consist of two parts: an encoder, which compresses the original input into a lower-dimensional latent space, and a decoder, which reconstructs the input from this compressed representation. Because these models are trained to preserve the most informative patterns needed for reconstruction, the learned latent representation can be used as a data-driven phenotype embedding (Hinton & Salakhutdinov, 2006). As a class of neural networks, autoencoders can represent nonlinear functions and are not restricted to orthogonal components or linear factor structure, potentially capturing patterns in symptom profiles that arise from interactions, thresholds, or other nonlinearities (Bengio et al., 2013).

In this study, we evaluate a novel, non-linear dimensionality-reduction pipeline for youth mental health phenotyping from survey items. Building on the dimensionality reduction and evaluation framework established by Michelini et al. (2019), we examine whether non-linear representation learning can derive low-dimensional phenotypes that are reconstructive and interpretable and benchmark this approach against PCA, EFA, and kernel PCA. Finally, we examine interpretability using canonical-correlation analysis (CCA)-based analyses to relate the learned phenotypes to the original survey items. Our results indicate that nonlinear representation learning can support the construction of reproducible low-dimensional phenotypes for developmental mental health research.

Methods

Participants

This study used data from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study, an ongoing multisite longitudinal cohort in the United States that recruited approximately 11,500 children through school systems at 21 research sites (Garavan et al., 2018). Participants were aged 9-10 years at the baseline assessment from 2016 to 2018 and are planned to be followed for approximately 10 years into early adulthood. The ABCD protocol includes harmonized neuroimaging acquisition across the 21 sites (Casey et al., 2018). Data were accessed via the NIMH Data Archive (NDA) using the ABCD Curated Annual Release 5.1 (DOI: 10.15154/z563-zd24). ABCD data collection procedures were reviewed and approved by a central institutional review board and, when applicable, local review boards, with written informed consent from caregivers and assent from children. Secondary use of the ABCD data complied with NDA data use requirements, and the present analyses received approval from an NIH Data Access Committee.

Measures

Psychopathology symptoms were assessed using the Child Behaviour Checklist (CBCL), a widely used dimensional measure of emotional and behavioural problems in children and adolescents (Achenbach & Ruffle, 2000). Parents rated symptom items at baseline using the standard three-point CBCL response format, with higher scores indicating greater symptom endorsement. For the present analyses, we extracted CBCL data and validator data collected at baseline. Item-level CBCL data were used as the input symptom features for dimensionality reduction analyses. Baseline validator data were extracted to assess the external validity of the derived latent phenotypes across clinical, cognitive, family, school, developmental, and peerrelated domains. To reduce familial aggregation and the non-independence arising from shared genetic and environmental factors in households with multiple enrolled children, we retained only one randomly selected child per family, after dropping missing data, yielding a final analytic sample of 9799 out of 11862.

Preprocessing

The measurement properties of questionnaire data require additional preprocessing of input data. Because questionnaire items are ordinal, association measures that explicitly model ordinality can be preferable to simple Pearson correlations. In practice, ordinal estimation strategies and the use of polychoric correlations have been shown to affect recovered dimensional structure, particularly when items have few categories or when distributions are skewed (Holgado-Tello et al., 2010; Rhemtulla et al., 2012). At the same time, high dimensional symptoms data often contain redundant items such as near duplicates or tightly coupled content clusters, which can complicate factor models.

Preprocessing was performed prior to model fitting. To reduce sparsity and to support ordinal association modelling, each CBCL item was screened for extremely low response frequency. Items were excluded if more than 99.5% of observed nonmissing responses were "No". All remaining items were treated as ordered categorical variables. A single polychoric correlation matrix was then estimated using pairwise handling of missing data, such that each correlation was computed from participants with non-missing responses on the corresponding item pair.

To reduce redundancy among retained items, a graph clustering approach was applied. A thresholded polychoric correlation matrix was constructed, in which items were connected when their pairwise association exceeded r > 0.75. Connected components were identified and each component was aggregated into a composite variable. For each participant, the composite value was computed as the mean of the component's item responses and was then rounded to the nearest integer. The original items contributing to each composite were removed, and the derived composites were appended to the dataset.

Benchmarking pipeline

A benchmarking pipeline was implemented to learn low dimensional latent scores from the CBCL feature matrix. We compared autoencoder with PCA, EFA, and kernel PCA using reconstruction performance evaluated on held out data. Analysis was done using Python v3.11.6, NumPy v1.23.5, and PyTorch v2.2.1. To promote reproducibility, we fixed random seeds across all packages, including CUDA where available, constrained thread usage, and enabled deterministic computation settings for the autoencoder where supported.

For autoencoder training and evaluation, the dataset was split into train, validation, and test partitions (80%/10%/10%). Within the training set, a nested cross-validation framework was employed for hyperparameter tuning and model selection. Specifically, the training data were further divided into outer and inner folds. The inner loop was used to optimise hyperparameters, while the outer loop provided an unbiased estimate of model performance during model development. The final model, with optimised hyperparameters, was retrained on the full training data and evaluated on the independent test set.

To minimise data leakage, feature standardisation was always estimated using training data only and then applied unchanged to validation and test data. Features were zscored based on the distribution of the training partition. Within cross-validation, this procedure was implemented separately in each fold using scikit-learn pipelines, ensuring that fold-specific training statistics were not informed by held-out data.

Parallel analyses were used to guide the number of factors, and all models were evaluated at target latent dimensionalities from 1 to 10, chosen to reflect a parsimonious range consistent with prior dimensional work in child psychopathology and to enable fair comparison across methods. For each target latent dimensionality 𝑑, EFA, PCA and kernel PCA were fit on the standardised training data. Reconstructions were obtained via the corresponding inverse mappings for PCA and kernel PCA, whereas EFA reconstructions were computed in the standardised space by backprojecting factor scores through the rotated loading matrix. The reconstruction explained variance ratio (EVR) was computed as:

Explained variance ratio = 1 -Sum of squared reconstruction errors Sum of squares of the original inputs

When necessary, hyperparameters were tuned with a randomised search strategy with cross-validation. Generalisation was assessed using a nested procedure with an outer 5-fold KFold, which separates model selection from performance estimation and reduces selection bias.

Autoencoder Model:

An autoencoder was implemented in PyTorch to learn a low dimensional latent representation from the input feature matrix X (tabular questionnaire or phenotype features) by minimizing reconstruction error using an encoder-decoder architecture (Hinton & Salakhutdinov, 2006). The autoencoder comprised a multilayer perceptron encoder with three fully connected (linear) layers that mapped the input to two hidden layers and then to a 𝑑-dimensional latent code. Gaussian error linear unit (GELU) nonlinearities were applied after the first two encoder layers. The decoder mirrored the encoder and used GELU nonlinearities after the first two decoder layers. The training objective was the MSE between the reconstructed output and the original uncorrupted inputs.

During training, denoising was induced via batch-swap noise, implemented by a feature-wise swapping of values across samples within each mini-batch with probability 0.1, while the reconstruction target remained the uncorrupted batch (Vincent et al., 2010). An optional decorrelation penalty was also tested. Within each mini-batch, the latent variables were mean-centred by subtracting the mean, and a covariance matrix was then computed from the centred latent representations to quantify dependence among latent dimensions, and the squared Frobenius norm of the off-diagonal elements was added to the loss with weight λ = 0.005 tuned over an empirically selected range from 0.001 to 0.01. The Adam optimizer was used with a learning rate of 0.003 and a L2 weight decay. Models were trained with a batch size of 32 for up to 1,000 epochs using a fixed training and validation split, with early stopping (patience = 10) based on validation. The learning rate was reduced by a factor of 10 when validation loss plateaued for 5 consecutive epochs. After training, latent factors, reconstructed inputs, reconstruction MSE per sample and EVR per factor were computed on held-out data.

Hyperparameters search strategy

Hidden-layer widths were searched over a discrete candidate set of 64 to 256 in increments of 32. We treated this architectural degree of freedom as a coarse design choice rather than an integer variable that required single-unit resolution. Restricting widths to meaningful discrete values reduced the effective dimensionality of the search space and improved the practicality of derivative-free optimization.

To identify a robust hyperparameter configuration for the final autoencoder model, we used 5-fold nested cross-validation. In each outer fold, hyperparameter optimization was performed using the corresponding training set and an inner validation procedure. Candidate configurations were proposed using a combination of the tree-structured Parzen estimator (TPE) sampler and covariance matrix adaptation evolution strategy (CMA-ES). The search procedure consisted of an initial broad search using TPE for 50 trials, followed by a more fine-grained optimisation using CMA-ES. Each candidate was evaluated using the prespecified reconstruction objective (EVR) computed on the inner validation data. This yielded one selected configuration per outer fold, together with its performance estimate on the held-out outer test partition.

Rather than selecting hyperparameters from the single outer fold with the highest outer test performance, which can introduce optimistic bias when test partitions influence model selection, we aggregated fold-wise selected hyperparameters across the five outer folds (Cawley & Talbot, 2010; Varma & Simon, 2006). For discrete architectural parameters, we selected the modal value across folds. For continuous hyperparameters, we summarized across folds using the median. This aggregation produced a final configuration intended to reflect stability across resamples rather than idiosyncrasies of a single split.

External Validation

To assess the external predictive validity of the latent representations, we replicated the analysis approach used by Michelini et al. (2019). Gradient boosting regression trees (GBRT) were used to model associations between latent factor scores and a set of external validators spanning clinical service use, medical history, medication use, family functioning, school-related measures, cognitive ability, developmental delays, and peer relationships. Clinical and health-related validators included parent-reported mental health service use, medical history, and medication use. Family and school validators included parent-and youth-reported measures of family functioning, school performance, and school-related difficulties. Cognitive validators were derived from baseline cognitive assessments, and developmental validators included parentreported developmental delay indicators. Peer-related validators captured social functioning and peer relationship difficulties. These validators were not used during dimensionality reduction and were included only in downstream external validity analyses.

GBRT were trained to predict each validator from the latent factor scores derived using the autoencoder and EFA, with model performance quantified using total R² (Nagelkerke R² for binary outcomes). GBRT was chosen for its ability to capture nonlinear relationships between predictor and target variables; given that the autoencoder method has the capacity to identify non-linear solutions, we may expect associations with external validators to also be non-linear. For each target dimensionality from 1 to 5, separate GBRT models were trained using latent factors derived from the autoencoder and EFA as predictors. For the GBRT models, we used the histogrambased gradient boosting implementation in scikit-learn. Specifically, the maximum number of boosting iterations was set to 100, corresponding to up to 100 trees; the learning rate was 0.10; the maximum number of leaf nodes per tree was 31; no explicit maximum tree depth was imposed; and L2 regularisation was set to 0. The minimum number of samples per leaf was 20. Hyperparameters were tuned using 5fold cross-validation within the training data, and the optimised model was then evaluated on held-out data. Prediction performance was evaluated out of sample using the total coefficient of determination R² , with Nagelkerke R² reported for binary validators. The same modelling framework and evaluation procedure were applied across all validators and representation types to ensure a fair comparison.

Interpretability

This study applied a multivariate association method, canonical correlation analysis (CCA), to seek linear combinations of latent space and original items that are maximally correlated, offering a natural framework for relating learned symptom phenotypes.

After hyperparameter selection, the final hyperparameter configuration was fixed and used to train the model repeatedly under multiple random seeds. For each run, we set the random seed across all packages and configured deterministic execution settings.

Within each seed, model fitting was performed using 5-fold cross-validation. To obtain a single model instance for interpretability, we selected the fold whose mean reconstruction error was closest to the median across folds to avoid selecting an unusually favourable split, and we extracted latent factors for the CCA dataset using the trained encoder.

CCA was implemented between original items and latent factors. Before fitting CCA, both observed features and latent factors were standardized within the CCA routine using sample standard deviations. The number of canonical components was set to the same as latent factors. Canonical correlations were computed as the Pearson correlation between paired canonical variates. Permutation testing was performed with 1000 permutations during the process. Variable loadings were defined as correlations between standardized variables and canonical variates, and the top observed items per canonical component were summarized by absolute loading magnitude.

To examine the clinical interpretability of the autoencoder-derived latent factors, we evaluated their alignment with established Hierarchical Taxonomy of Psychopathology (HiTOP) spectra (Conway et al., 2019; Kotov et al., 2017). Each latent factor was represented by its item-level loading or contribution profile. To provide an indication of how these dimensions map onto existing taxonomies, we calculated an approximate mapping from our dimensions onto HiTOP symptom spectra. Alignment was quantified by mapping each item in the CBCL to the closest HiTOP dimension based on its content. For each autoencoder-derived latent factor, we then summed the weights for items within each HiTOP dimension. This analysis was used descriptively and approximately to assess whether the learned latent space captured differentiated and clinically interpretable symptom configurations.

To assess the correspondence among dimensional solutions, Pearson correlations were calculated between latent dimensions derived from 5-factor solutions derived from the autoencoder, EFA, PCA, and kernel PCA models. To account for multiple testing across correlation analyses, p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate procedure, with statistical significance defined as FDR-adjusted q < 0.05.

Results

Parallel analyses tested factor solutions with dimensions up to 9 dimensions (supplementary Figure 1). Based on subsequent inspection of factor interpretability and pattern stability, the 3, 4, 5 solutions were retained for further comparison (supplementary Figure 2, 3, 4).

Across encoding dimensions 3 to 5, the autoencoder yielded the largest values of explained variance relative to all baseline methods, with its advantage remaining consistent at each evaluated dimensionality. Explained variance increased monotonically with higher encoding dimension for every method, but the autoencoder showed a fast rise, reaching approximately 0.42 at dimension 5. Among all selected kernels used for kernel PCA, the radial basis function kernel (RBF) kernel PCA provided the best performance, so only RBF kernel PCA is reported here. PCA and kernel PCA showed an intermediate range and across dimensions, with kernel PCA marginally higher than PCA at each setting. EFA produced the lowest explained variance throughout. Overall, the primary result is that the autoencoder achieves the highest total explained variance consistently across the examined encoding dimensions. The alignment between the autoencoder derived latent space and established psychopathology dimensions was assessed by projecting each latent factor approximately onto the HiTOP spectra. Descriptively, largely distinct correspondence patterns were observed across the five latent factors, indicating that the learned representations captured differentiated configurations of psychopathological features rather than a single undifferentiated dimension (Figure 3). However, we also observed some overlap across factors, indicating that they do not reflect entirely distinct dimensions. Factor 1 was primarily characterized by antagonism and disinhibition, forming a latent factor aligned with externalizing behavioural disorders and showing weaker representation of internalizing and somatoform dimensions. In contrast, factor 2 reflected a mixed antagonism and internalizing dimension, capturing the existence of oppositional tendencies and internal distress, while showing comparatively limited contributions from thought disorder and disinhibition, and thereby differentiates antagonism accompanied by negative emotions from disinhibitory patterns. Factor 3 showed a dominant association with the somatoform dimension, with minimal contributions from other spectra, indicating a latent factor based on bodily and somatic symptom expression. Factors 4 and 5 captured mixed latent features. Factor 4 was also characterized by combined antagonism and internalizing dimensions, but represented a broader configuration of internalizing and antagonistic content than factor 2, reflecting a combination of oppositional conflict and negative emotion.

Factor 5 was characterized by the somatoform and internalizing spectra, integrating bodily symptom expression with internal emotional distress. Together, these patterns demonstrate that the autoencoder latent space differentiated distinct configurations of psychopathological dimensions aligned with the HiTOP framework. These dimensions should therefore be interpreted as partially overlapping, data-driven representations with post-hoc correspondence to established psychopathology domains, rather than as discrete or one-to-one HiTOP spectra. The cross-method correlation analysis showed partial convergence between autoencoder-derived dimensions and dimensions obtained from EFA, PCA, and kernel PCA. Although several autoencoder-derived dimensions were moderately to strongly associated with dimensions from the comparison methods, the overall pattern did not suggest a simple one-to-one mapping. This indicates that the autoencoder solution captured related but non-identical representations of adolescent mental health symptoms. Full correlation heatmaps are reported in Supplementary Figure 6.

Predictive validity across external validators

To evaluate the predictive validity of the learned latent representations, we examined prediction performance on a held-out test set of external validators spanning mental health service use, medical history, medication use, family conflict, school connectedness, cognitive ability, academic performance, developmental delays, and peer relationships. Across all domains, predictive performance increased monotonically as a function of the number of latent factors, indicating that higher dimensional representations captured progressively more information relevant to downstream functional, clinical, and cognitive outcomes (Figure 4). This pattern was consistent with expectations from hierarchical models of childhood psychopathology (e.g., Michelini et al., 2019), in which broader dimensions account for substantial variance at low dimensionalities, with incremental gains achieved by higher dimensional factors.

Comparing representation learning approaches, autoencoder derived factors demonstrated equal or superior predictive validity relative to EFA across most validators, particularly at moderate to higher dimensionalities. The largest advantages of autoencoder were observed for cognitive and academic outcomes, including fluid intelligence, crystallized intelligence, and school grades, where autoencoder representations yielded consistently higher explained variance than EFA. For health service related and clinical history measures (e.g., mental health services use, medical history, medication use), both approaches showed comparable performance, with small but consistent gains for autoencoder at higher dimensions.

Importantly, no domain showed systematic superiority of EFA over autoencoder across dimensionalities, suggesting that the nonlinear representation learned by the autoencoder captured clinically and functionally meaningful structure beyond that obtained using linear factor models. Together, these results support the external validity of the learned latent dimensions and indicate that representation learning approaches provide a flexible and predictive framework for modelling hierarchical structure in childhood psychopathology. Both autoencoderand EFA-derived factors showed increasing explanatory power as the number of factors increased, and no validator domain demonstrated consistent superiority of EFA over autoencoder across the range of dimensionalities examined.

Discussion

This study evaluated whether denoising autoencoders could derive low-dimensional latent representations of youth psychopathology that are generalisable, interpretable, and externally valid. In this study, we addressed the need for more robust empirical evaluation of representation-learning approaches for transdiagnostic phenotyping via a denoising autoencoder and explicitly benchmarked this approach against commonly used linear and nonlinear alternatives within a held-out reconstruction and externalvalidation framework. Three main findings emerged. First, across the examined latent dimensionalities, the autoencoder achieved higher held-out reconstruction explained variance than PCA, EFA, and kernel PCA. Second, the learned dimensions showed interpretable item-level patterns, although these should be understood as data-driven, post-hoc mappings rather than direct one-to-one representations of HiTOP spectra or the original CBCL syndrome scales. Third, the autoencoder-derived dimensions showed external predictive validity across clinical, cognitive, family, school, developmental, and peer-related validators, with performance comparable to or modestly stronger than EFA across most domains.

Our findings suggest that representation learning with an autoencoder can capture structured, generalisable variance in youth symptom data that is not fully recovered by conventional linear factor models. One plausible explanation for the superior reconstruction performance is that the nonlinear encoder-decoder architecture can accommodate interactions, thresholds, and curved relationships among symptoms that violate linear factor assumptions, especially after ordinal-aware preprocessing. This interpretation is supported by prior work showing that autoencoders can represent complex structure in high-dimensional behavioural data without imposing orthogonality or simple covariance constraints (Bengio et al., 2013; Hinton & Salakhutdinov, 2006; Milano et al., 2024). Nonetheless, this substantial increase in explained variance within the questionnaire measures relative to linear approaches did not translate into an equal increase in variance explained within external validators, and future research could investigate the source of this discrepancy. For example, more directly testing how these subtle patterns within questionnaire responding may be used to predict external variances may be fruitful.

By training the autoencoder to reconstruct clean inputs from corrupted versions and by aggregating highly redundant items before model fitting, the learned latent space may emphasise stable, shared signal rather than item-specific noise. This could contribute to both improved generalisation and clearer alignment with broad psychopathology spectra, although the relative contribution of denoising versus architectural nonlinearity cannot be disentangled within the present design.

The differentiated HiTOP alignment across factors suggests that the learned representations capture more than a single broad dimension of psychopathology, but instead reflect configurations of higher-order dimensions, including mixed antagonism-internalizing and somatoform-internalizing patterns. This is consistent with hierarchical models proposing both broad general liability and partially independent spectra, rather than a single dominant dimension (Caspi et al., 2014; Kotov et al., 2017; Lahey et al., 2021).

Importantly, while these alignments support construct validity, they do not imply that the autoencoder directly estimates HiTOP dimensions; rather, the correspondence suggests convergent structure. The stronger predictive performance of autoencoder representations for cognitive and academic validators may indicate that nonlinear symptom embeddings can better capture behaviourally relevant variance that generalises beyond clinical service use or diagnostic proxies. This interpretation remains cautious, as prediction performance differences were modest and domainspecific, and causal mechanisms cannot be inferred from the present analyses.

The present findings are broadly consistent with the literature (Michelini et al., 2019) in showing monotonic gains in external validity with increasing dimensionality and in recovering dimensions aligned with antagonism, internalizing, and somatic symptom domains. A key contribution of this study is the explicit benchmarking of representation learning against conventional dimensionality reduction approaches under a held-out reconstruction framework, while using nested cross-validation and seed control to reduce optimism and underspecification. Prior applications of autoencoders in mental-health and health-record data have demonstrated their potential for phenotyping and prediction (Miotto et al., 2016), but often without direct comparison to factor models under equivalent evaluation conditions. The present results suggest that, when evaluated on generalisation rather than in-sample fit, autoencoders can recover representations that are at least as clinically meaningful as EFA while offering greater flexibility. The results do not imply that autoencoders replace factor analysis; rather, they highlight complementary inductive biases that may be advantageous for different inferential goals.

The findings also support the use of representation learning as a viable tool for constructing low-dimensional, generalisable symptom phenotypes in developmental mental health research, particularly when downstream validation and reproducibility are explicit goals. Autoencoder-derived latent factors may offer advantages for studies aiming to relate symptom structure to cognitive, educational, or biological measures, where nonlinear and interactive effects are plausible. However, these implications are supported only for the specific outcomes and dimensionalities examined here; broader clinical translation would require additional validation, including longitudinal prediction and replication in independent cohorts.

Our study has some limitations. All analyses relied on parent-reported CBCL data at a single baseline time point, limiting conclusions about developmental change or informant generalisability. This affects the temporal interpretation of latent structure and could be addressed with longitudinal modelling in future work. Moreover, the study focused on reconstruction EVR and external prediction as evaluation criteria; other notions of validity, such as sensitivity to rare symptoms or clinical decision thresholds, were not examined.

Future work could extend these findings in a number of ways. Replication using independent cohorts would test the stability of the learned representations over development. Including multi-informant data could test whether representation learning helps reconcile informant discrepancies. Linking latent factors to neuroimaging measures within the same framework would address mechanistic interpretability. These findings also have practical implications for researchers using the ABCD Study and similar large-scale developmental cohorts. The autoencoderderived dimensions should not be viewed as replacements for the original CBCL syndrome scales or for EFA-derived factors. The original CBCL scales remain valuable when the goal is clinical interpretability, comparability with established norms, or alignment with prior CBCL-based research. EFA-derived factors remain useful when the aim is to estimate transparent, theory-aligned dimensions of psychopathology. In contrast, autoencoder-derived dimensions may be most useful when researchers aim to reduce high-dimensional item-level symptom data into compact representations that preserve out-of-sample reconstructive information and may capture non-linear patterns not fully represented by linear methods. Thus, the main implication is not that ABCD researchers should universally prefer autoencoder scores, but that these scores may provide a complementary representation for sensitivity analyses, predictive modelling, and studies focused on transdiagnostic symptom structure.

In summary, this study addresses the need for dimensionality reduction approaches in developmental psychopathology that consider the properties of questionnaire data while yielding generalisable and interpretable latent phenotypes. A further strength of this study is the use of a nested cross-validation framework with controlled random seeds, which provides a more rigorous assessment of model generalisability and reduces the risk that the reported findings are driven by a favourable data split or hyperparameter selection. By accounting for the ordinal nature of questionnaire data, redundancy reduction, and representation learning, and by benchmarking against established methods under rigorous evaluation, we show that autoencoder-derived representations can capture meaningful hierarchical structure and support external validity. These findings highlight both the promise and the boundaries of representation learning for mental health phenotypes, underscoring the importance of evaluation design and cautious interpretation. Continued work is needed to test robustness across time, informants, and biological domains.

This table reports the item-level loadings of the original symptom features on each of the five autoencoder-derived latent factors. Loadings were calculated as correlations between standardized observed features and canonical variates obtained from the canonical correlation analysis. Larger absolute values indicate stronger item-level contributions to the corresponding latent factor. The table is provided to support the interpretability of the five-factor solution and to allow readers to inspect the symptom content underlying each latent representation.
