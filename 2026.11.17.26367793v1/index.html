Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

INTRODUCTION

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a biologically heterogeneous hematologic malignancy characterized by abnormal proliferation and impaired differentiation of myeloid blasts, diverse genetic alterations, variable treatment responses, and markedly different clinical outcomes. Current risk stratification systems, including the European LeukemiaNet (ELN), recommend incorporating recurrent cytogenetic and genetic abnormalities to define prognostic risk groups 1, 2 . Although these molecular classifications have substantially improved AML management, they still do not fully capture prognostic heterogeneity, particularly among intermediate-risk patients 2, 3 . This has motivated the search for complementary biomarkers that can capture prognostic information beyond established clinical and genetic risk factors.

Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) are involved in biological processes that are important for tumorigenesis and cancer progression, including cell cycle regulation, proliferation, apoptosis, migration, and genome stability 4, 5 . They can regulate gene expression through multiple mechanisms, including chromatin remodeling, transcription, RNA splicing, RNA stability, and translation 6, 7 . In hematopoiesis and leukemia, lncRNAs have been linked to proliferation, differentiation, therapy resistance, and treatment outcomes 8, 9 . These functions suggest that lncRNA expression may capture biological features of AML that are not fully reflected by genetic alterations. Therefore, studying lncRNAs may provide additional prognostic information for patients with AML. In this study, we developed and validated lncSigAML, a bone marrow-derived lncRNA signature for AML prognosis. We used TARGET-AML bone marrow samples as the discovery cohort and applied a multistep approach to identify prognostic lncRNAs. We benchmarked six survival prediction methods using cross-validation and selected the best-performing model to construct lncSigAML. RESULTS

Study Overview.

We developed lncSigAML, a machine learning-based lncRNA signature, in TARGET-AML bone marrow RNA-seq data (BM1) and validated it on four independent datasets from three cohorts, including TARGET-AML (PB1), Beat AML (BM2 and PB2), and TCGA-LAML (PB3) (Fig. 1A). We first performed multi-step prognostic screening in BM1 and identified an optimal set of 10-prognostic lncRNA signature for AML (lncSigAML), including MEF2C-AS1, LINC00477, PROSER2-AS1, LINC01122, KCNJ2-AS1, LINC01108, AP001042.1, SOCS2-AS1, ST3GAL6-AS1, and LINC00355 (Fig. 1B). This process was followed by 5-fold cross-validation to select the optimal ML approach, with random survival forest (RSF) achieving the best performance (Fig. 1C-D). The resulting lncSigAML model was then evaluated across four independent datasets spanning bone marrow and peripheral blood samples, where it consistently demonstrated prognostic significance and provided additional prognostic value beyond established clinical, genetic and cytogenetic risk factors, as demonstrated by multivariable Cox regression and partial likelihood ratio tests (Figs. 1E-F).

Pathway analyses identified significant enrichment of gene sets related to oncogenic signaling (KRAS/STK33), immune and hematopoietic signaling (IL-2/IL-21/LEF1), proliferation and cell-state regulation (MEL18/RB-p130), and metabolic/translational regulation (mTOR/EIF4GI/BCAT) in AML bone marrow samples associated with lncSigAML. These findings suggest that lncSigAML captures a distinct AML transcriptional state characterized by coordinated remodeling of oncogenic, immune, proliferative, and metabolic programs (Fig. 1G). To facilitate the broad accessibility and potential clinical application of lncSigAML, we developed a user-friendly interactive web application that enables users to calculate lncSigAML scores from either individual patient-level lncRNA expression profiles or batch expression data from multiple patients in CSV format, with scoring results available for download (Fig. 1H).

Because AML originates in the bone marrow, we began by conducting a multistep prognostic screen of bone marrow transcriptomes to identify top prognostic lncRNAs. Using the comprehensive TARGET-AML dataset (dbGaP Study Accession: phs000465.v23.p8), we compiled the BM1 cohort of bone marrow samples from 1,594 patients (Table S1 and Fig. 1A). By applying unsupervised filtering based on the standard deviation of log-transformed read counts, we identified 217 highly variable lncRNAs across samples (Table S2). We next performed supervised screening using univariate Cox proportional hazards analyses on these lncRNAs with multiple hypothesis correction (Bonferroni) and identified the 24 most significant lncRNAs associated with overall survival (OS-associated lncRNAs) (adjusted p < 0.001) (Fig. S1A). We then applied a stepwise saturation algorithm based on the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) with Cox regression model to the 24 OS-associated lncRNAs (Fig. S1A), and identified an optimal set of 10 lncRNAs, beyond which additional lncRNAs provided minimal improvement in model fit and increased the risk of overfitting (Figs. 1B and 2A). Among the identified lncRNAs, six were antisense to nearby protein-coding genes; five overlapped the corresponding gene body and promoter region, whereas one was divergently transcribed (Figs. 2A and S1B-H). Four were intergenic lncRNAs, with two located upstream and two downstream of their respective neighboring coding genes (Figs. 2A and S1I-L). Of note, high expression of eight lncRNAs was associated with poor overall survival (OS), whereas low expression of two lncRNAs was associated with poor OS (Figs. 2A and S1B). We hereafter refer to this set of OS-associated lncRNAs as the lncRNA prognostic signature for AML (lncSigAML).

Construction of the lncSigAML-based machine-learning model with a robust survival prediction.

We evaluated the standardized lncSigAML feature matrix in the BM1 cohort using six commonly used machine-learning algorithms for survival prediction (Fig. 1C). These include Cox proportional hazards (CoxPH) regression 11 , LASSO regression 12 , Elastic Net regression 13 , random survival forest (RSF) 14 , XGBoost survival model 15 , and GradientBoost 16 . We performed five-fold cross-validation and calculated the mean and standard deviation of the Harrell's concordance index (C-index) 10 across the five folds for each model (Fig. 1C). Among the six models evaluated, random survival forest demonstrated the highest predictive performance for computing lncSigAML signature, achieving the highest cross-validated C-index across the 5-fold cross-validation (mean C-index of 0.653 ± 0.023). This was followed by the standard CoxPH model (mean C-index = 0.649 ± 0.015), Elastic Net (mean Cindex = 0.648 ± 0.015), and LASSO regression model (mean C-index = 0.648 ± 0.016), all of which exhibited comparable performance (Figs. 1C and 3A). GradientBoost and XGBoost yielded lower mean C-indices of 0.629 (± 0.020) and 0.612 (± 0.021), respectively (Fig. 3A).

To assess internal validity and generalizability, we performed 5-fold cross-validation of the lncSigAML model. The model achieved a mean C-index of 0.653 ± 0.023 (95% CI: 0.630-0.676), with fold-specific values ranging from 0.613 to 0.670, indicating stable discriminative performance across folds and suggesting that results were not driven by any single data subset (Fig. 3B). Next, to demonstrate the prognostic effect of the lncSigAML model, we stratified 1,594 AML patients (BM1 cohort) into low-risk (n = 1,036) and high-risk (n = 558) groups based on the maximally selected rank statistics (MSRS) that provides the strongest separation in overall survival between the resulting risk groups. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis confirmed a statistically significant difference in OS between the two risk groups (logrank P < 0.001) (Fig. 3C), wherein high-risk patients exhibited markedly worse outcomes compared with their low-risk counterparts, corresponding to a hazard ratio of 2.87 (95% CI: 2.43-3.40; P < 0.001) (Fig. 3D). The 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival probabilities were 73.6% (70.0% -77.4%), 47.5% (43.4% -51.9%), and 41.9% (37.8%-46.4%) in the high-risk group versus 89.2% (87.4 -91.1%), 77.8% (75.3% -80.4%), and 74.6% (71.8% -77.4%) in the low-risk group, respectively. This indicates that the high-risk lncSigAML group has substantially worse overall survival than the low-risk group, and the difference becomes particularly pronounced by 3-5 years.

We next assessed calibration, the agreement between predicted and observed 1-, 3-, 5-year survival. 1-, 3-, and 5-year calibration plots showed close agreement between predicted and observed survival probabilities across the full risk range. The bootstrap-corrected curve closely followed the ideal 45° line, indicating that lncSigAML provided well-calibrated survival predictions (Figs 3F and S2). Furthermore, time-dependent receiver operating characteristics (ROC) analyses at 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year time points showed AUCs of 0.645 (95%CI = 0.609 -0.681), 0.677 (95%CI = 0.649 -0.706), and 0.694 (95%CI = 0.663 -0.725), respectively (Fig. 3G). The stability of AUC estimates across time points suggests that the prognostic signal encoded by lncSigAML is not confined to short-term risk prediction but persists over longer follow-up durations, underscoring the potential clinical utility of this model as a temporally consistent prognostic tool for patients with AML. Thus, we selectedrandom survival forest as the optimal model for subsequent analyses constructing the lncRNA-based prognostic model for AML patients.

lncSigAML provides independent prognostic information beyond clinical and molecular risk factors

To further evaluate whether the lncRNA-derived risk score provided prognostic information independent of basic clinical characteristics, we performed multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression using cross-validated patient-level risk scores in the TARGET-AML bone marrow cohort (BM1). Specifically, each patient's risk score was generated by the cross-validation version of lncSigAML random forest where the patient belongs to the held-out test fold, ensuring that the prediction for every individual was obtained from a model that had not been trained on that patient's data. This approach minimized information leakage and provided a less biased estimate of the prognostic value of the lncRNA signature. The cross-validated lncRNA risk score remained independently associated with overall survival after adjustment for age, sex, and race (HR = 2.62, 95% CI: 2.20-3.13, P < 0.001) (Table 1). Multivariable analyses of other datasets (BM2, PB1-3) yielded consistent findings (Table S3). Thus, lncSigAML provides prognostic information beyond clinical variables.

Given that AML patients with different genetic subtypes (including cytogenetic and molecular classifications) exhibit distinct clinical outcomes and survival probabilities 17 , we investigated whether the prognostic value of lncSigAML was confounded by underlying genetic subtype. The Beat AML bone marrow (BM2) cohort contains comprehensive genomic annotation, enabling adjustment for this important clinical variable (Table S1). We therefore performed a multivariable Cox proportional hazards analysis including both the lncSigAML risk group and AML genetic subtype as covariates. After adjusting for genetic subtypes, lncSigAML remained an independent predictor of overall survival. Patients classified into the high-risk group exhibited a significantly increased risk of mortality compared with those in the low-risk group (HR = 1.91, 95% CI: 1.14 -3.21, P = 0.014) (Table 2). These findings demonstrate that the prognostic value of lncSigAML is not merely attributable to differences in the distribution of AML genetic subtypes but instead provides additional prognostic information beyond established genomic classification. A similar result was found in the Beat AML peripheral blood (PB2) cohort (Table S4). The partial likelihood ratio (PLR) test showed that lncSigAML adds a statistically significant prognostic value to the current genetic classification (P = 0.009, BM2; P = 0.019, PB2) (Table S4). These findings suggest that lncSigAML provides prognostic information beyond AML genetic subtype.

Because cytogenetic risk is a major prognostic factor 17 , we further assessed whether the prognostic value of lncSigAML was independent of established cytogenetic risk. Since cytogenetic risk is available in PB3 dataset, we performed a multivariable Cox proportional hazards analysis including both the lncSigAML risk group and cytogenetic risk as covariates in the TCGA-LAML peripheral blood (PB3) cohort. Consistent with the findings in the Beat AML cohort, patients classified into the lncSigAML highrisk group remained at significantly greater risk of mortality after adjustment for cytogenetic risk (HR = 2.12, 95% CI: 1.307-3.453, P = 0.002). PLR test showed that lncSigAML adds a statistically significant prognostic value to the current cytogenetic risk category (P = 0.002, PB3) (Table 3). These findings indicate that lncSigAML provides prognostic information beyond conventional AML cytogenetic risk classification.

High-risk lncSigAML is associated with enhanced proliferative capacity, increased immune and microenvironmental signaling, and metabolic remodeling in AML

To assess the biological role of and provide scientific basis for future experimental examination of lncSigAML, we first examined expression pattern of lncSigAML lncRNA members in low-risk and highrisk AML groups across test datasets. Consistence with our finding of high expression associated with poor survival (Fig. 2), high expression of eight lncRNAs was associated with poor overall survival (OS), expression of these genes is up-regulated in high-risk groups across the data sets (Fig. 5A). Similarly, low expression of two lncRNAs was associated with poor OS (Fig. 2) and these genes are downregulated in high-risk groups across the data sets (Fig. 5A). We next performed differential expression (DEA) analysis between low-and high-risk lncSigAML groups and identified a repertoire of genes differentially expressed between risk groups (Fig. 5B, Table S5). Meta-analysis of gene-level differential expression 18, 19 results identified the top 50 genes with consistent differential expression across independent test cohorts showing large expression differences (all adjusted P < 0.001) (Fig. 5C, Table S5). These genes included hematopoietic and transcriptional regulators (MECOM, HLF, HOXA11, HOXA13), immune and cytokine signaling genes (SOCS2, IL12RB2, IL12A-AS1, KLRF2, NCR2), and genes involved in developmental and growth factor signaling (GLI2, FGF10, GPC4, SPOCK3) (Fig. 5C).

To investigate the oncogenic processes associated with the lncSigAML risk signature, we performed gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) 20 comparing the high-risk and low-risk AML groups using the C6 category of the MSigDB database (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org). The top negatively enriched pathways included translational and growth-regulatory processes, including EIF4GI, mTOR, RB/p130, and PDGFassociated signatures (NES = -1.46 to -1.69; adjusted P < 0.01) (Fig. 5D, Table S6). On the other hand, the high-risk group showed significant enrichment of pathways involved in cellular proliferation, immune signaling, and ion channel activity (Fig. 5E, Table S6). In particular, there was significant enrichment of transcriptional programs related to oncogenic signaling (KRAS/STK33; NES = 1.67-1.88), immune and hematopoietic signaling (IL-2/IL-21/LEF1; NES = 1.73-1.76), and proliferation and cell-state regulation (MEL18; NES = 1.75), with the leading positively enriched programs showing adjusted P values ranging from 2.9 × 10⁻⁷ to 1.7 × 10⁻⁵ in lncSigAML high-risk group (Fig. 5E, Table S6). . Collectively, these findings suggest that lncSigAML captures an AML transcriptional state associated with increased cellular proliferation, cytokine signaling, and growth-factor activity, along with broader oncogenic and regulatory alterations.

An interactive web application for personalized prognostic assessment

To facilitate clinical translation and broaden accessibility of lncSigAML as an RNA-based machine learning prognostic model, we developed lncSigAML webapp, an interactive web-based application using the R Shiny framework (Fig. 6). lncSigAML webapp enables users to obtain individualized prognostic assessments directly from patient-level RNA expression data without requiring specialized bioinformatics expertise or local software installation. The web interface comprises four sections: Overview, Application, Survival Table, and Instructions (Fig. 6A). The Overview provides a concise introduction to the lncSigAML framework, which predicts overall survival from lncRNA expression profiles. It summarizes the model architecture, including the Random Forest-based prediction approach, training cohort, input features, and cross-validation performance. A schematic of the study workflow and model development process is also provided. The Application provides an interactive interface for patient-specific survival prediction.

Users can enter raw read-count expression values for the 10 lncRNAs comprising the lncSigAML signature and initiate prediction by clicking the Analyze Patient button. The application also supports uploading expression datasets in csv, txt, tsv, or excel format for batch analysis. Users can clear entered values and process multiple patient samples through the uploaded dataset. Users can also remove the currently entered patient data using the delete function. Following prediction, three analytical modules-Survival Curves, Risk Profile, and Expression Profile-provide complementary views of the predicted survival probability, risk classification, and expression patterns of the signature lncRNAs. The Survival Table provides the predicted survival probabilities in tabular form, while the Instructions section provides guidance on data input, file formatting, and use of the prediction functions (Fig. 6A). The clinical workflow begins with blood or bone marrow samples collected from patients, followed by RNA sequencing to generate raw read-count expression data. These data are uploaded to the lncSigAML webapp for individualized prognostic assessment (Fig. 6B). Within the application, the raw expression data are first filtered to extract the 10 lncRNAs comprising the lncSigAML signature and subsequently subjected to log1p transformation. The processed expression values are then entered into the pretrained Random Forest model embedded in the application backend to calculate an individualized lncSigAML risk score.

Based on the resulting risk score, patients are classified into low-, intermediate-, or high-risk groups (Fig. 6C). The application dynamically generates individualized prognostic outputs, including the assigned risk level, predicted survival probabilities at key clinical time points (6 months, 1, 3, 5, and 10 years), predicted survival curves, and expression profiles of the 10 signature lncRNAs (Fig. 6D). Users can also enter multiple patients to compare predicted survival trajectories and review or export numerical survival estimates. Together, these features provide an accessible interface for translating the lncSigAML prognostic model into an individualized risk assessment framework. In summary, lncSigAML provided robust prognostic stratification across independent AML cohorts and tissue sources, with prognostic information beyond established clinical, genetic, and cytogenetic risk factors. The high-risk lncSigAML state was associated with coordinated oncogenic, immune, proliferative, and metabolic programs, and was implemented in an accessible web application for individualized prognostic assessment (Fig. 1).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we developed and validated a bone marrow-derived lncRNA prognostic framework for acute myeloid leukemia using transcriptomic profiles from five datasets and three independent cohorts comprising 2,681 pediatric and adult patients. By conducting a multi-step lncRNA prognostic screening, we established a ten-lncRNA signature that demonstrated consistent prognostic performance across independent datasets. The selected lncRNAs include MEF2C-AS1, LINC00477, PROSER2-AS1, LINC01122, KCNJ2-AS1, LINC01108, AP001042.1, SOCS2-AS1, ST3GAL6-AS1, and LINC00355. The final random survival forest model maintained stable prognostic discrimination through multiple external validation and sensitivity analyses, while remaining independently associated with overall survival after adjustment for basic clinical variables and genetic subtypes, including cytogenetic and molecular classifications. To facilitate reproducibility and accessibility, the final model was implemented as a publicly available web application. Collectively, these findings suggest that the signature derived from these selected lncRNAs was reproducibly prognostic.

lncRNAs have emerged as important regulators of hematopoiesis and leukemogenesis through their involvement in chromatin remodeling 21 , transcriptional regulation 22 , RNA stability 23 , and signaling pathways 24, 25 . Consistent with previous reports, our findings further support the prognostic value of lncRNA expression in AML. Rather than relying on a single biomarker, we developed a signature derived from ten identified prognostic lncRNAs, many of which have established roles in AML biology. This combination of established and relatively unexplored lncRNAs suggests that clinically relevant prognostic information may arise from coordinated transcriptional programs rather than individual biomarkers alone.

Further functional studies are warranted to elucidate the biological mechanisms through which these lncRNAs contribute to AML progression and treatment response.

Several lncRNAs within the signature, including SOCS2-AS1 26 , LINC01108 22 , and AP001042.1 (known as LINC00265) 27, 28 have previously been implicated in cancer biology or hematologic malignancies, whereas others remain poorly characterized. We found the opposite evidence of LINC01108 as a biomarker of poorer prognosis across datasets compared to a previous study 22 . While MEF2C plays a well-characterized, prominent oncogenic and prognostic role in acute leukemia 29 , direct evidence specifically linking MEF2C-AS1 to the pathogenesis of acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is sparse. Other lncRNAs, such as LINC00355 30 , PROSER2-AS1 31 , LINC01122 32 , and ST3GAL6-AS1 33 are also known to have functional roles in cancer although their explicit mechanisms in AML have not been explored.

An important strength of this study is its emphasis on reproducibility and generalizability. Many previously published lncRNA prognostic models [34] [35] [36] have been derived from relatively small cohorts, frequently using only the TCGA-LAML dataset, and often lack external validation or systematic robustness analyses. In contrast, our study incorporated two bone marrow and three peripheral blood datasets from three independent cohorts representing clinically distinct AML populations, including adult patients from Beat AML and TCGA-LAML, and pediatric patients from TARGET-AML.

Candidate prognostic lncRNAs as well as lncSigAML signature were required to demonstrate reproducible prognostic associations across all datasets, thereby reducing the likelihood of cohort-specific overfitting. Furthermore, sensitivity analyses demonstrated that the prognostic signal was preserved despite substantial differences in patient age, follow-up duration, and disease characteristics between the two cohorts. Together, these findings support the potential generalizability of the proposed lncSigAML signature.

Another distinguishing feature of our study is the interpretability of the selected component lncRNAs and the systematic comparison of multiple survival modeling strategies. First, our multi-step prognostic screening allows the preservation of interpretable properties of the candidate lncRNAs, such as high variability across samples and individual association with survival. Second, we evaluated multiple modeling approaches, including penalized Cox regression, gradient boosting, XGBoost, and random forest under an identical feature set and validation framework. Among these approaches, random survival forests achieved the highest overall predictive performance while demonstrating consistent performance across cross-validation folds. Tree-based ensemble survival models are capable of modeling nonlinear associations and high-order interactions without requiring explicit specification of these relationships, making them well-suited for biologically heterogeneous diseases such as AML. Importantly, rather than assuming that more sophisticated machine-learning methods would outperform conventional approaches, our study provides an empirical comparison that supports the selection of random survival forests for this particular prognostic task.

The comprehensive validation strategy further strengthens the translational relevance of this work.

We divided our validation process into mutual-tissue external validation and cross-tissue external validation. We finally performed a meta-analysis to extract the final in addition to conventional validation, we conducted cross-platform validation and robustness to substantial levels of missing expression data.

Of importance, model calibration analyses were reported across all validation processes. Calibration is frequently overlooked in prognostic biomarker studies despite being essential for clinical decisionmaking, as accurate risk estimation requires not only discrimination but also agreement between predicted and observed outcomes. Likewise, sensitivity analyses demonstrated that predictive performance remained relatively stable following imputation of substantial proportions of missing lncRNA measurements, suggesting that the model may retain utility under imperfect real-world data acquisition conditions. Although prospective validation will ultimately be required before clinical implementation, these analyses provide evidence that the proposed framework is resilient across multiple clinically relevant scenarios.

From a clinical perspective, improved molecular risk stratification remains an important objective in AML management 1 . Current prognostic classification systems rely primarily on cytogenetic abnormalities and recurrent gene mutations but continue to leave many patients within intermediate-risk categories where treatment decisions remain challenging 3 . Our findings suggest that bone marrow-derived lncRNA expression profiles may provide complementary prognostic information that could potentially augment existing risk stratification systems, as the lncSigAML signature was demonstrated as an independent prognostic factor to clinical variables as well as genetic and cytogenetic subtypes via multivariable Cox analyses and the PLR test. Furthermore, because the signature demonstrated prognostic utility across both pediatric and adult AML cohorts, it may offer broader applicability than models developed exclusively within a single age group. The development of a publicly accessible Shiny application further lowers barriers for independent validation and future clinical investigation by allowing researchers and clinicians to evaluate patient risk without requiring specialized computational expertise.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this study was based on retrospective analyses of publicly available transcriptomic datasets, and prospective validation in independent clinical cohorts will be necessary before routine clinical application.

Second, although the signature demonstrated reproducibility across three large AML cohorts, additional validation using geographically diverse populations and different sequencing platforms would further strengthen its generalizability. Third, while the identified lncRNAs exhibited reproducible prognostic associations, the biological functions of several signature components remain incompletely understood and require in vitro functional experiments. Functional studies examining their roles in leukemogenesis, disease progression, and therapeutic resistance will provide important mechanistic insight. Finally, although our multivariable analyses demonstrated that the lncRNA signature provides prognostic information beyond basic clinical variables, future studies should evaluate its incremental value alongside comprehensive molecular risk factors, including ELN risk classification 1 , recurrent somatic mutations 37, 38 , and measurable residual disease status 39.

In conclusion, we developed a bone marrow-derived ten-lncRNA prognostic framework for AML using three cohorts encompassing both pediatric and adult patients. Through systematic biomarker discovery, comprehensive model benchmarking, rigorous validation, and public deployment, this study extends beyond the development of a conventional prognostic signature and proposes a reproducible framework for lncRNA-based prognostic modeling in AML. Future prospective validation and biological characterization of the identified lncRNAs may further support their integration into precision medicine strategies for patients with AML.
