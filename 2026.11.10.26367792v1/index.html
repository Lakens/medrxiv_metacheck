Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 3 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. 1. Introduction

Chronic exposure to environmental chemicals can induce physiological and molecular alterations that may contribute to the development and progression of chronic diseases (Landrigan et al., 2018). Heavy metals, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), and volatile organic compounds (VOCs) are major environmental hazards to which humans are widely exposed through industrial activities and the surrounding environment, and have been associated with biological processes including oxidative stress, inflammation, and metabolic dysfunction (Bolden et al., 2015; Kim et al., 2013; Tchounwou et al., 2012; Valko et al., 2005). Emerging geroscience perspectives further identify the environment as a potentially modifiable determinant of biological aging trajectories, with implications for preventing aging-related disease and reducing disparities in healthy aging (Belsky & Baccarelli, 2023). However, how these environmental exposures are associated with biological aging and how such alterations relate to human health remain incompletely understood.

DNA methylation-based epigenetic clocks have emerged as important biomarkers for quantifying biological aging (Bell et al., 2019). The Hannum and Horvath clocks were primarily developed to predict chronological age, whereas subsequent generations, including PhenoAge and GrimAge, were designed to better capture biological characteristics related to health and mortality (Hannum et al., 2013; Horvath, 2013; Levine et al., 2018; Lu et al., 2019). Epigenetic age acceleration (EAA), representing an epigenetic age greater than expected based on chronological age, may reflect accelerated biological aging. EAA may therefore serve as a potential biomarker for evaluating biological alterations associated with environmental exposures and their health implications (Fransquet et al., 2019; Hillary et al., 2020; Oblak et al., 2021).

Growing evidence suggests that exposure to air pollution, metals, and other environmental chemicals may be associated with EAA (Li et al., 2018; Nwanaji-Enwerem et al., 2020; Van der Laan et al., 2022; Ward-Caviness et al., 2016). Recent studies have further linked internal chemical exposures, including individual metals and metal mixtures, to multiple epigenetic aging measures (Boyer et al., 2023; Khodasevich et al., 2025; Mi et al., 2025). Environmental chemical exposures have also been associated with clinical alterations relevant to aging and disease, including associations between PAH metabolites and serum gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT) (Ryu & Hong, 2024). However, these relationships have largely been investigated separately, and relatively little is known about how exposure-related alterations in epigenetic aging relate to physiological and clinical changes within the same population. Evidence also remains limited for some environmental chemicals, including VOC metabolites, in relation to DNA methylation-based aging measures. Integrating environmental exposures, epigenetic aging, and health-related phenotypes within a single cohort may therefore provide a more comprehensive understanding of the potential biological pathways linking environmental exposures to adverse health outcomes.

The FROM cohort provides an opportunity to address these gaps in older adults residing in environmentally vulnerable areas. Previous analyses of this cohort have identified heavy metal-associated multi-omic alterations and plasma proteomic profiles implicating immune, lipid, and oxidative-stress pathways (Heo et al., 2026; Kim et al., 2025). In the present study, we evaluated associations of 13 environmental exposure biomarkers, including heavy metals, PAHs, and VOCs, with multiple epigenetic aging measures and additionally assessed the effects of combined exposures. We further examined associations between epigenetic aging and clinical traits and evaluated the potential mediating role of epigenetic aging in the relationships between environmental exposures and clinical alterations. Finally, we used Mendelian randomization (MR) based on East Asian genetic instruments to investigate potential causal relationships between epigenetic aging and clinical traits and extended these analyses to metabolic and age-related disease outcomes in Biobank Japan (BBJ) (Ishigaki et al., 2020). Together, these analyses provide an integrated framework for examining potential links among environmental chemical exposures, epigenetic aging, clinical alterations, and age-related diseases.

2. Materials and methods

>Materials and methods 115

2.1. Study cohorts and data sources

The FROM cohort served as the primary study population (Kwon et al., 2024). The FROM study has enrolled participants in successive annual recruitment waves, and the present analyses used two non-overlapping waves recruited in 2023 and 2024. The main analyses included 666 participants from the 2024 recruitment wave (hereafter the main analytic cohort) with available DNA methylation, genotype, environmental exposure, and clinical data. Of these, 553 participants were residents of environmentally vulnerable areas, including areas affected by a waste incinerator, a coal-fired power plant, a cement factory, an area with a high concentration of factories, or high exposure to particulate matter. The remaining 113 participants were classified as residents of control areas. Control areas were located at least 10 km from major industrial facilities and were not designated as environmentally vulnerable areas. These areas were selected to have demographic characteristics, including age and sex distributions, comparable to those of the exposure areas (Kwon et al., 2024). This cohort was used for the main analyses evaluating associations between environmental exposures and EAA, mixture effects, associations between EAA and clinical traits, and mediation effects.

Additionally, genotype and clinical data from 491 participants in the 2023 recruitment wave (hereafter the MR outcome cohort), none of whom overlapped with the main analytic cohort, were used for MR analyses of clinical traits. For disease-related MR analyses, data from the Korean Genome and Epidemiology Study (KoGES) were additionally used (Kim et al., 2017). The present study included 1,226 KoGES participants with available DNA methylation and genotype data. Genetic association estimates for EAA derived from KoGES and the main analytic cohort were meta-analyzed to generate exposure summary statistics for the disease MR analyses. Genome-wide association study (GWAS) summary statistics for disease outcomes were obtained from BBJ, comprising approximately 170,000 participants.

An overview of the available data and the specific roles of each cohort in the present study is provided in Fig. 1a.

2.2. Environmental exposure and clinical variable measurement

A total of 13 environmental exposure biomarkers were analyzed, including heavy metals, PAHs, and VOCs. Heavy metal-related biomarkers included blood lead (bPb), blood cadmium (bCd), blood mercury (bHg), urinary cadmium (uCd), and the sum of urinary arsenic species (As III , As V , and MMA). PAH metabolites included 2-naphthol (2-NAP), 1hydroxypyrene (1-OHP), 2-hydroxyfluorene (2-HF), and 1-hydroxyphenanthrene (1-HPH), while VOC metabolites included phenylglyoxylic acid (PGA), trans,trans-muconic acid (tt-MA), methylhippuric acid (MHA), and benzylmercapturic acid (BMA). All urinary exposure biomarkers were adjusted for urinary creatinine concentration, whereas bPb, bCd, and bHg were analyzed as blood-based biomarkers.

A total of 24 clinical variables were evaluated: platelet count, hemoglobin, hematocrit, white blood cell count (WBC), red blood cell count (RBC), mean corpuscular volume (MCV), mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH), mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration (MCHC), total protein, serum albumin, total calcium, inorganic phosphate, aspartate transaminase (AST), alanine transaminase (ALT), gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), lactic acid dehydrogenase (LDH), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), total cholesterol, triglyceride (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL), lowdensity lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL), N-acetyl-β-D-glucosaminidase (NAG), and beta-2 microglobulin (β2-MG). β2-MG measurements were unavailable for nine participants; therefore, analyses involving β2-MG were conducted using the 657 participants with available measurements. Detailed procedures for sample collection and measurement of environmental exposure biomarkers and clinical variables have been described previously (Kwon et al., 2024). The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Kangwon National University Hospital (KNUH-B-2021-06-001) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant guidelines and regulations. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

2.3. DNA methylation profiling and preprocessing

Genomic DNA isolated from peripheral blood was used for DNA methylation profiling. Genome-wide DNA methylation was measured using the Infinium MethylationEPIC BeadChip (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), including data generated using the EPIC v1.0 (n = 85) and v2.0 (n = 586) arrays. DNA methylation data preprocessing and quality control were performed using the minfi R package (Aryee et al., 2014). Five subjects showing a mismatch between reported sex and sex predicted from X-and Y-chromosome probe intensities were excluded. All included samples showed detection P < 1 × 10¹ for more than 95% of probes. Background adjustment, dye-bias correction, and functional normalization were performed using the preprocessFunnorm function.

For CpG-level QC, probes with detection P > 1 × 10¹ in more than 5% of samples were excluded. Probes located on sex chromosomes, probes containing SNPs at the CpG or single-base extension sites, and known cross-reactive probes were also removed. The EPIC v2.0 probes were subsequently mapped to their corresponding v1.0 probe identifiers to harmonize the two array datasets, resulting in 666 subjects and 674,337 CpG sites for downstream analyses (Fig. 1b). To minimize technical variability, batch effects across 87 array slides were corrected using the ComBat function from the sva R package (Leek et al., 2012). Multidimensional scaling (MDS) plots were examined before and after ComBat correction, and no clear separation between EPIC v1.0 and v2.0 samples was observed after correction (Fig. S1). Blood cell-type proportions, including CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, NK cells, B cells, and monocytes, were estimated using the Houseman algorithm with an IDOL-optimized blood reference panel (Houseman et al., 2012; Koestler et al., 2016).

2.4. Estimation of epigenetic aging measures

Seven DNA methylation-based epigenetic aging measures were estimated, including the Horvath, Hannum, Zhang, PhenoAge, GrimAge, epiTOC, and DunedinPACE measures (Belsky et al., 2022; Hannum et al., 2013; Horvath, 2013; Levine et al., 2018; Lu et al., 2019; Yang et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2019). Horvath, Hannum, Zhang, PhenoAge, epiTOC, and DunedinPACE were calculated using the dnaMethyAge R package (Wang et al., 2024), whereas GrimAge v1 was estimated using the meffonym R package (Suderman). For CpG sites required by individual algorithms but unavailable in the preprocessed methylation dataset, reference-based imputation implemented in the corresponding algorithms was applied.

For Horvath, Hannum, Zhang, PhenoAge, and GrimAge, age acceleration (AA) was calculated to quantify epigenetic aging independent of chronological age (Horvath & Raj, 2018). Each epigenetic age estimate was regressed on chronological age using a linear regression model, and the resulting residuals were defined as AA. Positive AA values therefore indicated faster epigenetic aging than expected based on chronological age. As epiTOC and DunedinPACE reflect mitotic aging and the pace of aging, respectively, their original estimates were used in subsequent analyses without calculating AA (Fig. S2).

2.5. SNP genotyping and quality control

Blood-derived DNA from FROM participants was genotyped using KoreanChip version 1.1 (Moon et al., 2019). To minimize batch effects during genotype calling, a Kmedoid clustering-based approach was applied (Seo et al., 2019), and genotype quality control was performed using PLINK and ONETOOL (Song et al., 2018). At the subject level, participants with sex discrepancies or genotype call rates below 95% were excluded. At the variant level, single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) with call rates below 95% or Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium (HWE) P < 1 × 10 were removed. Genotype imputation was performed using the Korean Reference Genome panel through the Korean Imputation Server (Hwang et al., 2022), with Eagle v2.4 for pre-phasing and Minimac4 for imputation. Following imputation, SNPs with R² < 0.8, duplicate variants, genotype missing rates >5%, HWE P < 1 × 10, or minor allele frequencies (MAFs) <5% were excluded. Subject-level QC was further performed using an identity-by-state threshold of 0.99, and outliers were removed based on multidimensional scaling using LD-pruned SNPs. SNP coordinates were harmonized to the human genome build hg38 using the rtracklayer R package (Lawrence et al., 2009). A total of 5,030,771 SNPs were retained for subsequent analyses.

KoGES genotype data were generated using the Affymetrix 5.0 or 6.0 Array or the Korea Biobank Array version 1.0 or 1.1. Variants were excluded if they had a missing rate >5%, HWE P < 1 × 10, or MAF <5%, while participants were excluded for sex discrepancies, missing rates >5%, or heterozygosity outliers (P < 1 × 10). Genotype imputation was performed using the Northeast Asian Reference Database imputation server, and SNPs with an INFO score >0.8 were retained for subsequent analyses. Detailed procedures for KoGES genotyping, QC, and imputation have been described previously (Seo et al., 2024).

2.6. Statistical analysis

>Statistical analysis

2.6.1. Comparison of epigenetic aging measures between exposure and control groups

Differences in epigenetic aging measures were compared between residents of environmentally vulnerable areas (exposure group) and those from control areas (control group) using Welch's two-sample t-tests. Multiple testing was controlled using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (BH-FDR) method (Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995).

The distributions of epigenetic aging measures were presented using violin plots with embedded box plots.

2.6.2. Associations between environmental exposures and epigenetic aging

As the first step of the analytical framework (Fig. 1c), associations between the 13 environmental exposure biomarkers and epigenetic aging measures were evaluated using multiple linear regression. Urinary exposure biomarkers were adjusted for urinary creatinine, and all environmental exposure variables were log2-transformed prior to analysis. Each environmental exposure biomarker was evaluated separately, with adjustment for age, sex, smoking status, BMI, and estimated blood cell proportions. Regression coefficients, standard errors, and P values were obtained from each model. Multiple testing was controlled using the BH-FDR method across the 13 environmental exposures separately for each epigenetic aging measure, with FDR < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

For GrimAge AA, the robustness of associations involving urinary exposure biomarkers to the method of urinary dilution adjustment was assessed in a sensitivity analysis using uncorrected urinary concentrations, with urinary creatinine included as a separate covariate instead of using creatinine-standardized concentrations. To assess potential nonlinear effects of chronological age, a sensitivity analysis for GrimAge AA was performed by replacing the linear age term in the exposure-GrimAge AA regression models with a natural cubic spline with 3 degrees of freedom, while retaining the same exposure variables and other covariates as in the primary models. To further assess potential residual confounding by smoking, additional sensitivity analyses for GrimAge AA were performed by replacing smoking status with cumulative smoking exposure quantified as pack-years and by restricting the analysis to never-smokers. Pack-years were calculated as the number of cigarettes smoked per day divided by 20 and multiplied by the duration of smoking in years, with never-smokers assigned a value of zero. The same exposure variables and other covariates as in the primary models were retained. For each sensitivity analysis, multiple testing across the 13 environmental exposures was controlled using the BH-FDR method.

Additional sensitivity analyses for GrimAge AA were conducted by stratifying the study population by sex and age (<70 and ≥70 years), with 70 years selected as the cutoff because it approximated the mean age of the study population (69.76 years). Effect modification was further assessed in multivariable regression models by including exposure-by-sex interaction terms, with men as the reference group, and exposure-by-age interaction terms, with age modeled as a continuous variable.

2.6.3. Environmental mixture analysis

Following the individual exposure analyses (Fig. 1c), quantile g-computation (qgcomp) (Keil et al., 2020) and Bayesian kernel machine regression (BKMR) (Bobb et al., 2015) were performed to evaluate the association between combined environmental exposures and GrimAge AA. The mixture comprised 12 environmental exposure biomarkers, including heavy metals, PAHs, and VOCs. Because of the correlation between blood and urinary Cd (Spearman ρ = [0.552]), blood Cd was excluded from the mixture analysis to minimize redundancy, and urinary Cd was used as the representative measure of Cd exposure.

All models were adjusted for age, sex, smoking status, BMI, and estimated blood cell proportions (CD8+ T cells, CD4+ T cells, NK cells, B cells, and monocytes). To assess the robustness of the mixture analysis results to the choice of Cd biomarker, sensitivity analyses were performed by replacing urinary Cd with blood Cd in both the qgcomp and BKMR models, while retaining all other exposure variables, covariates, and model specifications.

Qgcomp was performed using the qgcomp R package (Keil et al., 2020).

Environmental exposures were categorized into quartiles (q = 4), and the overall mixture effect was estimated as the change in GrimAge AA associated with a simultaneous onequartile increase in all mixture components. An additive linear model was used to estimate the overall mixture effect and the relative contribution (weight) of each exposure in the positive or negative direction. A marginal structural model with 1,000 bootstrap iterations was additionally used to estimate the overall mixture effect and its 95% confidence interval (CI).

BKMR was performed using the bkmr R package to evaluate the overall mixture effect and potential nonlinear exposure-response relationships of individual exposures (Bobb et al., 2015). Environmental exposure variables were standardized prior to analysis, and 10,000 Markov chain Monte Carlo iterations were performed with variable selection. The relative importance of individual exposures within the mixture was evaluated using posterior inclusion probabilities (PIPs). Exposure-response relationships between individual exposures and GrimAge AA were further evaluated while holding the remaining mixture components at their median levels.

2.6.4. Associations and causal inference between epigenetic aging and clinical traits

As the next step of the analytical framework (Fig. 1c), associations between GrimAge AA and 24 clinical traits were evaluated using multiple linear regression. All clinical traits were log2-transformed prior to analysis and modeled as outcomes, with GrimAge AA as the predictor. Models were adjusted for age, sex, smoking status, BMI, and estimated blood cell proportions. Regression coefficients, standard errors, and P values were estimated for the associations between GrimAge AA and each clinical trait, and multiple testing across the 24 traits was controlled using the BH-FDR method. An additional sensitivity analysis was performed for all 24 clinical traits with further adjustment for current drinking status to account for the potential influence of alcohol consumption.

For WBC, GGT, and LDL, which showed FDR < 0.05 in the regression analyses, twosample MR analyses were subsequently performed to evaluate potential causal relationships with GrimAge AA (Davey Smith & Hemani, 2014). Genetic associations with GrimAge AA were estimated in the main analytic cohort, whereas genetic associations with the clinical traits were estimated in the MR outcome cohort. Because the two recruitment waves comprised different participants, the exposure and outcome samples in the MR analyses did not overlap. Genome-wide association analyses were adjusted for age, sex, smoking status, BMI, and the first 10 genetic principal components. SNPs associated with GrimAge AA at P < 5 × 10 were initially selected as candidate instrumental variables, while SNPs associated with age, sex, smoking, BMI, or the corresponding clinical outcome at P < 5 × 10 were excluded. Independent instruments were subsequently selected by linkage disequilibrium clumping using r² < 0.001.

The inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method was used as the primary MR estimator. MR-Egger, weighted median, and MR-RAPS methods were additionally applied as sensitivity analyses to evaluate the robustness of the IVW estimates (Bowden et al., 2015; Zhao et al., 2020). Instrument strength was assessed using F-statistics, and I²GX was calculated to evaluate potential regression dilution in MR-Egger analyses. Heterogeneity and horizontal pleiotropy were assessed using Cochran's Q test, the MR-Egger intercept test, and the MR-PRESSO global test (Bowden et al., 2017; Burgess & Thompson, 2011; Verbanck et al., 2018). Steiger directionality analysis was additionally performed to assess whether the genetic instruments explained greater variance in GrimAge AA than in the corresponding clinical outcome. Multiple testing across WBC, GGT, and LDL was controlled separately for each MR method using the BH-FDR procedure.

2.6.5. Mediation analysis

Mediation analysis was performed to evaluate whether GrimAge AA mediated the association between environmental exposures and GGT (Fig. 1c). GGT, which showed an FDR-significant association with GrimAge AA in the observational analysis and nominal evidence of an effect in the MR analysis, was selected as the outcome, while each of the 13 environmental exposure biomarkers was evaluated separately as the exposure and GrimAge AA was specified as the mediator. All environmental exposure variables and GGT were log2transformed prior to analysis. In the mediator model, GrimAge AA was regressed on each environmental exposure and covariates, whereas in the outcome model, GGT was regressed on the corresponding environmental exposure, GrimAge AA, and covariates. Both models were adjusted for age, sex, smoking status, BMI, and estimated blood cell proportions.

Mediation effects were estimated using the R mediation package with 5,000 bootstrap simulations to estimate the average causal mediation effect (ACME), average direct effect (ADE), total effect, proportion mediated, and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (Imai et al., 2010; Tingley et al., 2014). As a sensitivity analysis, the mediation models were additionally adjusted for current drinking status to account for the potential influence of alcohol consumption on GGT.

2.6.6. Mendelian randomization analysis of GrimAge AA and disease outcomes

Among the disease phenotypes available in BBJ, 38 disease outcomes were selected based on their biological relevance to aging and environmental exposure (Table S1). Genetic association estimates for GrimAge AA were obtained by metaanalyzing GWAS summary statistics from the main analytic cohort and KoGES using an inverse-variance weighted fixed-effect model to improve the precision of SNP-GrimAge AA association estimates. Effect alleles were harmonized across the two datasets prior to metaanalysis. Disease outcome summary statistics were obtained from BBJ. 3.1. Characteristics of the study population

A total of 666 participants were included in the analysis, comprising 553 individuals from environmentally vulnerable areas (exposure group) and 113 individuals from control areas (Table S2). The mean age was 70.44 years in the exposure group and 66.40 years in the control group, while the distributions of sex, BMI, and smoking status were comparable between the groups. Levels of several exposure biomarkers, including bPb, 2-HF, 1-HPH, tt-MA, MHA, and BMA, were significantly higher in the exposure group, whereas bHg was higher in the control group and the remaining biomarkers, including bCd and uCd, did not differ significantly between groups (Table S2).

We next compared epigenetic aging measures between the exposure and control groups (Fig. 2a; Table S3). GrimAge AA was significantly higher in the exposure group and remained significant after FDR correction. Hannum AA and DunedinPACE were also higher in the exposure group at nominal significance (P < 0.05), although these associations did not remain significant after FDR correction. epiTOC also remained significant after FDR correction; however, its level was higher in the control group than in the exposure group. No significant differences were observed for Horvath AA, Zhang AA, or PhenoAge AA.

3.2. Associations between environmental exposures and epigenetic aging

Associations between the 13 environmental exposure biomarkers and seven epigenetic aging measures were evaluated using multiple linear regression (Fig. 2b, c; Fig. S3; Table S4). Significant associations after FDR correction were observed exclusively for GrimAge AA, whereas no environmental exposure biomarker was significantly associated with the other epigenetic aging measures after multiple-testing correction (Fig. S3). For GrimAge AA, blood Cd, 2-NAP, 2-HF, and MHA were positively associated with age acceleration after FDR correction (Fig. 2b, c). In particular, a two-fold increase in 2-HF was associated with a 0.491-year increase in GrimAge AA (β = 0.491 years, P = 1.22 × 10 -14 ). The associations were robust to an alternative approach for urinary dilution adjustment using urinary concentrations without creatinine standardization and including urinary creatinine as a separate covariate, with effect estimates similar to those in the primary analysis. After FDR correction across all 13 environmental exposures, significant positive associations were observed for the urinary biomarkers 2-NAP, 2-HF, MHA, and tt-MA, as well as for blood Cd, which was unaffected by the alternative urinary dilution adjustment (Table S5). The results were also largely consistent when chronological age was modeled using a natural cubic spline, although the association with tt-MA no longer reached statistical significance after FDR correction (Table S6). The primary associations were also retained after replacing smoking status with pack-years, with 2-NAP, 2-HF, MHA, and blood Cd remaining significant after FDR correction. In analyses restricted to never-smokers, the associations of 2-HF and MHA with GrimAge AA remained significant after FDR correction, whereas the associations of 2-NAP and blood Cd were attenuated (Table S7). In sensitivity analyses stratified by sex, the positive association between 2-HF and GrimAge AA was consistently observed in both men and women, with no evidence of effect modification by sex. 3.4. Associations and causal inference between GrimAge acceleration and clinical traits

To further investigate whether the associations identified in the regression analyses were consistent with potential causal effects, two-sample MR analyses were performed for WBC, GGT, and LDL, which met the FDR < 0.05 threshold in the observational analyses (Fig. 4a; Table S18). In the primary IVW analysis, GrimAge AA showed a positive effect estimate for GGT that reached nominal significance (β = 0.067, SE = 0.030, P = 0.024; 7 SNPs), although the association did not remain significant after correction for the three clinical outcomes (FDR = 0.073). MR-RAPS yielded a similar positive effect estimate for GGT (β = 0.069, SE = 0.032, P = 0.028; FDR = 0.085), whereas the weighted median and MR-Egger estimates were not statistically significant. No evidence of an effect was observed for WBC or LDL. The mean F-statistic was 23.49, and there was no evidence of substantial heterogeneity or directional horizontal pleiotropy based on Cochran's Q, the MR-Egger intercept test, and the MR-PRESSO global test. Steiger directionality analyses supported the hypothesized direction from GrimAge AA to each clinical outcome (all Steiger P < 2.1 × 10¹). Detailed MR estimates and instrument diagnostics are provided in Table S18, and information on the individual SNPs used as instrumental variables is provided in Tables S19-S21.

3.5. Mediation of the association between environmental exposures and GGT by GrimAge acceleration

Given the significant observational association between GrimAge AA and GGT and the nominally significant IVW MR estimate, mediation analyses were subsequently performed to investigate whether GrimAge AA mediated the associations between environmental exposures and GGT. Each of the 13 environmental exposure biomarkers was evaluated as an exposure, with GrimAge AA as the mediator and GGT as the outcome (Fig. S6; Table S22). Significant indirect effects were observed for five environmental exposures (2-HF, 2-NAP, bCd, MHA, and tt-MA) (Fig. 4b). Among these exposures, all except tt-MA remained significant after BH-FDR correction. These findings were robust to additional adjustment for current drinking status, with the same indirect effects remaining significant after BH-FDR correction (Table S23).

3.6. Potential causal effects of GrimAge acceleration on disease outcomes

MR analyses were performed to investigate the potential causal effects of GrimAge AA on a broad range of disease outcomes. Among the 38 disease outcomes evaluated, four showed nominally significant associations (P < 0.05): diabetic nephropathy (β = 0.1812, P = 0.0025), osteoporosis (β = 0.0282, P = 0.0054), cardiac valvular disease (β = -0.0545, P = 0.0074), and dilated cardiomyopathy (β = 0.1213, P = 0.0388). After FDR correction, diabetic nephropathy, osteoporosis, and cardiac valvular disease showed suggestive associations (FDR = 0.0933), although none of the disease associations met the conventional FDR < 0.05 threshold. The associations were positive for diabetic nephropathy and osteoporosis and inverse for cardiac valvular disease. 4. Discussion

In this community-based study of 666 older adults, including 553 residents of environmentally vulnerable areas and 113 controls, environmental exposure was associated with accelerated epigenetic aging and downstream physiological alterations. Four findings were central. First, among seven epigenetic aging measures, GrimAge AA showed the most consistent signal across both the exposed-versus-control comparison and biomarker-based analyses. Second, PAH biomarkers, particularly 2-HF and 2-NAP, emerged as reproducible correlates of GrimAge AA across individual-and mixture-exposure models. Third, GrimAge AA was associated with WBC, GGT, and LDL, but MR provided nominal support for a directional effect only for GGT; mediation analyses further identified indirect associations of 2-HF, 2-NAP, bCd, and MHA with GGT through GrimAge AA that remained significant after FDR correction. Finally, exploratory disease MR suggested potential associations with diabetic nephropathy, osteoporosis, and cardiac valvular disease at FDR < 0.10. Together, these findings suggest that pollutant-associated GrimAge AA may reflect physiologically relevant biological alterations.

The specificity of the exposure signal to GrimAge is biologically informative. In the regional comparison, Hannum AA and DunedinPACE were nominally significant, whereas epiTOC remained significant after FDR correction but was higher in controls. In quantitative exposure models, only GrimAge AA retained associations after FDR correction, specifically with bCd, 2-NAP, 2-HF, and MHA. First-generation clocks such as Horvath and Hannum were optimized primarily to predict chronological age, whereas GrimAge was trained to predict mortality using DNAm surrogates of smoking pack-years and mortality-related plasma proteins (Hannum et al., 2013; Horvath, 2013; Lu et al., 2019). These components capture smoking-related, inflammatory, vascular, renal, and metabolic information, potentially explaining the greater sensitivity of GrimAge to combustion-and toxicant-related exposures. The opposite-direction epiTOC finding further suggests that mitotic age and mortality-related biological age represent distinct aging domains. This interpretation is consistent with previous environmental epigenetic-aging studies.

Occupational and community PAH exposure has been associated with accelerated DNAm aging (Li et al., 2018), and PAH-related exposure from household smoky-coal combustion was particularly associated with GrimAge AA among never-smoking women (Blechter et al., 2023). Occupational benzene and trichloroethylene exposure has also been related to epigenetic aging, although affected clocks differed across exposures and populations (Van der Laan et al., 2022). For metals, cadmium and a nonessential-metal mixture containing cadmium were associated with greater GrimAge AA and faster DunedinPACE in American Indian populations (Boyer et al., 2023), while an exposome-wide analysis of 2,346 US adults identified serum cadmium as most strongly associated with GrimAge AA (Khodasevich et al., 2025). Together, these studies support exposure-specific heterogeneity across epigenetic clocks and suggest that GrimAge may be particularly responsive to environmental exposures involving smoking-related, inflammatory, and multisystem stress.

The PAH findings were particularly consistent across analytical approaches. In single-pollutant models, 2-HF showed a strong association with GrimAge AA (β = 0.491, P = 1.22 × 10 -14 ), which was observed in both men and women without evidence of sex interaction. Qgcomp estimated a positive joint mixture effect, with positive weights for MHA, 2-HF, 2-NAP, and 1-HPH, while BKMR assigned posterior inclusion probabilities of 1.000 to both 2-HF and 2-NAP. Their convergence across conventional regression, qgcomp, and BKMR therefore identifies 2-HF and 2-NAP as prominent contributors to the observed exposure signal. Because these urinary metabolites reflect exposure to fluorene, naphthalene, and correlated combustion sources, however, the findings are more appropriately interpreted as markers of PAH-related exposure rather than evidence of toxicity from the measured metabolites themselves.

Several mechanisms may connect PAH exposure with GrimAge AA. Parent PAHs can activate the aryl hydrocarbon receptor and induce CYP1A1 and CYP1B1, while metabolic activation generates electrophilic intermediates and o-quinones capable of forming DNA adducts and generating reactive oxygen species, DNA-strand breaks, and oxidative DNA damage (Endo et al., 2008; Park et al., 2005; Park et al., 2009; Xue & Warshawsky, 2005).

Consistent with these mechanisms, urinary hydroxylated PAHs have been associated with oxidative nucleic-acid damage, lipid peroxidation, C-reactive protein, and WBC count in human studies (Alshaarawy et al., 2013; Kim et al., 2003; Lu et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2020).

Notably, 2/3-hydroxyfluorene and 2-hydroxynaphthalene were recently identified as important contributors to urinary oxidative nucleic-acid damage biomarkers (Cao et al., 2024), while experimental chronic PAH exposure has produced persistent oxidative DNA damage and genomic instability (Bai et al., 2017).

Recurrent oxidative and inflammatory stress may subsequently alter DNA methylation. Oxidative DNA damage can recruit DNMT1-and DNMT3B-containing repressive complexes to damaged chromatin (O'Hagan et al., 2011), while human PAH exposure has been associated with methylation changes in OGG1 and AHRR, age-related loci including FHL2 and ELOVL2, and accelerated DNAm aging (Chen et al., 2024; Fu et al., 2019; Li et al., 2018). This mechanism is particularly compatible with GrimAge, which incorporates DNAmPACKYRS and DNAm surrogates for proteins involved in inflammatory, vascular, renal, stress-response, and tissue-remodeling processes (Lu et al., 2019).

Consistently, smoky-coal combustion exposure has been associated with GrimAge AA even among never-smoking women (Blechter et al., 2023).

The accompanying bCd and MHA findings, together with the more tentative tt-MA signal, broaden the interpretation from PAHs alone to a multi-pollutant aging signal.

Cadmium is retained in the body, promotes mitochondrial dysfunction, glutathione depletion, inflammation, and oxidative injury, and has now been linked to GrimAge AA in several populations (Boyer et al., 2023; Khodasevich et al., 2025). tt-MA and MHA are urinary biomarkers used to index benzene and xylene exposure, respectively, and therefore may represent additional components of an inhaled or occupationally patterned mixture rather than independent causal agents. Residential VOC biomarker studies have associated low-level exposure with hepatocyte injury and altered liver enzymes, while occupational benzene exposure has been associated with epigenetic age acceleration (Van der Laan et al., 2022; Wahlang et al., 2022). Shared mechanisms-including reactive metabolite formation, glutathione consumption, oxidative stress, and inflammatory signaling-could therefore explain why chemically distinct biomarkers converged on GrimAge AA and GGT. At the same time, the short half-lives and source nonspecificity of several urinary metabolites require caution when assigning effects to individual parent compounds.

The clinical-trait analyses support the biological and clinical relevance of GrimAge AA beyond its role as a methylation-derived computational score. GrimAge AA was positively associated with WBC and GGT and inversely associated with LDL after FDR correction. The WBC association is compatible with systemic inflammatory or immune-cell activation, one of the pathways through which PAHs and metals may affect aging. By contrast, the inverse LDL association was not supported by MR and may reflect lipidlowering treatment, comorbidity, nutrition, frailty, or survival-related selection in this older population; it should not be interpreted as a protective effect of accelerated aging. Similarly, the observational association with WBC was not supported by MR. GGT was therefore the most coherent downstream phenotype because it was supported by both the observational association and the MR analysis.

GGT provides a biologically plausible link between pollutant exposure and GrimAge-associated physiological dysregulation. Beyond its conventional role as a hepatobiliary marker, GGT participates in extracellular glutathione metabolism and may increase in response to oxidative stress (Lee et al., 2004; Zhang & Forman, 2009). This is particularly relevant to the PAH findings: urinary 2-HF has been associated with elevated GGT in US adults (Mallah et al., 2023), while a PAH mixture was positively associated with GGT in Korean adults, with 2-NAP and 2-HF among the largest contributors (Ryu & Hong, 2024). Extending these findings, our study links internal PAH exposure in residents of environmentally vulnerable areas to GGT through GrimAge AA, suggesting that exposurerelated epigenetic aging may represent an intermediate biological process connecting pollutant exposure with physiological dysregulation. Inflammation and oxidative stress have also been implicated in PAH-liver-function associations, with 2-HF contributing prominently to elevated GGT (Zhou et al., 2024), and these metabolites have been associated with ALT elevation or fatty-liver-related phenotypes (Choi et al., 2023; Lee et al., 2025). Cadmium has likewise been associated with higher GGT and other liver-injury markers (Kim et al., 2021; Yan et al., 2022). Moreover, higher GGT has prospectively predicted type 2 diabetes, chronic kidney disease, and osteoporotic fracture in Korean populations (Kim et al., 2014; Lee et al., 2020; Ryu et al., 2007), suggesting that it may reflect broader redox and metabolic dysregulation rather than liver injury alone.

The mediation results provide the most direct synthesis of these findings. Significant indirect effects were observed for 2-HF, 2-NAP, bCd, and MHA in relation to GGT through GrimAge AA. For 2-HF, the estimated average causal mediation effect was 0.019 (P = 0.0072), whereas the average direct effect was 0.024 (P = 0.336). This pattern is compatible with a pathway in which repeated toxicant-related oxidative and inflammatory stress becomes embedded in a mortality-related DNAm signature, which then tracks or contributes to a glutathione-dependent hepatometabolic response reflected by GGT. The result is strongest for 2-HF and 2-NAP because both were supported in the individual and mixture analyses, and because independent studies have linked them to GGT or related liver outcomes. Importantly, a nonsignificant direct effect is not proof of complete mediation, and GrimAge should not be treated as a single molecular effector. It is more plausibly an integrative record of smokinglike exposure, inflammation, protein regulation, and cumulative organ stress. Moreover, the MR result supports the direction from GrimAge AA to GGT but does not validate the exposure-to-GrimAge portion of the mediation model. Accordingly, the pathway should be interpreted as biologically coherent and hypothesis-generating rather than as definitive temporal causation.

Exploratory disease MR provided additional, although suggestive, evidence of clinical relevance. Among 38 outcomes, diabetic nephropathy (β = 0.1812, P = 0.0025), osteoporosis (β = 0.0282, P = 0.0054), and cardiac valvular disease (β = -0.0545, P = 0.0074) showed suggestive associations with GrimAge AA at FDR < 0.10, although none met FDR < 0.05. Whereas the associations with diabetic nephropathy and osteoporosis were positive, the association with cardiac valvular disease was inverse; given the limited evidence supporting a protective relationship between accelerated epigenetic aging and valvular disease, this finding should be interpreted cautiously and requires independent replication. Biological aging and diabetic kidney disease share mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, inflammation, and cellular senescence; human and experimental studies have demonstrated increased senescence and impaired mitochondrial quality control in diabetic nephropathy (Chen et al., 2018; Kitada et al., 2014; Verzola et al., 2008). DNAm aging measures have also been associated with renal dysfunction and progression of diabetic complications (Matías-García et al., 2021; Vetter et al., 2023). Similarly, cellular senescence contributes to age-related bone loss (Farr et al., 2017), and a recent twin study identified GrimAge AA as a consistent epigenetic-aging correlate of osteoporosis and fracture phenotypes (Soerensen et al., 2025). A previous bidirectional MR study found no evidence for causal effects of GrimAge AA on bone mineral density at the femoral neck, forearm, or lumbar spine; however, the reverse analysis suggested that lower lumbar spine bone mineral density may contribute to accelerated GrimAge (Liang et al., 2023). Thus, these disease findings require replication and should be interpreted as suggestive links between accelerated epigenetic aging and agerelated disease rather than definitive causal relationships.

This study has several notable strengths. First, it evaluated seven epigenetic aging measures that capture distinct dimensions of biological aging, allowing the relative specificity of the GrimAge signal to be assessed rather than assumed. Second, the exposure findings were examined at complementary levels: regional exposure-group comparisons, individual biomarker associations, and multi-pollutant mixture models. The concordance of results across multivariable regression, quantile g-computation, and BKMR-particularly for 2-HF and 2-NAP-strengthens confidence that these findings were not driven by a single modeling approach. Third, the integration of clinical-trait analyses, mediation analyses, and Mendelian randomization extended the investigation beyond exposure-DNAm associations to evaluate their potential physiological and disease relevance. Finally, the focus on older Korean adults residing in environmentally vulnerable areas addresses a population in which cumulative exposure burdens and susceptibility to aging-related effects may be especially relevant.

Several limitations should be considered. First, exposure biomarkers, DNAm, and clinical measurements were largely assessed cross-sectionally, limiting temporal and causal interpretation, while single urine measurements may not adequately represent usual exposure to rapidly cleared PAHs and VOCs. Second, residual confounding by smoking, second-hand smoke, alcohol use, diet, medication, occupation, socioeconomic conditions, and pre-existing disease remains possible; this is particularly relevant because GrimAge incorporates a DNAm smoking surrogate. Third, the exposed group was older than controls, and residual age-related differences may remain despite the use of age-acceleration measures and covariate adjustment. In addition, the relatively small control group (n = 113) limited the statistical power of the regional comparisons. Fourth, although multiple epigenetic aging measures were evaluated, more recently developed measures, such as GrimAge2 and PCGrimAge, were not included, which may limit the comprehensiveness of our assessment of exposurerelated epigenetic aging. Fifth, mediation analysis relies on strong no-unmeasuredconfounding assumptions that cannot be verified in this observational setting. Sixth, DNA methylation was measured using two EPIC array versions, and although batch effects were corrected, residual array-related variation cannot be fully ruled out. Finally, although we conducted sensitivity analyses to assess instrument strength, heterogeneity, and potential horizontal pleiotropy, the genetic instruments for GrimAge AA were derived from relatively small GWAS (n = 666 for the clinical-trait MR and n = 1,892 for the disease MR) using a relaxed significance threshold (P < 5 × 10). Therefore, winner's curse, weak-instrument bias, and residual horizontal pleiotropy cannot be excluded. In the clinical-trait MR analyses, the nominal association observed for GGT did not remain significant after correction for multiple testing across the three clinical outcomes. Similarly, the disease MR findings achieved only suggestive significance at FDR < 0.10. Differences in ancestry, phenotype definitions, and genetic architecture may further limit transportability, underscoring the need for independent replication.

5. Conclusions

This study indicates that environmental pollutant exposure-most consistently PAH exposure indexed by 2-HF and 2-NAP-is associated with accelerated mortality-related epigenetic aging. GrimAge was the only aging measure that showed a coherent adverseexposure signal across regional and biomarker-based analyses, and its associations with GGT, mediation of several pollutant-GGT relationships, and suggestive MR links to diabetic nephropathy and osteoporosis support biological and clinical relevance. These results support a broader framework linking environmental pollutants to accelerated epigenetic aging and, in turn, to adverse physiological change, with GGT serving as one downstream redoxhepatometabolic indicator rather than a terminal endpoint. Longitudinal studies with repeated exposure measurements, serial DNAm profiling, component-level GrimAge analyses, and independently replicated genetic instruments are needed to determine whether reducing PAH and co-pollutant exposure can slow biological aging and lessen aging-related disease risk.
