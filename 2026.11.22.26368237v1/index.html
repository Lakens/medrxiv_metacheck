Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. Full text

Abstract

School-aged children are thought to be an important driver of influenza transmission. In this paper we study the direct and indirect health effects of a school-based live-attenuated influenza vaccination (LAIV) programme in Australia, using a stochastic, age-stratified model of seasonal influenza transmission. The model was calibrated using Approximate Bayesian Computation (ABC) in order to capture uncertainty in influenza infection dynamics. The calibrated model was used to evaluate programmes varying in target age group, vaccination coverage and vaccine effectiveness. On average, school-based LAIV programmes reduced infections and hospitalisations at the population level across all scenarios, with greater reductions at higher coverage and vaccine effectiveness and when both primary-and secondaryschool-aged children were targeted. However, estimated effects varied substantially across plausible transmission dynamics, particularly indirect effects -for some programme scenarios, we estimated that in non-target age groups both near complete suppression or a small increase in infections were possible outcomes. For all scenarios, we estimated that school-based LAIV programmes would lead to a reduction in infections and hospitalisations in target age groups.

1 Introduction

Influenza viruses circulate continuously through the human population with transmission driven by immunological, demographic, evolutionary, and seasonal processes. In temperate regions, where seasonal processes are stronger compared to the tropics, influenza viruses typically cause yearly epidemics with activity peaking during the colder months. In tropical and subtropical regions, seasonality is less distinct and various transmission regimes are observed including yearround activity or multiple peaks [1]. While the majority of influenza infections result in mild illness [2], a small proportion of infections result in more severe outcomes (e.g. hospitalisation and death); due to the high rates of influenza infections annually, this small proportion leads to substantial mortality and morbidity [3, 4]. While vaccination against influenza can be effective, 1 adaptive evolution of influenza viruses leads to new antigenically distinct strains ("antigenic drift") that can reinfect previously immune individuals [5]. As such, influenza vaccines require frequent updates (vaccine composition assessed twice a year for the northern and southern hemispheres influenza seasons) [6], and many countries including Australia recommend annual vaccination for all individuals (except very young children) [7]. The effectiveness of seasonal influenza vaccines at preventing infection and hospitalisation varies year-on-year depending on how well the vaccine strain composition matches with circulating strains, among other factors [8]. For standard injectable inactivated influenza vaccines (IIV), effectiveness is generally measured to be around 40-60% against medically-attended illness and 50-70% against hospitalisation [9].

The clinical severity of influenza infections is heterogeneous with age, with higher rates of severe outcomes observed in older individuals [10]. Thus while vaccination is recommended to all age groups, vaccination campaigns often prioritise groups most at risk of severe infection, including older individuals (e.g. by offering free and subsidised vaccines); in Australia vaccination coverage in 2025 was highest in individuals aged 65 and over (60.7%) and lowest in ages 12-<18 (12.1%) [11]. While older age groups are at greater risk of severe outcomes, it is thought that transmission dynamics are predominantly driven by younger individuals, particularly schoolaged children, who have higher rates of social contact [12, 13]. Increasing influenza vaccination coverage in school-aged children is therefore considered to have substantial potential to reduce transmission, indirectly leading to reductions in severe outcomes in more at-risk age groups (and unvaccinated school children). The advent of live attenuated influenza vaccines (LAIV) over the past decade, has led to an interest in paediatric vaccination strategies, particularly as intranasal delivery of LAIV may be more acceptable to children, and when combined with a school-based rollout programme, high coverage could be achieved in this age group. Furthermore, recent vaccine effectiveness (VE) studies of LAIV (e.g. [14]) indicate that VE is commensurate, if not higher, than standard IIV. A 2024 study from the United Kingdom reported vaccine effectiveness against primary-care attended influenza infection of 66% (95% CI: 53%-76%) in children aged 2-17 years (predominately receiving LAIV) and 47% (95% CI: 37%-56%) in adults aged 18-64 (predominantly receiving IIV) [14].

Assessing the likely effect of school-based LAIV programmes, including a quantification of uncertainty, is required to inform policy decisions. However, it is non-trivial to estimate the potential impact of such a programme due to the non-linear processes that drive influenza transmission and limitations in epidemiological data. For example, information on the true burden of influenza infections is scarce, as data is collected predominantly on downstream outcomes representing only a fraction of overall infections (e.g. cases, hospitalisations, deaths). Additionally, there are likely high levels of heterogeneity in the level of pre-existing immunity to influenza strains circulating in a given season -serological data that would allow this heterogeneity to be quantified are not routinely collected. Further, this pre-existing immunity to circulating strains can decay over time due to either: the waning of short-term immunity [15]; or the emergence of novel strains via antigenic drift. Considering the uncertainty in these dynamics is paramount for robustly quantifying uncertainty in the effectiveness of school-based vaccination programmes.

Here we develop a stochastic model of seasonal influenza transmission stratified by age, vaccination, and prior immunity status. The model is calibrated using a two-step process. First, we use Approximate Bayesian Computation (ABC) with a set of summary statistics based on the expected influenza infection dynamics in the Australian context, allowing a wide range of uncertainty in infection dynamics to be considered. Then, for each infection trajectory accepted by the ABC algorithm, we computed age-stratified infection hospitalisation rates according to observed rates of hospitalisation by age. The model was then used to estimate the population-level and age-specific impact of school-based LAIV programmes, and examine how results vary under different assumptions regarding vaccine effectiveness, vaccine coverage and target age groups. This work was conducted as part of a multi-model exercise coordinated by the Australia-Aotearoa Scenario Modelling Hub.

2 Results

2.1 Baseline infection dynamics of influenza

Approximate Bayesian Computation (ABC) identified a set of parameter combinations (model particles) that reproduced key characteristics of seasonal influenza epidemics in Australia under the current IIV vaccination programme-the Baseline scenario (Figure 1). Daily infection dynamics of accepted simulations over the one-year horizon were consistent with epidemic dynamics observed in influenza case notification data from Australian temperate regions (Figure S8). Specifically, 518 of 70,000 model particles met our ABC acceptance criteria (see Methods). These criteria targeted Baseline epidemic trajectories with a peak timing between day 147 (May 27) and day 289 (October 16) (Figure 1, panel A), an annual cumulative infection proportion between 10-45% (Figure 1, panel B), and IIV VE against infection between 38-50% (Figure 1, panel C). An example of the daily and cumulative infection incidence for such an accepted particle is shown in Figure S24 and Figure S25, respectively.

The relationship between accepted parameter combinations were highly complex due to the high dimensionality of the system (eight free parameters), but we investigated their pairwise distributions (Figure S7). We observed moderate correlation between the fraction with preexisting immunity (S 1 compartment in Methods) and the average annual transmissibility (β 0 ), and moderate negative correlation between the fraction with pre-existing immunity and the degree of seasonality (β 1 ). Accepted parameter values of β 0 corresponded to average annual values (averaged over seasonal variation) of the basic reproduction number -R 0 , the expected number of secondary infections caused by an initial infection in a fully susceptible population -between 1.58 and 2.34, with a median value of 1.74. Accepted parameter combinations of β 0 and β 1 corresponded to maximum values (i.e. when transmissibility is highest during the season) of R 0 between 1.67 and 3.00, with a median of 2.11 (see Figure S29).

As expected, due to their higher rates of assumed contacts and lower Baseline vaccination coverage, the highest infection rates were in school-aged children with annual infection attack rates per 100,000 ranging from 11,162-63,986 for children aged 5-<12 and 14,096-70,570 for ages 12-<18 (Figure S27). The lowest infection rates were in the oldest age-groups with infection attack rates per 100,000 ranging from 2,400-23,424 for ages 65-<80 and 1,616-16,283 for ages 80+. The greatest number of total infections was in ages 18-<65 reflecting the larger population size of this age group (see Figure S26). Trends in infections in school-aged children led the overall dynamics of the epidemics with peaks in infections in school-aged children leading peaks in other age groups by less than 1 week (Figures S30, S31, and S28).

2.2 Population-level impacts of school-based vaccination programmes on influenza infection dynamics

For all LAIV programme scenarios examined (see Section 4.5 for details), we estimated median reductions in infections at the population-level compared to the Baseline scenario. However, we estimated substantial variability around those estimates, including an increase in infections for a subset of model particles (Figure 2). For each of the (518) accepted particles, we simulated influenza infection dynamics for each scenario (50 simulations each) and compared the overall infection burden to simulations of the Baseline scenario (50 simulations as well). Outcomes were summarised by their median over 50 simulations to reduce uncertainty in the estimated impact of LAIV programmes due to stochastic (process) noise, and thus better characterise the uncertainty due to parameter uncertainty. Minimal variance was observed across 50 simulations and hence deemed sufficient for reducing stochastic noise (Figures S1-S4). The daily infection incidence as well as the infection attack rate across all simulated scenarios can be found in the the supplementary material (Figures S22 and S13).

As expected, the magnitude of median reductions in overall infections depended strongly on the LAIV coverage, LAIV effectiveness against infection, and target age group(s). Across all LAIV scenarios, vaccinating both primary and secondary school children (ages 5-<18) prevented a larger median proportion of infections overall than vaccinating only primary school children (ages 5-<12), by a factor ranging from 1.30 to 3.00 (Figure 2). For example, 4.2% of infections were prevented in a scenario of Central LAIV effectiveness (44-69%) and 40% coverage when LAIV was rolled out to primary school children only, which increased to 8.1% for a scenario where LAIV was also rolled out to secondary school children. Expected benefits increased with increasing LAIV coverage and effectiveness. For an LAIV programme targeting primary school children only and Central LAIV effectiveness, the median proportion of infections prevented increased from 1.7% to 9.8% as coverage increased from 20% to 80%. The greatest overall effects were observed under the most Optimistic vaccine assumptions (coverage 80%, LAIV effectiveness 63-88%) with median reductions of 13.6% (primary only) and 40.6% (both primary and secondary). In a sensitivity analysis we found that the effect of school-based LAIV programmes was highly dependent on the Baseline infection attack rate. An LAIV programme in primary and secondary school children (with Central VE and 60% coverage) led to near com-plete suppression of the epidemic for a high proportion of particles when the Baseline attack rate was lower (10-25%); whereas when the Baseline attack rate was higher (35-45%) there was a far greater proportion of particles in which the number of infections in non-target age groups increased (Figure S19). In additional sensitivity analyses, we found that school-based LAIV programmes led to greater reductions in infections (on average) when: seasonality in transmission was weaker (Figure S15); mean transmissibility (R 0 ) was lower (Figure S16); maximum transmissibility was lower (Figure S17); maximum transmissibility occurred earlier in the season (Figure S18); the Baseline peak infection incidence time was later (Figure S21); and waning of pre-existing immunity was slower (Figure S20).

We estimated a high degree of uncertainty in infections prevented across all LAIV scenarios. This uncertainty increased for scenarios with higher levels of LAIV coverage and effectiveness or broader coverage (i.e. targeting both primary and secondary school children). For instance, the 95% interval width of 24.4% (-0.2%-24.2% infections prevented) when only primary school children were targeted, increased to 71.7%(-3.6%-68.1% infections prevented) when secondary school children were also targeted (Central LAIV effectiveness and 40% coverage). While the 95% credible intervals were substantially overlapping across LAIV scenarios (due to the high degree of variability in infections prevented across model particles within each scenario), when outputs were examined by scenario and model particle, the ranked impact of each particle was similar across scenarios (Figures S13 and S5). That is, the same particles generally led to the greatest (least) proportion of infections prevented across all scenarios. This provides us with strong evidence that the ranking of scenarios in terms of expected impact is robust.

The impact of school-based LAIV programmes was greatest in the target age groups, resulting from a combination of both direct and indirect effects (Figure 3A). Infections were also prevented in non-target age groups (such as older adults) in all scenarios, for the majority of model particles, indicating indirect effects. For instance, in the LAIV scenario of Central VE (44-69%) and 40% coverage targeting children aged 5-<18, we estimated a median reduction in infections of 26.8% for those aged 12-<18, and 3.0% in ages 65-<80. However, for a fraction of model particles, an increase in infections was observed in non-target age groups for LAIV scenarios compared to Baseline, indicating potentially negative impacts of LAIV programme introduction. As this effect was computed from the median over 50 stochastic simulations for each model particle, it is not simply due to process noise. For a programme targeting children aged 5-<18, with Central VE and 20% coverage, the median proportion of infections prevented was approximately zero for all non-target age groups. However, an increase in infections (relative to Baseline) was estimated in (for example) those aged 65-79 years of greater than 5.1% for 10% of model particles. As LAIV coverage increased, the median proportion of infections prevented in non-target age groups increased-with some model particles leading to near complete suppression of transmission-but so too did the potential negative impacts; at 80% coverage we estimated an increase in infections in ages 65-79 of greater than 13.3% for 10% of model particles. These age-specific effects translated to a portion of model particles producing negative impacts at the population-level (ranging from 25.9% for 20% coverage to 8.1% for 80% coverage). The population-level infections and hospitalisations reductions across all modelled scenarios are shown in Table S1 and Table S2, respectively.

The underlying mechanism for the negative impacts was due to interactions between the timings of realised epidemics and seasonal forcing (that scales relative transmissibility). In the Baseline scenario, the model considers a wide range of parameter combinations that give rise to typical influenza epidemics in the Australian setting, including the strength and timing of seasonal forcing over the year. The model particles that led to negative impacts in non-target age groups for LAIV scenarios all exhibited similar dynamics in their corresponding Baseline scenarios: the peak in modelled infections occurred earlier in the season than the peak in seasonal forcing (See Figure 3 B and C). LAIV scenarios led to reduced transmission early in the year/season (due to higher vaccination coverage overall), thus shifting the epidemic to occur later in the seasoninto a period in which the relative transmissibility was higher, resulting in more infections in non-target age groups than at Baseline. In contrast, model particles that led to near complete epidemic suppression in LAIV scenarios exhibited Baseline peaks in modelled infections around the same time as their peak in relative transmissibility. For these particles, LAIV scenarios also shifted the epidemics to occur later, into a period in which relative transmissibility was lower (Figure S33). In sensitivity analyses, we found less uncertainty in our estimates (of the impact of school-based LAIV programmes) when seasonal changes in transmissibility were less gradual (i.e. more rapid change in transmissibility at season onset). However, some parameter combinations still led to near complete suppression or increases in infections in non-target age groups (relative to Baseline) for all patterns of seasonality in transmission considered (Figure S32).

2.3 Impact of the timing of school-based LAIV rollout

While our main analysis assumed an early rollout of vaccines to school-age children during term 1 (days 59-100, late-February to early-April), well ahead of the expected influenza season onset, we also considered a later rollout in term 2 (days 110-170, mid-April to mid-June). The median impact of an LAIV programme was similar for both rollout timings (Figure 4). The more striking result of this analysis was the reduction in uncertainty in the estimated proportion of infections prevented for a term 2 rollout compared to term 1. The timing of the term 2 rollout was later in the year, during a period in which, for some model particles, infections were already increasing (Figure 4); as such the LAIV programme had less impact on early transmission, and so epidemics were not shifted as late in the season (Figure S12). Consequently, fewer model particles produced epidemics under LAIV scenarios that: (1) resulted in increased infections in non-target age groups; or (2) greatly suppressed infections overall. This substantially reduced the range of possible outcomes and hence reduced uncertainty overall.

2.4 Impact of school-based LAIV programmes on clinical outcomes

The impact of a school-based LAIV programme on clinical outcomes depended on the assumed distribution of clinical outcomes by age. For each model particle, the total cumulative modelled infections at Baseline was used to compute age-stratified infection hospitalisation ratios (IHRs) according to a specified distribution of annual hospitalisations by age group. Those IHRs were then used in forward simulations to evaluate the impact of school-based LAIV programmes on hospitalisations.

We assumed an age distribution in hospitalisations based on Australian Institute of Health and Welfare data for 2023 (Figure S6). While the proportion of hospitalisations prevented by age group had a similar distribution to the proportion of infections prevented by age group (as expected), the proportion of hospitalisations prevented at the population-level was lower. This was because the target age groups (where we estimated the greatest impact on infections) have a lower rate of hospitalisation compared to non-target age groups. The absolute number of hospitalisations prevented by age group was dependent on the assumed distribution of annual hospitalisations by age. When a more severe influenza year/season (in terms of clinical burden) was used to calibrate the model, school-based LAIV programmes led to greater median reductions in overall hospitalisations. For example, under a 5-<18 rollout scenario of Central LAIV effectiveness and 40% coverage, we estimated median reductions in the absolute number of hospitalisations in ages 65-<80 per 100,000 people ranging from 25.8 during a 'high' burden year/season recorded by Australian jurisdictional influenza surveillance since 2020 (2024 in the state of Victoria) to 3.4 during a 'low' burden year/season (2023 in the state of New South Wales) (Figure 5). Additionally, the number of hospitalisations prevented per 100,000 people by LAIV effectiveness scenario (Figure S23) and the proportion of hospitalisations prevented per age group (Figure S14) are shown in the supplementary material.

3 Discussion

We have estimated the population-level and age-specific impact of school-based LAIV programmes in Australia under multiple scenarios for LAIV effectiveness and coverage, by developing a stochastic model of seasonal influenza transmission and calibrating it to data from Australian temperate regions. Overall, our findings suggest that school-based LAIV programmes could avert substantial influenza-associated health burden, including indirect effects in nontarget age groups. While on average school-based LAIV programmes reduced infections and hospitalisations at the population level across all scenarios explored, effects varied across plausible transmission dynamics, particularly indirect effects. For some programme scenarios, we estimated that in non-target age groups both near complete suppression or a small increase in infections were possible outcomes. However, both of these outcomes were rare when LAIV was rolled out to school children in term 2 (rather than term 1 as in the primary analysis).

Limited previous modelling studies have estimated the impact of school-based LAIV programmes; the only existing model that we identified is described in Baguelin et al [16] (with the same model also used in Wenzel et al. [17] to perform a cost-effectiveness analysis). Our estimates exhibited a far greater level of uncertainty than Baguelin et al-likely due to differences in calibration processes (see below)-and were not directly comparable due to differences in the scenarios they considered. For example, compared to our analysis Baguelin et al considered different vaccination coverages in target age groups (15%, 30%, 50% and 70%), a slightly narrower target age group (5-16 year olds) and a different VE in working age adults (e.g. 70% in well matched years), and assumed that vaccines prevented infections only (i.e. no differences in severity for breakthrough infections). Additionally, Baguelin et al assumed that influenza vaccines provide 'all-or-nothing' protection, whereas we assume a vaccine that provides 'leaky' protection. While we calibrated vaccine effects in our model to recover the targeted vaccine effectiveness [18], the different mode of protection means that even if we had constructed the same vaccine coverage and effectiveness scenarios as Baguelin et al, we would not be able to directly compare outputs.

Most routine data collected to monitor trends in infection levels are inherently biased [19]. As such we calibrated our model to summary statistics using an Approximate Bayesian Computation approach, rather than calibrating the model to specific influenza time-series which would require an observation model with additional modelling assumptions. Baguelin et al [16] calibrated their model to age-stratified time series of subtype specific "ILI+" for England and Wales. While this surveillance indicator has been recommended for improved monitoring of trends in influenza infection incidence [20] it is not a true measure of infection incidence, and thus requires additional assumptions about the probability of symptoms and health-care/test seeking behaviour-that can vary by age and over time [21, 22]-to relate this data to modelled infection incidence. For Australia, we only had access to influenza case (daily, state-level) and hospitalisation (annual, national-level) data, which are subject to similar biases. We chose to calibrate our model to summary statistics on the expected infection dynamics in Australia to avoid making specific assumptions about the data generating processes, while incorporating a range of plausible infection dynamics that could explain the data. Accordingly, our results exhibited a high degree of uncertainty, reflecting general uncertainty in the underlying dynamics of influenza transmission. If data on age-stratified influenza infection incidence could be collected, more informative prior distributions and summary statistics could be defined and incorporated into our approach, improving the precision in our estimates, including the expected impacts on clinical outcomes.

The relative timing of underlying transmissibility (i.e. seasonality) and of realised epidemics was a key sensitivity on the estimated effects of a school-based LAIV programme. Influenza epidemics peak at similar times each year in temperate regions, with limited activity or growth during the summer months. Thus our model incorporated two mechanisms that act to modulate the timing of epidemic onset: seasonality in transmissibility and waning of pre-existing immunity. The model used in Baguelin et al. did not include either mechanism; as such, infections would begin increasing immediately at the start of the simulation and thus peak timing would be heavily controlled by the initial conditions and the basic reproduction number; depending on the initial conditions, this could lead to estimates of the reproduction number being biased downwards (upwards) to delay (bring forward) the epidemic peak when calibrating the model to data. In our main analysis, accepted model particles that resulted in increases in infections compared to the Baseline scenario had the following two properties: a peak in transmissibility (i.e. peak in seasonal forcing) late in the season; and a peak in infections (for the Baseline scenario) early in the season. However, it is unclear how common that combination of properties would be in reality. Theoretical models of recurrent infection dynamics have demonstrated that it is possible for peaks in transmissibility to lead or lag epidemics [23, 24], but their relative timings for influenza are likely sensitive to complex immune-evolutionary processes (and probably vary between years). Absolute humidity is considered to be a strong driver in the seasonality of influenza transmission [25], with lower levels of absolute humidity leading to higher levels of transmissibility; data of absolute humidity for Melbourne, Australia, suggests troughs in absolute humidity approximately consistent with the timings of peak transmissibility in our accepted model particles [26]. Our sensitivity analysis explored different assumptions about seasonal variation in transmissibility. When we assumed that the function varied less smoothly over time, i.e. more closely resembling a square wave than a sinusoidal function as in the primary analysis, we found fewer (but not zero) accepted parameter combinations led to increases in infections. While further exploration of the impact of modelling choices for seasonality is warranted, our current results indicate that the timing of school-based LAIV rollout is an important consideration for policymakers. We found that the timing of Baseline epidemics for model particles producing negative impacts in the main analysis (term 1 rollout) were not shifted as late in the season when LAIV was rolled out in term 2. This meant that projected epidemics under a term 2 rollout were less likely to coincide with the peak in transmissibility/seasonal forcing, resulting in fewer negative impacts and increasing the median estimated impact of the programme. Our potentially non-intuitive finding-where increased use of a transmission mitigating intervention can lead to overall higher disease burden-has been reported previously. Theoretical modelling studies investigating the effects of intervention timing in seasonally-forced transmission systems have reported model outcomes in which vaccination or treatment strategies (that reduce transmission) result in increased peak or final epidemic sizes [27, 28].

In this study we only considered a single influenza pathogen, when in reality the overall influenza burden each year can be composed of multiple influenza types/subtypes; though our estimates can still be interpreted as the effect on a single influenza subtype for a given year. In relating the effect on influenza infections to hospitalisations, we assumed that all allowed Baseline infection trajectories could explain the observed hospitalisation burden. In reality, variation in infection and hospitalisation burden between years would be correlated; in other words, when considering a year with more hospitalisations, we might expect parameter combinations that lead to greater numbers of infections to be more likely. We assumed that pre-existing immunity was uniform across age groups, but in reality we expect there to be some degree of heterogeneity. Patterns in heterogeneity may also vary between years. Age groups with lower relative levels of immunity will contribute relatively more to transmission, and so interventions targeting such age groups will likely be relatively more effective. It is unclear if school-age children will have higher or lower levels of pre-existing immunity: school-age children are younger and so will have accumulated fewer infections in their lifetime (i.e. less immunity), but higher contact rates will likely result in shorter times since the most recent infection (i.e. greater immunity).

Our model only considers influenza dynamics over a single year. Although our calibration procedure ensured that modelled infection dynamics were consistent with a typical influenza season, there can be substantial variation in influenza infection dynamics between years that our model was unable to capture. The effects of interventions were also only assessed over a single year. However, interventions in one year may influence dynamics in subsequent years due to resultant changes in the immune landscape. For example, lower attack rates due to interventions in one year could lead to greater levels of susceptibility (and attack rates) in following years [29]. While Bageulin et al. [16] considered multiple years of data, their model (like ours) only considered a single year in isolation. Predicting the ongoing impact of an annual school-based vaccination programme would require a model of the long-term dynamics of influenza, incorporating the effects of antigenic evolution and immunity (such as in [29]).

Overall, we found that high levels of uncertainty in underlying seasonal influenza transmission dynamics translated to a broad range of estimated outcomes under different school-based LAIV programme scenarios. Despite this uncertainty, we estimated that benefits of programme introduction could be substantial, including indirect effects in non-target age groups. We also showed that the timing of programme implementation may be important, with a later start to the programme (during the influenza season) found to mitigate against the risk of increased influenza infections in adults.

4 Methods

4.1 Methodological overview

We developed a stochastic, age-stratified SIR-type compartmental model of seasonal influenza transmission that incorporates vaccination and waning of pre-existing immunity (Figure 6). Within each age group, the population was separated into (mutually exclusive) compartments according to infection status, vaccination status, and the presence or absence of partial preexisting immunity. Transitions between compartments represent the events of infection, recovery, vaccination, or waning of pre-existing immunity.

The model represents influenza transmission in a population with the age structure of Australia under a temperate seasonal transmission setting, producing a single annual winter influenza epidemic. Simulations were performed over a one-year period (January to December). Model parameter values consistent with the Australian context were identified using a combination of Approximate Bayesian Computation (ABC) and Latin Hypercube Sampling (LHS). The resulting posterior parameter sets were used to simulate a range of school-based live-attenuated influenza vaccine (LAIV) programmes under different assumptions regarding vaccine effectiveness (VE), vaccination coverage, and target age groups. For all scenarios, baseline inactivated influenza vaccine (IIV) programmes were assumed to be maintained in non-target age groups. The impact of introducing a school-based LAIV programme was evaluated by comparing epidemiological outcomes under each intervention scenario with a Baseline scenario representing current IIV vaccination coverage.

4.2 Model structure

The model stratifies the population by age group and epidemiological status. The ten epidemiological compartments considered include four susceptible compartments, four infectious compartments, and two recovered compartments. The susceptible compartments are S 0 i (fully susceptible), S 1 i (susceptible with partial pre-existing immunity), V 0 i (vaccinated without preexisting immunity), and V 1 i (vaccinated with partial pre-existing immunity). Each susceptible compartment has a corresponding infectious compartment, denoted I S 0 i , I S 1 i , I V 0 i , and I V 1 i . The two recovered compartments, R S i and R V i , represent recovered, unvaccinated and vaccinated populations, respectively. The allowed transitions are shown as arrows in Figure 6.

Our model allows for waning of pre-existing immunity in both unvaccinated (transitions from S 1 i to S 0 i ) and vaccinated (transitions from V 1 i to V 0 i ) populations. Because our simulations covered a single influenza season, immunity acquired during the simulation (due to infection or vaccination) was assumed not to wane (i.e. there are no transitions from removed (R) compartments back to susceptible (S) or vaccinated compartments (V ) to susceptible compartments).

At each time step, infection, recovery, and waning events were sampled using binomial random variables, and the resulting compartment changes were applied simultaneously.

Vaccines were administered at the start of each day, before infection, recovery, and waning events were simulated, provided the simulation was within the corresponding vaccination rollout period. For each age group, the daily number of vaccine doses was predetermined by the specified vaccination coverage and rollout schedule. These doses were allocated across all unvaccinated compartments in proportion to compartment size. Integer allocations were obtained using the largest remainder method: proportional allocations were first rounded down, and the remaining doses were assigned to the compartments with the largest fractional remainders, ensuring that the prescribed total number of doses was administered.

Model calibration was performed using a simple rejection-sampling ABC algorithm. Recently, Vollert et al. Following this framework, we defined a set of calibration targets representing characteristic features of seasonal influenza epidemics in Australia.

We sampled 70, 000 candidate parameter vectors from the prior distributions using LHS. For each parameter vector, ten stochastic realisations were simulated. Three summary statistics were calculated for each realisation. A parameter vector was accepted if at least nine of the ten realisations fell within the acceptance ranges for all three summary statistics, otherwise it was rejected. 4.5 Simulated intervention scenarios

Following model calibration, accepted parameter vectors were used to simulate multiple scenarios for a school-based LAIV programme. The simulated scenarios differed along three possible axes: the target age group for the intervention (either primary school only (5 --< 11) or including secondary school as well (5 --< 18)); the coverage of the LAIV within the target age group (20%, 40%, 60%, or 80%); and the VE against infection (categorised as Pessimistic (VE ∈ [0.38, 0.50]), Central (VE ∈ [0.44, 0.69]), or Optimistic (VE ∈ [0.63, 0.88]) within the target age group. The LAIV campaigns were implemented over a six week period starting on March 1 (day 59-100, consistent with rollout during term 1 of the school year) with vaccines administered at a constant rate (see Figure S10). We also considered a sensitivity analysis in which the LAIV campaign was implemented over a 9 week period starting on April 20 (day 110-170, consistent with a rollout during term 2 of the school year). The effects of each intervention scenario were compared with the Baseline scenario, in which no LAIV intervention occurred.

For each accepted particle, new values for φ V 0 and φ V 1 were generated representing susceptibility reductions due to LAIV (as opposed to IIV, that were obtained in the calibration step). Age groups that did not receive LAIV retained the original φ values from the calibration step, effectively making φ V 0 and φ V 1 age-dependent. The new φ values (for LAIV) were generated by an additional ABC step. For this step, simulations used Baseline vaccination coverage, with LAIV replacing IIV for ages 5-< 18 years. VE was calculated using Equation 2, restricted to the 5-< 18 year age groups receiving LAIV. The target range for the VE in this additional ABC step were the same as the ones mentioned previously: Pessimistic (VE ∈ [0.38, 0.50]), Central (VE ∈ [0.44, 0.69]), or Optimistic (VE ∈ [0.63, 0.88]). The VE was calculated using definition (2) restricted to the age groups receiving LAIV. In this additional ABC step, we also imposed the constraints φ V 1 < φ S 1 and φ V 1 < φ V 0 , such that the new LAIV-associated values retained the assumption that hybrid immunity provides greater protection than either prior infection or vaccination alone.

For each accepted particle we simulated 50 influenza epidemics for the Baseline scenario and for each intervention scenario. For a given particle, the outcomes of each simulation (e.g. total infections by age group) for an intervention scenario were compared with the outcomes of simulations of the Baseline scenario (randomly paired), yielding 50 estimates of the intervention's effect. The mean of these 50 estimates was then calculated for each parameter set, resulting in one estimate of the intervention effect per accepted particle. This pairing ensured that intervention and Baseline outcomes were compared using the same underlying parameter combinations.

These two years likely do not accurately represent the true variation in hospitalisation rates in Australia between years (due to the small sample size), but offer a representative example as to how our results will vary based on the overall hospitalisation burden used for calibration.
