Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Background Systemic sclerosis (SSc) and dermatomyositis (DM) are autoimmune diseases that have been proposed as models of cancer immunoediting. Autoantibody-defined subsets show divergent cancer risks, yet malignancy often remains clinically occult. We tested whether circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) can reveal subclinical cancer in new-onset SSc and DM.

We prospectively recruited adults with recent SSc or DM onset. Plasma ctDNA was assessed using a targeted next-generation sequencing panel covering commonly mutated cancer genes. Patients were followed for 18-24 months on average. ctDNA positivity was correlated with subsequent cancer diagnoses.

Findings Among 134 patients (69 SSc, 65 DM), cancer was diagnosed in six (4•5%; three SSc, three DM). ctDNA was detected in four of these six patients (67%) a median of 13 months (range 1•4-17) before clinical diagnosis; in three of four patients, mutations detected in plasma matched those subsequently identified in the same patients' primary tumors. ctDNA was also detected in 16 of 128 patients without a cancer diagnosis. ctDNA positivity conferred an 11•4fold higher risk of malignancy relative to ctDNA negativity and yielded a negative predictive value of 98% and a positive predictive value of 20% for cancer within the observation window.

Interpretation ctDNA testing identified most cancers that emerged within 18 months in newonset SSc and DM and stratified near-term cancer risk. These findings support larger, interventional trials to determine whether ctDNA-guided surveillance improves timeliness of cancer detection, optimizes imaging and biopsy strategies, and enhances outcomes in patients with SSc and DM.

RESEARCH IN CONTEXT

1. Evidence before this study: Patients with new onset systemic sclerosis (SSc) and dermatomyositis (DM) have an increased risk of contemporaneous cancer in distinct autoantibody subgroups, and mechanistic studies suggest that these patients may have cancerinduced autoimmunity. The optimal approach to cancer detection, and whether subclinical malignancy may be present in patients with new onset rheumatic disease, remains unknown.

2. Added value of this study:

We show that circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) is present in ~15% of patients with new onset SSc and DM. Patients with ctDNA have a higher risk of subsequent malignancy, with data suggesting that it can detect cancer earlier than conventional methods in this patient population.

3. Implications of all the available evidence:

These findings suggest that subclinical malignancy may be present in a subset of patients with new onset SSc and DM and that ctDNA may be a valuable tool to risk stratify patients for cancer.

INTRODUCTION

The relationship between cancer and autoimmunity has attracted increasing attention over the past two decades. The concept of cancer immunoediting, encompassing the processes of cancer elimination, equilibrium, and escape, has been central to elucidating interactions between the immune system and cancer. This framework has advanced understanding of both effective immune surveillance and immune dysregulation, including the emergence of autoimmune phenomena. Systemic sclerosis (SSc) and dermatomyositis (DM) are two autoimmune rheumatic diseases characterized by a substantial association with cancer in patient subgroups defined by distinct autoantibodies (1, 2). For example, patients with anti-RNA polymerase III (POLR3)positive SSc and anti-TIF1γ-positive DM have an increased risk of cancer within 3 years of rheumatic disease onset. Prior research has identified genetic alterations, including somatic mutations and loss of heterozygosity in the POLR3A and TRIM33 genes which encode POLR3 and TIF1γ, respectively, in tumors from patients with anti-POLR3-positive SSc and anti-TIF1γpositive DM (3) (4). Furthermore, in patients with SSc, it has been shown that there are mutationspecific T cell and cross-reactive autoantibody responses to both mutated and wildtype forms of these antigens. Collectively, these findings suggest that an immune response initiated by somatic mutations in emerging tumors may inadvertently result in the targeting of healthy tissues, thereby triggering autoimmunity (5). Over the last decade, this emerging model has been supported by additional studies (6) (7) (8), reinforcing the concept that the immune system's responses to tumors can have far-reaching consequences, in some cases leading to autoimmune disease.

Multiple studies have shown that only a subset of anti-POLR3-positive SSc and anti-TIF1γpositive DM patients develop clinically detectable cancer (1-2, 6-8). One explanation for this heterogeneity is that the immune responses to POLR3 and TIF1γ might be induced by multiple mechanisms, with cancer being only one of these. Alternately, it is possible that cancer is a driver of these autoimmune responses in all patients within these serologically-defined subsets.

The second explanation posits that in some patients, the immune system may successfully hold a nascent tumor in equilibrium preventing its progression to a clinically manifest cancer, perhaps even destroying the cancer. Support for this idea comes from studies showing that patients with specific SSc and DM autoantibodies have a lower than expected risk of cancer (1, 2). In particular, patients with SSc and anti-POLR3 plus anti-RNA polymerase 1 (POLR1) or anticentromere (CENP) antibodies have a lower cancer risk relative to the general population.

Additionally, breast cancer patients without SSc who have anti-CENP antibodies have improved cancer survival (9). In other patients, the immune response is not strong enough to control tumor progression, and clinically manifest cancer. Note that in both of these hypothetical scenarios, the cancer precedes the autoimmune disease.

Until recently, technological limitations restricted the detection and monitoring of early malignancies to modalities such as whole body [ 18 F] fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/computed tomography (FDG-PET/CT) imaging and flow cytometry). The advent of liquid biopsy technologies, which enable detection of circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) (10) (11) (12), has broadened this potential. In the current study, we aimed to determine whether ctDNA could be detected in patients with early onset SSc and DM prior to clinical presentation of cancer.

METHODS

Study Design and Participants

We conducted a prospective observational cohort study across two disease-specific centers, recruiting 134 patients from the Johns Hopkins Division of Rheumatology Scleroderma and Myositis Centers. Eligible patients had either SSc meeting 2013 ACR/EULAR criteria or DM meeting 2017 ACR/EULAR criteria, age ≥ 18 years, and a disease duration of less than 3 years.

No history of cancer within 3 years of disease onset was allowed, excluding non-melanoma skin cancer. Patient recruitment began in November 2018, initially targeting individuals with SSc who were anti-POLR3 positive. In 2021, the criteria were expanded to include all patients with SSc, regardless of autoantibody status, to enable comparisons across serologic subgroups.

Eligible DM patients were recruited consecutively for enrollment starting in May of 2022. The study protocols were approved by the Johns Hopkins Institutional Review Board (NA_00036834 and IRB00235256). Written informed consent was obtained from participants prior to study participation. All patients were followed longitudinally at the Johns Hopkins Scleroderma and Myositis Centers.

Procedures

All 134 patients had at least one blood sample collected. Of these, 73 had a second sample collected at least 3 months after the first. Plasma from 50 mL of blood collected in Streck tubes was purified using BioChain cfDNA Extraction Kit (BioChain, cat #K5011610) using the manufacturer's protocol. cfDNA was quantified using either electrophoresis (TapeStation cfDNA, Agilent catalog #5067-5630) or via qPCR using Sso Advanced SYBR Green Supermix (BioRad Cat #1725271) as directed by the manufacturer and employing the following primers: Amplification and fluorescence detection conditions were as follows: one cycle of 98°C for 120 seconds and then 30 cycles of 98°C for 10 seconds, 57°C for 120 seconds, and 72°C for 120 seconds. Leukocyte DNA was purified from whole blood and sheared to an average size of ~200bp using a Hielscher UIP400MTP sonicator prior to library preparation as previously described (13). DNA was purified from macro-dissected tumors as previously described (14).

Library preparation and targeted sequencing

SaferSeqS libraries were made from plasma and leukocytes as previously described (15) with the following modifications. KAPA HiFi HotStart ReadyMix (Roche) was used to amplify the DNA following ligations using the following conditions: 98°C for 45 seconds, followed by 8 cycles of 98°C for 15 seconds, 60°C for 30 seconds and 72°C for 30 seconds. The amplified DNA was purified using 1•8 X SPRI beads and eluted in 100 uL EB buffer (Qiagen). Ten uL of eluted DNA was used per well for targeted enrichment using multiplex, anchored hemi-nested PCR.

Targeted sequencing was done as previously described on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 instrument or a Complete Genomics T7 instrument (16).

Mutation Analysis

To identify somatic mutations, a driver gene panel consisting of 121 commonly mutated regions in a total of 40 driver genes was evaluated (13, 15). Only mutations present in both the Watson and Crick strands, absent in the matched normal, and defined as oncogenic were considered further. Mutations were defined as oncogenic if they were hotspots in oncogenes, or inactivating mutations (those predicted to truncate the protein product) in tumor suppressors genes -except for TP53, in which both inactivating mutations and hotspot mutations were defined as oncogenic (17) (18). Hotspot mutations in TP53 were defined as those which were non-synonymous and previously reported to be somatic in at least two different instances in the COSMIC database (19). Plasma DNA samples were scored as positive if there were two or more distinct mutant molecules identified in plasma DNA and there were no mutant molecules identified in the matched leukocyte DNA from the same patient. Positive scores in plasma could either reflect the presence of a tumor or some other clonal process, such as clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP) (13). To exclude CHIP with high confidence, we analyzed matched leukocytes from every patient for every mutation identified in the plasma sample. The number of leukocyte DNA molecules assessed was in excess of the number needed to ensure that the same mutation was not present with >99•99% probability based on a binomial distribution. In these distributions, the expected probability was set as the mutant allele fraction (MAF) observed in the plasma for that specific mutation. For example, if the MAF for a given mutation in the plasma was 0•02%, we evaluated >46047 leukocyte DNA molecules. The number of DNA molecules was calculated as the number of duplex UIDs observed in the sequencing, as there is a 1:1 correspondence between the number of duplex UIDs and the number of DNA molecules present in the library made from any DNA sample (15).

Outcomes

The outcomes of interest were ctDNA positivity and a clinical cancer diagnosis. Only patients who had biopsy proven histopathologic diagnosis of invasion were classified as having cancer; patients with benign tumors, including non-invasive in situ tumors of the skin, were not classified as having cancer.

Statistical analysis

Statistical tests were performed to assess whether there were differences in demographic, clinical and autoantibody characteristics between ctDNA-positive and ctDNA-negative patients. Detailed definitions and autoantibody assay methods are provided in the Supplement. The Student's t-test was used for continuous variables and Fisher's exact test for categorical variables. The Benjamini-Hochberg method was used to correct for multiple comparisons. All analyses were performed using R version 4•3•2.

RESULTS

Demographic and Disease Characteristics

A total of 134 patients were enrolled in the study, comprising 69 with SSc and 65 with DM.

Both cohorts consisted predominantly of white females (64% female, 83% white for SSc; 78% female, 83% white for DM). The median age of rheumatic disease onset was 57 years (IQR 50-67) for SSc and 53 years (IQR 37-62) for DM [Tables 1 2]. These demographic characteristics are consistent with those of our overall cohorts. Among the 69 SSc patients, the most prevalent autoantibody was anti-POLR3 (n=28, 49%), followed by anti-RNPC3 (n=17, 30%), Ro52 (n=11, 19%), and anti-centromere, anti-Scl-70, and anti-Th/To (all n=8, 14%). In the 65 DM patients, anti-TIF1γ was the most frequent autoantibody (n=27, 43%), followed by anti-Ro52 (n=17, 26%) and anti-NXP2 (n=13, 21%) [Tables 1 2

Somatic mutations

After excluding 68 mutations attributable to clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP; 45 mutations in 23 patients in the SSc cohort, 23 mutations in 9 patients in the DM cohort), 16% (11/69) of SSc patients and 9% (6/65) of DM patients had at least one mutation that was scored positive at baseline. There were no significant differences in sex, race, or ethnicity between ctDNA-positive and negative patients [Tables 1 2]. After correction for multiple comparisons, no non-neoplastic disease feature or disease activity measure differed significantly between ctDNA-positive and ctDNA-negative patients [Tables 1 2, Supplemental Tables 1 2].

However, there were several interesting trends including ctDNA-positive patients with SSc were more likely to have limited SSc (73% vs 22%, p=0•123), a lower mean modified Rodnan skin score (mRSS) at the time of ctDNA blood draw (6 vs 11, p=0•322), and positive anti-centromere autoantibodies (40% vs 9%, p=0•123) [Table 1, Supplemental Table 1]. In both SSc and DM, there were no differences in immunosuppressive treatment regimens by ctDNA positivity [Supplemental Tables 3 4].

Among both diseases, mutations in the TP53 gene were the most prevalent at baseline. In SSc, eight out of 11 (73%) patients who were ctDNA positive had a TP53 mutation, and one patient each (9%) had mutations in PTEN, PIK3CA and FGFR3. In the DM cohort, three of six patients (50%) harbored TP53 mutations, and one patient each (17%) had mutations in PTEN, PIK3CA and EGFR. Among the 11 SSc ctDNA-positive patients, two were diagnosed with cancer within a median follow-up of 1•4 years [Tables 3 4]. Among the 6 DM ctDNA-positive patients on baseline testing, one was diagnosed with cancer within a median follow-up of 2•4 years.

In approximately half of the patients (n=27 in SSc and n=46 in DM), a second blood draw was obtained and available for analysis. Among the 17 patients who were ctDNA-positive at baseline, 11 (five SSc, six DM) had a second sample available. In SSc, none of the five (0%) patients had a positive second sample (and none had cancer), whereas in DM, two of six (33%) patients remained positive for the same mutation(s). Of the two DM patients who were positive in both the first and second samples, one later developed cancer (MYOS 59), while the other patient remained cancer-free after 2•5 years of follow-up. Additional data on somatic mutations on the subgroup of patients with a second ctDNA measurement are found in the Supplement.

Cancer Detection

In the SSc cohort, three of 69 patients (4•3%) were diagnosed with cancer during follow-up, including a prostate cancer (Gleason grade 7), a stage IV lung squamous cell carcinoma, and an invasive squamous cell carcinoma of the ear with perineural invasion [Table 4]. Two of the three patients with cancer had detectable ctDNA: One patient, SCL 212, had a detectable mutation (TP53 p•E258K with mutant allele frequency (MAF) 0•173%; Table 3) 17 months prior to a Stage IV lung cancer diagnosis. Interestingly, this patient had a negative PET/CT 16 months prior to lung cancer diagnosis despite ctDNA positivity. Analysis of the corresponding tumor tissue from this patient identified the same mutation that was detected in plasma. The second patient, SCL 200, had a positive mutation (TP53 p•S371X with MAF 0•02%; Table 4) 17 months prior to being diagnosed with an invasive squamous cell carcinoma of the ear; this mutation was not detected in the corresponding cancer tissue. We did not detect ctDNA in the patient with prostate cancer, SCL 208, in a plasma sample taken 65 months prior to diagnosis.

In the DM cohort, three of 65 patients (4•6%) were diagnosed with cancer, which included a stage III lung adenocarcinoma, a stage I squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) of the tonsils, and a stage I bladder carcinoma [Table 4]. The patient with lung adenocarcinoma, MYOS 59, had a positive ctDNA test (EGFR p•L858R with MAF at 3•03%; Table 3) 8•5 months prior to lung cancer diagnosis. The EGFR mutation was identified in the corresponding primary tumor. This patient underwent a contrast-enhanced chest CT seven months before diagnosis that identified a pulmonary nodule with a clinical recommendation for repeat imaging in one year. The patient who was diagnosed with bladder cancer, MYOS 43, had negative ctDNA eight months prior to cancer diagnosis, but a positive ctDNA test one month prior to cancer diagnosis. Two mutations were identified at this timepoint: a TP53 p•R280T mutation, which was also found in the matched tumor, and a VHL splice site mutation that was not identified in the primary tumor. This patient had a negative PET/CT scan six months before diagnosis. In the patient with tonsillar SCC, MYOS107, ctDNA was not detectable in a blood sample collected four months prior to cancer diagnosis.

Across both diseases, four of six patients who developed cancer had a positive ctDNA test (two in SSc, two in DM), whereas the assay was positive in 16 of the 128 patients who remained cancer-free during follow-up (nine in SSc, seven in DM) [Figure 2]. Thus, ctDNA positivity was associated with a 11•4-fold higher risk of subclinical malignancy compared with ctDNA negativity, with a negative predictive value of 98% and a positive predictive value of 20%.

DISCUSSION

Compelling epidemiologic and mechanistic studies suggest that cancer may drive the development of autoimmunity in subgroups of patients with SSc and DM identified by distinct autoantibody responses. However, most patients within these autoantibody subgroups do not have a clinically diagnosed cancer. In this study, we detected evidence of cancer, through analysis of ctDNA in blood, in four of six patients who developed a clinically detectable cancer at a median of 13 months prior to clinical diagnosis. Of note, in two of these 4 cancers, diagnostic PET/CTs around the time of ctDNA positivity were unremarkable, highlighting the potential value of ctDNA assessment as a cancer screening tool.

One striking finding of our work is that of 20 patients with detectable ctDNA mutations, only four developed a clinically detectable cancer despite intensive cancer surveillance over the ensuing months. And in one (SCL 200) of the four patients with a positive ctDNA test, the mutation was not identified in the patient's subsequent cancer biopsy tissue. Though the nominal positive predictive value (PPV 20%) of our test was higher than that for mammography for breast cancer or spiral CT for lung cancer, it is still lower than optimal from an interventional standpoint. There are at least two hypotheses to explain the low PPV. The most intellectually exciting of these is that more of these ctDNA-positive patients actually had cancer, but the cancer was not large enough to cause symptoms or to be detected by routine surveillance measures. According to this hypothesis, some of the ctDNA-positive patients will be diagnosed with cancer in the future. Indeed, ctDNA can be detected up to three years prior to clinical cancer diagnosis (13), and the median follow-up time for patients in the current study was only 17 months. The presence of cell-free DNA but the absence of clinically detectable cancer could accordingly represent a state of equilibrium between the immune system and cancer (7). This hypothesis is consistent with the underlying rationale for our study, i.e., the immune response elicited by the tumor is sufficient to inhibit its progression, sometimes to the extent that clinical cancer never appears.

The other hypotheses explain the ctDNA-positives as either technical or biological artifacts, so there is no cancer at all present in the patients with positive ctDNA results. When the same ctDNA test is applied to young healthy individuals, false positives are rare, so technical artifacts are minimized (15). However, biological "artifacts" are still possible. The most likely type of biological artifact is clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP). Though we evaluated very large numbers of molecules to confidently exclude leukocyte DNA mutations in even a minor fraction of leukocytes, we could not exclude mutations that arose from an even smaller fraction of lymphocytes, such as those that contributed cfDNA in excess of their representation in peripheral blood counts. Prior studies using DNA methylation have indeed shown that B-lymphocytes contribute more cfDNA than expected from their peripheral blood cell fraction (20). Moreover, other studies have suggested a higher prevalence of clonal hematopoiesis of CHIP in patients with autoimmune disease (21) (22). Longitudinal monitoring of circulating tumor DNA mutations, paired with clinical phenotyping of patients' disease activity and autoantibody profiles, is currently underway to test these relationships.

A noteworthy strength of our study design was to purposefully focus on patients with newly diagnosed autoimmune disease (<3 years), as this is the population at highest risk of cancer diagnosis. Additionally, our inclusion of patients with a broad range of autoantibodies with different epidemiologic cancer associations was informative. Multiple epidemiologic studies have shown that specific autoantibody profiles stratify cancer risk within the first three years after rheumatic disease onset, with some profiles associated with increased risk, others with decreased risk, and others with no change (1, 2). However, several of the ctDNA positive patients had autoantibodies, such as anti-centromere, that have been linked to a decreased cancer risk in cohort studies or when compared to the general population. A potential explanation is that these autoantibody specificities are a surrogate for a highly effective anti-tumor immune response, accounting for the negative association observed in prior epidemiologic studies. It is noteworthy that among the six SSc patients who had an initial positive ctDNA test followed by a negative test, three had anti-centromere antibodies (SCL250, SCL252, SCL254).

Our study has several limitations. While we tried to employ systematic cancer screening strategies, not all patients underwent recommended tests in part due to the Covid-19 pandemic, and in part due to patient preference. Only a subset of patients had a second sample available for ctDNA analysis, and an important priority is examining the dynamic relationship between ctDNA levels, evolution of the immune response, and cancer emergence over time. As noted above, the follow-up time is relatively short, so the false-positive rate may be overestimated.

And the relatively small number (n=six) of patients that developed cancer in this cohort engender wide confidence limits in all of the reported outcomes.

What are the potential clinical implications of our findings? From a rheumatology perspective, the earlier detection and treatment of cancer may lead to improvement, if not remission, of the rheumatic disease [23] [24]. From an oncologic perspective, the earlier detection of cancer could lead to improved survival, and even cure, of the cancers. There is evidence that a delay of even a month between diagnosis of a cancer and its surgical removal has a substantial effect on survival (25) (26) (27). And there is also much data indicating that anti-cancer drugs of all types are much more likely to induce prolonged remissions or cures when a smaller tumor burden is present at the time of treatment (28) (29) (30). Conversely, the relatively high ctDNA positive rate observed in our study is concerning, as it could lead to additional anxiety, or needless procedures, in patients who already have a serious autoimmune disease.

In summary, we have unequivocally shown that the cancers in some patients with specific types of autoimmune diseases can be detected with a blood test before conventional methods are able to detect them. This study thereby sets the stage for larger, prospective studies to determine whether such testing can benefit patients.
