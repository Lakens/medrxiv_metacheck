To fix Exact P-Values

What we found

We found 4 imprecise p values out of 82 detected p values.

Show details

Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 48 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

The Effect Size check searches for regular expressions that match typical ways in which effect sizes are reported. It subsequently checks different ways in which Cohen’s d, g, ηp2, and ωp2 can be computed against the reported value. If effects are missing, or might be incorrect, you the module provides a warning. The module was validated on APA reported statistical tests, and might miss effect sizes that were reported in other reporting styles. It was validated by the Metacheck team on papers published in Psychological Science.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

The mammillary bodies are a central component of the hippocampal-diencephalic-cingulate network that is critically involved in emotion processing and memory function, yet their genetic architecture remains unknown. We conducted a genome-wide association study (GWAS) of mammillary body volume in 38,064 participants from the UK Biobank and replicated our findings in the Rhineland Study cohort (N = 4,860). Mammillary body and hypothalamic subregional volumes were derived from structural brain MRIs using FastSurfer-HypVINN, an established and validated deep learning-based segmentation tool. Univariate GWAS identified 34 genomic loci associated with mammillary body volume, including 26 novel loci, with 20 loci showing replication in the Rhineland Study. Metaanalysis across both cohorts (N = 42,924) identified 44 associated loci, of which 35 were novel. Multivariate GWAS of hypothalamic subregions identified 55 loci, including 40 novel associations. Integrative cross-omics analyses identified MEI1 and CAPZA1 as key molecular candidates, with genetically regulated MEI1 expression and circulating CAPZA1 protein levels partially mediating genetic effects on mammillary body volume. Genetic correlation and Mendelian randomization analyses further revealed a bidirectional relationship between mammillary body volume and major depressive disorder. Together, our findings elucidate the genetic architecture of mammillary body volume and its relation to mental disorders.

INTRODUCTION

The mammillary bodies are a pair of small, rounded nuclei located on the posteroinferior surface of the hypothalamus (1). They are a central component of the Papez circuit (2), receiving input from the hippocampus via the fornix and projecting predominantly through the mammillothalamic tract to the anterior thalamic nuclei. This network plays a fundamental role in emotion and memory processing, particularly episodic and spatial memory consolidation (1). In addition to hippocampal inputs, the mammillary bodies also receive inputs from brainstem tegmental nuclei (1), indicating a broader role in limbic circuitry, beyond a simple hippocampal relay function.

The mammillary bodies have been implicated in different neurological and psychiatric diseases, the most prominent being Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome, in which mammillary body dysfunction leads to severe anterograde amnesia (3). Furthermore, postmortem studies have found alterations in mammillary body volume in patients with bipolar disorder and a significant volume reduction in major depressive disorder (MDD) compared to individuals without heart failure (5), suggesting an unrecognized role in these two common affective disorders as well. Another postmortem study showed selective vulnerability and neuronal degeneration of mammillary body neurons in Alzheimer's disease, suggesting that dysfunction of this structure may also contribute neurodegeneration (3). In addition, in an analysis of two large population-based cohorts, we recently demonstrated that mammillary body volume substantially decreases with age in both sexes (4). Conversely, larger mammillary body volume was associated with better global cognition as well as better domain-specific cognitive performance, including working memory, episodic memory, executive function and processing speed (4). Together, these findings support the relevance of the mammillary bodies in affective diseases and age-associated cognitive decline, warranting further scrutiny of the determinants of their structure and function.

Surprisingly, despite their critical role in cognitive and affective processes, the genetic and molecular mechanisms underlying interindividual differences in mammillary body volume remain largely unknown. Although a recent genome-wide association study (GWAS) of the hypothalamus and its subregions identified 23 genomic loci associated with the overall volume of the hypothalamus and its neighboring subregions, the mammillary bodies were analyzed only as part of the posterior hypothalamus, together with the lateral hypothalamus and the tuberomammillary nucleus (5). However, the genetic architecture specific to the mammillary bodies could not be resolved independently from these neighboring structures. Moreover, brain images were analyzed using an earlier hypothalamic segmentation tool (6), which was trained on manually labeled hypothalamic subunits derived from T1-weighted MRI scans. More advanced, multimodal, fully automated segmentation tools of the hypothalamus and its adjacent structures have been developed since (7), which enable a more fine-grained delineation of hypothalamic substructures, including distinct parcellation of the mammillary bodies.

Using the FastSurfer-HypVINN tool, which enables accurate multimodal segmentation of the hypothalamus and its subregions, we performed a GWAS of mammillary body volume, followed by a multivariate GWAS incorporating all major hypothalamic subregions, including the anterior, medial, tuberal, lateral and posterior subregions. We estimated the heritability of mammillary body volume and other hypothalamic subregions using linkage disequilibrium score regression (LDSC) and assessed the genetic overlap with volumetric measures in other brain regions. In addition, to investigate the relationships with mood-related traits, psychiatric disorders, and neurodegenerative diseases, we examined genetic correlations between these outcomes and mammillary body volume and conducted causal inference analyses using two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) analyses. For the genomic loci identified to be associated with mammillary body volume, we also performed expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) and protein quantitative trait loci (pQTL) analyses. Lastly, we evaluated gene and protein expression associations with mammillary body volume. Overall, through this integrative, multi-omics approach we identified several key genes and proteins that underpin the structural integrity of the mammillary bodies and found (potentially causal) relationships between mammillary body volume and psychiatric, neurodevelopmental and neuro-degenerative disorders.

METHODS

Study population

For the discovery GWAS we included 38,064 participants from the UK Biobank (UKBB) with available structural brain MRI scans and genetic data. For replication analyses, we included 4,860 participants from the Rhineland Study, who had structural brain MRI scans and genetic data available. Imaging datasets that failed quality control following visual inspection of the MRI scans were excluded from the analyses. By combining the discovery and replication cohort, we performed a GWAS meta-analysis with a total sample size of 42,924 participants. The UKBB study was approved by the National Information Governance Board for Health and Social Care and the National Health Service North West Centre for Research Ethics Committee (Ref: 11/NW/0382). The Rhineland Study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Medical Faculty of the University of Bonn and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent.

Imaging data

In UKBB, brain imaging data were acquired using Siemens Skyra 3T MRI scanners equipped with 32-channel head coils. Imaging was initially conducted at a single scanning site in Cheadle (Manchester, UK) using one scanner and was later expanded to include two additional scanners located in Newcastle and Reading (8). Detailed information on the MRI acquisition protocol and sequence parameters is available on the UKBB website (biobank.ctsu.ox.ac.uk/crystal/crystal/docs/brain_mri.pdf). In the Rhineland Study, imaging data were acquired using two identical 3T Siemens MAGNETOM Prisma MRI scanners equipped with 64-channel head-neck coils. MRI scans were acquired at two examination centers in Bonn, Germany (9). Segmentation of the mammillary bodies and hypothalamic subregions, including the anterior, medial, lateral, tuberal, and posterior region, was performed using the fully automated deep learning-based segmentation tool FastSurfer-HypVINN (7). In the Rhineland Study, segmentation was performed using 0.8-mm isotropic T1-and T2-weighted images, whereas in UKBB, 1.0-mm isotropic T1-weighted images were used. FastSurfer-HypVINN has previously been validated against manually segmented ground-truth MRI data from both the UKBB and the Rhineland Study. Estimated total intracranial volume (eTIV) was derived for both cohorts using the standard FreeSurfer (version 6.0) processing pipeline (10).

For image quality control within the Rhineland Study, all scans underwent visual inspection based on predefined criteria. Conversely, due to the larger sample size of the UKBB Imaging Study, we implemented a targeted approach: 1,561 scans were initially flagged as statistical outliers because their volumetric measures deviated by more than 2 standard deviations from the mean. All of these flagged datasets were subsequently reviewed manually. Additionally, a random subset of 500 scans from the remaining pool was visually inspected to evaluate segmentation quality, yielding a 96.4% pass rate. Exclusion criteria included severe motion artifacts, poor tissue contract, or incomplete hypothalamic subregions coverage that compromised reliable segmentation.

Genotyping data

In UKBB, genetic quality control was performed according to standard protocols (11). Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) were excluded based on low call rate (<95%), minor allele frequency (MAF<1%), and deviation from Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium (HWE; p < 1×10 ). At the sample level, participants were excluded based on low call rate, abnormal heterozygosity, and non-European ancestry. Genotype imputation was performed using the Haplotype Reference Consortium (HRC) imputation panel. In the Rhineland Study, genotyping was performed using the Illumina Omni 2.5 Exome Array. Signal intensity files were processed using GenomeStudio (version 2.0.5). SNP and sample-level quality control procedures were conducted using PLINK (version 1.9). SNPs were excluded based on missingness (>2%), deviation from HWE (p < 1×10 ), and MAF <1%. Samples were excluded based on low call rate, abnormal heterozygosity, and non-European ancestry, which was determined using principal component analysis implemented in EIGENSTRAT (version 6.1.4) (12). Quality-controlled genotypes were imputed to the HRC reference panel (version r1.1) using the Helmholtz Munich Imputation Server (13).

Transcriptomic analysis

We functionally validated our results using gene expression data of the first 3000 consecutively enrolled participants of the Rhineland Study. Samples used for RNA sequencing were stored in PAXgene Blood RNA tubes (PreAnalytix/Qiagen), and were thawed and incubated at room temperature to increase RNA yields. Total RNA was isolated according to the manufacturer's instructions using PAXgene Blood miRNA Kit following their automated purification protocol (PreAnalytix/Qiagen). RNA integrity and quantity were evaluated using the TapeStation RNA assay on a TapeStation4200 instrument (Agilent). The Tapestation RNA assay (Tapestation4200 instrument from Agilent). After using 750 ng of total RNA to generate next generation-sequencing libraries for total RNA sequencing (TruSeq stranded total RNA kit, Illumina), a Ribo-Zero Globin reduction was performed. Libraries were quantified using Qubit HS dsDNA assay (Invitrogen) and clustered at 250 pM concentrations on a NovaSeq6000 instrument. Quality control of the sequencing reads was performed with FastQC (v0.11.9). Following the filtering of low-quality score reads with Trimmomatic (v.0.39), sequencing reads were aligned to the human reference genome GRCh38.p13 using STAR (v2.7.1). The count matrix was generated with STAR -quantMode GeneCounts using the human gene annotation version GRCh38.101. Genes with overall mean expression > 15 reads and expressed in at least 5% of the participants were retained for further analysis. Raw counts were normalized and transformed using the varianceStabilizingTransformation function from DESeq2 (v1.30.1). Following quality control and merging with genomic and imaging data, 1,891 participants were included in the downstream eQTL and gene-expression association analyses.

Proteomic analysis using OLINK

In the Rhineland Study, among the first 5,500 participants enrolled before April 2020, 2,852 were selected using age-and sex-stratified random sampling for proteomics analysis. Plasma proteins were measured using the Olink Explore platform, which incorporates internal and external quality control (QC) procedures to ensure assay and sample reliability. Two QC metrics were evaluated: QC_warning at the sample level and Assay_warning at the protein level. Samples received a QC_warning (WARN) if average matched counts were <500 or if Incubation or Amplification Control deviated by ±0.3 NPX from the plate median. Manually flagged samples were labeled MANUAL_WARN. At the assay level, an Assay_warning (WARN) was assigned if the median of triplicate negative controls deviated by >5 SD from predefined validation thresholds. As an initial filtering step, proteins flagged as "Excluded" were removed; if a protein was excluded within a sample-panel combination, all proteins from that panel in that sample were removed. In total, 56 proteins were excluded. Outlier detection was performed within each panel using: 1) principle component analysis (PC1 or PC2 > ±6 SD), and 2) NPX distribution metrics (median or interquartile range > ±6 SD). One sample was consistently identified as an outlier across all panels and was excluded. In the final dataset comprising 2,888 protein assays, 123 assays (4.3%) had Assay_warning flags. QC_warning percentages ranged from 0% to 8.6%, with higher rates mainly in the Oncology II panel. Most assays showed 0% warnings, indicating high technical reliability. Assay warnings ≥ 5% were rare and primarily observed in the Oncology II and Inflammation II panels. Following quality control and merging with genomic and imaging data, 2,299 participants were included in the downstream OLINK-based pQTL and protein association analyses.

Proteomic analysis using SEER

Among participants enrolled in the Rhineland Study before November 26, 2021, 551 were selected randomly after stratifying by age and sex, creating a random representative subset of the entire cohort. Plasma (240 μl) from each participant was sent to Seer Inc.® for quantification with the Proteograph XT platform, providing untargeted and deep proteomic profiling via liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry. Liquid chromatography was performed via the Thermo Scientific Vanquish Neo UHPLC system, while mass spectrometry was performed via two Thermo Fisher Orbitrap Astral instruments. Peptide identification relied on the data-independent acquisition workflow, DIA-NN, using search engine v1.8.1 with Seer's in-house database, uniprot_homosapiens_isoforms_con_20221209.fasta. Additionally, library-free in silico searches were performed using the UniProt reference database. Seer applied a 1% false discovery rate threshold for the identification of peptide and protein group levels. An updated internal Seer pipeline was used for additional quality control. The pipeline first median-normalized the data within each LC-MS run, using precursors detected in 80% or more of the samples. The MaxLFO algorithm was then used to combine the separate XT nanoparticle wells into a single protein abundance estimate for each sample. Finally, plate-level batch effects were corrected using the LIMMA R. This returned 7,960 uniquely identified proteins across the 551 samples. Protein intensities were then ranktransformed and principle component analysis was run on these transformed intensities. Only proteins with intensity measurements available in at least 10% of samples were included in downstream analyses. Following quality control and merging with genomic and imaging data, 376 participants were included in the downstream SEER-based pQTL and protein association analyses.

Univariate GWAS

Univariate GWASs were conducted separately for each phenotype including mammillary body volume and volumes of all other hypothalamic subregions using the univariate framework implemented in MOSTest (14). For each phenotype, residuals were calculated after adjusting for age, age², sex, top 10 genetic principal components, eTIV, and cortical volumes of the frontal, parietal, temporal, and occipital lobes. In addition, analyses accounted for cohort-specific technical covariates, including MRI scanner site, batch effects, and genotyping array where applicable. Rank-based inverse normal transformation was applied to each phenotype prior to the MOSTest (14) analysis. SNP-wise Z-statistics were obtained for each phenotype from the univariate GWAS.

Multivariate GWAS

The univariate Z-scores were subsequently used as inputs for the multivariate analysis. The phenotypic covariance structure was estimated using genome-wide Z-scores under permutation to model the null distribution while accounting for correlations among traits using MOSTest (14). For each SNP, a multivariate test statistic was computed that integrates the vector of univariate GWAS Z-scores while adjusting for their covariance structure, thereby capturing distributed genetic effects across correlated hypothalamic subregions. Under the null hypothesis of no association, the test statistic follows a chi-square distribution with degrees of freedom equal to the number of phenotypes. MOSTest fits a gamma distribution to the empirical null obtained from permutations to accurately estimate tail probabilities and extrapolate genome-wide significant p-values below 5 × 10 without requiring an infeasible number of permutations. Genome-wide significance was defined at p < 5 × 10 .

Meta-analysis GWAS

The univariate GWAS summary statistics for mammillary body volume were used to perform a GWAS meta-analysis using METAL (15). We performed a fixed-effects, inverse varianceweighted meta-analysis using the GWAS summary statistics from both cohorts.

Definition of genomic risk loci

The FUMA (16) platform was used to identify genomic risk loci for univariate and multivariate GWAS. Genomic risk loci were defined as genomic regions containing independent significant SNPs and the SNPs in high linkage disequilibrium (r² > 0.6) with them. LD blocks of independent significant SNPs located within 250 kb of each other were merged into a single genomic risk locus. Lead SNPs were defined as independent significant SNPs with r² < 0.1 with one another. We used the 1000 Genomes Phase 3 (version 5) reference panel (17) for LD calculation and loci mapping.

Gene and functional mapping

Using ANNOVAR (18), SNPs were positionally mapped to genes located within 10 kb of the variant. Using eQTL mapping, SNPs were further mapped to genes expressed in brain tissue databases, including GTEx v8 (19), BRAINEAC (20), PsychENCODE (21), and the CommonMind Consortium (22). Using chromatin interaction mapping, genes involved in significant chromatin interactions with SNP-associated regions were identified using datasets from PsychENCODE EP links (20), PsychENCODE promoter-anchored loops (22), Hi-C data from brain tissues (including adult cortex (23), fetal cortex (23), dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (24), and hippocampus ( 24)), Hi-C data from neural progenitor cell lines (24), and enhancer annotations from 17 brain-related repositories of the Roadmap Epigenomics Project (25) (i.e., E053, E054, E067, E068, E069, E070, E071, E072, E073, E074, E081, E082, E003, E008, E007, E009, E010, E057). Enhancer regions were defined as 250 bp upstream and 500 bp downstream of transcription start sites. Interactions passing the default false discovery rate (FDR) threshold (p < 1 × 10 ) were visualized using Circos plots. Using the GENE2FUNC tool within FUMA, we further investigated the biological context of the mapped genes by examining their enrichment in 53 GTEx tissue-specific gene expression sets (19), curated MSigDB gene sets (26), and the GWAS Catalog (27) of previously reported genetic variant-trait associations. Gene sets were considered significant if at least two input genes overlapped with the tested gene set and survived Bonferroni-adjusted p < 0.05.

Gene-based association and gene-set enrichment analyses

Gene-based analyses were conducted using GWAS summary statistics from the univariate, multivariate, and meta-GWAS analyses in MAGMA (28). For gene-based association analyses, FUMA tested 18,818 protein-coding genes and applied a Bonferroni correction to account for multiple testing across all genes. Using 17,009 curated gene sets available in MSigDB version 2023.1. Hs (29, 30), gene-set enrichment analyses were conducted to test whether genes within predefined biological process and disease pathway gene sets showed stronger associations (i.e., lower p-values) compared to genes outside those sets.

Heritability and genetic correlation of hypothalamic subregions with regional brain volumes

Using GWAS summary statistics from the meta-analysis, SNP-based heritability (h²_SNP) was estimated with LDSC (31) for mammillary body volume and volumes of other hypothalamic subregions using HapMap3 SNPs. Genetic correlations were subsequently calculated between the meta-analysis GWAS results for mammillary body volume and volumes for adjacent hypothalamic subregions. In addition, genetic correlation analyses were performed to compare our univariate meta-analysis GWAS summary statistics with previously published GWAS of hypothalamic subregions (5). Genetic correlation was also estimated with 101 regional brain volumetric measures (32). For this analysis, mammillary body volume GWAS summary statistics additionally adjusted for total brain volume (TBV) were used.

Genetic correlation with traits and diseases

We estimated genetic correlations using LDSC between mammillary body volume, and hypothalamic subregions and a range of traits and diseases, including body mass index (BMI) (33), cognitive performance (34), daytime napping (35), chronotype (36), sleep duration (35), neurodegenerative diseases including Alzheimer's disease (37) Parkinson's disease, dementia and AvoHILMO-defined dementia (38), neurological symptoms including memory impairment (39), migraine with aura (40) and abnormal involuntary movement (40), psychiatric disorders including major depressive disorder (41) , schizophrenia (42) disorders of psychological development (38), habit and impulse disorders (38), mental and behavioral disorders due to opioids, stress-related disorder (38).

Two-sample Mendelian Randomization

Using the TwoSampleMR R package (v0.5.7), bidirectional MR analyses were conducted to investigate the causal associations between mammillary body volume and the traits and diseases examined in the genetic correlation analyses. To minimize potential sample overlap arising from previous GWAS that included UKBB participants, GWAS for daytime napping and sleep duration were re-run in the UKBB using previously described models. For MDD, summary statistics excluding UKBB participants (pgc-mdd2025_no23andMe-noUKBB_eur_v3-49-24-11.txt) were used. For systolic and diastolic blood pressure, summary statistics from a large independent GWAS conducted in the Million Veteran Program (MVP) were used. The primary method for causal estimation was the inversevariance weighted (IVW) (43) regression approach. Sensitivity analyses were performed using additional MR methods, including the weighted median, weighted mode (44), and MR-Egger regression (45). For the IVW and MR-Egger models, heterogeneity among SNP instruments was assessed using Cochran's Q test (46). When significant heterogeneity was detected, outlier instruments were identified and removed using radial MR (47). To assess horizontal pleiotropy, the MR-Egger intercept test was applied. Independent genetic instruments were selected using linkage disequilibrium (LD) clumping with a 10,000 kb window and an r² threshold of 0.001, retaining SNPs reaching genome-wide significance (p < 5 × 10 ) based on the European ancestry reference panel from the 1000 Genomes Project.

SNP instruments with an F-statistic < 10 were excluded to minimize weak instrument bias (48, 49).

Functional validation through gene expression analyses

Candidate genes were identified through positional, expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL), and chromatin interaction mapping implemented in FUMA. The eQTL analyses were performed for the independent lead SNPs (r 2 < 0.1) and candidate SNPs in linkage disequilibrium with the lead SNPs (r 2 > 0.6) using residualized gene expression levels. Gene expression levels were adjusted for age, sex, the first 10 genetic principal components, red and white blood cell counts, the relative fractions of basophils, eosinophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, and neutrophils, and batch effect. Statistical significance was defined using an FDR threshold of < 0.05. To functionally validate prioritized candidate genes, association between mammillary body volume (dependent variable) with residualized gene expression levels (independent variables) were assessed using linear regression models. Gene expression residuals were generated after adjusting for the same covariates as in the eQTL analysis, namely age, sex, batch effects, red and white blood cell counts, and the proportions of basophils, eosinophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, and neutrophils. A nominal significance threshold of p < 0.05 was applied for this step. For gene-SNP combinations showing significant SNP-gene and gene-mammillary body volume associations, mediation analyses were performed using structural equation modeling in the R package lavaan (v0.6-11). A standard three-variable mediation framework was used as: gene expression ~ a × SNP, mammillary body volume ~ b × gene expression + c′ × SNP, with path a representing the effect of the SNP on gene expression, path b representing the effect of gene expression on mammillary body volume conditional on the SNP effect, and path c′ the direct effect of the SNP on mammillary body volume conditional on gene expression. The indirect effect was calculated as a × b, and the total effect as a × b + c′. Models were fitted using the sem() function in lavaan, and confidence intervals were estimated using 1,000 bootstrap resamples. For mediation analyses, gene expression residuals were adjusted for age, sex, batch effects, and blood cell counts, while mammillary body volume residuals were adjusted for age, age², sex, eTIV, genetic principal components and frontal, parietal, temporal, and occipital cortical volumes.

Functional validation through protein expression analyses

For protein-level validation, we analyzed circulating plasma proteins, focusing on proteins linked to lead SNPs (r 2 < 0.1) and candidate SNPs (r 2 > 0.6) mapped to candidate genes using SEER and OLINK proteomics platforms. pQTL analyses were conducted with models adjusted for age, sex, batch effects, the first 10 genetic principal components, and the first principal component of protein expression (for SEER), as well as estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR). For OLINK data, adjustment for plate effects and protein-expression principal components was not required. To evaluate potential causal effects of circulating protein levels on mammillary body volume, we additionally performed Mendelian randomization using publicly available pQTL summary statistics from the deCODE Genetics large-scale plasma proteomics study as the exposure and the mammillary body volume GWAS meta-analysis as the outcome.

Colocalization and Mendelian randomization analysis

We performed colocalization analyses to determine whether genetic variants associated with candidate genes showing significant mediation effects between SNPs and mammillary body volume shared causal variants with eQTLs identified in whole blood and brain tissues from GTEx v8. Variants located within ±500 kb of the lead SNPs were extracted and merged with GTEx v8 eQTL summary statistics. Colocalization analyses were conducted using the coloc.abf() function implemented in the R package coloc (50). This method estimates posterior probabilities for five hypotheses: H0, neither trait is associated with the region; H1 and H2, only one of the two traits is associated with this region; H3, both traits are associated but with distinct causal variants; and H4, both traits are associated and share a common causal variant. The prior probability of a shared causal variant (H4) was set to 1×10 . Evidence for colocalization was considered strong when the posterior probability for H4 (PP.H4) exceeded 0.75. To investigate potential causal relationships between gene expression and mammillary body volume, we also performed two-sample MR analyses using the R package TwoSampleMR as described above. eQTLs from whole blood and brain tissues were used as instrumental variables for gene expression (exposure), while mammillary body volume was used as the outcome. Exposure and outcome datasets were harmonized to align effect alleles and ensure consistent effect directions.

RESULTS

Sample characteristics

We included genetically unrelated participants from both cohorts, with sample sizes of 38,064 and 4,860 individuals from UKBB and the Rhineland Study, respectively (Supplementary Table 1). The average age was higher among UKBB (64.5 ± 7.7 years) than Rhineland Study participants (55.6 ± 13.4 years). The mean hypothalamic volume was slightly larger in participants of the Rhineland Study (1125.9 ± 105.5 mm 3 ) compared to those of UKBB (1103.5 ± 119.7 mm 3 ). In contrast, the mean mammillary body volume was slightly larger in UKBB (217.1 ± 29.8 mm 3 ) as compared to the Rhineland Study (210 ± 26.9 mm 3 ) participants.

The mammillary body volume GWAS identified 34 genomic risk loci, of which 26 were novel, after adjustment for adjacent cortical brain structures (Table 1, Figure 1A, Supplementary Table 2). Meta-analysis GWAS of mammillary body volume

The meta-analysis GWAS combining summary statistics from UKBB and the Rhineland Study for mammillary body volume replicated 29 genome-wide significant genomic risk loci identified in the discovery GWAS and identified 15 additional genomic loci (Figure 3, Table 2, Supplementary Table 12). These loci included CAPZA1, EXOC4, STMN4, SFRP1, RSPO2, ZNF462, KIAA1328, UBOX5:FASTKD5 and NHP2L1. In total, among 44 genomic loci identified, 35 were novel. Across these 44 genomic loci, 53 independent lead SNPs (r² < 0.1) were identified (Supplementary Table 13), which were in linkage disequilibrium (r² > 0.6) with 5,921 candidate SNPs (Supplementary Table 14).

Using independent lead SNPs and candidate SNPs in LD (r² > 0.6) with the lead variants based on meta-analysis GWAS summary statistics, gene mapping was performed through multiple strategies (Supplementary Table 14). Positional mapping identified 47 genes, eQTL mapping identified 57 genes, and chromatin interaction mapping identified 178 genes. Mammillary body volume showed significant positive genetic correlations with multiple regional brain volumes, including the right ventral diencephalon (rg = 0.3333, SE = 0.0406, FDR-adjusted P = 2.35 × 10 19, Figure 6).

Genetic correlation of mammillary body volume meta-GWAS with traits and diseases

Given the important role of the hypothalamus in the regulation of a diverse array of homeostatic functions, we estimated the genetic correlation between mammillary body volume and anthropometric measures (including BMI), cognition, neurological symptoms such as memory impairment, abnormal involuntary movements and migraine with preceding neurological symptoms, neurodegenerative diseases such as Parkinson's disease and Alzheimer's disease, mental disorders such as all-cause dementia, MDD, disorders of psychological development, habit impulse disorders, opioid-related and severe stress related disorders, and sleep related traits such as daytime napping, chronotype and sleep duration. Among these traits, significant genetic correlations were observed for all-cause dementia (rg = -0.1579, SE = 0.0659, P = 0.0166, FDR-adjusted P = 0.0383), MDD (rg = -0.0617, SE = 0.0240, P = 0.0101, FDR-adjusted P = 0.0336), opioid related disorder (rg = -0.1767, SE = 0.0725, P = 0.0148, FDR-adjusted P = 0.0383), severe stress related disorder (rg = -0.1414, SE = 0.054, P = 0.0088, FDR-adjusted P = 0.033), disorders of psychological development (rg = 0.2509, SE = 0.1026, P = 0.0145, FRD-P = 0.0383) and abnormal involuntary movements (rg = -0.1037, SE = 0.0431, P = 0.0162, FDR-adjusted P = 0.0383) (Supplementary Table 21, Figure 7).

Two-sample Mendelian Randomization

We investigated potential causal relationships between mammillary body volume and disorders that survived FDR correction in the genetic correlation analysis using bidirectional two-sample MR. Among these, only MDD showed evidence of causal association. In the forward MR analysis, the primary inverse-variance weighted (IVW) analysis showed that smaller mammillary body volume was associated with an increased risk of MDD, although the association did not reach statistical significance (IVW: β = -0.026, SE = 0.019, P = 0.176). Significant heterogeneity was detected among the genetic instruments (Cochran's Q = 107.35, P = 1.56 × 10 ).

After excluding outlier SNPs identified by radial MR, the association became statistically significant (IVW: β = -0.037, SE = 0.012, P = 1.72 × 10 ³, FDR-adjusted P = 0.012), indicating that a decrease in mammillary body volume was associated with an increased risk of MDD. The weighted median analysis showed a consistent direction of effect and remained statistically significant following outlier removal (β = -0.034, SE = 0.016, P = 0.035) Figure 8A). In the reverse MR analysis, a higher genetic liability to MDD was also associated with smaller mammillary body volume following removal of outlier SNPs (IVW: β = -0.10, SE = 0.03, P = 1.75 × 10 ³, FDR-adjusted P = 0.01). Together, these findings suggest a bidirectional association between mammillary body volume and MDD (Supplementary Table 22, Figure 8B).

Transcriptomic analysis

To further investigate the genes mapped to the lead SNPs from the meta-GWAS, we performed functional validation using individual-level transcriptomic data available in the Rhineland Study. Specifically, we leveraged 177 candidate genes originally identified through positional and functional annotation of 5921 SNPs in LD with 115 independent significant SNPs from the meta-GWAS summary statistics. Out of these, expression data on 51 genes and 1627 candidate SNPs were available in the Rhineland Study for the transcriptomic analysis. In the eQTL analysis, we identified 706 SNPs showing significant associations with the expression of 20 genes. The strongest association was observed for rs139545, which was associated with decreased expression of the MEI1 gene (FDR-adjusted P = 1.74 × 10 55, P = 1.91 × 10 57 , β = -0.57, SE = 0.03). This was followed by rs11006122, which was associated with UBE2D1 gene expression (FDR-adjusted P =2.41 × 10 52 , P = 9.46 × 10 54 , β = -0.36, SE = 0.02) (Supplementary Table 23, Supplementary Figure 2A).

For genes that survived FDR correction, we further assessed the association between gene expression and mammillary body volume. Among these, MEI1 showed a nominally significant association, with higher MEI1 expression associated with smaller mammillary body volume (FDR = 0.42, P = 0.022, β = -0.06, SE = 0.03), followed by GSTP1 (FDR= 0.42, P = 0.042, β = -0.061, SE = 0.03) (Supplementary Table 24, Supplementary Figure 2B). In addition, we performed mediation analyses using the expression level of the MEI1 gene that was significantly associated with both mammillary body volume and its corresponding SNP (Supplementary Table 25). Mediation analysis revealed that the effect of 104 SNPs on mammillary body volume were significantly mediated through MEI1 expression (indirect p < 0.05, FDR < 0.02), with the absolute ratio of the indirect effect of the total effect size ranging from 78% to over 100%.

Causal association of MEI1 gene with mammillary body volume

MR analysis provided support for a potential causal relationship between MEI1 expression and mammillary body volume across multiple GTEx tissues. In whole blood, a significant negative causal association was observed (IVW β = -0.0851, SE = 0.0150, FDR-adjusted P = 2.75 × 10 ). In other brain tissues, significant causal associations were also observed, including cortex (β = -0.1111, SE = 0.0191, FDR-adjusted P = 1.70 × 10), caudate basal ganglia (β = -0.1526, SE = 0.0245, FDR-adjusted P = 3.58 × 10 ), hippocampus (β = -0.1048, SE = 0.0189, FDR-adjusted P = 4.83 × 10), putamen (β = -0.1422, SE = 0.0244, FDR-adjusted P = 1.70 × 10), hypothalamus (β = -0.0951, SE = 0.0210, FDR-adjusted P = 6.92 × 10), and anterior cingulate cortex BA24 (β = -0.0639, SE = 0.0117, FDRadjusted P = 6.32 × 10 ) (Supplementary Table 27); these estimates were based on the Wald ratio test due to the availability of only a single SNP instrument.

To further evaluate whether the same underlying genetic variants influenced both MEI1 expression and mammillary body volume, we performed colocalization analyses across tissues. Strong evidence of colocalization (posterior probability for a shared causal variant, PP.H4 > 0.75) was observed between the mammillary body volume GWAS signal and MEI1 eQTLs in the caudate (PP.H4 = 0.901), putamen (PP.H4 = 0.789), hypothalamus (PP.H4 = 0.783), whole blood (PP.H4 = 0.767), cortex (PP.H4 = 0.755), and hippocampus (PP.H4 = 0.755) (Supplementary Table 26, Supplementary Figure 3).

Proteomic analysis

We conducted pQTL analyses for candidate SNPs and proteins encoded by 177 candidate genes using data from the SEER and OLINK proteomic platforms. In the SEER dataset, 31 proteins were available for both pQTL analyses and protein association analyses with mammillary body volume, whereas approximately 10 proteins were available in the OLINK dataset. In the SEER platform, after correction for multiple testing we identified 63 SNPprotein associations involving four proteins (GSTP1, CAPZA1, RPS6KB2 and ZC3H7B) (Supplementary Table 28, Supplementary Figure 4A). In the OLINK dataset, 27 SNPprotein associations involving four proteins (TNFRSF13C, SLIT2, PMCH & GSTP1) survived FDR correction (Supplementary Table 31). We further examined the association between circulating protein levels and mammillary body volume in both datasets. Only CAPZA1 protein levels derived from the SEER platform showed a significant association with mammillary body volume after multiple testing correction (FDR-adjusted P = 0.015, P = 0.003, β = 0.48, SE = 0.16) (Supplementary Table 29, Supplementary Figure 4B). Finally, we performed mediation analyses to assess whether CAPZA1 protein levels mediated the association between CAPZA1 pQTL variants and mammillary body volume. Mediation analysis revealed that seven CAPZA1 pQTL variants showed indirect effects through CAPZA1 protein levels, with a mediation effect accounting for 60.2% of the total genetic effect on mammillary body volume (Supplementary Table 30). To further investigate causality, we performed Mendelian randomization using the lead CAPZA1 pQTL variant as an instrumental variable. The Wald ratio analysis showed that genetically predicted higher CAPZA1 protein levels were associated with larger mammillary body volume (β = 0.35, SE = 0.15, P = 0.020).

DISCUSSION

Leveraging data from two large-scale population-based cohorts (N=42,924), we performed the first GWAS of mammillary body volume. Our meta-GWAS identified 44 genomic loci associated with mammillary body volume, of which 35 are novel. Moreover, we found that compared to other hypothalamic subregions, mammillary body volume had the highest SNP-heritability (i.e., 29%). These findings provide a wealth of new insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying interindividual differences in mammillary body volume.

Interestingly, SNORA73 (tagged by rs77126132) was identified as the most significant locus associated with mammillary body volume, followed by loci near BICD1, ESRRG, RFC3P1, RP11-361D14.2, AC138647.1, NAMPTL and RP11-210L7.1. Indeed, in our study, genetic correlation analyses showed significant genetic overlap between mammillary body volume and those of the posterior hypothalamus and other hypothalamic structures, consistent with previous reports (6). SNORA73 has previously been implicated in cellular metabolism through the regulation of oxidative phosphorylation and mTOR signaling pathway (51). The identification of SNORA73 as the most significant locus for mammillary body volume suggests that RNA-mediated metabolic regulation may contribute to structural variation in mammillary bodies. Given the role of the hypothalamus in energy homeostasis and metabolic processes (52), this finding provides a potential molecular link between metabolic regulation and the size of mammillary bodies. Notably, the mammillary bodies are highly susceptible to neuropathological damage caused by Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome. In this syndrome, a deficiency of thiamine leads to impaired energy metabolism and causes specific neuronal damage (53). Although a direct association between SNORA73 and thiamine metabolism has not yet been established, both are crucial in managing cellular energy pathways. Therefore, the variation in SNORA73-mediated regulation of cellular energy metabolism may influence the metabolic stability of the mammillary bodies, making them more susceptible to this neurological disorder. Although this hypothesis is speculative, it provides a plausible molecular link between SNORA73-energy regulation and mammillary bodies' physiology. Future experimental studies are necessary to investigate this potential link. Similarly, BICD1 (Bicaudal Cargo Adaptor 1), another associated locus, encodes an adaptor protein that regulates microtubule-based intracellular cargo transport via the dynein motor complex and is involved in cytoskeletal organization (54), which is critical for neuronal structural integrity and brain development (55). Our transcriptomic analysis revealed that the lead SNP rs7298381 and its neighboring SNPs in LD (r² > 0.6) act as eQTLs for BICD1 in whole blood. Moreover, querying of the GTEx database further supported the role of these variants as eQTLs of BICD1 in several other brain regions, including the caudate, hippocampus, and cerebellar hemisphere. Together, these findings support a potential role for BICD1 in the structural development and maintenance of the mammillary bodies.

Among the genes prioritized by positional, eQTL, and chromatin interaction mapping, several have been previously implicated in neuronal development and brain structure, making them particularly plausible candidates for influencing mammillary body volume. These genes included MOV10, RSPO2, CCDC53, XRCC4, TOB2, DESI1, NHP2L1, MEI1, CCDC134, KIAA1841 and XPO1. MOV10 has been shown to be involved in neuronal development through cytoskeletal mRNA regulation, supporting normal brain development and function, with reduced MOV10 expression impairing dendritic arborization in hippocampal neurons (56). RSPO2, which encodes R-spondin 2 protein enhances Wnt signaling through leucinerich repeat-containing G protein-coupled receptors (LGR4-6), and is expressed in the brain (57). Moreover, RSPO2 has been previously associated with subcortical volume and brain morphology (58), while loss of RSPO2 in hippocampal CA3 neurons impairs synaptic plasticity and memory formation in mice (59). However, it remains to be shown how MOV10, RSPO2 as well as the other genes associated with mammillary body volume, precisely affect the development and morphology of this structure.

Positional gene-set analysis further identified significant enrichment at chromosome 22q13 locus, which encompasses TOB2, DESI1, NHP2L1, MEI1, and CCDC134. Deletions involving the 22q13 region have been associated with Phelan-McDermid syndrome, a neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by intellectual disability, severely delayed language development, and autism spectrum features (60), further highlighting the neurodevelopmental relevance of this genomic region and its candidate genes as potential determinants of mammillary body structure.

Using individual-level transcriptomic data from the Rhineland Study, we identified 706 SNPgene expression associations involving 20 genes. Among them, MEI1 emerged as a particularly interesting candidate. We found genetically regulated MEI1 expression to be associated with mammillary body volume, with additional mediation analysis revealing that MEI1 expression partially mediates the relationship between variants in this gene and mammillary body volume. Similarly, colocalization analysis provided strong evidence for a shared causal variant influencing both mammillary body volume and MEI1 expression across multiple tissues, including the hypothalamus, caudate, putamen, hippocampus, and whole blood. These findings suggests that MEI1 expression in these tissues is regulated by the same genetic variants that influence mammillary body volume. MEI1 (Meiotic Double-Strand Break Formation Protein 1) is primarily known for its role in meiotic recombination based on studies in mice (61). Our findings thus indicate that MEI1 may represent a novel candidate gene influencing mammillary body volume and suggest that it may have previously unrecognized functions in the adult human brain, warranting further investigation.

Our proteomic analyses identified significant pQTL associations involving seven proteins (including GSTP1, CAPZA1, RPS6KB2, ZC3H7B, TNFRSF13C, SLIT2 & PMCH). Among these, CAPZA1 emerged as the most interesting protein, as circulating CAPZA1 protein levels were also significantly associated with mammillary body volume. Several CAPZA1 pQTL variants were additionally associated with CAPZA1 protein abundance and showed significant mediation effects, indicating that CAPZA1 protein levels partially mediate the relationship between variants in this gene and mammillary body volume. CAPZA1 encodes for the alpha subunit of the F-actin capping protein, involved in maintaining cytoskeletal organization through regulation of cortical actin filaments (62), a mechanism critical for neuronal structural integrity and synaptic maintenance. Moreover, findings from mice studies suggest that CAPZA1 interacts with PCDH19, a calcium-dependent cell-adhesion protein, to affect brain development, neuron migration and differentiation (63).

Our multivariate GWAS, including the mammillary bodies alongside all other hypothalamic subregions, identified 55 genomic loci, of which 40 are novel compared to previous multivariate hypothalamic GWAS findings (5). Moreover, using gene mapping strategies, we identified 28 genes that showed significant enrichment in the hypothalamus, anterior cingulate cortex and frontal cortex, brain regions that play a critical role in neuroendocrine regulation, cognition and emotional processing. Genetic correlation analyses revealed that mammillary body volume shares its genetic architecture with several neighboring brain structures, most notably the ventral diencephalon, hippocampus, thalamus, and pallidum. In particular, the strongest genetic correlation was observed with the ventral diencephalon bilaterally, consistent with its anatomical proximity and shared developmental origin. The genetic overlap with the hippocampus and thalamus is particularly meaningful, as these structures together with the mammillary bodies form key components of the Papez circuit, a network central to episodic memory, spatial navigation, and emotional processing. Intriguingly, we found that larger mammillary bodies showed significant genetic overlap with lower risks of dementia, MDD, opioid-related disorders and severe stress related disorder. Interestingly, among these disorders showing significant genetic overlap with mammillary body volume, seven genes located within the mammillary body volume risk loci (i.e., ASCC3, CELF4, ESRRG, EXOC4, SIM1, Y_RNA and ZFHX3) have previously been implicated in the pathogenesis of depression, suggesting the presence of shared biological pathways between mammillary body structure and MDD. Indeed, through two-sample MR we found evidence for a bidirectional relationship between mammillary body volume and MDD risk. On the one hand, genetic instruments associated with mammillary body volume were associated with an increased risk of depression, suggesting that reduced mammillary body volume may contribute to depression susceptibility. On the other hand, higher genetic liability to MDD was also associated with reduced mammillary body volume, suggesting depression-related biological processes may also contribute to structural changes in mammillary bodies. Previous large-scale imaging studies have already shown that the hippocampus is smaller in people with depression (64). While most research has focused on the hippocampus, our study shows that the mammillary bodies, which connect directly to the hippocampus, also share a causal link with depression. Although these two regions are closely connected, our findings highlight the mammillary bodies as a distinct genetic factor in depression, paving the way for future studies to look at how these structures interact as a network.

Our study has several limitations. First, the sample size of our discovery GWAS, while substantial, was modest compared to GWAS of other brain structures. Nevertheless, we identified novel genetic loci associated with mammillary body volume, a large proportion of which were also replicated in the independent Rhineland Study cohort, providing novel genetic insights into mammillary body structure through meta-analysis. Second, we were unable to assess whether the identified loci are associated with temporal changes in mammillary body volume, which would be valuable for identifying genetic determinants of age-related volumetric changes. Incorporating longitudinal data on mammillary body volume in future GWAS studies would overcome this limitation and facilitate the identification of loci associated with changes over time. Finally, our GWAS was restricted to individuals of European ancestry, which limits the generalizability of our findings to other populations. Future large-scale multi-ancestry GWASs are warranted to further help assess the heterogeneity of genetic effects and identify additional genetic determinants of mammillary body size.

In this study, we identified numerous genetic loci associated with volumes of the mammillary bodies, which showed a highly heritable and distinct genetic architecture within the hypothalamus. Through integrative multi-omics analyses, we pinpointed several genes and biological pathways that may underlie our identified GWAS findings. In genetic correlation and causal inference analyses, we delineated potentially causal links between common neurodegenerative and affective disorders and volumes of the mammillary bodies. Given that the mammillary bodies are a relatively understudied component of the Papez circuit and existing evidence points towards their involvement in different neurological and psychiatric disorders, our findings provide a unique and valuable foundation to understand their genetic determinants and thereby lay the groundwork for further delineation of their precise role in the pathogenesis of many brain disorders.
