Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Motor dysfunction is a major contributor to disability in pediatric-onset monogenic neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs), yet systematic quantifications of motor phenotypes across development using validated clinical scales remain underdeveloped. Existing studies have classified motor phenotypes rather than quantified domain-specific severity, limiting their use as outcome measures in clinical trials. Here, we systematically quantify motor dysfunction using established clinical rating scales to explore their utility as motor endpoints.

In this cross-sectional observational study, 102 individuals with NDDs (0.5-38 years) secondary to pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in five genes (SCN2A, KCNQ2, CSNK2A1, CACNA1A, SCN8A) and 31 unaffected sibling controls underwent standardized motor assessments using the Scale for Assessment and Rating of Ataxia, Burke-Fahn-Marsden Dystonia Scale, and the Modified Ashworth Scale. Regional and functional categorization of parameters enabled differentiated interpretation. Gene-specific patterns were evaluated using z-score standardization across the pooled affected cohort, with gene-level averaging and nonparametric bootstrap-derived 95% confidence intervals.

Dysfunction was present in individuals with NDDs across speech (90%), coordination (87%), dystonia (83.3%), gross motor (82.4%) and spasticity (42.2%), with minimal impairment in controls (0-12.9%). We observed substantial heterogeneity across motor domains and subdomains; ataxic dysarthria had the highest score distribution (median 0.83 [IQR, 0.17-1.0]), whereas spasticity showed near-floor scores. Individuals with SCN2A variants demonstrated higher mean z-scores across multiple subdomains than other genes, with more consistent deviations observed in coordination and speech domains (Coordination: mean z = 0.29, 95% CI 0.03-0.54; Speech: mean z = 0.3, 95% CI 0.03-0.57), whereas domain-specific or less pronounced patterns were observed in other genes. The ataxia and dystonia scales demonstrated low floor and ceiling effects. Discussion: Using three clinical scales across a broad developmental and functional range, our study moves motor phenotyping in monogenic NDDs from categorical description toward quantitative, domain-resolved severity assessment. Exploratory analysis indicates marked heterogeneity in severity profiles across genes, most notably in SCN2A, suggesting potential selective motor vulnerability. These findings provide evidence for the feasibility of motor phenotyping and provide preliminary, cross-sectional benchmark severity distributions. Subsequent longitudinal validation and validation in larger, longitudinal, and variant-stratified cohorts will enable natural history studies and future therapeutic trials alongside seizure outcomes.

Introduction

Monogenic neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs) are characterized by diverse neurological, cognitive and autonomic impairments. 1, 2 Motor dysfunction, ranging from abnormalities of tone to severe movement disorders, is a commonly observed yet poorly studied feature, despite its substantial impact on long-term functional outcomes and quality of life. [3] [4] [5] Recent evidence suggests motor dysfunction in individuals with NDDs is diverse 6 , present across a broad age range, 7, 8 and associated with most NDD-related genes. 9, 10 Similarly, large repositories of parent-reported data such as Simons Searchlight 11 and metaanalyses 12, 13 provide evidence for the near-universal presence of motor dysfunction in monogenic NDDs, emphasizing that it is not merely a gene-or syndrome-specific feature. It is thus imperative to develop tools to reliably detect motor dysfunction and monitor its evolution. However, the motor outcome measures validated for NDDs remain underdeveloped, especially in contrast to seizure-related outcomes, due to several limitations of our current knowledgebase. Firstly, motor dysfunction can be subtle in severity and difficult to distinguish from seizures, complicating detection and clinical management. 14 As such, much of the prior work has focused on identification and subtype classification, rather than on quantifying severity. 6 The limited previous application of standardized motor rating scales across a wide age range has further restricted the quantification of motor dysfunction and inclusion of motor outcome measures in clinical trials. 15, 16 Second, monogenic NDDs are rare, constraining prior studies by cohort size. Therefore, many analyses rely on parent-reported outcomes and retrospective chart reviews. 17, 18 Due to the dependence on multi-gene cohorts with often fewer than 10 individuals per gene 6 or larger parent-reported registries 9, 11 , gene-specific patterns of motor dysfunction are not well understood either. Finally, although videobased rating approaches have been developed in rare disease contexts, these have emphasized screening and rapid identification of motor dysfunction rather than deep, multidomain severity profiling intended for endpoint selection. 19 Ultimately, studies using uniform, validated motor assessment instruments across individuals with NDDs remain elusive. To define severity estimates for motor dysfunction in individuals with monogenic NDDs, we quantified motor phenotypes in a large, cross-sectional cohort spanning five monogenic NDDs from infancy through adulthood using established rating scales. We provide domain-level quantification of motor dysfunction and establish genotype-associated patterns. By anchoring motor phenotyping to three clinician-assessed scales, this work provides preliminary benchmark distributions of motor dysfunction severity and frequency. Subsequent longitudinal follow-up studies will evaluate within-person trajectories, test-retest reliability, and responsiveness to clinically meaningful change. This will support their eventual use as quantitative outcome measures for motor function in therapeutic trials for NDDs. 20

Results

Cohort Overview

We analyzed motor phenotypes in 102 individuals with a neurodevelopmental disorder secondary to a pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in five NDD-associated genes and 31 unaffected sibling controls (controls) using unique, standardized motor assessments (Table 1). Among individuals with NDDs, SCN2A was the largest group (n = 48), followed by KCNQ2 and CSNK2A1 (n = 20 each), CACNA1A (n = 8), and SCN8A (n = 6) (Table 1). Participants were recruited from multidisciplinary clinics at UTHealth Houston or from genespecific advocacy events across the United States and Europe between August 2024 and September 2025. The age at assessment ranged from five months to 38 years (median 10 years; interquartile range [IQR] 5 -15 years). Two controls and seven affected individuals were under three years of age. The cohort was predominantly White (~78%), with limited representation from Asian, Hispanic, and Black participants, reflecting known recruitment biases in rare disease cohorts. 21

Individuals with NDDs exhibit a broad range of motor impairments

Motor phenotypes were assessed using standardized clinical scales for spasticity (Modified Ashworth Scale [MAS]), 22 dystonia (Burke-Fahn-Marsden Scale [BFMS]), 23 and ataxia (Scale for the Assessment and Rating of Ataxia [SARA] 24, 25 . We first screened for the presence of motor dysfunction in the combined cohort of individuals with NDDs and controls across five domains (Figure 1A, Table 2). Speech dysfunction, including dysarthria and language-related dysfunction, was most prominent, occurring in 90% of individuals with NDDs. Gross motor and coordination deficits, and dystonia were observed in 82.4%, 87% and 83.3% of individuals with NDD, respectively, whereas spasticity was less common (42.2%; Figure 1A). In contrast, controls exhibited minimal impairment (0 -12.9% across domains; Figure 1A). Overall, these results demonstrate that the scales detect abnormal motor phenotypes, and that minimal dysfunction is seen in controls. Given this low frequency of motor dysfunction across domains in controls, subsequent analyses focused on assessments involving individuals with NDDs.

Motor impairment severities follow subdomain-specific patterns

To capture heterogeneity of motor dysfunction in body part involvement, we assessed impairment across granular subdomains in all individuals with NDDs (Table 2; Figure 1B). Speech dysfunction as scored by the SARA which includes ataxic dysarthria and anarthria unatribbutable to a ataxia specifically, had the highest score distribution, with a median score of 0.83 (IQR 0.17 -1), indicating maximal impairment in at least half of the cohort. Coordination deficits (median 0.44, IQR 0.12 -0.9) and gross motor deficits had lower score distributions (median 0.17, IQR 0.06 -0.58). Speech dysfunction as scored by the BFMDS was also prominent (median 0.25, IQR 0 -0.75), whereas axial, limb, and orobuccal dystonia remained in the lower range (medians 0.03 -0.09, IQR 0 -0.25). Spasticity parameters had near floor-level distribution across axial and limb regions (Figure 1B). To asssessed consistency between raters, we evaluated inter-rater reliability for BFMS assessments. Agreement between the primary rater and an expert committee was excellent (ICC(2,1) = 0.90, 95% CI 0.84-0.94; n = 57 complete paired assessments; Table S2). We also estimated ceiling and floor effects for the three scales to identify measurement-related confounding. All spasticity-related subdomains demonstrated a pronounced floor effect (70-86%). In contrast, we observed minimal floor effects for ataxia (10-16%) and dystonia (22-55%) subdomains (Figure S1). Ceiling effects were generally low across subdomains, except for the speech parameter scored by the SARA (~50%) (Figure S1).

Genotypes exhibit subdomain-specific motor impairments

We next explored gene-associated patterns across the cohort using group-level z-score averaging and descriptive statistics (Figure 2 and S2). No formal statistical comparisons were performed across genotype groups due to power constraints given the small gene-specific cohorts; confidence intervals are provided to illustrate variability in these exploratory estimates. Individuals with SCN2A variants demonstrated higher mean z-scores across most subdomains, suggesting greater overall severity relative to other genes in our analysis (Figure 2). Mean z-scores for ataxia coordination and speech (Atax_Coord: mean z = 0.28, 95% CI 0.03-0.54; Atax_Speech: mean z = 0.3, 95% CI 0.03-0.57; Table S3) indicated dysfunction in these domains with consistent deviation from the cohort mean. However, confidence intervals overlapped zero across several other domains, reflecting uncertainty in the magnitude of differences (Table S3). In contrast, individuals with KCNQ2 and CACNA1A variants exhibited domain-specific patterns with considerable uncertainty. KCNQ2 was associated with higher mean z-scores in speech dystonia (Dys_Speech: mean z = 0.27, 95% CI -0.22-0.78), 81 whereas CACNA1A-associated dysfunction was largely confined to gross motor ataxia (mean z = 0.19, 95% CI 82 -0.47-0.9; Table S3). Individuals with CSNK2A1 and SCN8A variants showed lower relative severity across 83 subdomains in this cohort, although confidence intervals for most domains overlapped zero, indicating 84 substantial uncertainty in these estimates (Figure 2 and S2; Table S3). Given the overall low cohort sizes, all 85 genotype-related findings should be considered hypothesis-generating, highlighting selective domain-level 86 differences rather than uniform genotype effects. 87 88 89

Discussion

Motor dysfunction is widespread across monogenic NDDs but poorly defined and monitored. 26 In this prospectively recruited, cross-sectional study, we quantify motor dysfunction frequency and severity in 102 individuals with monogenic NDDs using three complementary scales assessing ataxia, spasticity, and dystonia, with particularly deep quantification of individuals with SCN2A, KCNQ2, and CSNK2A1-related NDDs. Unlike prior studies utilizing retrospective chart review or video assessment, we utilize a standardized video protocol across five monogenic NDDs with domain-resolved quantification, providing greater phenotypic resolution and measurement rigor than categorical descriptions of motor dysfunction alone. Although gene-stratified results are exploratory, our observations suggest potential genotype-specific motor profiles that may inform the design of larger, adequately powered studies. Overall, our results provide crucial benchmark distributions of domainspecific motor dysfunction in monogenic NDDs. Subsequent longitudinal validation will support the development of motor endpoints for natural history studies and clinical trial readiness in rare diseases. Prior studies have established the presence of motor dysfunction in individuals with monogenic NDDs. Van der Veen et al. reported the diversity of movement disorders in 77 individuals covering 38 genetic DEEs with known movement disorders, identifying both hypo-and hyperkinetic disorders using caregiver-submitted and archived clinical videos. 6 Montanucci et al. similarly provide frequency estimates of motor phenotypes in 1210 probands across 87 NDD-associated genes without in-depth quantification. 27 More recently, Bernardi et al summarized the growing body of knowledge suggesting motor dysfunction is prominent in epilepsy-associated NDDs. 28 Gene-specific studies also suggest disease mechanism-dependent motor dysfunction but they primarily describe motor phenotypes as comorbidities rather than quantifying frequencies and genotypeassociated expression patterns. 10, [29] [30] [31] [32] [33] A key retrospective study by Mastrangelo et al. provides important reference ages for motor phenotypes. 34 Thus, the major gap is no longer whether motor dysfunction occurs, but how to measure its severity so that it can be used in natural history studies and trials. Our work targets this gap by applying validated, domain-resolved scales across a wide age, building a foundation for systematic motor outcome assessment in NDDs. Our analyses demonstrate low frequency and lower score distributions of spasticity across the NDD cohort. Spasticity reflects velocity-dependent hypertonia arising from corticospinal tract injury, classically associated with perinatal brain injury. 35 In monogenic NDDs, focal corticospinal injury is not typically expected or rare, in line with our observation of an overall low spasticity burden. Accordingly, the pronounced floor effect observed for the MAS may reflect a genuinely low prevalence of spasticity in this population. However, given the substantial floor effects across spasticity-related subdomains (70-86%), reduced sensitivity of the scale to detect milder forms of hypertonia cannot be excluded. These findings should therefore be interpreted cautiously, and spasticity-related results may be influenced by both true low frequency and measurement limitations. Hypotonia is the most frequently reported motor phenotype across NDDs and developmental and epileptic encephalopathies. 9, 36 We did not systematically measure hypotonia due to the lack of standardized scales applicable across development, but it was clinically apparent in most assessments, and we hypothesize that our data provides indirect evidence for this observation. A large proportion of our cohort exhibited dystonia; multiple lines of evidence suggest that hypotonia may predispose to secondary dystonic posturing, particularly of distal extremities. 37 In line with this hypothesis, dystonic features were often discrete and limited to distal extremities, possibly also reflecting abnormal sensory processing, which can contribute to focal dystonia. 38, 39 Together, these factors provide a biological basis for the higher severity of dystonic features and the possible association with widespread hypotonia observed in this cohort. The use of the SARA across a broad developmental range introduces important limitations, as several components (e.g., gait and stance) were originally validated in ambulatory populations without developmental disorders. 24 For non-ambulatory participants, maximum scores were assigned for mobility-dependent items, which may contribute to ceiling effects and reduce sensitivity to gradations of dysfunction. For pre-ambulatory participants, these items were not scored and designated as not applicable. However, in the absence of validated motor scales applicable across both ambulatory and non-ambulatory individuals, this approach allowed for pragmatic stratification of motor severity across the cohort. Speech dysfunction was prominent across our cohort, including among individuals with milder limb or axial motor dysfunction. The SARA speech item assesses the intelligibility of spoken language as a measure of ataxic dysarthria. However, it has not been validated for individuals who have not acquired speech because of developmental impairment. 25 In these participants, a high score cannot be attributed specifically to ataxic dysarthria and may instead reflect language or cognitive impairment. 40 The pronounced ceiling effect in our cohort may partly reflect this limitation; the item also does not capture vocalizations, gestures, or other forms of communication particularly relevant to cognitively impaired individuals. [41] [42] [43] We therefore interpreted these scores as measures of observed function, not isolated evidence of cerebellar impairment. Approximately one third of individuals in our cohort also showed elevated scores for speech on the BFMS, suggestive phonetic or vocalization deficits as well. However, it also cannot adequately distinguish whether speech impairment is related to cognitive or motor vocalization. Overall, the value of speech assessment as a functional outcome measure cannot be understated. Granular, developmentally appropriate tools to disentangle linguistic and cognitive determinants of speech impairment in NDDs are much needed future optimizations to our protocol. 44 While limited by sample size, our data suggest gene-associated patterns of motor dysfunction. Individuals with SCN2A variants demonstrated higher mean z-scores across multiple subdomains. These findings align with the known phenotypic spectrum of SCN2A-related disorders, which range from severe developmental and epileptic encephalopathies (DEEs) to milder behavioral phenotypes. 29 KCNQ2 and CACNA1A variants exhibited domain-specific patterns with substantial uncertainty. CSNK2A1-related NDD exhibited comparatively milder global motor impairment, with language and cognitive features frequently predominating, findings directionally consistent with observed clinical patterns. 45 Taken together, our results highlight selective domainlevel differences rather than uniform genotype effects and should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating for larger gene-stratified studies. Key drawbacks that must be mentioned are that our cohort may not be representative of all NDDs given its limitation to five genes and the pre-dominance of channelopathy-related genes. Moreover, the lack of variantlevel stratification within the gene groups may also limit the generalizability of our findings. For instance, SCN2A variants exhibit substantial functional heterogeneity, with gain-of-function variants typically associated with severe DEE and loss-of-function variants more commonly linked to milder neurodevelopmental phenotypes. 46 Pooling these mechanistically distinct variant classes within a single gene-level group may obscure meaningful differences in motor dysfunction expression and contribute to variability in the observed severity patterns. This may partially explain the variability and confidence intervals overlapping zero observed within the SCN2A group. Accordingly, the gene-level estimates presented here should be interpreted as aggregate patterns rather than mechanism-specific effects. Future studies incorporating more homogeneous cohorts or stratification by variant-level biology, severity, and age of onset will be necessary to disentangle gene-specific effects from underlying clinical heterogeneity. Our study has several other limitations that should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, recruitment from gene-specific advocacy events and a tertiary academic center likely introduce ascertainment bias. Participation in these settings requires travel and caregiver availability, likely limiting inclusion of individuals with more severe disabilities. These individuals are also more likely to receive ancillary therapy support, limiting generalizability to individuals without access to these services. Moreover, increased mortality among individuals with severe NDDs may contribute to an overrepresentation of milder phenotypes in older age groups. Therefore, age-associated findings should be interpreted cautiously. Our recruitment sites likely also introduced socioeconomic biases, reflected in the predominantly White cohort which limits external validity to racial and ethnic minorities. This limitation is common in rare disease research and underscores the need for strategies to improve accessibility and inclusivity across socioeconomic backgrounds. 21 Further collaboration with patient-advocacy groups to address this gap, for instance by promoting virtual, in-person assessments may improve generalizability and access to larger, representative cohorts. Although missing data were infrequent, incomplete assessments occurred primarily because of behavioral intolerance, fatigue, or time constraints and therefore may not have been completely random. However, fewer than 5% of observations were excluded for most analyses (Table S4), reducing the likelihood that missingness substantially influenced the overall findings. Previous studies have demonstrated the presence of additional motor phenotypes such as chorea and myoclonus in patients with NDDs. 6, 27 Although we frequently observed these features, they were not systematically analyzed. Many of these phenotypes lack standardized severity scales that are broadly applicable across developmental ages. Our focus on ataxia, dystonia, and spasticity was therefore driven by the availability of validated rating instruments, highlighting the need to develop scalable, quantitative tools to enable inclusion of a wider range of motor phenotypes as outcome measures. Additionally, motor assessments were scored by a single expert rater, supervised by developmental neurology and movement disorder specialists. Inter-rater reliability testing between the primary rater and an expert committee demonstrated excellent agreement for the BFMS (ICC(2,1) = 0.90). However, scales such as the MAS require in-person examination and cannot be reliably scored post-hoc using videos alone, limiting formal assessment of reliability for all scales. Future studies would benefit from incorporating protocols optimized for remote and asynchronous scoring to facilitate broader participation, and independent validation. Moreover, we did not systematically collect detailed longitudinal clinical data on potential confounders such as seizure burden, antiseizure medication exposure, or prior neurosurgical interventions. As many participants were recruited at advocacy conferences, comprehensive medical histories were not uniformly available, and we cannot rule out additional underlying structural brain abnormalities which may influence motor phenotypes and increase unmeasured heterogeneity within the cohort. Future studies incorporating structured clinical data collection and medication histories will be necessary to disentangle intrinsic genotype-associated motor dysfunction from treatment or comorbidity-related effects. Finally, we emphasize the limited sample size of our study, which may affect statistical power and generalizability; findings should therefore be interpreted within the context of an exploratory study of rare monogenic NDDs. In summary, this study provides a much needed standardized, domain-resolved quantification of motor dysfunction across developmental stages in monogenic NDDs. Our findings demonstrate that motor assessment scales are straightforward to administer and can be reliably applied with appropriate training, making them well suited for routine clinical use and research. Their broader adoption as standard tools in clinical practice would substantially expand the availability of structured motor data across diverse settings. This framework establishes a foundation for longitudinal motor phenotyping and supports the development of quantitative motor outcome measures for natural history studies and emerging clinical trials. 47

Methods

Cohort Recruitment

This prospectively recruited, cross-sectional observational study was approved by the Committee for Protection of Human Subjects of UTHealth Houston (IRB# HSC-MS-24-0370). All individuals with variants in NDD-associated genes and controls were prospectively recruited over 13 months (08/2024 -09/2025) at the SCN2A and CACNA1A multidisciplinary clinics at UTHealth Houston and at six patient advocacy group conferences: FamilieSCN2A Foundation 2024 and 2025 (https://www.scn2a.org/), The Cute Syndrome Foundation 2024 (https://www.thecutesyndrome.com/), CSNK2A1 Foundation 2025 (https://www.csnk2a1foundation.org/), SCN2A/SCN8A Alliance 2025 (https://scn8a.eu/) and the KCNQ2 Cure Alliance 2025 (https://www.kcnq2cure.org/). The inclusion criterion for individuals with NDDs was the presence of a pathogenic or likely pathogenic variant in the five NDD-associated genes we studied as per American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) guidelines 48 . Seizure history was not required for inclusion and was not used to define the analytic cohort. Individuals with acute illnesses that could interfere with safe participation in the neurological examination were excluded from the study. Unaffected siblings of enrolled individuals with NDDs were included as controls without prior phenotype matching to facilitate pragmatic recruitment and reduce selection bias. Gene selection was driven by feasibility of recruitment, including patients evaluated in multidisciplinary clinics and at genespecific advocacy events during the study period. As such, the cohort represents a pragmatic sample of individuals with monogenic NDDs rather than a biologically uniform group selected for mechanistic similarity. The only inclusion criterion for controls was no known history of any NDD-associated variants. However, no explicit genetic testing was performed to rule out any incidental genetic findings. A formal a priori sample size calculation was not performed due to the rarity and heterogeneity of the studied NDDs. Recruitment was based on feasibility, and all eligible individuals during the recruitment period were invited to participate; therefore, we emphasize effect sizes and confidence intervals rather than post-hoc power estimates. All participants and/or legal guardians underwent a written informed consent process prior to study procedures. Age groups were defined as infancy (0-2 years), early (3-5 years) and late childhood (6-10 years), adolescence (11-18 years) and adulthood (>18 years) (Table 1).

Data Collection and Curation

All consents, identifiable participant information and clinical data were stored in an instance of UTHealth RedCap to ensure strict adherence to data protection regulations. Each participant was assigned a unique identifier (UID); demographic and clinical data were de-identified prior to extraction from RedCap for analysis. Motor exam scores were collected in a blinded dataset containing the UID as the only identifier. The primary inperson rater was not blinded to recruitment context in all cases, whereas post-hoc expert committee BFMS rerating was performed blinded to participant identity, genotype, and clinical information.

Variant Pathogenicity Classification

Variant pathogenicity (re)classification was performed by variant analysis experts and genetic counselors in the Lal Group following ACMG criteria and using established in silico tools. We followed the recent variant curation expert panel (VCEP) guidelines developed for epilepsy-associated voltage-gated sodium channels (https://clinicalgenome.org/docs/clingen-variant-curation-expert-panel-vcep-protocol/).

Motor Examination Setup

Assessments took place at UTHealth's UT Physicians Clinic at the Texas Medical Center or in dedicated examination rooms at conference venues. The rater conducted examinations and team members assisted with documentation. We utilized two uniquely positioned Sony HDRCX405 Handycam Camcorders to record all standardized assessments for retrospective curation and independent rating for reliability testing. Caregivers were present throughout to ensure participants' safety and comfort.

Movement Rating Scales

Motor phenotypes were quantified using the Scale for the Assessment and Rating of Ataxia (SARA), the Burke-Fahn-Marsden Dystonia Rating Scale (BFMS), and the Modified Ashworth Scale (MAS) for spasticity. Domains and subdomains were established by stratifying individual parameters into clinically meaningful groups based on body region or motor function (Table 2). For non-ambulatory participants, SARA items requiring independent mobility (e.g., gait and stance) were assigned the maximum score, reflecting inability to perform the task. For pre-ambulatory participants, these items were not scored and were designated as not applicable. This approach was selected to preserve the ordinal structure of the scale and enable consistent severity categorization across the cohort in the absence of validated measures spanning the full developmental and functional spectrum. For the BFMS, final scores were calculated by multiplying raw scores with the provocation factor -defined as the presence of a phenotype at rest or only in certain ranges of motion -as described previously. 23

Handling of Missing Data

No imputation of missing data was performed. Analyses were conducted using an available-case approach, with observations included when the variables required for a given analysis were available to maximize sample size in this observational study. Consequently, denominators varied across analyses depending on the completeness of the relevant motor assessment. Composite motor subdomain scores were derived by summing the available component scores within each subdomain, with available-item maximum used for proportion calculations. Participants for whom all component parameters within a subdomain were unavailable were assigned a missing value for that composite subdomain. When at least one component parameter was available, the composite score was calculated using the available observations without imputation.

Binary motor phenotypes were classified as present when at least one contributing parameter had a score ≥1 and absent when one or more contributing parameters were observed and all observed parameters had scores of 0. Binary phenotypes were considered missing only when all contributing parameters were unavailable. Accordingly, frequency estimates were calculated using the number of participants with available data for each phenotype as the denominator. For exploratory gene-level analyses, patient-referenced z-scores were calculated only for observations with available motor scores and parameters demonstrating non-zero variability across the affected cohort. Parameters without measurable variability in the affected cohort were excluded from z-score standardization (Table S4). Inter-rater reliability analyses used only jointly scored items from both raters, and the primary ICC analysis was restricted to assessments with complete paired ratings across all nine BFMS items. No imputation was performed for missing paired ratings. The number and proportion of observations excluded because of missing data at each stage of the analysis are summarized in Table S4.

Motor Examination Scoring and Inter-Rater Reliability Testing

All motor assessments were scored by a single primary rater (S.B.) with formal training in the use of the SARA, BFMS, and MAS. Training consisted of supervised scoring of representative videos, scoring guideline review prior to study initiation under direct supervision by two pediatric neurologists (N.T. and G.V.A.) and a physical therapist (S.E.M.) specialized in pediatric movement disorders. This supervision occurred as part of routine multidisciplinary clinical care. Participants with incomplete motor examinations due to fatigue, behavioral intolerance, or time constraints were retained in the analysis. No participants were excluded post-enrollment; individuals unable to participate in motor assessments were not enrolled in the study. Post-hoc blind, independent re-rating of 59 BFMS assessments was conducted by a movement disorder expert committee (N.T., S.E.M. and R.F). Inter-rater reliability was evaluated using a two-way random-effects, absoluteagreement, single-measure intraclass correlation coefficient [ICC(2,1)]. Total BFMS scores were calculated using items scored by both raters. The primary analysis was restricted to assessments with complete paired ratings across all nine BFMS items; two assessments with one missing paired item were excluded. No imputation was performed. An available-case sensitivity analysis including all 59 assessments produced nearly identical results.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed in R (v4.4.1) (RStudio v2024.0.2). For frequency analysis (Figure 1A), participants were classified according to the presence or absence of five broad motor phenotypes (gross motor deficit, coordination deficit, speech deficit, dystonia, and spasticity). Sums and percentages were calculated separately for controls and individuals with NDDs. Differences between groups were evaluated Fisher's exact tests. P values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) procedure. Motor subdomain severity in individuals with NDDs was summarized by calculating composite subdomain scores and expressing each as a proportion of the maximum possible score (Figure 1B). Continuous variables were summarized using medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs). Floor and ceiling effects for each subdomain (Figure S1) were defined as scores <5% or >95% of the maximum possible score, respectively. To characterize gene-level motor phenotype patterns (Figure 2), controls were excluded and individual motor parameter scores were standardized relative to the pooled affected cohort. Parameter-level z-scores were averaged within assessments across parameters belonging to the same motor subdomain, followed by aggregation within genotype groups. Mean z-scores therefore represent the average deviation from the affected-cohort mean. Uncertainty was estimated using nonparametric bootstrap resampling (2,000 iterations) with percentile-based 95% confidence intervals (Table S3). Gene-level analyses were descriptive and exploratory; no formal statistical comparisons were performed because of limited and unequal genotypespecific sample sizes.
