Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Objectives To assess the completeness, consistency, plausibility, uniqueness and analytical usability of routine maternity and newborn records used for preterm and small newborn care, and how facility health-information-system readiness and working conditions explain these patterns.

Design Multicentre cross-sectional mixed-methods study of routine health data with embedded explanatory qualitative interviews. Setting Twelve health facilities (levels 3-5) in Bungoma and one neighbouring county, Western Kenya. Participants 349 maternity-register and 291 newborn records for deliveries from July 2024 to May 2026; 10 facility readiness assessments; two provider key informant interviews. Primary and secondary outcome measures Variable-and record-level completeness from original uncleaned abstractions; prespecified consistency and plausibility rule violations; duplicates; agreement between recorded and rule-derived preterm, low-birth-weight (LBW) and very-low-birth-weight classifications.

Results Median record completeness was 100% (IQR 94.7-100) for register and 93.5% (IQR 84.8-100) for newborn records; a complete core record was present in 175/349 (50.1%, 95% CI 44.9 to 55.4) and 77/291 (26.5%, 95% CI 21.7 to 31.8) respectively. No maternal age, gestational age, birth weight or Apgar value was implausible, but chronological errors were common: recorded and date-derived length of stay differed by >1 day in 92/225 (40.9%) and admission preceded birth in 35/257 (13.6%). Agreement with rule-derived classification was high for very low birth weight (kappa 0.89, 95% CI 0.83 to 0.95) but moderate for LBW (kappa 0.53, 95% CI 0.32 to 0.73; McNemar p=0.001). Complete documentation was more likely with three or more record sources (adjusted prevalence ratio 1.80, 95% CI 1.05 to 3.08) and less likely for newborns who died (0.46, 95% CI 0.25 to 0.84). Providers described documentation competing with workload.

Conclusions Identification, gestational age, birth weight and disposition were documented sufficiently for classification and mortality analysis; timing fields, maternal history and transport variables were not. Preterm-birth prevalence was not estimable without an alldelivery denominator.

Strengths and limitations of this study

• All completeness, consistency and plausibility estimates were computed from the original, unedited abstractions, without imputation or correction, so that the records were assessed as they exist in routine practice.

• Three routine data sources from twelve facilities were assessed against prespecified rules, and every dataset was verified cell by cell against the original electronic export before analysis.

• Recalculated classifications served only as a rules-based consistency reference; no independent clinical gold standard (such as direct observation or re-measurement) was available, so accuracy could not be assessed.

• Only ten facilities contributed readiness data, so facility-level correlations are exploratory and imprecise, and no complex facility-level model was fitted.

• The explanatory qualitative component comprised two provider interviews at the county referral hospital and therefore illustrates rather than represents documentation practice across all facility levels.

Introduction

Routine health information is the operational backbone of newborn care. Facility registers, admission records, monitoring charts and discharge summaries determine what clinicians know at the bedside, what audit committees can review, and what counties can count. The World Health Organization Standards for improving the quality of care for small and sick newborns make this explicit: actionable health information systems are one of eight standards, on the reasoning that care cannot be improved if it cannot be described. [1] The Every Newborn Action Plan measurement roadmap reaches the same conclusion from the measurement side, identifying care of small and sick newborns as the area where routine data are weakest. [2] An estimated 13.4 million babies were born preterm in 2020, with sub-Saharan Africa and southern Asia carrying most of the burden and most of the associated mortality, [3, 4] and Kenya's neonatal mortality rate stands at 21 deaths per 1,000 live births. [5] Preterm and small newborns place unusually heavy demands on documentation. Their classification depends on two measurements -gestational age and birth weight -that are themselves difficult to obtain reliably where last menstrual period rather than early ultrasound remains the dominant dating method. [6, 7, 8] Their management is time-critical, so the value of a record depends on timestamps as much as on values. Their outcomes are frequently determined within hours, so documentation opportunities are unevenly distributed between newborns who die early and those who survive to discharge. Each feature creates a distinct way for a record to fail.

Where routine records are adequate they support surveillance, clinical audit, perinatal death review, quality improvement and planning without the cost of bespoke data collection. [9] Where they are not, the consequences propagate silently: indicators are computed from unstable denominators, mortality audits proceed without the fields needed to identify modifiable factors, and research inherits errors invisible at analysis. A systematic review of routine facility data for newborn indicators in low-and middle-income countries found completeness ranging from 0% to 100% across registers and case notes, with only four studies reporting internal consistency, and concluded that the evidence base is thin relative to the investment made in these systems; [10] a recent four-country assessment reached similar conclusions. [11] Kenya has invested substantially in routine health information. A nationwide evaluation of the Kenya Health Information System (KHIS), built on DHIS2, reported a mean facility-5 . quarter completeness score of 61.1% alongside a consistency score of 80.0%, [12] and qualitative work across 15 counties described limited analytical capacity as a persistent constraint on data use, [13] within a wider Eastern and Southern African pattern of uneven performance. [14] Newborn care has benefited from the Clinical Information Network (CIN), a KEMRI-coordinated learning health system in which structured newborn admission records feed a common platform supporting audit and feedback. [15, 16, 17] Yet capturing neonatal data for global monitoring remains difficult even where systems exist, [18] hospital information systems often cannot support evidence-informed decisions, [19] and verification of childbirth registers in Western Kenya has documented substantial gaps. [20] What remains largely unexamined is the quality of the specific records a county would use to assess its own preterm and small newborn care. Most assessments concentrate on registers or aggregate DHIS2 reports; few examine individual newborn case notes, and fewer still assess linked maternity and newborn records together with the facility information-system capacity that produces them. [10] This matters because the fields that fail are not the fields most often audited: completeness of birth weight says little about whether admission time was recorded, whether the two places a birth weight is written agree, or whether length of stay can be computed.

We therefore assessed the completeness, internal consistency, plausibility, uniqueness and analytical usability of routine maternity and newborn records across twelve facilities in Western Kenya, and examined how facility information-system readiness and frontline working conditions help explain these patterns. Objectives were to quantify variable-level and record-level completeness; assess consistency among logically related variables; assess agreement between recorded and rule-derived classifications; quantify implausible values, chronological errors and duplicates; determine which indicators the data support; examine facility variation and its relationship with readiness; identify record-level predictors of complete documentation; and integrate provider accounts with the quantitative findings.

Methods

Study design and reporting

This was a multicentre cross-sectional assessment of routinely collected health data with an embedded explanatory qualitative component, nested within a mixed-methods study of health-system readiness and quality of care for preterm births in Western Kenya. Reporting follows the RECORD extension to STROBE, [21, 22] with selected COREQ principles applied to the embedded provider component (completed RECORD checklist, online supplemental file 1). [23]

Setting

Bungoma County operates a tiered public health system comprising health centres (level 3), sub-county hospitals (level 4) and a county referral hospital (level 5). Twelve public facilities contributed data: eleven in Bungoma County and the county referral hospital of neighbouring Busia County. Ten underwent a facility readiness assessment, nine (all in Bungoma) contributed patient-level records, and seven contributed both. All provided maternity services; of the ten assessed facilities, seven had a newborn unit and two a neonatal intensive care unit. The Bungoma county referral hospital participates in the Clinical Information Network, uses REDCap-based abstraction from structured paper forms and receives partnersupported dashboard reporting.

Data sources

Three structured instruments, approved before data collection, were administered by trained research assistants using Kobo Toolbox on tablets. Tool 1 abstracted maternity-register entries for recorded deliveries (57 variables). Tool 2 assessed facility readiness across 11 domains on a three-point scale (0 = not available;1 = available but non-functional or inconsistently used;2 = fully available and functional), with free-text fields on strengths, gaps and recommendations (122 variables). Tool 3 abstracted individual newborn clinical records, including a 31-item patient-level quality score (PLQS) covering eight care domains scored on the same scale with an explicit 'not applicable' option (186 variables). Data were collected between 4 May and 26 June 2026. Two provider key informant interviews were conducted at the county referral hospital, with the consultant paediatrician and head of the newborn unit and with the newborn-unit nurse manager; both were audio-recorded with consent and transcribed verbatim.

Data management

Because this study assesses the quality of the original documentation, all completeness, consistency and plausibility estimates were computed from the abstractions as entered, without imputation, correction or recoding of missing values.

Cleaned datasets were used only for facility-name standardisation, duplicate identification and unique record counts. Before analysis, each dataset was verified cell by cell against the original Kobo Toolbox export, and all analyses were re-run to confirm that outputs were reproducible.

Facility standardisation and duplicate resolution

Facility names were free-text and appeared in numerous forms; a master crosswalk mapping every observed alias to a standard name, KHIS code, sub-county and level was applied to both patient-level datasets. Potential duplicates were identified using facility, record identifier, date and newborn characteristics, and classified as confirmed duplicate, true multiple birth or distinct record. Within confirmed duplicate groups the record with the highest weighted completeness was retained and singular non-conflicting values recovered from complementary records; contradictory values were never silently merged and remain visible in the duplicate-resolution log. Documented multiple births were retained as separate newborns.

Data-quality definitions

Consistency, plausibility and agreement analysis

Sixteen register rules and 25 newborn rules were prespecified and are listed in online supplemental table 1; construction followed the WHO data quality review framework and the PRISM approach. [24, 25] Working plausibility ranges were maternal age 10-55 years, gestational age 20-44 weeks, birth weight 300-6,000 g, Apgar 0-10, gravidity 1-20, parity 0-19 and length of stay 0-180 days. Gestational age and birth weight were treated as incompatible if gestational age was below 28 weeks with weight of at least 2,000 g, or at least 34 weeks with weight below 800 g. Recorded preterm, low-birth-weight (LBW) and very-low-birth-weight (VLBW) classifications were compared with classifications recalculated from the underlying gestational age and birth weight. The recalculated classification is a rules-based consistency reference, not an independent clinical gold standard, and results are reported as agreement rather than diagnostic validity. For each comparison we report the cross-tabulation, percentage agreement, positive and negative agreement, Cohen's kappa with a 95% confidence interval and an exact McNemar test. Where the same quantity was captured in two fields (gestational age; birth weight) we report exact agreement, median absolute difference, the proportion within tolerance (1 week; 50 g) and Bland-Altman limits of agreement. [26] Correlation was not used as evidence of agreement.

Analytical usability

A fitness-for-analysis matrix listed analyses commonly required for monitoring and research on preterm and small newborns, each classified as fully supported, supported with limitations, or not currently supported, with a stated reason. No numerical usability index was created.

Facility, HIS and predictor analyses

Facility readiness scores were recalculated from observed items. Health-information-system (HIS) readiness comprised four items (newborn registers, gestational-age and birth-weight documentation, perinatal death audits, KHIS/DHIS2 reporting); quality-improvement (QI) and governance readiness comprised four items (active QI team, review meetings, use of audit data, supportive supervision). Facility-level completeness, inconsistency rate and duplicate rate were linked to these scores using the audited facility identifier. Because only ten facilities were assessed and seven contributed both readiness and patient-level data, relationships were examined using facility profiles, scatterplots and Spearman correlation with 95% confidence intervals, and are reported as exploratory and ecological. No facilitylevel multivariable model was fitted.

Record-level predictors of a complete core record were examined separately for each instrument using modified Poisson regression with robust variance to estimate prevalence ratios. [27] Covariates were selected conceptually rather than by stepwise procedures: the register model included facility level, period of delivery and birth outcome; the newborn model included facility level, ward or unit, number of record sources consulted, mode of entry, disposition and period of birth. A separate model restricted to facilities with a readiness assessment estimated associations with HIS and QI readiness per 25 percentage points. All completeness outcomes were derived from original documentation only.

Missing data

Missingness is the outcome here rather than a nuisance, and was never imputed. Blank entries, explicit 'not recorded', 'unknown', 'not assessed' and structurally inapplicable responses were distinguished throughout. Complete-case analysis was used for all regression models, and sensitivity analyses (online supplemental table 5) include one in which any entry, including an explicit 'not recorded', was counted as documented.

Qualitative analysis

Provider transcripts and Tool 2 free-text responses were analysed using the framework method. [28] A deductive codebook developed from the study instruments covered routine documentation, paper registers, electronic systems, dashboards, workload and staffing, documentation burden, discharge records, weekly and monthly audits, perinatal death review, feedback and mentorship, and use of data for quality improvement; inductive codes were 10 . added as they emerged. Two analysts coded independently and reconciled by consensus. The component is explanatory: with two interviews, both from the county referral hospital, no claim of thematic saturation or representativeness across facility levels is made. Providers are identified as Provider KII 1 (consultant paediatrician and head of the newborn unit) and Provider KII 2 (newborn-unit nurse manager). All quotations are verbatim.

Mixed-method integration

Datasets were analysed separately and then integrated using a joint display pairing each quantitative finding with its facility information-system context, provider explanation, clinical implication and corrective action, classified as convergence, complementarity, expansion or discordance. Qualitative evidence was not forced to explain findings for which no supporting account existed.

Statistical methods

All tests were two-sided with an alpha of 0.05 and estimates are reported with 95% confidence intervals, emphasising effect estimates over p-values. Binomial proportions use Wilson intervals; [29] kappa standard errors follow the Fleiss method; [30] McNemar tests are exact; Spearman confidence intervals use the Fisher z transformation. Analyses were conducted in Python 3 (pandas, NumPy, SciPy).

Patient and public involvement

There was no patient or public involvement in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination of this study.

Ethics and research authorisations

The study was approved by the Masinde Muliro University of Science and Technology Institutional Scientific and Ethics Review Committee (MMUST-ISERC). Retrospective abstraction of routine records was approved without individual patient consent but facility in charge assent to conduct the abstraction. No direct patient identifiers were carried into the analytical datasets; facility and newborn identifiers were used only for duplicate resolution and are not reported. Providers gave informed consent to participate and to be audiorecorded. All reporting is aggregated and quotations are anonymised. Full approval details appear under Declarations.

Results

Study records and facilities

The three instruments yielded 349 maternity-register records, 291 newborn clinical records and 10 facility readiness assessments. Patient-level records came from nine Bungoma facilities (one level 5 county referral hospital, six level 4 hospitals and two level 3 health centres); readiness assessments covered ten facilities, including the Busia county referral hospital, and seven facilities contributed both. Recorded delivery dates spanned 15 July 2024 to 21 May 2026. Characteristics of the data sources, facilities and records are shown in table 1.

Data verification and uniqueness

Cell-by-cell verification against the original electronic exports found no discrepancy in any dataset (19,950 register, 1,342 facility-assessment and 54,312 newborn cells), and no record or variable was added or lost.

Facility identification was the single largest threat to record linkage. Thirty distinct facilityname strings in the register dataset and 32 in the newborn dataset resolved to nine facilities, and 15 sub-county spellings to seven sub-counties; one facility appeared under ten written forms. Ten duplicate groups were identified in the register dataset, of which nine were confirmed duplicates (10 records excluded) and one a documented twin pair correctly retained as two newborns; four confirmed duplicate groups were identified in the newborn dataset (4 records excluded). Three records in each dataset were reconciled by recovering singular non-conflicting values, leaving 339 unique register and 287 unique newborn records (figure 1; online supplemental table 4). Duplicates were concentrated in three facilities and reached 10.0% of register records at one health centre. All exclusions remain reversible through the record-lineage file.

Completeness of maternity-register data

Register documentation was strong. Facility, register number, date of delivery and birth outcome were documented in all 349 records, and 14 of 19 core variables exceeded 97% completeness (table 2, panel A; online supplemental figure 1; all variables in online supplemental table 2). Median record-level completeness was 100% (IQR 94.7 to 100; mean 96.7%, SD 4.7; range 38.9 to 100), with identification and outcome domains complete for every record. A fully complete core record was nevertheless present in only 175 of 349 (50.1%, 95% CI 44.9 to 55.4), because the shortfall concentrated in three fields most records did not escape: maternal comorbidity status (259/349; 74.2%, 95% CI 69.4 to 78.5, with 52 blanks and 38 explicit 'not recorded'), antenatal booking trimester (298/349; 85.4%) and residence type (327/349; 93.7%). Gestational age was recorded for 341 records (97.7%) and its source for 343 (98.3%), but the source was last menstrual period in 78.5% and ultrasound in only 19.2%.

Completeness of newborn clinical records

Newborn records were less complete and far more variable (table 2 and five-minute Apgar 244/291 (83.8%). Among the 174 newborns discharged alive, discharge weight was documented in 169 (97.1%) and feeding at discharge in 166 (95.4%), so growth and feeding documentation was good where structurally applicable. The 31 PLQS items contained no blank cells; 87.2% of item-cells carried a numerical score and 12.8% were marked not applicable, the kangaroo mother care domain accounting for the largest share (26 to 49 per item).

Two facilities drove much of the variation (figure 2). At one sub-county hospital the entire maternal domain was blank in all 53 records -mother's age, gravidity, parity and antenatal attendance documented in none -while gestational age, birth weight and disposition were near-complete, a pattern consistent with abstraction from a newborn-unit source that does not carry maternal history. At a second sub-county hospital contributing 16 records, completeness was low across nearly every domain (mean 34.9%, median 14.0%), including no five-minute Apgar and admission date and time in 6 of 16 records.

Internal consistency and plausibility

Biological plausibility was not the problem: no maternal age, gestational age, birth weight, Apgar score, parity value or daily weight change in either dataset fell outside its prespecified range, and only three gravidity values (0.9%) and two gestational-age and birth-weight combinations (0.6%) were flagged (table 3).

Chronology was. Recorded length of stay differed from the date-derived interval by more than one day in 92 of 225 newborn records (40.9%, 95% CI 34.7 to 47.4); admission preceded birth in 35 of 257 records with both timestamps (13.6%, 95% CI 10.0 to 18.4), of which 25 by more than one hour and 14 by more than 30 days, indicating year or month transcription errors rather than clock imprecision; and the outcome date-time preceded admission in 18 of 236 (7.6%). Four birth dates and five outcome dates in newborn records, and one delivery date in the register, were later than the date on which the record was abstracted, one birth date by more than nine months.

Contradictions clustered around fields captured more than once or alongside a 'not recorded' switch. The two birth-weight fields disagreed in 26 of 259 paired records (10.0%) and the two gestational-age fields in 21 of 238 (8.8%). Recorded LBW status contradicted the entered birth weight in 16 of 249 records (6.4%) and VLBW status in 12 of 228 (5.3%). Feeding at discharge was documented for 19 newborns not discharged alive (10.3% of completed fields).

In the register dataset, the 'birth weight not recorded' switch contradicted the entered weight in 20 of 262 records (7.6%), the recorded prematurity category disagreed with the gestational-age band in 21 of 325 (6.5%), and the abstractor's assessment that key variables were complete disagreed with computed completeness in 11 of 349 (3.2%). Composite 'none recorded' indicators performed well, with no maternal-infection contradiction and only four comorbidity contradictions (1.3%).

Agreement between recorded and rule-derived classifications

Agreement varied by classification and was consistently limited by the rarity of the negative category (table 4). In the register dataset, recorded preterm status agreed with the gestationalage-derived classification in 329 of 340 records (96.8%), but because 339 of 349 records were classified preterm, kappa was only 0.40 (95% CI 0.12 to 0.69) with negative agreement of 42.1%. The multi-category prematurity classification performed better, with 93.5% agreement and kappa 0.87 (95% CI 0.82 to 0.93); disagreements concentrated at the 28-week boundary, where positive agreement for extremely preterm was 69.6% against 98.1% for moderate-to-late preterm.

In the newborn dataset, preterm status agreed in 266 of 272 records (99.3%; kappa 0.80, 95% CI 0.52 to 1.00) and VLBW status in 216 of 228 (94.7%; kappa 0.89, 95% CI 0.83 to 0.95).

LBW classification was weakest: 233 of 249 agreed (93.6%) but kappa was 0.53 (95% CI 0.32 to 0.73), and disagreement was directional -15 newborns with a birth weight below 2,500 g were not recorded as LBW while only one was recorded as LBW without a qualifying weight (exact McNemar p=0.001) -indicating systematic under-recording rather than random error.

Where the same measurement appeared in two fields, most pairs agreed exactly (gestational age 91.2%, birth weight 90.0%) and most of the remainder fell within tolerance (94.1% within 1 week; 94.2% within 50 g). The disagreements that remained were large rather than marginal: Bland-Altman limits of agreement for birth weight were -291 g to +313 g with a maximum absolute difference of 1,725 g, and the maximum gestational-age difference was 7 weeks (online supplemental figure 2). A small minority of records therefore contain a discrepancy wide enough to move a newborn between weight categories.

Analytical usability

Table 5 summarises what the datasets can and cannot support. Preterm identification, prematurity severity, birth-weight classification, stillbirth among recorded deliveries, inhospital mortality among newborns with a known final outcome, and referral status were fully supported or supported with limitations. Length of stay was computable from valid dates in 218 of 291 records (74.9%) and weight change from paired admission and discharge weights in 219 (75.3%); a known final outcome was available for 239 of 291 (82.1%), with 44 referred out or still admitted and 8 with no disposition recorded. Two analyses were not supported. Referral timing was computable in 222 of 291 records (76.3%) once invalid intervals were excluded, but structured transport variables -departure and arrival time, mode, oxygen and escort -were absent, so referral delay can be described only as a birth-to-admission interval, not as a transport process. Preterm-birth prevalence could not be estimated at all: the register export contains no facility-period total-delivery denominator and 339 of 349 abstracted records were already classified preterm, so dividing recorded preterm births by abstracted records would return a figure describing the abstraction rather than the population. Prevalence estimation requires facility-month totals for all deliveries, live births and stillbirths, which were not collected.

Facility variation and information-system readiness

Mean register completeness ranged from 93.1% to 99.3% across the nine facilities, but the proportion of complete core records ranged from 0% to 86.7% and the record-level inconsistency rate from 2.1% to 70.6%; newborn-record completeness varied far more widely, from 34.9% to 100% (online supplemental figure 3; online supplemental table 3).

Recalculated HIS readiness ranged from 50.0% to 100.0% and QI and governance readiness from 12.5% to 100.0%, with six of ten facilities scoring 12.5% on the QI domain, reflecting an absent QI team and no review meetings. Among the seven facilities contributing both readiness and patient-level data, correlations were positive but imprecise and none reached conventional significance: overall readiness with the proportion of complete core newborn records gave rho 0.66 (95% CI -0.25 to 0.95, p=0.111); HIS readiness with mean register completeness rho 0.27 (95% CI -0.64 to 0.87, p=0.554); and HIS readiness with mean newborn-record completeness rho 0.04 (95% CI -0.76 to 0.79, p=0.938) (online supplemental figure 4). With seven facilities these relationships are ecological, exploratory and compatible with a wide range of underlying associations.

Factors associated with complete documentation

Predictors differed sharply between the two record types (table 6). For register records, only facility level was associated with complete documentation: level 5 versus level 4 gave an adjusted prevalence ratio of 2.24 (95% CI 1.82 to 2.75), with no association for period of delivery or birth outcome. In the model restricted to facilities with a readiness assessment (n=279 records, 140 complete), each 25-percentage-point increment in HIS readiness was associated with a prevalence ratio of 1.44 (95% CI 1.04 to 1.99), while QI readiness showed no association (0.93, 95% CI 0.82 to 1.05).

For newborn records, the strongest association was temporal: records for newborns born from January 2026 onwards were markedly more likely to be complete (adjusted prevalence ratio 4.43, 95% CI 2.29 to 8.57). Consulting three or more record sources was associated with more complete abstraction (1.80, 95% CI 1.05 to 3.08), and records for newborns who died were less complete (0.46, 95% CI 0.25 to 0.84), consistent with reduced opportunity to document care and outcome for newborns who die early. Level 5 records were less complete than level 4 records after adjustment (0.49, 95% CI 0.26 to 0.92), the opposite of the register finding -the referral hospital documents registers well but carries the highest patient volume and the greatest reliance on parallel clinical sources. Ward or unit and mode of entry were not associated with completeness.

Sensitivity analyses (online supplemental table 5) did not alter these conclusions. Restricting to unique records after duplicate resolution changed mean register completeness by less than 0.1 percentage points and newborn completeness by 0.2. Counting any entry, including explicit 'not recorded', as documented raised mean completeness to 99.1% and 90.3% respectively, showing that much of the newborn shortfall is genuine blank space. Restricting to facilities contributing at least ten records, and excluding records with invalid date sequences, produced estimates within 1.4 percentage points of the primary analysis.

Provider perspectives

Both providers described parallel paper and electronic pathways. Structured paper forms are transcribed after discharge -'As we take care of the baby, after discharging the baby, they take the patient file and enter that information' (Provider KII 1) -and the unit receives monthly Clinical Information Network reports and partner dashboards, using both: 'This data, we use it to make decisions.' Provider KII 2 described the paper layer: 'we have the daily bed return. We have the admission registers. Then we have the kangaroo mother care register.' Both located the failure point at the moment of recording rather than in the tools. Provider KII 2 stated it directly: 'in terms of documentation, yes, we have the registers and the papers required for documentation. But now, we normally miss each other. We normally focus on, is this baby sick? Yes. Is the baby out of danger? Yes.' The same provider linked monitoring documentation explicitly to staffing: 'we also monitor vitals hourly. That is the bad thing.

Hourly with the shortage, sometimes we miss, sometimes we do it.' Audit and feedback were described as established and as having changed in character: 'At first, we thought that audits were for shame, but as we've been carrying them out, they've been educational for us' (Provider KII 2). Unit reviews are weekly, data are audited monthly, and errors identified centrally are returned for clarification: 'when that data is having errors, I'll be called to clarify what happened on such and such a baby, or such and such a file.' Perinatal death review was described as durable -'every third Friday of the month is MPDSR. So it's permanent' (Provider KII 1) -but implementation of resulting recommendations was the identified weak link: 'Our only weakness has been in implementation of actual plans because some gaps keep recurring and recurring.' Workload displaced scheduled review: 'we had like 18 admissions and we are supposed to have a meeting at 10, we postponed it, we didn't do it.' Free-text facility responses converged on the same constraint, staff shortage being named as a major gap by nine of ten facilities.

Integrated mixed-method findings

The joint display is presented in online supplemental table 6. Integration was convergent for the staffing-completeness relationship, where the quantitative gradient in maternal-history and monitoring fields matched the providers' account of documentation deferred under clinical pressure, and for the mortality gradient in documentation completeness. It was complementary for the electronic-system findings: the presence of CIN and dashboard reporting explains why the referral hospital's register documentation and identification fields perform well without explaining why its individual newborn records do not. It was expansive for duplicated fields and free-text facility naming, which providers did not raise but which the quantitative analysis identified as material threats to linkage. Discussion

Principal findings

Across twelve facilities in Western Kenya, routine maternity and newborn records were substantially complete for the fields that identify a newborn and describe their immediate clinical state, and substantially incomplete for the fields describing when things happened, what preceded the birth, and how the newborn arrived. Median record completeness was 100% for register records and 93.5% for newborn records, but only 26.5% of newborn records had every applicable core field documented. Biological implausibility was essentially absent; chronological error was not, with over 40% of newborn records showing a length-ofstay discrepancy exceeding one day and 13.6% recording admission before birth. Recorded classifications agreed well with recalculated classifications for VLBW but only moderately for LBW, with directional under-recording. Preterm-birth prevalence could not be estimated because no all-delivery denominator exists.

Interpreting the completeness findings

The register findings sit at the better end of published experience. The systematic review of routine facility data for newborn indicators in low-and middle-income countries found register completeness above 60% for gestational age, sex, birth weight and place of delivery but with wide ranges, [10] and a data-quality strengthening campaign across maternity registers in Kenya and Uganda reported centre-averaged completeness of 87-97% for birth weight and 52-92% for gestational age. [31] Our register gestational-age completeness of 97.7% and birth-weight completeness of 99.4% sit at or above the top of those ranges. The Kenyan national KHIS evaluation reported a mean facility-quarter completeness score of 61.1% for aggregate reporting, [12] and the gap is instructive: paper registers are the first point of capture and are typically more complete than the aggregate summaries derived from them, so completeness measured at the register overstates what reaches the national system.

The newborn-record findings are more concerning precisely because these are the records clinical audit and quality improvement depend on. The pattern is not random attrition: it follows a gradient from the newborn's present state (near-complete) through the newborn's outcome (85-97%) to the mother's history (56-78%) to the transport process (0.7%). This is what one would expect if documentation is completed at the bedside under time pressure, prioritising information needed for the next clinical decision -the mechanism providers in the EN-BIRTH register study described when asked why fields go unrecorded. [32] Provider KII 2's formulation, that the focus is whether the baby is sick and whether the baby is out of danger, describes exactly this gradient. The facility-level outliers reinforce the point: at one hospital an entire maternal domain was blank in all 53 records while newborn clinical fields were near-complete, which is a source-document problem rather than a diligence problem.

Consistency, classification agreement and their consequences

The directional under-recording of LBW status is the most clinically consequential consistency finding. Fifteen newborns weighing under 2,500 g were not flagged as LBW while only one was flagged without a qualifying weight. If the recorded flag rather than the measured weight drives triage, referral or reporting, a subset of low-birth-weight newborns becomes invisible. Comparable directional discordance has been reported elsewhere: the EN-BIRTH validation study found that register-recorded coverage systematically diverged from observed coverage for several newborn interventions, and that accuracy varied between hospitals even where register design was identical. [33, 34] Our finding differs in an important way -we compared a recorded flag against a measurement written in the same record, so the discordance cannot be attributed to differences between data sources. Multi-country record review has likewise found that the presence of a field in an instrument is a poor guide to whether it is completed usefully. [35, 36] The chronological findings deserve emphasis because they are rarely reported. Most published assessments of newborn records address completeness and, less often, internal consistency of values; date-sequence validity is seldom examined. [10, 37] Yet the fields affected are precisely those needed to describe time-critical care. An admission timestamp preceding birth by more than 30 days is a transcription error that no plausibility range on a clinical value would detect, and a length-of-stay discrepancy above one day in 40.9% of records means that recorded and date-derived length of stay are not interchangeable. For a county wishing to monitor birth-to-admission intervals as a referral-quality indicator, this is the binding constraint.

The duplicated-field findings point to an instrument design lesson. Capturing gestational age and birth weight twice created a useful internal check, and roughly 90% of pairs agreed exactly, but the disagreements that occurred were wide -up to 1,725 g and 7 weeks.

Repeated capture is valuable for detecting error, but only if the discrepancies it surfaces are resolved rather than carried forward.

The denominator problem

That preterm-birth prevalence cannot be estimated is a finding rather than a failure of analysis, and it generalises beyond this study. The register instrument captured individual delivery records and a preterm classification but no facility-period total-deliveries field, and the realised abstraction was overwhelmingly preterm. This is the structural issue identified in the global assessment of national routine data for low birthweight and preterm births, which 20 found that gaps in gestational-age metadata rather than in birthweight capture are the principal obstacle to routine preterm estimation in sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia, [6] and work in Western Kenya on using maternity-register gestational age reached a similar conclusion about what registers can bear. [7] A numerator-only extract is easy to produce and difficult to detect once analysis begins. Recovering facility-month totals for all deliveries, live births and stillbirths would make prevalence estimable without any change to the individual-record instrument.

Facility variation and information-system readiness

Facility variation in newborn-record completeness (34.9% to 100%) far exceeded variation in register completeness (93.1% to 99.3%), suggesting the constraint is local documentation practice and source-document availability rather than instrument design. The association between HIS readiness and register completeness (adjusted prevalence ratio 1.44 per 25 percentage points) was in the expected direction, but facility-level correlations were imprecise and non-significant with seven facilities and should not be read as evidence of either presence or absence of an effect; multi-facility assessments in Tanzania and Ethiopia suggest facility-level determinants become estimable only at substantially larger scale. [37, 38] The finding that level 5 records were more complete in the register but less complete at the newborn level after adjustment is worth noting for planners: it is compatible with a referral hospital that has invested in register systems and electronic reporting while carrying the highest volume and the most fragmented set of bedside sources, and cautions against assuming that information-system investment at higher levels automatically produces better individual clinical documentation. The markedly higher completeness of records for newborns born from January 2026 may reflect the condition of files at retrieval, recent files being more often intact and in active use, rather than a change in documentation practice, and should not be read as a trend.

Provider explanations and the audit paradox

The qualitative component identified an implementation gap the quantitative data alone would not have revealed. Both providers described functioning audit structures -weekly unit reviews, monthly data audits, a fixed monthly perinatal death review -and described audit as having shifted from a blaming exercise to an educational one, precisely the culture change that assessments of maternal and perinatal death surveillance and response across sub-Saharan Africa identify as necessary and often absent. [39, 40] Yet Provider KII 1 named implementation of action plans as the standing weakness, and Provider KII 2 described a scheduled review postponed because of an 18-admission day. Analyses of MPDSR across African countries have repeatedly found that reports can appear comprehensive while masking weak follow-through on recommendations, [39] and our data are consistent with that pattern in a specific and testable way: date-sequence errors persist at high frequency in a setting where monthly data review demonstrably occurs, which implies that what is reviewed does not include the checks that would catch them. This suggests a targeted rather than a general remedy. The problem is not the absence of audit but the absence of specific validation rules within it. Automated date-sequence and range checks at the point of data entry would convert a class of error that currently survives monthly review into one that cannot be entered at all. Before-and-after work in Nigerian primary facilities showed that targeted register redesign and supervision improved routine maternal and newborn data quality, [41, 42] the Kenyan Clinical Information Network experience shows documentation completeness improving measurably after hospitals join a structured audit-and-feedback network, [15, 16, 17] as does the Kenyan experience with repeated electronic data quality assessment, [43] and improvements in admission-temperature documentation within the same network illustrate what is achievable for a single field. [44]

Strengths

The principal methodological strength is that every estimate was computed from the original, unedited abstractions; had cleaned or imputed values been used, completeness would have been overstated. Second, every dataset was verified cell by cell against the original electronic export before analysis, so the estimates are traceable to the source data. Third, prespecified rules were applied across three linked data sources rather than a single register, allowing consistency to be assessed within and between instruments. Fourth, distinguishing blanks from explicit 'not recorded' responses showed that most of the newborn shortfall is genuine blank space, which has different implications for remediation.

Limitations

Ten limitations bear on interpretation. First, no independent clinical gold standard was collected, so accuracy was not and cannot be assessed here; recalculated classifications served only as a rules-based consistency reference. Second, documentation quality is not care quality -a missing field does not establish that care was not delivered, and a complete field does not establish that it was delivered well. Third, missing documentation is not evidence of omitted care, particularly for newborns who died early, whose records were measurably less complete. Fourth, the register dataset lacks a valid all-delivery denominator, so no prevalence claim can be supported. Fifth, only ten facilities contributed readiness data and seven contributed both readiness and patient-level records, so facility-level relationships are exploratory and imprecise. Sixth, the qualitative component comprises two interviews from a single referral hospital and cannot represent documentation practice at lower-level facilities, where the quantitative data suggest the problems differ in kind. Seventh, retrospective abstraction introduces its own error, and abstractor-reported completeness disagreed with computed completeness in 3.2% of register records. Eighth, duplicate records required reconciliation; although exclusions are reversible and conflicts preserved, some contradictory values remain unresolved because source documents could not be re-consulted. Ninth, facility-level correlation cannot establish causality, and the association between HIS readiness and completeness is equally compatible with better-resourced facilities performing better on both. Tenth, one facility lies outside Bungoma County; it contributed readiness data only and no patient-level record, so it does not affect any completeness estimate. For research using these data, three implications follow. Analyses of mortality, prematurity severity and birth-weight classification can proceed with the caveats in table 5. Analyses of referral timing must be restricted to records with valid intervals and framed as birth-toadmission interval rather than transport delay. Analyses of documentation-derived quality scores must recognise that survivors have more opportunity to accumulate documented care 23 . than newborns who die, and should therefore use early-care rather than whole-admission exposures for mortality models. For counties, the broader point is that the fields that fail are not the fields most often audited, and a data-quality review examining only completeness of birth weight and outcome will report a system in better health than it is in.

Implications for practice, policy and research

Conclusion

Routine maternity and newborn records in these Western Kenyan facilities were sufficiently complete and internally consistent to identify preterm and low-birth-weight newborns, classify prematurity severity, and analyse in-hospital mortality among newborns with a known final outcome. They were not sufficient to estimate preterm-birth prevalence, to characterise the referral and transport process, or to compute length of stay reliably from recorded dates. The dominant defects were chronological rather than biological, and the dominant gaps were in maternal history and timing fields rather than in the newborn's clinical description. Mandatory timing fields with point-of-entry validation, automatic derivation of birth-weight categories, routine capture of facility-month delivery denominators, and coded facility identifiers would address most of what we found using systems that already exist.

Because two of the nine facilities contributing newborn records accounted for a large share of the variation and the provider evidence comes from a single referral hospital, these findings describe the assessed facilities and should not be generalised to Western Kenya as a whole. lineage logs, and the analysis code are held by the study team at Kibabii University and may be made available to qualified researchers on reasonable request to the corresponding author, subject to approval by MMUST-ISERC, the County Government of Bungoma Department of Health and Kibabii University data-governance requirements, and to a data-sharing agreement. Five

Completeness was the presence of usable information in a required field; a value counted as documented only if a usable entry was present, with blanks and explicit 'not recorded', 'unknown' or 'not documented' entries both counted as not documented but tabulated separately, since they carry different implications for instrument design. Internal consistency was agreement between logically related variables; plausibility whether values, dates and combinations fell within defensible limits; uniqueness whether each record represented one distinct delivery or newborn; and analytical usability whether the data contained enough information to compute relevant indicators. Accuracy was not assessed, because no independent gold standard such as direct observation or re-measurement was collected. Completeness assessment Nineteen core register and 34 core newborn variables were prespecified across documentation domains (identification; maternal demographics or maternal and obstetric information; antenatal and obstetric data; newborn baseline; entry and referral; admission assessment; outcome; discharge). Four

Making admission date and time, survival status and outcome date and time mandatory fields with point-of-entry date-sequence validation would remove the largest single class of error. Adding facility-month totals for all deliveries, live births and stillbirths to routine reporting would make preterm and low-birth-weight prevalence estimable from data already being collected. Deriving the LBW and VLBW flags automatically from the recorded weight, rather than capturing them as independent items, would eliminate the directional discordance observed. Assigning KHIS facility codes at the point of data entry rather than relying on freetext names would remove a linkage hazard that produced 30 distinct spellings of nine facilities.
