Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. Abstract

Background: Patients' difficulties with illness and care (not understanding a result, distress, daily restrictions, not knowing where to go, family strain) may not surface in clinical encounters or records. Regulatory guidance on patient-focused drug development recognizes social-media listening as a source of patient experience data, but no systematic framework exists for Traditional Chinese discussions in Taiwan, and keyword pipelines measure such content poorly.

Objective: To characterize patient-expressed needs, burdens and barriers ("frictions") in Taiwanese online health discussions with an eight-domain, Taiwan-contextualized framework, identify whose frictions they are, and assess how securely each is measured.

Methods: From a 13.6-million-unit archive of the PTT forum and three ask-a-physician services, seeded samples of 9,131 pipeline-flagged units and 1,000 unflagged forum articles (10,130 distinct units) were read in full by a large language model under rubrics for relevance, author (patient or family member) and eight domains: information gap, emotional burden, lifestyle burden, medication experience, system navigation, access barrier, caregiver burden and cost burden. Profiles were reported separately for forum articles and physician submissions and checked against a second vendor, two physicians' independent labels of 160 accepted and rejected units, and the archive's keyword pipeline.

Results: Among 2,395 model-confirmed forum articles, 83.1% voiced at least one friction: information gap 59.5% (physicians 86%-93%), emotional burden 39.0%, medication experience 24.1%, lifestyle burden 22.2% and system navigation 16.3%; access barrier, caregiver burden and cost burden were each below 7%. Of 3,105 submissions, nearly all were questions and 53.8% also carried a burden. Navigation rarely concerned insurance (9.1%).

Family-authored forum articles (14.5%) more often voiced emotional burden (54.9% vs 36.3%), navigation (34.8% vs 13.2%; instrument-dependent) and access barriers (16.4% vs 4.7%), and patient-authored articles lifestyle burden (24.8% vs 6.9%); caregiver burden came with emotional burden in 86.0% of articles voicing it. Emotional and lifestyle burden were commoner in forum articles than submissions under the model, a second vendor and both physicians; medication experience and navigation were instrument-dependent, and physicians disagreed on cost (32% vs 4%). The keyword pipeline had stored its information-gap output for 29.9% of model-confirmed reference-stratum units and over-counted insurance navigation 2.9-fold; its relevance flag was 50.5% precise for forum articles and reached an estimated 42.9% of patient-voice articles.

Conclusions: Systematic listening organized Taiwanese patients' and families' online accounts into eight clinically interpretable friction domains, led by the need to understand and by emotional burden, with family-authored posts more often voicing navigation and access difficulty. These expressed concerns may not surface in routine encounters; they identify candidate targets for patient-centred communication and services, which require evaluation.

Some domain levels depended on the reader, and keyword pipelines missed or distorted them, so such work needs full-text reading, explicit definitions and clinician checks.

Introduction

Living with illness creates difficulties that patients do not always raise in a consultation or that a structured record does not capture: not understanding a result or what to do next, distress, restrictions of diet, work or sleep, uncertainty about which service to approach, costs, and strain on the family members who help. Patients and families describe these difficulties in public online channels, and patient-voice analysis now informs pharmacovigilance, patient-focused drug development and health policy [1] [2] [3] [4]. Regulators recognize the passive observation of social media as a source of patient experience data, and best-practice guidance asks that whole forums be collected within platform terms, users pseudonymized, patients identified before coding, concepts coded against explicit definitions, automated coding validated against human annotation, and filtering steps reported [5] [6] [7]. We use friction for these recurring difficulties with illness and with the health system. A framework that names the frictions, measures how often they are voiced and by whom, and states how securely each is measured is a basis for patient-centred communication and services.

What patients struggle with is usually studied one disease, platform and construct at a time, and Taiwanese and Chinese-language listening studies have been single-community, smallscale or unvalidated [8] [9] [10] [11] [12] [13] [14] [15]. Taiwan's setting shapes how frictions are expressed: a singlepayer National Health Insurance (NHI) system whose vocabulary (NHI, reimbursement, selfpay) saturates health talk [16, 17]; direct access to hospitals and specialists, which turns choosing where to go into a task of its own; and family members who often write on a patient's behalf. Patients speak on PTT, a non-commercial, academically operated bulletin board founded in 1995 and an established research source [18], and in ask-a-physician services, including the Ministry of Health and Welfare (MOHW) Taiwan e-Hospital. We found no systematic framework of patient frictions for Traditional Chinese.

Such a framework needs measurement that can be trusted. Production pipelines lean on keyword classifiers that over-count passing mentions [19, 20] and under-count paraphrase [21], and the relevance flag that defines who counts as a patient can both include wrong units and miss real ones [22, 23]. Large language models (LLMs) can read full text and match crowd annotators on many tasks [24] [25] [26] [27], although they trail fine-tuned classifiers on some health benchmarks [28] and require validation against human judgment and stability reporting [29] [30] [31]. What a post contains also depends on where it is written: expert-facing services concentrate informational and treatment-seeking content, whereas peer spaces elicit experience-sharing and emotional support [32] [33] [34], and patients attach history, medication details and emotion to consultation questions [8, 9, 35]. A friction profile must therefore be reported by source rather than pooled.

Following the collection, selection and coding workflow of the patient-listening guideline [5, 6], we applied an eight-domain friction framework, contextualized for Taiwan, to the full text of a sample of Taiwan's online patient voice. We asked which frictions patients and families express, whose frictions they are (the patient's or a family member's), which frictions occur together, and how securely each domain can be measured by an LLM, a second vendor and two physicians. Secondarily, we asked how the source (a general-purpose forum or a physician service), the pipeline's relevance flag and its keyword classifier change the profile.

Methods

Study Design and Data Source

This observational, cross-sectional content analysis is reported according to STROBE, with TRIPOD-LLM items for the LLM instrument [36, 37] (Multimedia Appendix 3), and is organized along the collection, selection and coding workflow of the patient-listening guideline [6]. Posts were collected from Taiwanese public platforms by rate-limited crawlers between 2024 and 2026, covering material published from 1995 onward. Collection was exhaustive at the board or site level, not keyword filtered; disease and construct tags were applied after collection. Author identifiers were irreversibly hashed (salted SHA-256) at collection; publication dates, boards or departments and hashed author identifiers were joined to every analysed unit.

Platform Governance

We audited each platform's live terms of service, robots.txt directives and licences (verbatim snapshots archived 16 August 2026) before any corpus statistic was computed. Four platforms whose terms did not prohibit non-commercial academic analysis were included: PTT (ptt.cc; its user-content licence bars republication, so no content is reproduced), KingNet (kingnet.com.tw; robot-compliant collection), Taiwan e-Hospital (MOHW open-government data declaration) and tcm.tw (Chinese-medicine questions and answers). Dcard, BabyHome and 5914, whose terms or robots.txt prohibit automated collection, and unverifiable patientassociation sites were excluded (Table S1 in Multimedia Appendix 1).

The Friction Framework

The eight domains were defined by the investigators before this study, from the concepts of patient experience, burden of living with a condition, treatment experience and unmet need in patient-focused drug development guidance and the patient-listening guideline [5] [6] [7], and from clinical experience; they were not derived inductively from these posts. Each is a binary label, assigned only when the friction is present for the author and stated in the text (passing mentions do not count): information gap (seeks information or advice, asks a clinical question, or expresses diagnostic or treatment uncertainty); emotional burden (illness-tied distress); lifestyle burden (illness-driven restriction of diet, activity, work or sleep); medication experience (first-hand experience, efficacy doubt or side effects); system navigation (working through insurance coverage, eligibility or paperwork, or deciding which hospital, specialty or physician to approach); access barrier (concrete difficulty reaching care, such as appointments, referral, distance or waiting); caregiver burden (the author helps a patient and voices that burden); and cost burden (actual financial strain from care costs). The framework was contextualized for Taiwan in three respects: rubrics were applied to Traditional Chinese text, and insurance vocabulary counted as navigation or cost only when the author worked through it or was strained by it; navigation included choosing a hospital, specialty or physician, a frequent task where patients approach specialists directly; and the author was coded separately from the person whose health was discussed, so that a family member's own strain (caregiver burden) was distinguished from the patient's frictions. A post may carry several domains; domains are reported separately and are not summed into a severity score. The verbatim rubrics are in Multimedia Appendix 2. The framework's coverage of difficulties outside the eight domains was not audited in this study (see Limitations).

Units of Analysis and Sources

On PTT an article is an authored post, and a push is a one-line comment appended beneath an article by another user that the pipeline indexes and flags as a separate unit; pushes are reported as their own unit type and the open-forum source is PTT articles. On KingNet, Taiwan e-Hospital and tcm.tw every unit is a submission to a physician, a question by design.

The three services share that property (99.0-99.9% questions) and are pooled with design weights for their flagged populations (largest weighted-minus-unweighted difference 0.02 points; per-site rates in Table S2 in Multimedia Appendix 1). Friction profiles are reported separately for forum articles and physician submissions; no rate pooled across them is reported as an estimate. KingNet units carry the physician's reply; a question-only re-read was a sensitivity analysis.

Full-Text Friction Measurement

Each unit (title and body, truncated at 6,000 characters) was read under the friction rubric by gpt-6.1-sol (OpenAI; Codex command-line interface; 23 October 2026) in documents of 25 units that carried both verbatim rubrics; the model returned one JSON (JavaScript Object Notation) object per unit with its identifier echoed, documents were re-read until complete, sampling settings were provider defaults with no seed, and units were placed in documents as seeded mixtures of unit types and years. Only 25 articles (0.5%), and no other unit, exceeded 6,000 characters. Model and document size were chosen in a pilot of four models at 25, 100 and 300 units per document; the pilot served only these choices and is not reported further.

On the services every submission is a question by design, so the informative measure is the burden that accompanies the question. Prompts, except that of the strict NHI-navigation read, are in Multimedia Appendix 2.

Agreement and Replication

The instrument was compared with three references (a single-unit reference read, earlier physician reference sets and a second-vendor replica), and separately with two physicians (see Physician Comparison). First, the 3,631-unit stratum had been read unit by unit by GPT-5.5 (OpenAI; OpenRouter route openai/gpt-5.5; June-August 2026; single user message; provider-default temperature); agreement with this single-unit reference read measures consistency across reading formats of the same vendor, not accuracy against human judgment. Second, physician labels exist for information gap on a 50-unit Taiwanese reference set (two physicians; κ 0.79 between them on 29 units; drawn from a 91-post physician pool that included 19 posts from platforms later excluded or unrecorded) and for NHI navigation on a separate set of 92 units. Third, every unit was read end to end by a second vendor (Claude Sonnet 5.5, Anthropic) with the same documents and rubrics. A domain was pre-specified as instrument-dependent if its replica κ among units confirmed by both instruments was below 0.60, or if its services-minus-articles contrast changed sign or significance under the replica; such domains are reported descriptively.

Physician Comparison

Two physician authors (CT and CFK; CFK also designed the instrument) independently rated 160 units in a bilingual worksheet that reproduced the instrument's definitions of relevance, voice and the eight domains; the worksheet contained no model output, units were presented in shuffled order, and neither physician saw the other's ratings. Units were drawn with a fixed seed from the full-text annotation (all units read in full: the flagged sample and the unflagged articles): 95 instrument-confirmed units at random within unit type (the random sample: 37 articles, 17 push comments and 41 submissions), 30 units the instrument had not confirmed (15, 10 and 5), and 25 units enriched for rare domains and 10 for disagreement between the instrument and the replica. Each physician labelled relevance and voice for every unit and the domains for units they judged patient or caregiver voice. Each physician personally read every unit and entered the labels; no labels were pre-filled by a model or other software. There was no adjudication. Both physicians were aware of the study aims and one designed the instrument, so this comparison is a check against non-independent raters, not validation against a reference standard. The instrument was compared with each physician separately and benchmarked against agreement between the physicians, using Cohen κ and Gwet's AC1, which is robust to skewed prevalence [38]; κ intervals were not computed where either source had fewer than five positives. Weighting sampled units to their unit type's count in the full-text annotation, we estimated, by unit type, the share of instrument-confirmed units each physician confirmed, the share of unconfirmed units each physician confirmed, the resulting share of physician-identified patient voice that the instrument captured, and domain rates and services-minus-articles differences under each physician's labels, with percentile intervals from 2,000 resamples within strata. Because the full-text annotation combines a flagged sample with unflagged articles, these estimates describe the units read, by unit type, not the archive.

Keyword Classifier Benchmark

The archive's production pipeline relies on a rule-based heuristic (version 2.4) using Traditional Chinese keyword sets with jieba segmentation that assigns relevance classes and, for patient-voice units, friction aspects including an information gap. Its measurement was benchmarked on the reference stratum, where its outputs are stored, on two domains chosen a priori to span the breadth spectrum: information gap (broad), against the full-text model label, and NHI navigation (narrow; genuine engagement with the insurance system, distinguished from passing mentions), against a strict single-unit model read. Both references are model labels, not adjudicated human truth. The pipeline's stored information-gap output was missing for many units, apparently those outside its aspect-processing scope (on PTT, mostly older posts); we report the share with a stored output and compare rates both as stored (missing treated as absent, as in the production outputs) and on units with a stored output.

The keyword instrument for NHI navigation is a 24-term NHI lexicon (Multimedia Appendix 2); where the pipeline's detection and measurement lexicons overlap, measurement is circular: all but one of 2,930 aspect-labelled units containing the term for self-pay were labelled cost-positive by the pipeline, and all 908 containing the term for referral systemnavigation-positive, so such pipeline labels were not used.

Confirmed Units

Of 9,131 flagged units read, 5,734 were confirmed as health-related and patient-or familyauthored: 2,395 forum articles, 234 push comments and 3,105 submissions (KingNet 1,318, Taiwan e-Hospital 1,293, tcm.tw 494). Confirmed articles came from 2,274 authors on 33 of the 41 boards in the frame (median length 482 characters; median year 2015), and submissions from 73 departments (median length 170; median year 2012; Table S3 in Multimedia Appendix 1). Family members wrote 14.5% of confirmed articles (348) and 14.0% of submissions (436).

The Friction Profile

Among confirmed forum articles, 83.1% voiced at least one friction and 63.4% a burden beyond a question (Table 2; Figure 2). The need to understand led: 59.5% sought information, asked a clinical question or expressed uncertainty about a diagnosis or treatment.

Emotional burden followed (39.0%), then medication experience (24.1%), lifestyle burden (22.2%) and system navigation (16.3%). Access barriers (6.4%), caregiver burden (6.6%) and cost burden (2.9%) were each voiced in fewer than 7% of articles. Among submissions, 99.6% were questions by construction, and 53.8% carried a further burden with the question: medication experience 25.0%, emotional burden 20.4%, system navigation 15.0% and lifestyle burden 11.9%; access, caregiver and cost burden were each 2.5% or less. The two sources carried different frictions. Compared with forum articles, submissions carried less emotional burden (-18.7 points), lifestyle burden (-10.4), caregiver burden (-5.3), access barriers (-3.9) and cost burden (-2.3), all Holm-significant and reproduced by the replica, and preserved in each sensitivity analysis of the full sources (Table S4 in Multimedia Appendix 1); medication experience and navigation did not differ. Burden varied widely across boards: excluding the six appearance and fitness boards raised the article any-burden rate to 80.5% (Table S4), and psychiatry boards carried more emotional burden than psychiatry submissions (by 18.1 points; 95% CI 5.7 to 32.2). Whose Frictions: Patients and Family Members

Family-authored posts voiced different frictions from patient-authored ones (Table 3A). On the forum, family-authored articles more often voiced emotional burden (54.9% vs 36.3%; difference +18.5 points, 95% CI +12.9 to +24.1), system navigation (34.8% vs 13.2%), access barriers (16.4% vs 4.7%) and an information gap (71.3% vs 57.5%), and 44.3% voiced the family member's own caregiver burden. Patient-authored articles more often voiced lifestyle burden (24.8% vs 6.9%) and medication experience (25.6% vs 14.9%). In submissions, apart from caregiver burden, only the navigation (19.0% vs 14.3%), lifestyle (0.9% vs 13.6%) and medication (16.3% vs 26.5%) differences recurred, and family-authored submissions carried less burden overall (45.9% vs 55.1%; -9.2 points, 95% CI -14.2 to -4.2).

How Frictions Occur Together

Distress was as common with a question as without one: among forum articles with an information gap, 38.8% voiced emotional burden, against 39.3% of articles without one (Table 2B) and 39.0% of all articles (submissions 20.4% and 20.4%; Table 3B). Other frictions clustered. Among articles with emotional burden, 34.8% also voiced lifestyle burden (22.2% of all articles), and among those with system navigation, 24.6% also voiced an access barrier (6.4%; submissions 7.1% and 2.5%). Caregiver burden usually came with emotional burden (86.0% of articles and 72.5% of submissions with caregiver burden, against 39.0% and 20.4% overall).

How Selection Changes the Profile

The friction profile depended on which units were counted. The relevance read confirmed 50.5% (95% CI 49.1-52.0) of flagged articles, 19.7% (95% CI 17.5-22.1) of flagged push comments and 96.1%, 98.2% and 95.4% of KingNet, Taiwan e-Hospital and tcm.tw submissions (Table 1). On flagged rather than confirmed articles, 32.4% voiced a burden and 33.3% a question, against 63.4% and 59.5% of confirmed articles, so on flagged units the forum appeared the less burdened source (any burden 32.4% vs 49.3%-58.7% of flagged submissions on the three services), the reverse of the confirmed picture. The flag also missed patient voice: 244 of 1,000 unflagged articles (24.4%) were confirmed, so the flag reached an estimated 42.9% (95% CI 40.2-45.7) of patient-voice articles, and the archive holds about 225 thousand patient-voice articles (210-239 thousand) rather than the 96 thousand inside the flag. Missed articles were shorter (median 172 vs 482 characters), more often questions (77.9% vs 59.5%) and less often emotional (29.5% vs 39.0%), with similar overall burden (60.2% vs 63.4%); adding them mattered for the information gap more than for burden (33.3% on all flagged, 59.5% on confirmed flagged and 70.0% on all confirmed articles weighted to the archive; any burden 32.4%, 63.4% and 61.6%). These counts refer to the model's relevance read, which the physicians found conservative on articles; the physicians also rejected about a quarter of model-confirmed articles, so the archive-level direction of bias is uncertain, and both estimates are conditional on the 41 boards in the sample. Applied to the flagged frame, PTT's 501 thousand flagged units correspond to about 158 thousand model-confirmed units (95% CI 150-165 thousand), so PTT's share of model-confirmed patient voice in the frame is about 27% rather than 53%. A pooled rate estimates a specified mixture of sources correctly; the problem arises when the mixture is set by the sampling frame: at the projected article share among model-confirmed articles and submissions in the flagged frame (18.5%; the confirmed sample was 43.5% articles because of the oversampling), the pooled value lay outside both source CIs for seven of nine measures, and the pooled information-gap rate moves from 99.6% to 59.5% as the article share moves from 0 to 1 (Figure 3a); the three article denominators are compared in Figure 3b. Of the 2,343 flagged articles not confirmed, the relevance read coded 2,007 as other voices or topics, 258 as media and 78 as clinician voice, and neither removing the physician's reply from KingNet units nor excluding tcm.tw or KingNet changed any service domain rate by more than 2.1 points.

Discussion

Principal Findings

Reading the full text of a Taiwanese sample under an explicit eight-domain framework showed what patients and families bring to public channels. The need to understand (is this normal, what does this result mean, what should I do?) led under every reader, and emotional burden followed under the model and the second vendor; medication experience, lifestyle restriction and navigation were common, and access barriers, caregiver burden and explicit financial strain were voiced less often. Family members wrote one post in seven, and their forum posts more often voiced distress, navigation and access difficulty, with their own strain usually accompanied by distress, whereas patients' posts more often voiced daily-life restriction. Distress came with questions as often as without them, while some frictions clustered: distress with lifestyle restriction, navigation with access difficulty, and a relative's strain with distress. The forum carried more emotional and lifestyle burden than physician submissions on every reading, and more family burden under the model, and a physician submission was almost always a question, so the two sources show different parts of the same profile.

Domains differed in how securely they were measured. Emotional burden and the forum's excess of emotional and lifestyle burden were reproduced by a second vendor and by both physicians; the need to understand was common under every reader but its level was readerdependent (59.5% of forum articles by the model, 86%-93% by the physicians); medication experience and navigation were instrument-dependent; and cost and access burden were rare under the model and disputed between the physicians. Lower agreement on a domain limits its exact rate, not its relevance: a friction that two physicians label differently is still a friction patients express, and Table 4 states for each domain what can and cannot be claimed.

The profile was also sensitive to how units were selected. With 50.5% precision on articles and 19.7% on comments, the pipeline's flagged denominator diluted the forum's friction rates and reversed the overall source comparison, and with 42.9% recall the flag missed shorter, question-dominated posts. The production pipeline's stored outputs omitted the dominant friction for most units and its insurance lexicon over-counted a rare institutional one about threefold. Frictions that patients do express can therefore disappear, or be distorted, in a keyword-based summary.

Comparison With Prior Work

Platform features shape patient-generated content [8, 9, 32, 34, 35]; this study adds a multidomain friction profile for Traditional Chinese patient voice, with authorship and measurement security stated for each domain. Our finding that emotional content concentrates on a peer platform agrees with the Dutch comparison of platforms [32] (39.0% vs 20.4% in confirmed units), although our forum is a general-purpose board in a singlepayer setting. Against the patient-listening guideline [6], the study implements collection, selection and coding and reports counts at each step (Table 1); emotional and lifestyle burden map onto its quality-of-life facets and medication experience onto treatment experience, while the other domains add the health-system and informational concepts of unmet need and burden of living with a condition [5, 7]. Two departures are candidates for its next revision: on a general-purpose forum a keyword relevance flag does not yield a patient cohort, and family members writing for patients should be distinguished from patients, because they voice different frictions.

LLM annotation of health social-media text has been validated against human labels [25] [26] [27] [28] and keyword over-and under-counting are well documented [20, 21]. Here the instrument agreed with the physicians less than they agreed with each other on relevance and on information-seeking, and its relevance read was conservative on forum articles, which a check of confirmed units alone would not have revealed.

Implications

The frictions point to candidate responses that can be evaluated (Table 4). The most frequent need, understanding results, symptoms and next steps, points to patient-readable explanation delivered where patients already look. Two in five forum questions came with distress, so information alone may not be enough. Navigation rarely concerned insurance, which suggests that guidance on where to seek care matters in a system without gatekeeping. Familyauthored posts more often voiced navigation and access difficulty and their own strain, which argues for information and support addressed to the person helping, while respecting patients' own priorities. These are hypotheses for communication and service design: frequency is not severity or patient priority, an expressed concern is not proof of an unmet need, and none of these responses has been tested here.

For patient-focused drug development and surveillance, a friction framework with explicit definitions, author coding and per-domain measurement checks is transferable; the source and unit type are reporting units; and a relevance classifier's precision and recall, and whether a keyword pipeline stored a label at all, must be measured before its counts are trusted. For LLM-based listening, the transferable elements are a full-text relevance read, verbatim rubrics, an agreement chain to an independent read and physician labels, a physician check of both accepted and rejected units, a second-vendor replica with a pre-specified rule, and published prompts.

Limitations

First, the framework was defined by the investigators from existing concepts, not derived from these posts; frictions outside the eight domains were not measured. Patients and caregivers did not help define the domains [5, 6]. Second, the physician comparison was modest: two physician authors, aware of the study aims and one of whom designed the instrument, rated 160 units without adjudication; they agreed with each other only moderately (κ 0.21-0.71 across domains; 0.60 on patient-voice status, where the instrument's agreement with each physician, 0.32 and 0.37, was lower), and one labelled several domains far more often than the other. Third, relevance was confirmed by the model, and the physicians judged that it captured about half of the patient voice in the forum articles read (52% and 58%), estimated from only 15 rejected articles; a revised, recall-oriented relevance rubric, frozen (SHA-256) before use, was developed afterwards for a companion study (Liu ZY, Hung YC, Kuo CF; preprint submitted to medRxiv), and the rates here are not revised retrospectively.

Fourth, the information-gap domain combines an explicit request for information, expressed uncertainty and the question format of a consultation; a future codebook should separate these, and medication experience and navigation, which were instrument-dependent and can be neutral, should be split into neutral and burdensome subtypes. Fifth, rates describe what self-selected posters on four platforms expressed, not population prevalence, and how often a friction is voiced is not its severity, its importance to patients or evidence that care missed it; showing that such concerns are omitted from encounters or records would need a comparison with those records.

Sixth, recall was measured on forum articles of the sampled boards only.

Seventh, the archive spans 1995-2026; restriction to 2020 or later preserved the source contrast (-9.4 points, 95% CI -15.1 to -3.7), and author-clustered intervals were close to unit-level ones (width ratios 0.96-1.08; the 2020 restriction is in Table S4 in Multimedia Appendix 1). Eighth, the comparisons of patient-with family-authored posts and the co-occurrence summaries were specified after the primary results had been inspected and are descriptive. Ninth, sources differ in their authors, topics and eras (median year 2015 vs 2012), the rates come from one language and one pipeline, and the models may have seen public PTT posts during training. Finally, the companion study draws on the same archive (338 of the 36,564 distinct posts in its seven condition frames are among the units read here) but applies the framework to kidney-related posts and reports none of the results presented here.

Conclusions

Systematic listening to Taiwanese online health discussions, read in full under an explicit, Taiwan-contextualized framework, organized patients' and families' accounts into eight friction domains. The need to understand and emotional burden led; navigation rarely concerned insurance; family-authored posts more often voiced navigation, access difficulty and distress; and the forum carried more emotional and daily-life burden than physician submissions under every reader. These are concerns that may not surface in routine encounters and are candidates for patient-centred communication and services, which require evaluation. Their exact levels depended for some domains on the reader, and keyword pipelines missed or distorted them, so listening of this kind needs full-text reading, explicit definitions, author coding, unit-stratified denominators with measured precision and recall, and clinician checks of both accepted and rejected content.
