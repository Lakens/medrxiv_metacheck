To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 1 non-significant p value that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Sequence-to-omics (S2O) predictions and molecular outliers provide complementary evidence to prioritize functional rare variants and facilitate rare disease diagnoses. However, current approaches do not integrate both lines of evidence. We present Watershed-DL, a framework that integrates S2O predictions of variant effects from DNA and protein sequence with multi-omic outliers to identify large-effect rare variants. We applied Watershed-DL to 1000 Genomes samples with RNA-seq (n=1,030) to prioritize rare variants underlying expression, splicing, and allele-specific expression outliers, and, using a separate peak-level model, to samples with ATAC-seq (n=174) for chromatin accessibility outliers. Compared to a model without S2O predictions, Watershed-DL increased the number of prioritized variants by 61.4%, and S2O features were weighted highly in the model across all molecular signals. Prioritized variants were over 60-fold enriched for ClinVar pathogenic classifications. Matching prioritized variants to known pathogenic variants in the same gene with a concordant molecular effect identified 331 additional candidate recessive disease alleles. Applying this framework to the GREGoR rare disease cohort with RNA-seq (n=677) prioritized previously reported causal variants across 10 solved cases and identified 451 candidate variants in unsolved cases, including a diagnostic variant in PPP1R12A and a novel candidate disease gene, ATP5PF.

Results

Multi-omic outliers and S2O predictions provide complementary evidence of rare variant function Watershed-DL integrates S2O predictions and other variant-level annotations alongside personal multi-omic outlier signals in a probabilistic graphical model to predict the functional effects of rare variants (Figure 1A). The design of Watershed-DL allows for information sharing across different multi-omics data types and variant-level annotations. We integrated S2O predictions as variant annotations in Watershed-DL, including models of gene expression (Flashzoi-RNA 27 , PromoterAI 6 ), alternative splicing (SpliceAI https://paperpile.com/c/mSKw54/rNcJ , Pangolin 9 ), alternative polyadenylation (APARENT2 10 ), chromatin accessibility (ChromBPNet 7 ), and protein structure and function (ESM-v1 11 , PrimateAI 12 , AlphaMissense 13 ). We also develop a novel extension of the Watershed-DL framework for chromatin accessibility outliers, included as a separate model, to prioritize rare variants within ATAC-seq peaks.

We applied Watershed-DL to lymphoblastoid cell line (LCL) RNA-seq data from 1,030 donors from the 1KG generated as part of AFGR 28 , representing 5 African populations, and MAGE 29 , representing 26 global populations. From these samples, we identified gene-level outliers for RNA expression (eOutliers), allele-specific expression (aseOutliers), and alternative splicing (sOutliers) (see Methods). For the peak-level adaptation of Watershed-DL, we identified chromatin accessibility outliers (caOutliers) from LCL-derived ATAC-seq data from 174 samples, generated by AFGR 28 and by Kumasaka et al. (British (GBR) individuals) 30 . For each outlier signal, z-scores were corrected for batch-driven sample covariation (Figure S1; see Methods). After quality filtering, 14,312 genes were quantified for eOutlier identification, 12,740 for sOutliers, and 10,294 for aseOutliers, while 194,385 peaks were jointly called for caOutlier identification. We then applied Watershed-DL to 15.2 million rare variants (global maximum allele frequency < 1% in gnomAD v4.1) identified from 30X short-read whole genome sequencing (sr-WGS) of 1KG donors with matched RNA-seq or ATAC-seq (Figure S2).

We characterized the distribution and overlap of molecular outliers across individuals. Chromatin accessibility outliers were more abundant per individual than other molecular phenotypes measured at the gene level (Figure 1B), consistent with the greater number of accessibility peaks compared to genes. We observed a median of 383 caOutliers (interquartile range (IQR), 248-624), compared with 61 eOutliers (IQR, 38-98), 59 aseOutliers (IQR, 50-67), and 57 sOutliers (IQR, 43-78). Outliers were frequently shared across molecular phenotypes, with the strongest co-occurrence between eOutliers and aseOutliers (relative risk (RR) = 6.41), followed by eOutliers and sOutliers (RR = 4.90), eOutliers and caOutliers within 10 kilobases (RR = 4.60), and sOutliers and aseOutliers (RR = 3.48) (Figure 1C).

We first tested whether molecular outliers were associated with an increased burden of nearby rare variants. Without incorporating functional annotations, rare variants were enriched within 10 kb of genes with eOutliers, aseOutliers, and sOutliers, as well as within caOutlier peaks, with enrichment increasing with outlier strength (Figure 1D). At an absolute z-score > 3, caOutliers showed the strongest enrichment (RR = 6.61, P < 0.001), followed by aseOutliers (RR = 1.09, P = 2.18 x 10 -26 ), sOutliers (RR = 1.06, P = 4.35 x 10 -10 ), and eOutliers (RR = 1.02, P = 3.66 x 10 -5 ). The enrichment was further differentiated by variant consequence, with splice and coding variants showing stronger enrichment than non-coding variants (Figure 1E).

To assess whether S2O predictions of rare variants in the 1KG were associated with molecular outliers, we evaluated the enrichment of model predictions for corresponding outlier signals. At the 99th percentile of each S2O model, we observed the strongest enrichment of ChromBPNet for caOutliers (RR = 18.48), followed by PromoterAI for eOutliers (RR = 9.14), SpliceAI for sOutliers (RR = 5.68), and Pangolin for sOutliers (RR = 3.40) (Figure 1F). S2O predictions consistently showed the highest enrichment for their matched outlier type at their recommended thresholds (Figure 1G). These results support integrating S2O predictions with multi-omic outliers to prioritize functional rare variants with large molecular effects.

Watershed-DL improves prioritization of large effect non-coding rare variants

To assess whether integrating S2O predictions with molecular outliers improves rare variant prioritization, we trained Watershed-DL in the 1KG and compared its performance with two baselines: a Watershed model without S2O predictions, and a genome annotation model (GAM) which includes S2O predictions but does not integrate molecular outliers (see Methods). Compared to the Watershed baseline, Watershed-DL demonstrated improved precision-recall area under the curve (PR-AUC) across expression (0.028 vs. 0.023 without S2O), splicing (0.054 vs. 0.041), and chromatin accessibility (0.129 vs. 0.055) (Figure 2A). For ASE, which did not have a specifically matched S2O model, Watershed-DL showed comparable performance to the non-S2O model (PR-AUC = 0.013 vs. 0.014). We additionally compared with an S2O annotation-only baseline and found that the Watershed-DL model improved PR-AUC across all outlier signals (Figure S3). These results demonstrate that integrating S2O predictions with molecular outliers improves performance of functional rare variant discovery.

In addition, Watershed-DL prioritized more variants across all molecular signals compared to the baseline Watershed model, with the largest increase of prioritized variants in non-coding regions (Figure 2B). At posterior probability ≥0.6, Watershed-DL prioritized 2,555 variants for expression, 3,351 for splicing, 1,014 for ASE, and 1,690 for chromatin accessibility that were associated with at least one outlier carrier. Overall, integrating S2O predictions increased prioritized variants by 61.4% across all outlier signals. Further, 77.9% of newly prioritized variants were neither coding nor canonical splice-site variants, highlighting the ability of S2O predictions to identify functional non-coding variants compared to prior genome annotations based on coding annotations, splice site regions, or conservation metrics. Watershed-DL also prioritized more variants associated with two or more outlier signals compared to the baseline Watershed model. The largest increases were in variants prioritized for both expression and ASE (n = 389), followed by expression and splicing (n = 133), with 86.4% of multi-outlier prioritized variants uniquely identified by Watershed-DL (Figure 2C). Consistent with these overlaps, Watershed-DL learned highest-ranking edge weights connecting the expression and ASE latent variables (θ = 0.017), followed by expression and splicing (θ = 0.004; Figure 2D), indicating that the model leveraged shared functional status across these outlier signals.

Watershed-DL learned consistently high feature weights for S2O predictions across all outlier signals (Figure 2E). Molecular phenotype-matched S2O predictions were the top-weighted features across expression, splicing, and chromatin accessibility, with ChromBPNet as the most informative feature for chromatin accessibility (θ = 0.78), PromoterAI for expression (θ = 0.17), and SpliceAI and Pangolin for splicing (maximum θ = 0.10 for both). For ASE, which did not have a directly matched S2O model, PromoterAI ranked 2nd-most informative (θ = 0.07), and SpliceAI donor gain and loss ranked 4th and 5th respectively (θ = 0.05 for both). In contrast, the Watershed model without S2O predictions weighted allele frequency as the most informative feature for chromatin accessibility (θ = -0.33), loss-of-function classification for expression (θ = 0.10), and splice region annotation for splicing (θ = 0.13) (Figure S4). These findings demonstrate that S2O predictions are highly impactful for prioritizing outlier-associated rare variants across multiple transcriptomic and chromatin accessibility signals, even when compared to high-confidence coding annotations such as loss-of-function.

Watershed-DL reveals ancestry-specific recessive disease alleles

To evaluate the clinical relevance of prioritized variants in the 1KG, we examined their overlap with established disease gene sets and clinical variant classifications. Watershed-DL identified 1,432 prioritized variants in ClinGen, OMIM, and ACMG recessive disease genes (Figure S5A), providing candidates for further clinical interpretation. ClinVar representation varied across molecular phenotypes (Figure 3A), with ASE variants showing the highest proportion represented in ClinVar (18.41%), followed by expression (13.83%) and splicing (11.12%).

Chromatin accessibility variants showed the lowest representation (0.95%), consistent with the under-ascertainment of non-coding regulatory variants in clinical databases 31 . Among prioritized variants represented in ClinVar (n=801), 166 were previously classified as pathogenic or likely pathogenic (P/LP) and 225 as variants of uncertain significance (VUS). Excluding chromatin accessibility which had very low overlap, pathogenic and likely pathogenic variants were enriched among prioritized variants (RR = 65.20 and RR = 45.41, respectively), whereas benign and likely benign variants were depleted (RR = 0.15 and RR = 0.32, respectively) (Figure 3B). Enrichment for P/LP variants was comparable between the baseline Watershed model and Watershed-DL, consistent with the greater representation of coding and canonical splice-site variants among ClinVar P/LP variants, which are well captured by standard annotations 32 . S2O integration instead expanded the set of prioritized non-coding and non-canonical splice-site variants, which remain underrepresented in ClinVar but comprised 70.35% of variants prioritized by Watershed-DL.

ClinVar is further limited by the European-ancestry bias in clinical variant submissions, resulting in reduced representation of variants from non-European ancestries 33 . Excluding chromatin accessibility, where not all ancestry groups are represented, 45.20% of prioritized variants were identified only in donors from the five African (AFR) populations in AFGR. The remainder were distributed across the East Asian (EAS), South Asian (SAS), European (EUR), and Admixed American (AMR) ancestry groups (Figure S5B). Prioritized variants from donors of non-European ancestries were less likely to be represented in ClinVar than those from European ancestries (Figure 3C).

To address the under-ascertainment of non-coding variants and variants from donors of non-European ancestries in ClinVar, we developed a molecular effect matching approach to identify disease-relevant candidates among prioritized variants lacking a pathogenic classification. This approach is based on the hypothesis that variants producing a similar molecular effect on a gene as a known pathogenic variant may have similar phenotypic consequences. Among variants prioritized for known recessive disease genes, we first excluded those annotated as benign or likely benign in ClinVar or observed in a homozygous state in gnomAD (v4.1), as homozygous occurrence provides evidence against pathogenicity for variants underlying recessive disease. We then matched the remaining 1,112 prioritized variants, excluding known P/LP variants, to P/LP ClinVar variants using three complementary strategies. The first, concordant outlier matching, where prioritized variants have the same molecular outlier as an observed P/LP variant. The second, LoF matching, where prioritized variants with underexpression outliers are in the same gene as a known P/LP nonsense or frameshift variant. The third, CNV matching, where prioritized variants are in the same gene as a P/LP CNV and with gains matched to overexpression and losses to underexpression (Figure 3D). Together, these matches identified 331 candidate recessive disease alleles, including 57 variants currently classified as VUSs or with conflicting classifications in ClinVar (Figure 3E, Table S5).

Among variants prioritized for gene-level outliers, most were singletons across the 3,202 individuals with 30X sr-WGS in the 1KG (n = 3,599), followed by variants observed in a single population sample (n = 1,349), a single continental ancestry group (n = 724), and multiple ancestry groups (n = 560). Known P/LP variants and candidates identified through molecular effect matching were most concentrated among singletons and single-population variants, underscoring the need for disease allele discovery in ancestrally diverse cohorts (Figure 3F).

The clinical relevance of prioritized variants is further illustrated at ATM, an established ataxia-telangiectasia disease gene 34 , where six variants were prioritized for underexpression and/or splicing outliers, including two P/LP variants and three VUSs (Figure 3G). One VUS was prioritized for an sOutlier and strongly predicted to disrupt the adjacent splice donor (SpliceAI donor loss Δ = 0.71; Pangolin splice loss Δ = 0.76). Another, a missense variant, was prioritized for an underexpression outlier with modest predicted splicing and missense pathogenicity effects (SpliceAI donor gain Δ = 0.16; Pangolin splice gain Δ = 0.17; AlphaMissense = 0.21).

The third VUS, a missense variant with high predicted pathogenicity (AlphaMissense = 0.98) but no predicted splicing effect (SpliceAI and Pangolin Δ = 0.00), was prioritized for an underexpression outlier in an individual who also carried an intronic variant predicted to create a cryptic splice donor (SpliceAI donor gain Δ = 0.50; Pangolin splice gain Δ = 0.30). Although Watershed-DL can prioritize more than one variant for a given outlier, the intronic variant received a higher posterior (0.82 vs. 0.68), suggesting it better explains the observed underexpression. Together, this illustrates how Watershed-DL can prioritize variants for clinical interpretation based on both their predicted mechanism and observed molecular effect.

Watershed-DL prioritizes causal and candidate diagnostic variants in the GREGoR rare disease cohort

We assessed the utility of Watershed-DL for rare disease diagnosis using data from 677 individuals in the GREGoR Consortium (release U15) with matched whole blood RNA-seq and 30X whole genome sequencing. GREGoR enrolls individuals with suspected Mendelian disease, most of whom remain undiagnosed despite previous clinical genetic testing 26 . This provided an opportunity to benchmark Watershed-DL against known diagnostic variants in solved cases and identify candidate diagnoses in unsolved cases. The cohort included 311 probands, of whom 38 were solved, 27 were partially solved, and 246 remained unsolved (Figure 4A).

We trained Watershed-DL to predict eOutliers using a set of S2O predictions which included PromoterAI, SpliceAI, APARENT2, AlphaMissense, ESM-v1, and PrimateAI. Watershed-DL outperformed the genome annotation baseline in the GREGoR RNA-seq cohort (PR-AUC = 0.061 vs. 0.024; Figure S6A) and feature weights were comparable to the 1KG expression model (Figure S6B). For rare disease variant prioritization, we used less stringent outlier thresholds than in the 1KG analysis (posterior probability ≥ 0.45; |z-score| ≥ 2.5). Watershed-DL prioritized 9,324 eOutlier-associated variants across all GREGoR participants, which included 4,390 in probands. Of these, 1,154 were further supported by LOEUF constraint, 1,067 by OMIM disease association, and 581 by HPO phenotype matching, and 25 matched curated variants of interest in the GREGoR Genetics Findings table across solved and unsolved cases (Figure 4B).

To assess the sensitivity of Watershed-DL for recovering diagnostic variants, we examined posterior probabilities for diagnostic variants restricted to solved cases in the GREGoR cohort. As the Genetics Findings table includes all curated variants, not only those relevant to a patient's phenotypes, we selected diagnostic variants for each solved proband based on annotated phenotype contribution and mode of inheritance: one variant for autosomal dominant conditions, and either one homozygous variant or two compound heterozygous variants for autosomal recessive conditions (see Methods). This method selected 24 diagnostic variants across 20 probands. Of these, 13 were prioritized in Watershed-DL across 10 probands, which included all diagnostic variants associated with eOutliers in whole blood (Figure 4C). The https://paperpile.com/c/mSKw54/l4Ty1 diagnostic variants not prioritized by Watershed-DL comprised 6 missense variants, 2 intronic variants predicted as donor gain by SpliceAI, and 3 LOFTEE 1 loss-of-function variants, none of which were associated with eOutliers in whole blood. In a previously solved proband presenting with spastic diplegia and motor delays, Watershed-DL prioritized two compound heterozygous SNVs in COQ4 that were previously reported to explain the proband's phenotype 35 . These included a maternally inherited splice region variant (SpliceAI donor gain Δ = 0.12, PromoterAI: 0.386) marked as likely pathogenic in the GREGoR Genetic Findings table 36 , and a paternally inherited missense variant (ESM-v1: -7.496, PrimateAI: 0.544, AlphaMissense: 0.296, CADD: 29.8) marked as pathogenic.

To identify Watershed-DL prioritized variants consistent with rare disease inheritance, we filtered prioritized variants by population frequency, phenotype segregation, and phase confirmation where parental sequencing data were available (Table S6; see Methods). In solved probands, inheritance filtering retained 11 out of 13 prioritized diagnostic variants (Figure 4D). The two variants that were not retained by inheritance filtering were each SNVs that formed a compound heterozygous pair in trans with an unscored structural variant, and both SNVs were excluded from the autosomal dominant inheritance mode by population frequency criteria. In unsolved probands, inheritance filtering identified 451 candidate variant-gene pairs in 115 probands, including 106 heterozygous candidates for autosomal dominant inheritance, 238 compound heterozygous candidates and 113 homozygous candidates for autosomal recessive inheritance, and 11 candidates for X-linked recessive inheritance (Figure 4D).

Among these, we identified a diagnostic variant in PPP1R12A (ENST00000450142.7:c.737A>C) in an unsolved female proband presenting with semilobar holoprosencephaly, seizures, hearing loss, blindness, and scoliosis. The variant is annotated as missense and has an ambiguous prediction from AlphaMissense (score = 0.461). One report that classifies the variant as likely pathogenic exists in ClinVar (RCV005250607.1) but without associated condition or assessment criteria. PPP1R12A frameshift and nonsense variants have previously been shown to cause a new syndrome associating brain malformations (in XX and a subset of XY individuals) with urogenital features resulting from impaired testicular development (in XY), termed GUBS (OMIM #618820) 37 . The proband's presentation was consistent with the brain malformation features of this syndrome, but because she is an XX female, the absence of genital phenotype was not informative and a diagnosis of GUBS was not suspected prior to molecular testing. The research team had identified the PPP1R12A variant as a candidate, but several arguments had prevented reporting into the GREGoR Genetics Findings table. All variants reported in Hughes et al. were clear stop-gains and de novo in affected individuals. Here, the variant is annotated as missense, and examination of LRS reads showed that it did not arise on a maternal haplotype and the father's sample was not available for comparison (Figure S7A). Watershed-DL prioritized this variant because it was associated with a strong underexpression outlier (z = -3.99), in spite of modest SpliceAI scores for donor loss (Δ = 0.11) and acceptor loss (Δ = 0.06). Consistent with a splicing effect, FRASER 2.0 identified a strong splicing outlier (p < 0.001), and RNA-seq showed skipping of exon 5 in the proband resulting in a frameshift (Figure S7B). The same exon-skipping junction was detected at low levels (~2% of reads) in the proband's mother, who does not carry the variant, suggesting that the variant increases usage of a naturally occurring minor isoform rather than creating a novel splice junction. Together, these findings indicate that c.737A>C acts as a missense spliceogenic variant, a class of missense variants that cause disease by disrupting RNA splicing rather than by altering the protein sequence 38 . By integrating S2O predictions with observed molecular outliers, Watershed-DL revealed a loss-of-function mechanism not captured by the variant's existing classification, and the variant was confirmed as diagnostic for the proband.

As Watershed-DL is a variant-centric approach that does not rely on prior gene-phenotype relationships, it can also prioritize variants in candidate novel disease genes. In another unsolved proband, we identified a homozygous 5' UTR variant in ATP5PF (ENST00000284971.8:c.-9A>G) that is associated with an underexpression outlier (z = -4.60) and predicted by SpliceAI to cause donor loss (Δ = 0.59). The variant lies within a ~10.8 Mb region of homozygosity in the proband (chr21 q21.1-21.3) identified by CMA. In this region the parents had an IBS0 count of 12 out of 24,450 sites (0.05%), but were not related genome-wide (Sommalier relatedness 0.04), suggesting a shared haplotype on this region from a common ancestor. Both parents are heterozygous carriers of the prioritized variant, consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance and homozygosity by descent. The variant has no homozygous carriers in gnomAD (v4.1) or All of Us, and ATP5PF carries no homozygous loss-of-function variants in gnomAD. The proband presents with spastic quadriplegia, multifocal epilepsy, global developmental delay, dysmorphic features, feeding difficulties, and vision loss. Although ATP5PF has no established disease association, biallelic variants in ATP5ME, which encodes another subunit of mitochondrial complex V, have been associated with complex V deficiency and a broad range of clinical manifestations, including spasticity, seizures, encephalopathy, feeding difficulties, contractures, hearing loss and optic atrophy 39 . Similarly, pathogenic variants in other structural subunits of complex V have been linked to autosomal recessive disorders characterized by neurological and neuromuscular dysfunction, encephalopathy, and metabolic abnormalities, with variable disease severity and systemic involvement [40] [41] [42] [43] [44] [45] . Discussion

Rare variant prioritization remains a challenging problem with relevant clinical applications in rare disease diagnosis and personalized medicine. Existing prioritization methods typically rely on either molecular outlier signals or sequence-based predictions without integrating these complementary sources of evidence. Here, we present Watershed-DL, an extensible framework that jointly models S2O predictions and personal multi-omic outliers to prioritize rare variants that alter diverse mechanisms such as gene regulation, splicing, and protein function.

Watershed-DL improved prioritization of rare variants underlying expression, splicing, and chromatin accessibility outliers relative to both a genome annotation model with S2O predictions, and an outlier-based model without S2O predictions. These gains were greatest for non-coding and non-canonical splice-site variants, for which S2O predictions can provide evidence that is otherwise limited in conventional annotation frameworks. As S2O models continue to improve and multi-omic profiling expands to larger and more diverse cohorts, Watershed-DL offers a scalable framework for interpreting rare variant effects with the flexibility to incorporate improved S2O predictors and new molecular phenotypes as they become available.

Watershed-DL provides interpretable weights which give insight into specific molecular effects of prioritized rare variants and the overall utility of variant annotations to each outlier signal. S2O predictors from matched molecular phenotypes received the largest weights for expression, splicing, and chromatin accessibility, indicating that the model learns biologically meaningful relationships between predicted and observed molecular effects. The contribution of S2O predictions was less pronounced for ASE, where a matched S2O model was not available. In contrast, S2O predictions provided substantial additional evidence for caOutliers, with peak-level modeling extending variant prioritization to distal and intergenic regulatory regions that are often excluded from gene-centric analyses. Together, these results demonstrate that S2O predictions are most informative when they provide variant-level evidence that complements the molecular phenotype observed in an individual, particularly where conventional annotations provide limited evidence of variant function.

Integrating multi-omic outlier signals with S2O annotations enabled the interpretation of rare variants in both self-reported healthy individuals and patients with rare genetic diseases. In the 1KG cohort, Watershed-DL prioritized functional variation in known recessive disease genes, including ancestry-specific alleles absent from current clinical databases. These variants may represent novel recessive disease alleles, disease modifiers, or risk alleles whose clinical significance has yet to be established. In the GREGoR cohort, Watershed-DL prioritized known diagnostic variants in solved cases, including all diagnostic variants associated with eOutliers in whole blood. When combined with evaluation of allele frequency, inheritance patterns and patient phenotypes, it also identified a diagnostic variant in PPP1R12A and nominated a candidate gene, ATP5PF, not previously associated with disease. Since prioritization does not require genes to have an established disease association, Watershed-DL can also provide functional evidence in novel disease genes harboring rare variants that meet diagnostic criteria. This is particularly relevant for variants outside canonical coding and splice regions, which remain underrepresented in clinical databases and are often overlooked by current diagnostic pipelines.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting Watershed-DL prioritized variants.

Posterior probabilities reflect the combined strength of evidence that a rare variant affects a molecular phenotype, but do not establish that the variant causes the observed effect. A prioritized variant may co-occur with an outlier driven by another variant, an environmental factor, or technical variation, so establishing causality requires experimental validation. In the 1KG cohort, candidate recessive alleles were identified in heterozygous carriers, and the observed molecular effects may not reflect biallelic disruption. In particular, the extent to which partial changes in expression cause disease may be gene-or allele-specific, requiring observation of these variants in affected individuals, either in a homozygous state or in trans with a second pathogenic allele. In addition, Watershed-DL does not model relationships between variants and individual traits, and prioritized variants may occur in genes that are not relevant to a patient's phenotypes. Candidate diagnostic variants identified in GREGoR remain variants of uncertain significance until supported by additional evidence consistent with current RNA-seq diagnostic guidelines 32 , and may require additional functional or genetic testing specific to the disease or inheritance mode. More broadly, Watershed-DL can only detect effects on genes and regulatory elements that are active in the assayed tissue. Structural variants are also not modeled in the current framework, although approaches for integrating molecular outliers with structural variation have been described 25.

Extending Watershed-DL across diverse tissues and cohorts, together with continued improvements in S2O models, will broaden its ability to connect predicted and observed molecular effects to rare variant function. As multi-omic profiling becomes increasingly integrated into clinical genetic testing, Watershed-DL provides a foundation for the systematic variant interpretation needed to enable rare disease diagnosis and delivery of precision medicine.

Online Methods

RNA-seq data

RNA-seq data from AFGR 28 and MAGE 29 were combined to form the 1KG cohort. Lymphoblastoid cell line RNA-seq data for 599 samples from the AFGR cohort were obtained as previously described 28 . Lymphoblastoid cell line RNA-seq data for 731 samples from the MAGE cohort were downloaded from the Sequence Read Archive at accession PRJNA851328 29 . 166 samples from the MKK population of AFGR were excluded due to insufficient rare variant coverage in the VCF, leaving a total of 1,164 RNA-seq samples from 1,030 individuals. Reads were trimmed using Cutadapt 48 and aligned to GRCh38 using STAR (v2.7.11b) in two-pass mode with the --waspOutputMode flag. The GENCODE v44 reference annotation was used for alignment. Unless otherwise stated, all analyses were restricted to protein-coding genes.

Whole-blood RNA-seq data from 1,041 participants in the GREGoR Consortium (release U12, accession phs003047.v5.p4) were generated at five sites: University of California, Irvine (UCI; n = 534), Stanford University (n = 273), Illumina (n = 93), the Broad Institute (n = 92) and Baylor College of Medicine (n = 49). Participants came from 489 families and included 459 probands. In total, 499 participants were affected (459 probands and 40 family members), 499 were unaffected family members, 24 were possibly affected family members, and 19 were family members with unknown affected status. Library preparation protocols differed between sites, but all libraries were sequenced paired-end. Samples with <75% uniquely mapped reads were excluded.

RNA-seq alignment files, provided in BAM or CRAM format, were converted to FASTQ using SAMtools 49 . CRAM files were decoded using the GRCh38 full analysis set plus decoy and HLA sequences 50, 51 . Adapters were trimmed with Cutadapt 48 and read quality was assessed with FastQC 52 before and after trimming. Reads were then shuffled with SeqKit 53 for STAR 54 compatibility and realigned to a shared human reference (Ensembl v47 55 and GRCh38 primary assembly 50, 51 ). Samples with <75% uniquely mapped reads were excluded. Gene-level read counts were obtained for each sample with featureCounts from exon features summarised to genes, counting read pairs from primary alignments only. TPM values were computed from these counts using each gene's union exon length.

ATAC-seq data ATAC-seq data for 100 samples from the AFGR cohort were obtained as previously described 28.

ATAC-seq data for 100 samples from the Great British population were downloaded from the European Nucleotide Archive (ENA) under accession ERP110508 30 . Joint peak calling was done by downsampling the FASTQs to 3 million reads per sample and pooling as previously described 56 . Three pseudoreplicates of pooled FASTQs were generated and MACS2 peak calling was done using the ENCODE DCC ATAC-seq pipeline available at https://github.com/encode-dcc/atac-seq-pipeline. Genotype quality control in the 1000 Genomes cohort 30X short-read whole genome sequencing for samples in the 1KG were obtained from the International Genome Sample Resource (ISGR) 57 . Genotype quality control was performed using bcftools (v1.21) 58 . Multi-allelic sites were first split using bcftools norm -m -any. Genotypes with read depth < 10 or genotype quality < 20 were excluded. On the autosomes, variants with missingness > 0.1 or a Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium (HWE) P < 1 × 10⁻⁶ in any super-population were excluded. On the X chromosome, variants with missingness > 0.1 in either XX or XY samples, or with HWE P < 1 × 10⁻⁶ among XX individuals in any super-population were excluded. The pseudoautosomal regions (PAR) were excluded from the X chromosome. Finally, non-variable sites (minor allele count = 0) were removed from all chromosomes. 166 samples from the AFGR Maasai (MKK) subpopulation were excluded from the VCF due to high site missingness.

Genetic principal components in the 1000 Genomes cohort

Genetic principal components were calculated in the 1KG cohort using PLINK (v2.0) 59 . Regions with high linkage disequilibrium in hg38 were extracted from plinkQC 60 and excluded. For principal component estimation, biallelic autosomal SNVs with minor allele frequency > 5% were retained, and LD pruning was done using -indep-pairwise with a window size of 1Mb, step size of 50, and r 2 threshold of 0.05.

Multi-omics outlier calling

Gene expression outlier calling in the 1000 Genomes cohort Gene expression was quantified as transcripts per million (TPM) using RNASeQC (v2.4.3) 61 . TPM values were log2-transformed (log2(TPM + 2)) and then standardized for each gene to a mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 1. Lowly expressed genes, defined as those with TPM < 0.1 in at least 20% of samples, were excluded. To ensure that the leading principal components (PCs) were not driven by a few samples, we removed samples with highly divergent expression profiles using a sample-network approach implemented in the WGCNA R package 62 . Pairwise Pearson correlations between all samples were computed across genes and transformed into a signed adjacency matrix (adjacency = 0.5 + 0.5 x correlation). The connectivity of each sample, defined as the sum of its adjacencies to all other samples, was calculated and standardized to a z-score across samples. Samples with a standardized connectivity below -3 were considered global outliers and removed.

Hidden factors were then estimated using the PCAtools (v2.18) R package 63 on the filtered expression matrix, with genes centered and scaled. Missing values were imputed using k-nearest neighbors (impute R package 64 ) prior to PCA. A total of 45 expression PCs were retained as selected by the Marchenko-Pastur method. To obtain expression residuals, these expression PCs, the top 5 genotype principal components, batch, RNA Integrity Number (RIN), population, sex, and cohort were removed based on a linear regression model. Expression residuals were then re-scaled to obtain corrected z-scores for each individual per gene.

Gene expression outlier calling in the GREGoR cross-consortium cohort

Expression outliers were called on the pooled matrix of 1,041 RNA-seq samples. Lowly expressed protein-coding genes, defined as those with TPM < 1 in more than 20% of samples, were excluded, as were genes with transcription start sites within the major histocompatibility complex (MHC; chr6:28,477,797-33,448,354). TPM values were then log2-transformed (log2(TPM + 2)) and standardized for each gene to a mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 1. Hidden factors were estimated as described for the 1KG data, with the number of components set using the Gavish-Donoho optimal hard threshold for unknown noise. No explicit covariates were included, and the leading principal component explained 50.4% of the variance.

Allele-specific expression outlier calling in the 1000 Genomes cohort Heterozygous sites for each sample were extracted from the VCF using bcftools (v1.21) 58 . Haplotype-level ASE data were generated using phASER (v1.1.1) 65 , with a minimum mapping quality of 255 (uniquely mapped reads in STAR) and a minimum base quality of 10. Indels were excluded, and phASER blacklists were applied to remove sites and haplotypes prone to mapping artifacts. Haplotypes were phased genome-wide using the most common haplotype phase (--gw_phase_method 0), based on the phase information in the VCF.

To obtain gene-level ASE, GENCODE v44 annotations were collapsed so that each gene was represented by a single gene model of merged exons across its transcripts. Haplotypic counts were then aggregated per gene using phASER Gene AE, which sums read counts supporting each haplotype across the phased heterozygous variants within the gene's exons and reports the log2 allelic fold change (aFC) between the two haplotypes. Genes with missing values in at least 80% of samples were removed, and the remaining log2 aFCs were scaled for each gene to a mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 1. As described for gene expression outlier calling, samples with highly divergent ASE profiles were excluded prior to PCA, missing values were imputed, and hidden factors were estimated, retaining 65 ASE PCs selected by the Marchenko-Pastur method. ASE residuals were obtained by residualizing these PCs and the same covariates used for expression, then re-scaling for each gene to obtain corrected z-scores.

Splicing outlier calling in the 1000 Genomes cohort Splicing outliers were called using FRASER 2.0 22 , which models aberrant splicing using an intron Jaccard index. FRASER 2.0 was run using the FRASER R/Bioconductor package (v2.4.5) within the DROP pipeline (v1.4.0) 66 , with GENCODE v44 to annotate introns. Introns were retained with ≥ 20 split reads in one sample and ≥ 10 reads in 75%, matching DROP defaults. Low variance introns were removed using a minimum deltaPsi threshold of 0.3, which requires at least one sample to differ from the intron's mean Jaccard index by greater than 0.3. The default HGNC IDs were replaced with Ensembl IDs during the annotation step. The default DROP pipeline minimum read count and expression settings were used, filtering to a ≥ 20 split reads in one sample and ≥ 10 reads in 75% of samples. Sample covariation was removed with a PCA-based denoising autoencoder, and the encoding dimension was selected using the optimal hard threshold procedure. For each sample, a gene-level p-value was defined as the minimum Holm-adjusted p-value across all introns in that gene. Finally, splicing outliers (p < 0.0027) with a delta-Jaccard index below 0.3 were excluded to ensure significant outliers reflect large splicing changes.

Splicing outlier calling in the GREGoR cohort

Splicing outliers were called using FRASER 2.0 22 (v2.6.0) in the GREGoR UC Irvine RNA-seq cohort using GENCODE v47 basic annotation and the harmonised STAR alignments. 537 UCI whole-blood samples were fitted as a UCI-only cohort. Introns were kept with ≥ 20 split reads in one sample and ≥ 10 reads in 75% of samples, matching DROP pipeline defaults. The encoding dimension was selected with the optimal hard threshold procedure using the PCA implementation.

Chromatin accessibility outlier calling in the 1000 Genome cohort

Chromatin accessibility outliers were called using EpiOut (v0.0.1) https://paperpile.com/c/mSKw54/YizT on the joint peak set from all 200 AFGR and GBR ATAC-seq samples described above. Read counts per peak and sample were generated with EpiCount, with the default merging of nearby peaks disabled. EpiOut was then run on the resulting count table to model accessibility and obtain a p-value, observed and expected read count, and log2 fold change for each peak in each sample. To remove outliers with small effect sizes, the EpiOut effect size and coverage criteria were applied, excluding observations with p < 0.01 that had an absolute log2 fold change below 0.5, or that had both observed and expected read counts below 50. Peak-level p-values were then converted to z-scores, signed by the direction of the log2 fold change. A p-value threshold of p < 0.0027 was used to define chromatin accessibility outliers.

Rare variant filtering

For each cohort, variants were left-aligned and normalized, multiallelic sites were split into biallelic records, and spanning deletions were removed using bcftools (v1.21) 58 . Sites with FILTER = PASS or FILTER = "." were retained. Sites with ≥10% of genotypes missing were removed in the 1KG. In GREGoR, a higher threshold of ≥90% missing genotypes was used to avoid removing rare variants, as the cross-consortium U15 release VCF was merged from separately joint-called VCFs across 89 and 588 samples respectively. Variants were then restricted to those with a cohort-level allele frequency ≤5%, and insertions and deletions longer than 30 bp were removed. Finally, variants with allele frequency >1% in any of the AFR, AMR, ASJ, EAS, SAS or NFE populations of gnomAD (v4.1) 68 were removed. Variants absent from gnomAD were retained.

Rare variant annotation VEP annotations

To annotate rare variant consequences, VEP annotations were generated by running Ensembl VEP (v110.1, GRCh38, Gencode v44) 69 , using a window size of 10kb to define upstream and downstream consequences. Loss-of-function annotations were generated using the LOFTEE (GRCh38) plugin for VEP 1 . The UTRAnnotator VEP plugin 70 was used to annotate 5' UTR consequences such as premature start codon gain or loss. Annotated consequences were retained for transcripts with transcript support level 1 or 2, Ensembl canonical transcripts, and regulatory features.

For Watershed-DL, VEP consequences were each represented as categorical variables. For region-level aggregation, a per-column maximum over all rare variants in the region was used. For analyses requiring a single consequence per rare variant-gene pair, each variant was assigned its most severe VEP consequence according to the following hierarchy: coding [frameshift > stop > start > missense > other coding], splice [splice donor > splice acceptor > splice region > other splice], non-coding [5′ UTR > 3′ UTR > regulatory > intron > upstream > downstream > intergenic > other non-coding]. For the chromatin accessibility model, variants were assigned their most severe non-coding consequence only.

Polyadenylation site annotation

Polyadenylation sites were annotated using PolyA-DB v3.2 71, 72 after lifting over from hg19 to hg38 using the liftOver function from the rtracklayer R package 73 . Variant proximity to polyadenylation sites was represented as a binary annotation indicating whether the variant is within 50bp of an annotated polyadenylation site. The absolute distance to the nearest polyadenylation site was also included as an annotation. PolyA-DB genes were matched to the corresponding VEP gene annotation and outlier gene.

Enhancer-to-gene and CTCF loop annotations

To link distal regulatory regions to genes, enhancer-gene links and promoter-anchored CTCF loops were compiled from ENCODE-rE2G predictions and ChIA-PET data, respectively, in the lymphoblastoid cell line GM12878. ENCODE-rE2G predictions (accession ENCFF128MZW) and CTCF ChIA-PET loops (accession ENCFF780PGS) were obtained from the ENCODE portal. Each CTCF loop was split into its two anchors, and anchors overlapping an EPDnew v6 promoter (hg38; https://epd.expasy.org/epd/) were assigned to the corresponding gene. Variants were annotated with E2G or CTCF links for a given gene if they fell within an enhancer or opposite loop anchor linked to that gene.

ClinVar annotations

ClinVar classifications were obtained from the variant summary file (variant_summary.txt.gz) downloaded from the NCBI FTP site (https://ftp.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pub/clinvar/tab_delimited/) on September 6, 2026, and restricted to GRCh38 coordinates. Classifications were harmonized by merging subcategories into their parent terms (e.g., low-penetrance and VUS subcategories). Variants with multiple classifications for the same gene and phenotype were collapsed to a single classification according to the following priority: pathogenic > benign > likely pathogenic > likely benign > uncertain significance > conflicting classifications > not provided. Molecular consequences for each variant were obtained from the MC field of the ClinVar GRCh38 VCF (release 2026-09-05; https://ftp.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pub/clinvar/vcf_GRCh38/). For gene-level outliers, ClinVar classifications were assigned to prioritized variants only if the ClinVar gene matched the outlier gene.

Sequence-to-omics predictions Predictions of chromatin accessibility

Chromatin accessibility predictions were annotated for all SNVs and short indels (≤ 30bp) using ChromBPNet 7 . Pre-trained, bias-corrected ChromBPNet models trained on ATAC-seq samples from the AFGR cohort 28 were used, including 30 models (6 population-specific training sets, 5 folds each). Variants were restricted to those falling exactly within joint-called ATAC peaks. For each variant and model, the predicted effect on accessibility was calculated as a log-fold allele change between reference and alternate alleles. The average absolute log fold change across all 30 models was used as the final annotation.

Predictions of gene expression

RNA-seq read coverage in LCLs was predicted for rare SNVs and short indels (≤ 30bp) in the 1KG using Flashzoi 27 , an optimized implementation of Borzoi https://paperpile.com/c/mSKw54/g1eR , referred to here as Flashzoi-RNA. A single replicate of the pre-trained Flashzoi model, trained only on human data, was downloaded from Huggingface (https://huggingface.co/collections/johahi/flashzoi-models). Predictions were made using the full 524kb input sequence, with predicted RNA-seq read coverage output at 32 base-pair resolution. Scores were averaged across the GM12878 lymphoblastoid cell line RNA-seq tracks from ENCODE (ENCFF463TDW, ENCFF279MVV).

For the Watershed-DL transcriptomic model, all SNVs within 60kb of GENCODE v44 protein-coding genes were scored. The input window was centered at the gene's transcription start site, and coverage was summed across the gene's exons. Variant effects were quantified as the log-fold change in summed predicted coverage between reference and alternate alleles (logSED). For the chromatin accessibility model, all SNVs within ATAC-seq peaks were scored, with the 524kb input sequence centered on the variant. Variant effects were quantified as the L2 norm of the log difference in predicted coverage between reference and alternate alleles across the binned output sequence.

Prediction of promoter usage

Promoter site usage predictions were annotated with PromoterAI https://paperpile.com/c/mSKw54/zShn , which predicts CAGE-seq based on sequence. Pre-computed scores for all SNVs within promoters (+-100bp of TSS) of protein-coding genes annotated in Gencode v39 were downloaded from GitHub (https://github.com/Illumina/PromoterAI). To capture rare variant effects on expression, the PromoterAI model fine-tuned on outlier-associated rare variants in GTEx was used. Scores represent the difference in predicted CAGE-seq reads between reference and alternate alleles averaged across the output window, which includes 4096 nucleotides centered at the variant. Scores range from -1 to 1, with negative values indicating predicted decreases in promoter activity and positive values indicating predicted increases.

Predictions of splice site usage

Predicted splicing effects were annotated using SpliceAI 8 and Pangolin 9 . Pre-computed SpliceAI scores for SNVs within canonical GENCODE v24 transcripts, generated using a ± 50bp window around each variant, were downloaded from Illumina BaseSpace (https://basespace.illumina.com/s/otSPW8hnhaZR). Short indels (<30bp) in the 1KG cohort were scored separately with a pretrained SpliceAI model using the VEP SpliceAI plugin, using the distributed GENCODE v24 configuration and a ± 50bp scoring window around each variant. For each variant, delta scores for acceptor gain, acceptor loss, donor gain, and donor loss were annotated, defined as the change in predicted acceptor or donor probability between reference and alternate alleles. Each variant was assigned the SpliceAI score for the gene matching its outlier signal by HGNC ID.

Pre-trained Pangolin models were obtained from GitHub (https://github.com/tkzeng/Pangolin), which includes three models per tissue for heart, liver, brain, and testis. All SNVs and short indels (≤ 30bp) within GENCODE v44 gene bodies were scored using a 500bp scoring window around each variant. Splice site gain and loss scores were annotated as the largest gain and loss within the window across all tissues.

Predictions of transcript polyadenylation

Predicted effects on polyadenylation were annotated using APARENT2 10 . APARENT2 predicts the relative usage of a polyadenylation site, quantified by the proportion of RNA-seq transcripts cleaved at that site. Pre-computed scores for all SNVs near polyadenylation sites annotated in PolyADB-v3 71 were obtained from CADD (v1.7) 74 . Variant scores represent the log-odds ratio of predicted polyadenylation site usage between reference and alternate allele. Odds ratios were included as the final annotation.

Predictions of missense variant pathogenicity

Missense variants were annotated for predicted pathogenicity using AlphaMissense 13 , ESM-1v https://paperpile.com/c/mSKw54/ULAk , and PrimateAI https://paperpile.com/c/mSKw54/7VVI . AlphaMissense scores were annotated using the VEP plugin 69 , which uses pre-computed scores for all possible missense SNVs based on canonical protein-coding transcripts in GENCODE v32. AlphaMissense was included as a binary annotation representing whether a variant is predicted as likely pathogenic, defined by the standard threshold of predicted pathogenicity ≥ 0.564. PrimateAI (v0.2) scores were annotated using the VEP plugin and included as raw predicted scores, which range from 0 to 1 with higher scores indicating greater predicted pathogenicity. ESM-1v scores for all possible SNVs were obtained from CADD (v1.7) and included as raw scores. ESM-1v scores represent the log-likelihood ratio of predicted deleteriousness for reference and alternate amino acids, with more negative scores indicating greater predicted deleteriousness.

Watershed-DL model training and validation in the 1KG

Assigning variants to genes and peaks

To match variant annotations to gene-level and peak-level outlier signals, variants were first assigned to genes using GENCODE (v44) annotations or to peaks using the joint peak calls. GENCODE (v44) genes were filtered to protein-coding genes.

For gene-level outlier signals (expression, splicing, ASE), variants were assigned to a gene if they fell within a 10kb window around the gene body as defined by GENCODE (v44). VEP consequences were filtered to match the annotated GENCODE gene. For S2O predictions linked to a specific gene, the prediction for the assigned gene was used where available, and otherwise predictions were averaged across genes. For model training and evaluation, each annotation was summarized across all variants assigned to a given individual-gene pair, using the minimum or maximum so that the most severe annotation was retained (Table S1,S2). After training, Watershed-DL was applied to predict variant-level posterior probabilities, using each individual's observed outlier status for the assigned gene.

For chromatin accessibility outliers, variants were assigned to a peak if they overlapped the peak region. Similar to the gene-level model, each annotation was summarized across all variants within a peak for each individual during training and evaluation, using the minimum or maximum value (Table S2). After training, Watershed-DL was applied to predict variant-level posterior probabilities, using each individual's observed outlier status for the assigned peak.

Multi-omic outlier preprocessing

Prior to training Watershed-DL, AFGR samples from the Maasai population (MKK) were excluded as described above (17 samples from ATAC-seq and 166 from RNA-seq). RNA-seq and ATAC-seq samples not in the sr-WGS were also excluded, removing 1 sample from the RNA-seq and 9 samples from the ATAC-seq. Since 134 samples from AFGR and MAGE corresponded to the same donors, z-scores from replicate samples were averaged for each transcriptomic outlier signal. Across gene-level and peak-level outliers, a standard p-value threshold of p < 0.0027 (|z| ≥ 3) was used to define outliers. Expression and ASE p-values were signed by direction of effect. Splicing and chromatin accessibility p-values were unsigned. The Watershed-DL model thresholds unsigned p-values into inlier (p > 0.0027) and outlier (p < 0.0027) categories, and thresholds signed p-values into underlier (p < 0, |p| < 0.0027), overlier (p > 0, |p| < 0.0027), and inlier (|p| > 0.0027) categories, with independent model weights defining impact on functional status for each category. For each outlier signal, global outlier individuals were removed, defined as individuals carrying a higher outlier burden as defined by the mean plus a standard deviation threshold (σ ≥1.5 for expression and chromatin, σ ≥2 for ASE and splicing, Table S4). After filtering, outlier p-values were joined to the gene-level or peak-level aggregated features by individual and region.

Training the Watershed-DL model

The Watershed-DL model was trained with exact inference using the distributed R package (https://github.com/nicolerg/WatershedR). Models were trained on region-level summarized annotations, which reduces noise during training caused by individuals carrying multiple rare variants with mixed functional status in a given region. N2-pairs, defined as two individuals with the same set of variants in a given region (gene or peak), were excluded from training and held out for evaluation. For regions where three or more individuals carry identical rare variants, annotations and outlier signals were averaged across individuals prior to training. The default Dirichlet prior (C = 10) and a standardized L2 penalty (λ = 0.001) were used for all models and outlier signals. Models were trained until convergence, defined as a change of less than 1 x 10 -8 between iterations. To assess the impact of S2O annotations and multi-omic outliers in Watershed-DL, two baseline models were also trained: a Watershed model without S2O predictions, and a genome annotation model without outlier signals.

Validation on variant-matched pairs

Following previous Watershed work 15, [23] [24] [25] , performance was evaluated on held-out N2 pairs. For each pair, posterior probabilities were computed for one individual using their variant annotations and outlier status, and this posterior was used to predict the outlier status of the second individual. This approach provides an unbiased proxy of model performance when true functional status is unobserved. Performance was summarized as the area under the precision-recall curve across posterior thresholds.

Imputation at the variant level

After training, posterior probabilities were computed across all individuals and variants within each cohort, resulting in 1 prediction per individual, gene, and variant. In the 1KG, variants were considered prioritized if they had PP ≥ 0.6, following prior work 25 , and were carried by at least one individual with an outlier in the assigned gene or peak (|p| < 0.0027).

Identification of candidate recessive disease alleles in the 1KG

To identify candidate recessive disease alleles, prioritized variants were restricted to recessive disease genes curated from ClinGen, OMIM, and ACMG. ClinGen genes were defined as those with a haploinsufficiency score of 30 (gene associated with autosomal recessive phenotype) in the ClinGen Dosage Sensitivity curation list (accessed September 9, 2026) 75 . OMIM genes were defined as those with at least one phenotype annotated with autosomal recessive inheritance in the OMIM genemap2 file (accessed September 2, 2023) 76 . ACMG genes were defined as those with autosomal recessive inheritance in the ACMG Secondary Findings list (v3.3) 77.

Within these genes, prioritized variants were compared with pathogenic or likely pathogenic (P/LP) variants in ClinVar. Variants already classified as P/LP or as benign or likely benign in ClinVar were excluded, as were variants observed in a homozygous state in gnomAD (v4.1). Variants absent from gnomAD were retained. For each remaining variant, the direction of effect was defined as the sign of its mean outlier z-score across carriers. Each variant was then matched to ClinVar P/LP variants using three criteria: (1) concordant outlier match, in which a P/LP variant in the same gene was also prioritized in 1KG and its carriers showed outliers in the same direction; (2) LoF match, in which the variant was prioritized for an underexpression outlier and the gene contained at least one P/LP nonsense or frameshift variant; and (3) CNV match, in which a P/LP CNV overlapped the gene, with copy number gains matched to overexpression outliers and losses matched to underexpression outliers. Variants could satisfy more than one criterion. For analyses requiring a single match type per variant, matches were prioritized as follows: concordant outlier > CNV > LoF. All matches are reported in Table S5.

Watershed-DL model training and variant scoring

For Watershed-DL training and variant scoring in the GREGoR cohort, the GREGoR U15 joint-called VCFs were downloaded from AnVIL (release U15, accession phs003047.v5.p4) 26 . After training, variant-level posterior probabilities were computed over variant-gene pairs in all individuals, excluding global outliers and individuals with no outliers. Prioritization of previously curated variants

To analyze Watershed-DL prioritization of previously curated variants, the GREGoR Genetics Findings table (version U15) was downloaded from AnVIL. Variant-gene pairs scored in probands were matched to curated variants based on the variant position, alternate allele, and Ensembl gene ID.

To assess recovery of diagnostic variants in solved probands, curated findings were restricted to probands marked as Solved. For probands with findings in more than one gene, only genes with the strongest phenotype contribution were retained, ranked as Full > Partial > Uncertain. Multiple genes were retained only for probands with more than one gene marked as Partial and no gene marked Full. For findings annotated as autosomal recessive, probands were required to carry a homozygous or hemizygous variant, or at least two heterozygous variants in the annotated gene.

Inheritance filtering criteria

Prioritized variants were evaluated for family segregation and phase under several inheritance models: autosomal dominant, autosomal recessive (homozygous and compound heterozygous), and X-linked recessive (homozygous, hemizygous, and compound heterozygous) (Table S6). Genotypes for probands and sequenced relatives were extracted from the GREGoR joint-called VCF (version U15) using bcftools (v1.21) 58 , and the sex and affected status of relatives were obtained from the GREGoR Participant table (version U14). Population-level allele frequencies for all ancestry groups, homozygote counts, and hemizygote counts were extracted from gnomAD (v4.1). Population-level allele frequency and homozygote count was downloaded from the All of Us (AoU) public data browser. Variants absent from either resource were assigned an allele count of 0. Under the autosomal dominant model, variants were required to have an allele frequency ≤ 1 x 10 -6 in both gnomAD and AoU. Under recessive models, variants were required to have no homozygotes in gnomAD or AoU, and, for X-linked inheritance, allele frequency ≤ 1 x 10 -6 among males in gnomAD. Variant segregation was assessed based on the affected status, genotype, and sex of each sequenced relative. Relatives with unknown affected status were excluded from filtering criteria. For compound heterozygous variants, phase was considered confirmed when each parent carried exactly one of the two variants. Variants with no testable relatives were retained if they passed population-level allele frequency and count criteria, but were labeled as lacking family genotypes.
