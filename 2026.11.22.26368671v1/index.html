This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. Abstract

Antigenic evolution allows pathogens to escape pre-existing host immunity, but hosts differ in their immune histories because of population structure, demographic processes, and stochastic variation. Whether such immune heterogeneity promotes pathogen antigenic diversification remains poorly understood. We investigated how different sources of immune heterogeneity affect antigenic evolution. We developed a status-based multi-strain transmission model in which novel strains emerge stochastically within a tree-structured antigenic space. We examined immune heterogeneity arising from mixing between host subpopulations, aging, and stochastic variation in immune profiles, and evaluated its effects on strain coexistence and phylogenetic diversity. Limited mixing generated immune differences between host subpopulations, but increased phylogenetic diversity only under strong population separation. Aging alone had little effect on evolutionary dynamics but enhanced the effect of limited mixing on phylogenetic diversity. Stochastic immune variation promoted strain replacement rather than coexistence, primarily through variation in the overall susceptible population available to newly emerging strains. In general, immune heterogeneity alone does not necessarily promote antigenic diversification. Its effects depend on how immune differences are generated and maintained within the host population. More broadly, our findings highlight the importance of understanding how assumptions linking epidemiological and evolutionary processes shape the emergent dynamics of evolutionary-epidemiological models.

Introduction

Antigenic evolution allows many pathogens to escape pre-existing host immunity and persist in host populations. Rapid antigenic change has been observed in pathogens including respiratory syncytial virus [1], SARS-CoV-2 [2], and seasonal influenza [3, 4]. Because host immunity determines which strains can spread successfully, heterogeneity in immunity across a host population may alter the selective pressures acting on pathogen strains. In particular, if subpopulations of hosts differ in their susceptibility to antigenically distinct strains, these subpopulations may provide distinct immunological niches in which different strains can circulate. Such strain-specific immune heterogeneity could therefore affect strain competition and, over time, antigenic evolution.

Several mechanisms can generate heterogeneity in immune histories. When mixing between host subpopulations is limited because of geographical or social separation, different strains may circulate within different subpopulations. Their epidemic and immune histories can consequently diverge, potentially reinforcing differences in susceptibility to subsequently emerging strains. Geographical variation in strain circulation is well documented for seasonal influenza [5, 6] and has also been observed for SARS-CoV-2 [7]. Immune histories may also differ systematically between age groups because exposure to infection and vaccination accumulates with age. For influenza, neutralization assays have shown that immunity and the effects of viral mutations differ between age groups, suggesting that age groups may exert different selection pressures on circulating viruses [8]. Finally, random variation in immune profiles may arise from differences in HLA types [9], vaccination histories, seasonality, immune imprinting [10] [11] [12], and other sources of variation.

Despite these multiple sources of immune heterogeneity, their consequences for long-term antigenic evolution remain poorly understood. Previous modeling studies have investigated how mixing and host heterogeneity, including differences in immunity, affect particular evolutionary processes such as strain emergence [13, 14], coexistence between two competing strains [15], or overall disease persistence [16] [17] [18]. However, it remains unclear whether and under what conditions immune heterogeneity alters pathogen diversification when novel strains continuously arise and compete in a partially immune host population. Understanding this could ultimately help inform targeted interventions in populations that contribute disproportionately to the emergence of novel strains.

We hypothesize that strain-specific differences in immunity can reduce competition between pathogen strains by creating distinct subpopulations of susceptible hosts. Different strains may then preferentially circulate within these host subpopulations and independently generate antigenically novel descendants. Over time, this process could promote strain coexistence and increase phylogenetic diversity at the population level. Importantly, this mechanism may depend on how immune heterogeneity is generated and maintained. Structured differences in immune histories between host subpopulations may have different evolutionary consequences from stochastic variation in immune profiles.

Here, we investigate whether, and under what conditions, immune heterogeneity shapes pathogen antigenic evolution. We developed a parsimonious multi-strain model in which pathogen strains compete for susceptible hosts and interact through partial cross-immunity induced by previous infections. The model builds on a compartmental status-based framework that tracks the proportions of individuals susceptible to different strains [19] [20] [21] [22], which allows immune heterogeneity to be represented while remaining computationally tractable over long evolutionary timescales. We extended this framework by introducing novel strains stochastically within a tree-structured antigenic space. Using this model, we examine three mechanisms that can generate immune heterogeneity, i.e., mixing between host subpopulations, aging, and stochastic variation in immune profiles, and determine how each affects strain coexistence and antigenic diversity. Finally, we examine how assumptions governing the emergence of novel strains influence these evolutionary outcomes.

Methods

Overview

We developed a multi-strain compartmental model to investigate how immune differences between host subpopulations affect antigenic evolution (hereafter, 'evolution'). The model combines deterministic epidemiological dynamics with the stochastic emergence of novel strains in a tree-structured antigenic space. The host population is divided into multiple subpopulations connected through a contact matrix, with pathogen strains interacting through cross-immunity generated by previous infections. We considered three mechanisms that can generate immune differences between subpopulations: mixing, aging, and stochastic immune variation. We quantified the resulting epidemiological differences between subpopulations and their effects on two aspects of antigenic evolution: strain coexistence and phylogenetic diversity.

Evolutionary-epidemiological model

Population structure and transmission

We used a status-based multi-strain model [12, 23, 24], previously extended to multiple host subpopulations [22].

The model describes the circulation of N pathogen strains in a host population divided into M subpopulations. Unless otherwise specified, subpopulations are assumed to be of equal and constant size.

Susceptibility and infection status are tracked separately for each strain, while immunity is represented implicitly through the corresponding reduction in S a i (t) due to infection. Cross-immunity and antigenic distance

We defined K ab as the probability that exposure to strain b confers immunity to strain a, with0 ≤ K ab ≤ 1. We assumed complete homologous immunity, such that K aa = 1, and cross-immunity between different strains decreased exponentially with their antigenic distance [10, 12, 23, 24]: where D(a, b) is the antigenic distance between strains a and b, and d determines the breadth of cross-immunity.

Larger values of d correspond to broader cross-immunity, such that protection decays more slowly with antigenic distance. Smaller values correspond to more strain-specific immunity. Accordingly, K ab = 1 represents complete protection, whereas values approaching 0 represent increasingly weak cross-immunity. Strains occupy a tree-structured antigenic space. Each newly emerging strain differs from its parent by one antigenic mutation and therefore has an antigenic distance of one from its parent. The antigenic distance D(a, b) between any two strains is defined as the number of mutations along the unique path connecting them on the phylogenetic tree. Thus, D(a, a) = 0, directly related strains separated by one mutation have D(a, b) = 1, and increasingly antigenically distant strains have progressively larger values of D(a, b). We assumed no recurrent or back mutations. For a novel strain a, its parent p lies on the path to any other strain b, allowing their distance to be computed as D(a, b) = D(p, b) + 1.

Strain emergence and extinction

Novel strains emerged at stochastic time intervals drawn from an exponential distribution with mean te. At each emergence event, a parent strain and the subpopulation in which its descendant emerged were jointly selected from the currently circulating strains and subpopulations, respectively. Each novel strain differed from its parent by one antigenic mutation and was introduced at an initial prevalence of I a i (0) = 10 -6 in the selected subpopulation i. We considered two mechanisms linking strain emergence to the epidemiological dynamics. In the uniform emergence mechanism, used for the main analyses, the parent strain and subpopulation were jointly selected uniformly at random. Under this mechanism, the opportunity to generate an antigenically novel descendant is independent of the current prevalence of a strain and of its prevalence within a particular subpopulation. Uniform emergence therefore separates the generation of antigenic novelty from the epidemiological success of circulating strains.

We additionally considered a prevalence-weighted emergence mechanism, in which the probability that a novel strain descended from strain a in subpopulation i was proportional to its current prevalence, I a i (t). Under this mechanism, strains causing more infections have proportionally more opportunities to generate antigenically novel descendants. Prevalence-weighted emergence therefore couples the generation of antigenic novelty to the epidemiological abundance of circulating strains. Comparing the two mechanisms allowed us to determine whether the effects of immune differences on evolutionary dynamics depended on this coupling between epidemiological success and the generation of antigenic novelty.

When a novel strain a emerged, part of the host population was already immune through previous exposure to antigenically related strains. However, the proportion of individuals susceptible to this strain in subpopulation i, S a i (t), had not been tracked. Calculating S a i (t) exactly would require reconstructing the effects of all previous infections, which would be computationally demanding. A derivation of this approximation is provided in Supplementary Section (b). While novel strains emerged, circulating strains could also become extinct. At each strain-emergence event, strains with a prevalence below 10 -7 were considered extinct and removed from the system. Mechanisms generating immune differences between subpopulations

We considered three mechanisms that can generate immune differences between host subpopulations: mixing, aging, and stochastic immune variation. These mechanisms represent distinct ways in which strain-specific susceptibility profiles can become heterogeneous across a host population. Limited mixing allows subpopulations to develop different epidemiological and immune histories through partially separated transmission dynamics. Aging generates systematic differences in accumulated immunity between age groups. Stochastic immune variation introduces random differences in susceptibility to newly emerging strains. We examined each mechanism separately and additionally considered the combined effects of aging and limited mixing.

Mixing

We first considered whether limited mixing could generate immune differences by allowing pathogen strains to circulate differently across otherwise identical host subpopulations. Mixing was controlled through the ratio of between-to withinsubpopulation contact rates, where all within-subpopulation contact rates are assumed to be equal (C ii = C jj ) and all between-subpopulation contact rates are assumed to be equal (C ij = C kl for all i ̸ = j and k ̸ = l).

We therefore examined whether increasing population separation generated differences in strain circulation and immunity and whether these differences were accompanied by changes in strain coexistence and phylogenetic diversity.

Aging

We next considered aging as a mechanism that can generate systematic differences in immunity between host subpopulations. In this scenario, the M subpopulations represent consecutive age groups, with i = 1 denoting the youngest and i = M the oldest age group. Fully susceptible individuals enter the youngest age group and subsequently progress through successive age groups. As a result, older age groups have had more time to accumulate immunity through previous infections, whereas the continuous entry of susceptible individuals into the youngest age group can generate differences in susceptibility between age groups.

Results

Baseline epidemiological and evolutionary dynamics

We first considered the model dynamics in the absence of immune differences between subpopulations. Under homogeneous mixing, newly emerging strains spread rapidly across subpopulations and generated recurrent changes in strain frequencies, accompanied by fluctuations in overall infection prevalence (Figure 1). Over time, strains were replaced or gave rise to co-circulating lineages, resulting in branching of the phylogenetic tree. The balance between strain replacement and phylogenetic branching depended on the breadth of cross-immunity (Supplement Figure 6). Colors identify the same strains across subpopulations/panels.

Limited mixing affects evolution only under strong population separation

We first investigated how mixing between subpopulations affected strain circulation, immune differences, and evolutionary dynamics (Figure 2). Under homogeneous mixing (c = 1), individuals were equally likely to contact individuals within their own or other subpopulations. As mixing decreased, contacts became increasingly concentrated within subpopulations, reducing exposure to infections originating from other subpopulations. Reduced mixing delayed the invasion of strains into other subpopulations and slowed their spread through the overall population. Consequently, rather than generating a single epidemic peak, individual strains produced successive peaks as they spread from one subpopulation to another (Figure 2b). At sufficiently low mixing rates, this delayed spread resulted in differences in the relative prevalence of strains across subpopulations. These differences in strain circulation were accompanied by increasing immune differences between subpopulations, quantified from differences in their strain-specific susceptibility profiles.

Despite these epidemiological and immune differences, evolutionary dynamics changed appreciably only when mixing between subpopulations was extremely limited (Figure 2a, c ≤ 10 -5 ). Under these conditions, strain coexistence and phylogenetic diversity, measured by the average pairwise T MRCA , increased. Thus, limited mixing readily generated differences in strain circulation and immune histories, but substantially altered long-term evolutionary dynamics only under strong population separation.

Aging increases phylogenetic diversity only in combination with limited mixing

We next investigated aging as a mechanism for generating immune differences between subpopulations and thereby altering evolutionary dynamics (Figure 3). Because immune histories accumulate over time, younger age groups had higher susceptibility and consequently experienced more infections than older age groups (Supplement Figure 7). The magnitude of these age-specific differences was strongly influenced by the breadth of cross-immunity. Besides generating differences in overall susceptibility between age groups, aging produced strain-specific immune differences (Figure 3a). However, the strains circulating in different age groups remained similar, as reflected by small between-age-group differences in strain-specific infection patterns. Aging alone therefore had little effect on strain coexistence or phylogenetic diversity.

The effect of aging became more pronounced when combined with limited mixing. Whereas mixing alone altered evolutionary dynamics only at very low mixing rates, the addition of aging increased phylogenetic diversity already at c ≤ 0.01. This effect was particularly apparent in the youngest age group, which experienced higher infection prevalence and greater strain coexistence than older age groups (Figure 3b for c = 10 -4 ). These dynamics suggest that, under limited mixing, the highly susceptible youngest age group may facilitate the establishment of newly emerging strains while also allowing strains that have been replaced in older age groups to persist for longer. Thus, although age-related differences in immunity alone had little effect on evolutionary dynamics, age structure enhanced the diversification observed when transmission between subpopulations was limited.

This effect could arise not only from immune differences between subpopulations, but also because stochastic variation changed the total number of susceptible hosts available to each newly emerging strain. To distinguish these mechanisms, we considered a control scenario in which stochastic variation altered the total susceptibility to newly emerging strains while preserving the relative susceptibility profiles across subpopulations. This scenario therefore retained variation in the overall susceptible population available to different emerging strains without introducing additional immune differences between subpopulations.

The control scenario produced similar reductions in strain coexistence and phylogenetic diversity and a similar increase in lineage fixation. Thus, the shift towards strain replacement was primarily associated with variation in the overall susceptible population available to newly emerging strains, rather than with additional immune differences between subpopulations. This is consistent with emerging strains that encounter larger susceptible populations having a greater opportunity to spread and replace circulating strains (Figure 4b).

Evolutionary dynamics depend on the mechanism of strain emergence

We next investigated whether the evolutionary effects of immune differences depended on how novel strains emerged in the model. In the analyses above, the parent strain and subpopulation in which a novel strain emerged were selected uniformly at random, independently of their current prevalence.

We compared these dynamics with a prevalence-weighted strain-emergence mechanism, in which the probability that a novel strain descended from a particular strain in a particular subpopulation was proportional to its relative prevalence. Coupling strain emergence to prevalence changed the baseline evolutionary dynamics. Closely related strains tended to experience similar susceptibility profiles and spread under similar epidemiological conditions, producing pronounced periodic fluctuations in infection prevalence (Supplement Figure 8b). Phylogenetic diversity was also lower than under uniform strain emergence, with the median value of the average pairwise T MRCA decreasing from approximately 729 δ -1 to 263 δ -1 , and this difference may further increase under longer simulation times. Under uniform strain emergence, even rare strains had the same opportunity as prevalent strains to generate descendants. This increased branching and reduced lineage fixation, as the continual introduction of descendants from different circulating lineages repeatedly disrupted the fixation of prevalent lineages. Despite these differences in baseline evolutionary dynamics, limited mixing, especially in combination with aging, increased phylogenetic diversity under both mechanisms, although the magnitude of the effect was slightly stronger under prevalence-weighted strain emergence (Supplement Figure 9).

Thus, the qualitative relationship between population structure and phylogenetic diversification was robust to the mechanism of strain emergence, but the magnitude of this relationship did depend on the mechanism of strain emergence.

Sensitivity analyses

We assessed the robustness of the model dynamics to the frequency of strain emergence and the maximum number of cocirculating strains. Decreasing the frequency of strain emergence from te = 10 δ -1 to te = 100 δ -1 increased strain fixation and resulted in a less branched phylogeny (Supplement Figure 8c). Thus, the rate at which antigenically novel strains were introduced influenced the overall degree of phylogenetic diversification.

We also varied the maximum number of co-circulating strains, N , to determine whether the imposed limit constrained the evolutionary dynamics (Supplement Figure 10). We selected N = 25 for the main analyses because increasing this limit further had negligible effects on the outcome measures across the parameter ranges considered.

Discussion

We investigated whether immune differences between host subpopulations alter pathogen antigenic evolution. Using a computationally tractable evolutionary-epidemiological model based on a status-based multi-strain framework [19] [20] [21] [22], we considered immune differences generated by mixing, aging, and stochastic immune variation. Overall, our results show that immune heterogeneity does not necessarily promote antigenic diversification. Instead, its evolutionary effects depend on how immune differences are generated and maintained.

Limited mixing generated differences in strain circulation and, consequently, strain-specific immune differences between subpopulations. However, these differences appreciably affected evolutionary dynamics only when mixing was extremely limited (c ≤ 10 -5 ). Relating this threshold directly to empirical populations is difficult because c represents a simplified ratio of between-to within-subpopulation contacts, whereas real populations have heterogeneous and asymmetric contact and mobility patterns [5, 25, 26]. Nevertheless, the very small values of mixing required in our model suggest that population structure alone substantially increases strain coexistence and phylogenetic diversity only when transmission between subpopulations is strongly restricted. This finding is consistent with previous modeling work showing that spatial separation can promote strain coexistence [27] and with experimental evidence that spatial structure can promote strain diversity [28].

Aging generated a different form of immune heterogeneity. Because immune histories accumulated with age, younger age groups remained more susceptible and experienced more infections, particularly when cross-immunity was broad, reproducing epidemiological patterns observed for infections such as respiratory syncytial virus and measles [29, 30]. Under the uniform strain-emergence mechanism, however, aging alone generated relatively little strain-specific differences in immunity and consequently had little effect on strain coexistence or phylogenetic diversity. Its effects became more pronounced when combined with limited mixing, with phylogenetic diversity increasing at mixing levels for which mixing alone had little evolutionary effect. One possible explanation is that the highly susceptible youngest age group provides an epidemiological niche in which newly emerging strains can establish and existing lineages can persist for longer when transmission between age groups is restricted.

This mechanism is unlikely to operate strongly through age structure alone in realistic populations. Empirical contact studies show substantial assortative mixing by age, but also frequent contacts between age groups [25, 26]. In our model, by contrast, aging substantially increased phylogenetic diversity only when transmission between subpopulations was strongly restricted. Limited mixing and age-related differences in immune histories alone are therefore unlikely to explain the age-specific antigenic selection reported for influenza [8]. More generally, however, our results suggest that highly susceptible subpopulations that are partially separated from the rest of the population may provide epidemiological niches that facilitate the establishment and persistence of distinct pathogen lineages.

Stochastic immune variation produced a qualitatively different and initially counterintuitive effect. Rather than promoting strain coexistence and phylogenetic diversity, increasing stochastic variation favored lineage fixation and successive strain replacement. The control scenario helped distinguish the mechanism underlying this result. Stochastic variation generated not only immune differences between subpopulations but also variation among emerging strains in the overall susceptible population available to them. Retaining the latter variation while removing the additional betweensubpopulation immune differences produced similar evolutionary dynamics. This suggests that the shift towards lineage replacement was driven primarily by variation in the overall susceptible population encountered by emerging strains. Strains that encounter a relatively large susceptible population have a greater opportunity to spread, creating asymmetries in epidemiological success that favor replacement rather than coexistence. Mechanistically, this resembles how the strength and breadth of cross-immunity determine whether competing strains coexist or replace one another [31, 32].

Furthermore, assumptions governing the emergence of novel strains influence the evolutionary dynamics in our model. The qualitative effects of mixing, aging, and stochastic immune variation were similar under different emergence mechanisms, but their magnitude differed. When strain emergence was prevalence-weighted, epidemiological success increased the opportunity for a lineage to generate antigenically novel descendants, which may strengthen the evolutionary consequences of differences in transmission and susceptibility between subpopulations.

Taken together, these findings suggest that the evolutionary consequences of immune heterogeneity depend on the type of heterogeneity generated. Differences in overall susceptibility or transient stochastic variation are not sufficient to promote diversification and may instead favor replacement. In contrast, persistent differences in strain-specific susceptibility between partially separated host subpopulations can create distinct epidemiological and immunological environments in which different lineages persist. However, in our model, substantial effects generally required strong population separation or an interaction between population structure and other sources of heterogeneity. This is consistent with previous modeling showing that immune heterogeneity combined with assortative mixing can increase strain emergence, although the magnitude of this effect may be small relative to other determinants of pathogen evolution [14]. The framework could be extended to investigate whether other processes strengthen or weaken these effects, including heterogeneity in infectivity, seasonality, and contact rates [12], waning immunity [33], and immune imprinting or original antigenic sin [10, 24].

Beyond these biological findings, our results highlight several challenges inherent to evolutionary-epidemiological modeling. A first challenge is representing large numbers of antigenically related strains while retaining epidemiologically meaningful immune interactions. To remain computationally tractable, multi-strain models necessarily make simplifying assumptions. The status-based framework used here scales linearly with the number of strains but assumes independence among co-infecting strains and does not represent direct interactions between them [34]. History-based models instead track individual infection histories, but their state space grows rapidly with the number of strains and they therefore commonly require other simplifications, such as assuming that cross-immunity is determined primarily by the most recent infection [35] [36] [37]. Other approaches reduce the dimensionality of immune histories through age structure [38] or restrict antigenic evolution to occur between epidemic seasons [24]. Different modeling frameworks therefore make different trade-offs between epidemiological detail, immune history, evolutionary dynamics, and computational tractability.

A second challenge is representing the emergence of novel strains. Our results were quantitatively sensitive to whether novel strains emerged uniformly across circulating strains and subpopulations or in proportion to their prevalence, as well as to the frequency of strain emergence (see also [39]). These assumptions determine how strongly epidemiological success feeds back into evolutionary opportunity. Uniform emergence gives rare and prevalent lineages similar opportunities to generate descendants, whereas prevalence-weighted emergence favors lineages responsible for more infections. Neither representation captures the complete biological process of mutation and establishment. A natural extension would be to link the generation of novel variants directly to the number of infections while allowing their antigenic effects to vary, thereby distinguishing the generation of mutations from their subsequent epidemiological establishment.

A third challenge is determining which model behaviors can be meaningfully compared with empirical evolutionary dynamics. Summary measures calculated from simulations can depend on the simulation duration, burn-in period, temporal autocorrelation, and stochastic variation within and between simulations. Empirical measures derived from sequencing data, strain prevalence, or immune profiles are subject to different limitations, including incomplete sampling and measurement error. Direct comparisons between simulated and observed evolutionary patterns may therefore depend strongly on the outcome chosen. Developing robust and interpretable measures that can be calculated consistently from both model output and empirical data will be important for evaluating alternative model assumptions and determining which mechanisms reproduce observed pathogen evolutionary dynamics.

Our study has several limitations. The model deliberately represents antigenic evolution in a simplified manner. Each novel strain differs from its parent by a single antigenic step, antigenic distance is defined on a tree without recurrent or back mutations, and cross-immunity is determined solely by this distance. Strain emergence is imposed as a stochastic process rather than arising explicitly from mutation within infected hosts, and extinction is evaluated at strain-emergence events. The model also excludes several processes that may generate or modify immune heterogeneity in real populations, including waning immunity, vaccination, seasonality, variation in transmission rates, and more complex individual immune histories. These simplifications allowed us to isolate the mechanisms considered here, but they limit direct quantitative interpretation for any particular pathogen. The mixing thresholds identified in our simulations should therefore be interpreted as properties of the model and parameter regime rather than universal thresholds at which population structure begins to influence antigenic evolution. In conclusion, immune heterogeneity can shape antigenic evolution, but its presence alone does not necessarily promote pathogen diversification. In our model, diversification was favored when population structure generated sufficiently persistent strain-specific differences between host subpopulations, whereas stochastic variation in susceptibility instead promoted strain replacement. These findings highlight the potential role of host population structure in shaping antigenic evolution. However, further progress will require models whose assumptions about immune interactions and strain emergence can be systematically evaluated against robust measures derived from empirical pathogen and immunity data.
