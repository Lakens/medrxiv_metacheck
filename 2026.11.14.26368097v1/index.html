What we found

Show details

Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

P-Value Text
p-value<0.05 Genes with an FDR-adjusted p-value<0.05 were enriched with non-synonymous mutations and considered driver genes.
p-value<0.05 A p-value<0.05 or, when applicable, a padjusted value<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The APA manual states: Report exact p values (e.g., p = .031) to two or three decimal places. However, report p values less than .001 as p < .001. However, 2 decimals is too imprecise for many use-cases (e.g., a p value meta-analysis), so report p values with three digits.

American Psychological Association (2020). Publication manual of the American Psychological Association, 7 edition. American Psychological Association.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 1 non-significant p value that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Text Sentence
p=0.25 At the genomic level, D/P tumors showed a higher burden of SV deletions than other subtypes (adjusted p=0.0045) and contained RB1 mutations in 7 of 19 (37%), although this enrichment was not statistically significant after multiple-testing correction (adjusted p=0.25) (Supplementary Figure 10).

For metascientific articles demonstrating the rate of misinterpretations of non-significant results is high, see:

Aczel B, Palfi B, Szollosi A, Kovacs M, Szaszi B, Szecsi P, Zrubka M, Gronau Q, van den Bergh D, Wagenmakers E (2018). “Quantifying Support for the Null Hypothesis in Psychology: An Empirical Investigation.” Advances in Methods and Practices in Psychological Science, 1(3), 357–366. doi:10.1177/2515245918773742.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background: Cancer of unknown primary (CUP) is a metastatic, heterogeneous malignancy accounting for 2-5% of cancer diagnoses globally. Failure to identify the primary site precludes tumor-type-specific therapy and, together with an aggressive clinical course, contributes to poor survival. The biological mechanisms driving these behaviors remain poorly understood. A deeper understanding of CUP biology is therefore essential to improve diagnostic precision and therapeutic strategies. To address this knowledge gap, we comprehensively characterized the multi-omics landscape of CUP and identified biological and potentially therapeutic targets beyond tissue-of-origin (ToO) assignment.

Methods: Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) data from 283 CUP patients were analyzed for ToO predictions, mutational signatures, driver alterations, genetic immune escape (GIE), and clinically actionable alterations. Matched RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) data from 79 cases were used to characterize functional gene expression signatures and define transcriptomic subtypes. Clinical, WGS, and RNA-seq data were integrated via weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA).

Results: High-confidence ToO predictions (likelihood >80%) were obtained for 58% of CUPs, most commonly predicting lung, gastrointestinal, and biliary tract origins. Mutational signatures clustering revealed four subgroups dominated by tobacco smoking, defective DNA mismatch . repair (dMMR), UV exposure, and APOBEC activity, while a fifth group, comprising most tumors (n=169; 60%), showed mixed mutational processes. The cohort also frequently exhibited mutations in established cancer driver genes, including TP53, CDKN2A, KRAS, and epigenetic modifiers ARID1A, SMARCA4, and ARID2. GIE alterations occurred in 35% of tumors, mostly involving HLA-I genes. Transcriptomic profiling defined five subtypes with different degrees of immune infiltration, fibrosis, and tumor proliferation. These subtypes were not significantly associated with clinical or genomic features, indicating that the tumor microenvironment (TME) represents another layer of CUP heterogeneity. WGCNA further linked proliferative programs to cell-cycle, DNA-replication, and neuroendocrine features. WGS data identified potentially targetable alterations in 64% of the CUP patients.

Conclusions: CUP comprises biologically heterogeneous tumors with diverse predicted origins, mutational histories, and TME subtypes. Transcriptomic profiling and integrated genomics suggest that immune escape and rapid proliferation contribute to the clinical aggressive behavior. Together, these findings provide a molecular framework for understanding CUP heterogeneity and support therapeutic stratification by both ToO predictions and tumor-specific molecular alterations.

Introduction

Cancer of unknown primary (CUP) is a clinically and biologically heterogeneous group of metastatic tumors for which the anatomical site of origin remains unidentified despite extensive diagnostic evaluation [1]. While advances in diagnostic technologies have reduced the incidence, CUP still accounts for 2-5% of all newly diagnosed cancers worldwide [2]. In the Netherlands, for example, CUP accounts for 0.4% of all new cancer cases, corresponding to approximately 602 diagnoses in 2025 [3]. Because the primary tumor site cannot be identified, CUP poses significant clinical challenges. Treatment for most patients remains largely limited to empiric chemotherapy, as site-specific chemotherapy is not an option, contributing to a poor overall survival of only 6-8 months [4, 5]. However, the recent CUPISCO study showed improved outcomes with sequential chemotherapy and molecularly targeted therapy [6]. The clinical complexity, therapeutic uncertainty, and poor prognosis associated with CUP highlight the need for a deeper understanding of its biology.

Advances in molecular profiling technologies, particularly whole-genome sequencing (WGS) and RNA sequencing (RNA-seq), offer opportunities to investigate CUP biology. Although the underlying biological processes driving CUP tumorigenesis remain incompletely understood, multiple hypotheses have been proposed [7]. One hypothesis suggests that CUP represents metastasis from a conventional primary tumor that remains clinically occult because it is indistinguishable from metastatic disease, dormant, or regressed. Based on this concept, several tissue-of-origin (ToO) genomic prediction tools [8] [9] [10] have been developed, including the cancer of unknown primary prediction algorithms (CUPPA) [11], which uses positional mutational distributions, 96-trinucleotide single-base-substitution (SBS) context, driver gene profiles, and other genomic features to infer the likely tissue of origin. Likewise, RNA-based and methylationbased [10] classifiers have shown clinical utility by guiding site-specific therapy, and RNA-based classifiers has been associated with improved progression-free survival [12, 13]. An alternative hypothesis proposes that CUP may represent a distinct tumor entity, potentially arising from stem-like cells with high metastatic potential and no clear identifiable primary site [14]. This model is in line with transcriptomic studies showing poor correlation between CUP and known cancer types in The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) [7, 15, 16].

Irrespective of its origin, CUP is typically characterized by poor prognosis [17], driven in part by rapid proliferation and dissemination to distant sites at diagnosis [14]. In many cancer types, the tumor microenvironment (TME) actively contributes to tumor aggressiveness through diverse mechanisms, including angiogenesis, immune evasion, and metabolic reprogramming [18].

Beyond the TME, genomic profiling of CUP can identify clinically actionable alterations that may guide targeted therapies [15, 19]. Importantly, tumors with an inflamed, immune-infiltrated TME may be more responsive to immunotherapy, and several CUP case studies have reported favorable outcomes with immunotherapy combined with other treatments [20] [21] [22]. Therefore, integrating genomic profiling with TME characterization may provide novel biological insights and help identify therapeutic opportunities for patients with CUP. However, comprehensive multi-omics profiling of CUPs remains limited, as most studies are constrained by small cohort sizes and a lack of integrated genomic and transcriptomic analyses [23, 24].

In this study, we characterized the genomic landscape of 283 CUP patients using WGS and identified driver genes and cancer hallmarks. In addition, we obtained matched RNA-seq data from 79 CUP patients to explore the TME and link transcriptomic data to clinical and genomic traits. Overall, our integrated genomic and transcriptomic approach was designed to advance the understanding of CUP biology and support ongoing efforts to improve diagnosis and treatment for CUP patients.

Materials and Methods

Patient cohort

This study aimed to characterize the multi-omics landscape of CUP and identify biological and potentially therapeutic features. Therefore, patients diagnosed with CUP were included from [26], and (e) the Whole Genome Sequencing Implementation in standard Diagnostics for Every cancer patient (WIDE; NL68609.031.18) from the NKI [27].

Clinical data curation

Two clinicians curated the clinicopathological data: one medical oncologist (YvdW) from VieCuri Medical Center and one pathologist (PS) from the NKI. We excluded tumors with a histopathological diagnosis that follows ESMO Clinical Practice Guidelines (2011) (e.g., neuroendocrine tumors, sarcomas, high-grade serous carcinomas, melanomas, germ cell tumors, and mesotheliomas) [28]. Cases labeled as CUP in the HMF database but shown to have a known primary origin on retrospective clinicopathological review were excluded. We also excluded cases with WGS performed to aid the diagnostic work-up. For patients with multiple biopsies, only the first biopsy was included in the study to maintain consistency across samples.

Whole-genome sequencing data

Detailed characterization of WGS library preparation and processing is provided in the Supplementary Information (SI) document. In short, raw WGS data were processed by the HMF computational pipeline (https://github.com/hartwigmedical/pipeline5).

Driver genes enriched with non-synonymous mutations

Potential driver genes were identified through the ratio of non-synonymous (dN) and synonymous (dS) mutations using the dNdScv R package [29] (v0.0.1.0) based on the somatic mutations identified by SAGE. Genes with an FDR-adjusted p-value<0.05 were enriched with non-synonymous mutations and considered driver genes.

Driver genes frequently altered by CNAs sCNAs identified by GISTIC2 were mapped to genes annotated in GENCODE (release 38) using the GRCh37 reference genome. A minimum overlap of 10 base pairs was required between each significant GISTIC2 peak and a gene annotation. For each significant peak, all genes meeting this overlap requirement were initially assigned to the corresponding peak. When multiple genes overlapped the same peak, genes present in a manually curated cancer driver gene list were prioritized. If no genes overlapped a peak, the nearest GENCODE protein-coding gene was assigned. The manually curated driver gene list integrates data from multiple sources, including COSMIC Cancer Gene Census (v92), IntOGen [30, 31], Compendium Cancer Genes (02-2020), Priestley et al [25], Dietlein et al [32], Martincorena et al [29], and Bailey et al [33]. The complete list of driver genes is available in the R2CPCT package [34].

Tumor mutational burden

Tumor mutational burden (TMB) was calculated based on the total sum of SNVs, MNVs, and InDels per megabase over the total number of callable nucleotides in the human reference genome as previously described [35]. A TMB was classified as low (<5), medium (≥5-≤10), or high (>10).

Mutational signatures

A 96-trinucleotide mutation count matrix was constructed from the identified SNVs relative to the GRCh37 genome. Single base substitution (SBS) mutational signatures from the Catalogue of Somatic Mutations in Cancer (COSMIC; v3.1) [36] were retrieved using the mutationalPattern package [37] (v3.14.0). The optimal nonnegative linear combination of the trinucleotide contexts was fitted to the known COSMIC signatures to derive SBS signatures. The relative contribution of each signature was calculated and signatures contributing less than 5% were grouped into a separate category (Filtered <5%).

Putative therapeutic targets

Targetable genomic alterations were identified using the ESMO Scale for Clinical Actionability of molecular Targets (ESCAT) database [38]. Only alterations classified as tier I (ready for routine use) or tier II (investigational use) were retained.

Germline variants

Germline variants were annotated using the VEP with the gnomAD plugin. Variants with a maximum allele frequency <0.005 were retained. Variants were further filtered to those present in the ClinVar database [39] (downloaded 2024-09-16) and classified as pathogenic or likely pathogenic, with expert or guideline-based review status and germline or germline/somatic origin. Only variants associated with cancer-related phenotypes (cancer, tumor, neoplasm) and predicted to have high impact were included.

Whole-transcriptome sequencing

Detailed characterization of RNA-seq library preparation and pre-processing is provided in the SI document. In short, we used the nf-core RNA-seq pipeline (v3.12.0) [40] to pre-process raw RNA-seq data.

Tumor microenvironment analysis

To describe the TME in CUPs, we applied a set of knowledge-based functional gene expression signatures (Fges) that represent key immune and stromal components, curated by Bagaev et al [41]. Gene set variation analysis (GSVA) was performed using the GSVA R package [42] (v2.2.1) to quantify the expression activity of each signature across CUP patients. The input for GSVA was the VST-transformed gene expression matrix generated by DESeq2. To subtype the CUP TME, we adopted an approach similar to that of Bagaev et al. In short, a similarity matrix was constructed by calculating the Pearson's correlation coefficient between all CUP samples using the GSVA scores. The matrix was then used to generate a weighted graph, where each node represented a sample and the edge weights corresponded to the Pearson's correlation values between pairs of samples. Louvain clustering was subsequently applied to detect clusters within the graph using igraph R package[43] (v2.2.0). To determine the optimal weight threshold for defining clusters, we checked the number of samples in each cluster. Candidate thresholds that resulted in any cluster containing <5% of samples were excluded. Subsequently, we evaluated several clustering quality metrics to choose the optimal threshold, including Davies-Bouldin index, Calinski-Harabasz index, and Silhouette score.

Quantification of hallmark pathway and neuroendocrine activity

The activity of 50 hallmark pathways curated in the Molecular Signature Database [44] (MSigDB) was also quantified using GSVA. To further investigate neuroendocrine features in CUP, the Gavish_3Ca_Metaprogram_Epithelial_Neuroendocrine gene set was analyzed using the same approach.

Immune cell deconvolution with CIBERSORTx Immune cell composition was analyzed using CIBERSORTx [45] with the LM22 signature matrix in the relative mode. The analysis used transcripts per million (TPM)-normalized count matrix as input. A batch correction was performed using the B mode for removing technical biases between bulk mixture data and the reference signature, as recommended by the CIBERSORTx authors, and the analysis was carried out with 500 permutations to ensure robustness and accuracy.

Outcome analysis

Treatment response assessment for CUP patients in the CPCT-02 study was performed using the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST; v1.1) [46] score. For survival analysis, either the date of death (if available) or the date of the most recent RECIST assessment was used as the endpoint. Survival curves were generated using the survival [47] (v3.8.3) and survminer[48] (v0.5.0) R packages.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R (v4.5.1) and associated packages. Group comparisons were conducted using the Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables, and the χ 2 -test or Fisher's exact test for categorical variables. The BH procedure was used to adjust the p-values in the analyses of 1) differential gene expression, 2) pathway over-representation, 3) feature comparison between transcriptomic clusters. A p-value<0.05 or, when applicable, a padjusted value<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Clinical and genomic profiling captures the heterogeneity of CUP

A total of 369 tumor samples from patients initially labeled as CUP were included from the Dutch ACTIN, CORE, CPCT-02, DRUP, and WIDE studies (Figure 1A). Following clinicopathological review, 49 cases were excluded because a primary tumor site was known on review and 27 cases because patients were managed according to ESMO Clinical Practice Guidelines (2011) based on histopathological classification [28]. For patients with multiple available , we retained only the first biopsy for the analysis, resulting in the exclusion of 10 additional samples. The final CUP cohort comprised 283 patients, including 139 females and 144 males (Supplementary Table 1), with all tumor samples obtained through needle biopsies.

Age at biopsy was available for 51 patients from the CPCT-02 and DRUP studies, representing 18% of the total cohort. Among these patients, most were older than 65 years (n=31; 61% of patients with available age data; 11% of the total cohort), followed by patients aged 51-65 years (n=14; 27%; 5% of the total cohort) and 35-50 years (n=6; 12%; 2% of the total cohort) (Supplementary Table1). Biopsies were most frequently obtained from metastatic sites in the liver (n=88; 31%), lymph nodes (n=81; 29%), and soft tissue (n=35; 12%)(Figure 1B).

Histopathological analysis revealed diverse histological types, with adenocarcinomas representing the most common subtype (n=136; 48%). In addition, 14 patients (5%) received treatment before biopsy and 48 patients (20%) received treatment after biopsy, predominantly chemotherapy (Supplementary Table 1). Median survival from the date of biopsy was 6 months (Supplementary Figure 1A). Among the 48 patients who received post-biopsy treatment, 38 had first post-treatment RECIST assessments, of whom 8 (21%) had a partial response (PR), 18 (47%) had stable disease (SD), and 12 (32%) had progressive disease (PD). Sequential assessment showed changes in response status over time, including transitions from PR to SD or PD (Supplementary Figure 1B). This poor clinical response was consistent with the aggressive nature of CUP.

Next, we characterized the genomic landscape of the CUP cohort to identify the genomic alterations driving CUP. We processed WGS data from tumor biopsies and matched peripheral blood samples for all 283 patients. The median coverage depth was 38X for germline DNA and 100X for tumor DNA, with a median tumor purity of 0.53 (Supplementary Figures 2A-B).

Mutation etiology and cancer type-specific abbreviations can be used to predict tissue of origin (ToO). In addition, the distribution of somatic mutations reflects concordance with DNA methylation and, therefore, chromatin accessibility, which is tissue-specific. These characteristics are implemented in the CUPPA algorithm, which we used to predict ToO from WGS data for all samples. In total, 165 patients (58%) showed a reliable prediction likelihood of >80% for a known primary tumor type. The most frequent predicted tissue origins were nonsmall cell lung cancer (n=48; 17%), lower gastrointestinal tract (colorectum, appendix, and small intestine; n=21; 7%), and biliary tract (intra-and extrahepatic bile ducts and gallbladder) (n=16; 6%) (Figure 1C).

Mutational signature analysis revealed major mutagenic processes and genomic instability patterns in CUP

WGS enabled the identification of somatic small mutations ≤50bp and the inference of underlying mutational processes using COSMIC mutational signatures. Hierarchical clustering of mutational signature contributions identified five distinct mutational-signature subgroups within the CUP cohort (Figure 2A; Supplementary Data 1). Four subgroups were dominated by signatures associated with tobacco smoking (n=58; 20%), defective DNA mismatch repair (dMMR; n=14; 5%), UV exposure (n=9; 3%), and APOBEC activity (n=33; 12%). The remaining subgroup, comprising 169 tumors (60%), showed mixed contributions from clock-like, chemotherapy-related, and unknown-etiology signatures (Figure 2B).

Tumors in the tobacco smoking, dMMR, and UV exposure subgroups were enriched for high TMB, defined as >10 mutations/Mb, consistent with the mutagenic potential of these processes (Figure 2C). All but one tumor in the dMMR subgroup had microsatellite instability (MSI) and a high abundance of insertions and deletions (Figures 2C and 2F). Tumors with a high contribution of UV-induced mutagenesis exhibited a relatively high contribution of multinucleotide variants (MNVs), predominantly CC>TT substitutions (Figure 2C). Because CUPPA incorporates mutational-context and other genomic features, ToO predictions were expected to be associated with COSMIC mutational-signature subgroups. Consistent with this, tumors in the tobacco and UV subgroups were frequently assigned lung and skin origins, respectively (Figures 2B, 2D-2E). Evidence of viral integration was detected in 15 of 283 tumors, including Human Papillomavirus and Merkel Cell Polyomavirus (Figure 2G). Another characteristic of genome instability, homologous recombination deficiency (HRD), was detected in 15 tumors (Figure 2H).

Neither viral integration nor HRD was enriched within a specific mutational-signature subgroup or among tumors with conclusive ToO predictions. However, histological type provided context for signature groups. Squamous cell/basaloid carcinomas were significantly enriched within the UV-exposure subgroup (χ 2 test, p=5.13e-12), providing clinicopathological support for a possible cutaneous origin in a subset of these tumors (Figure 2I).

To further characterize genome instability in the CUP cohort, SVs were analyzed across all tumors (Supplementary Figures 3A-3B; Supplementary Data 2). The median number of SVs was 189, with the first (Q1) and third (Q3) quartiles ranging from 97 to 361, while the relative contribution of different SV types varied substantially between samples (Supplementary Figure 3C). Total SV burden did not differ among the five mutational-signature subgroups, but correlated positively with TMB (Spearman r s =0.42; p=7.9e-14), ploidy (r s =0.22 p=2.4e-4), and the number of kataegis foci (r s =0.46; p=6.7e-16) (Supplementary Figures 3D-3F). Among the mutational-signature subgroups, the APOBEC-enriched subgroup had significantly more kataegis foci than all other subgroups (Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn's post-hoc analysis with BH multiple testing correction, adjusted p=6.7e-09), consistent with APOBEC enzymes using single-stranded DNA as substrates. The frequency of chromothripsis, a hallmark of catastrophic chromosomal rearrangement, did not differ significantly among mutational-signature subgroups (Supplementary Figure 3G).

Genomic profiling highlights recurrent tumor suppressor loss and immune escape features in CUP

To investigate recurrent genomic alterations with potential functional relevance in CUP, we characterized somatic and pathogenic germline driver alterations, including non-synonymous driver mutations, copy number changes, hotspot mutations, and gene fusions (Figures 3A and 3B). The most frequently altered gene was TP53 (n=155; 55%), consistent with previous CUP studies [49, 50], followed by CDKN2A (n=104; 37%), which was predominantly affected by deep deletions (Supplementary Figure 4A). Both genes were canonical tumor suppressors with key roles in cell-cycle regulation. Other recurrently altered genes included LRP1B (n=67; 24%), KRAS (n=59; 21%), ARID1A (n=43; 15%), and PTPRD (n=42; 15%), which were involved in oncogenic signaling, chromatin remodeling, and other cancer-associated processes. Significant chromosomal-arm deletions were observed on 9q, 9p, 4q, 4p, 22q, 22p, 18q, 15q, and 19p, whereas 1q, 12q, 12p, and 20q were significantly amplified (Supplementary Figure 4B).

The genes TP53 (n=89; 31%), KRAS (n=53; 19%), and TERT promoter variants (n=25; 9%) were most frequently affected by hotspot mutations (Figure 3A; Supplementary Figure 5). We observed pathogenic germline variants in 24 patients (8%). BRCA2 (n=3; 1%) was the most frequently observed germline variant, followed by CHEK2, MLH1, MSH6, NTHL1, and PMS2, each identified in two patients, and all were related to DNA-repair pathways (Supplementary Figure 5). We also detected oncogenic fusions annotated in the actionability framework used in this study, including TMPRSS2-ERG (n=3; 1%), FGFR2-AHCYL1 (n=2; 0.7%), and BRD4-NUTM1 (n=1; 0.4%) (Figure 3B). These detected lineage-informative fusion events, such as ERG for prostate cancer and FGFR2 for cholangiocarcinoma, were concordant with CUPPA predictions (Figure 3C) and were included in the CUPPA training features.

Given the potential role of tumor-immune interactions in CUP biology, we next examined genomic alterations affecting established immune-escape pathways. Overall, 100 patients (35%) harbored mutations in at least one of the six genetic immune escape (GIE)-related pathways (Figure 3D). The most frequently affected category involved the HLA-I locus (n=74; 26%), primarily through non-synonymous mutations or loss of heterozygosity, followed by alterations in the interferon-γ signaling (n=23; 8%) and antigen presentation pathways (n=18; 6%). Less frequent events were detected in immune checkpoint (n=4; 1%), epigenetic escape (n=3; 1%), and co-stimulatory (n=2; 1%) pathways (Figure 3D and Supplementary Figure 6A). Tumors harboring GIE mutations exhibited higher TMB compared with those without (χ 2 -test; p=3.9e-11; Supplementary Figure 6B). In addition, the presence of GIE-related alterations was significantly associated with biopsy site (χ 2 -test; p=1.41e-09), with lymph node biopsies showing a higher frequency of GIE-related alterations than biopsies from other sites (Supplementary Figure 6C).

Transcriptomic molecular subtyping of CUP identified distinct immune, stromal and proliferative tumor microenvironment states

To explore immune evasion and the diversity of the tumor microenvironment (TME) in CUP, we analyzed RNA-seq data from 82 samples, of which 79 passed quality control (Supplementary Figures 2C and 2D). In metastatic samples, the bulk transcriptomic profiles may be influenced by including transcripts from healthy tissue of the biopsy site. We, therefore, assessed biopsy site-specific effects on the transcriptome. PCA and t-SNE revealed partial clustering according to biopsy site (Supplementary Figure 7) and considered biopsy-site effects when interpreting these expression patterns.

Unsupervised Louvain clustering of the 29 Fges defined five transcriptomic subtypes (Figure 4A): immune-enriched/fibrotic (IE/F; n=9; 11%), immune-enriched/non-fibrotic (IE; n=20; 25%), fibrotic (F; n=24; 30%), immune-depleted/tumor proliferated (D/P; n=19; 24%), and immune-depleted (D; n=7; 9%). These subtypes reflected gradients of immune activation, stromal remodeling, and tumor proliferation (Figure 4B).

The IE and IE/F tumors exhibited strong immune activation and elevated expression of checkpoint genes, including PDCD1, CD274, CTLA4, LAG3, and PDCD1LG2 (Figure 4C). The IE/F subtype additionally showed enrichment of fibrotic and epithelial-organization pathways, including apical junctions and myogenesis signatures, together with upregulation of immune, apoptosis, and p53 programs. This subtype also showed enrichment of oncogenic signaling pathways, including KRAS, Notch, TGF-β, Wnt/β-catenin (Supplementary Figures 8 and 9), suggesting complex immune-stromal interactions. The IE subtype shared interferon responses but was distinguished by increased PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling (Supplementary Figure 9). By contrast, the F, D/P, and D subtypes were largely immune-depleted. The F subtype showed fibroblast and ECM remodeling signatures, consistent with stromal activation (Supplementary Figure 8). The D/P subtype demonstrated pronounced expression of a neuroendocrineassociated signature (Figure 4D) together with proliferative programs enriched for cell-cycle, DNA-repair, and mTORC1 pathways (Supplementary Figure 9). At the genomic level, D/P tumors showed a higher burden of SV deletions than other subtypes (adjusted p=0.0045) and contained RB1 mutations in 7 of 19 (37%), although this enrichment was not statistically significant after multiple-testing correction (adjusted p=0.25) (Supplementary Figure 10). The D subtypes displayed liver-associated metabolic activity, including bile acid, cholesterol, xenobiotic metabolism, consistent with their biopsy origin of tumors in this group, together with modest neutrophil-related enrichment (Figure 4E and Supplementary Figure 11).

CIBERSORTx analysis supported the subtype assignments, showing higher relative proportions of CD8+ T cells, B cells, NK cells, and M1 macrophages in IE and IE/F tumors than in those of F and D/P (Supplementary Figure 11). No significant subtype differences were observed in driver mutations, genomic subgroups, GIE alterations, CUPPA predictions, or histological tumor types (Figures 4F-4J). Tumor purity differed across subtypes, consistent with variation in cellular composition inferred from the transcriptomic profiles (Figure 4K).

Clinically actionable alterations are common but context-dependent in CUP

To identify potentially actionable targets and explore therapeutic opportunities beyond empiric chemotherapy, we assessed clinically relevant genomic alterations using the ESCAT framework.

Among the 283 patients with CUP, 181 (64%) harbored at least one potentially targetable alteration, with some tumors carrying up to nine such alterations (Figure 5A). A subset of patients had high TMB, a biomarker to support treatment with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI)(Figure 5B). Among TMB-high tumors with available transcriptomic subtype information (n=28), 17 (61%) were classified as immune-enriched, including either IE or IE/F subtypes. The remaining TMB-high tumors displayed non-immune-enriched transcriptomic profiles, suggesting that TME context may further refine the interpretation of TMB as a potential immunotherapy biomarker in the context of CUP.

Actionable alteration-drug matches were identified across several therapeutic classes. FGFRdirected tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) were linked to tumors harboring FGFR alterations.

However, current clinical use of FGFR inhibitors remains largely dependent on tumor type and alteration type rather than tumor-agnostic [51]. Other frequently drug-alteration matches included PARP inhibitors (n=50;18%), mainly associated with ATM mutations or deletions; AKT inhibitors (n=35; 12%), linked to PTEN loss or mutation; and PI3K inhibitors (n=25; 9%), linked to PIK3CA hotspot mutations. KRAS G12C-directed therapy represented an additional potential match in approximately 3% of the cohort (Figure 5B). These assignments indicate molecular indication for treatment rather than demonstrated therapy benefit in this cohort.

Gene expression network connects key biological and clinical features of CUP

To further integrate transcriptomic programs with clinical and genomic traits of CUP, we performed weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) on RNA-seq data. This analysis identified 36 co-expression modules comprising between 37 and 1,814 genes, in addition to a small grey set of three unassigned genes (Supplementary Figure 12). Across the module-trait landscape, notable associations were observed for tumor proliferation, immune and stromal transcriptional signals, CUPPA prediction confidence and biopsy site (Supplementary Figure 13). The tumor proliferation score grouped one set of modules, strongest with the blue module (r s =0.91), followed by the pink (r s =0.76), turquoise (r s =0.45), and red modules (r s =0.40; Supplementary Figure 13). These associations should be interpreted cautiously because the proliferation signature partially overlaps with genes comprising these modules. Beyond proliferation, the blue, pink, and red modules were associated with tumor intrinsic characteristics, including higher tumor purity, tumor mutational burden (TMB), TP53 mutation, and the D/P transcriptomic subtype. The blue, pink, and red modules were also positively associated with neuroendocrine carcinoma histology and negatively associated with adenocarcinoma histology. In addition, the red module was positively associated with the epithelial-neuroendocrine metaprogram (r s =0.62).

Functional enrichment using Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) further distinguished the biological programs represented by these modules (Supplementary Figure 14). The blue module was primarily characterized by cell-cycle and mitotic processes, including cell-cycle checkpoints, DNA replication and M phase. Consistent with this profile, genes within this module, such as NDC80 [52] and CDCA5 [53] (Supplementary Figure 15), have previously been linked with TP53 mutation or increased TP53 mutation burden in pan-cancer or tumor-specific analyses. The pink module similarly reflects proliferation activity, with enrichment for DNA replication and replication initiation, chromosome condensation, and origin-of-replication complex assembly. In contrast, the red module was characterized by replication-stress and DNArepair processes, including ATR activation, homologous recombination and mitotic prometaphase. The turquoise module showed a distinct functional profile, enriched for translation, oxidative phosphorylation, and mitochondrial respiratory electron transport.

A second group of modules, brown, darkorange, black, and darkorange2 captured immune and stromal transcriptional signals (Supplementary Figure 13). Functional enrichment analysis delineated the biological processes represented by these modules (Supplementary Figure 16).

The brown module showed a mixed immune-stromal profile, with enrichment of extracellular matrix (ECM) organization and collagen formation alongside cytokine and type I interferon signaling. In contrast, the dark orange module was predominantly associated with immune signaling, including type I and type II interferon pathways and cytokine-cytokine receptor interactions. The black and darkorange2 modules showed dominantly stromal programs, with enrichment of ECM organization, collogen-and proteoglycan-related processes, and integrin signaling. These immune-and stromal-associated modules were also significantly related to our transcriptomic TME subtypes (Supplementary Figure 13).

We next examined the modules correlated with samples for which CUPPA did not yield a confident ToO prediction. Low-confidence CUPPA predictions showed more modest associations with the saddlebrown and darkgrey modules (both r s =0.23; Supplementary Figure 13). The darkgrey module was enriched for epigenetic and transcriptional processes, including DNA methylation, histone arginine methylation, and β-catenin/TCF-related transcription, whereas the saddlebrown module showed enrichment for primary-cilium-related processes and RNA polymerase II transcription (Supplementary Figure 17). Both modules were enriched for epigenetic and transcriptional processes, suggesting that tumors with low-confidence genomic ToO predictions may be characterized by altered regulatory states. It remains to be tested whether adding transcriptomic and methylation features would provide complementary information for ToO classification.Finally, given the increased liver-associated metabolic activity observed in transcriptomic subtype D, we investigated whether WGCNA modules were associated with the biopsy site. Indeed, the yellow (r s =0.70) and greenyellow (r s =0.52) modules correlated with liver biopsy site, and both were characterized by metabolic programs, including cytochrome P450-mediated drug and xenobiotic metabolism, biological oxidation, and retinal metabolism. The yellow module was additionally enriched for complement and coagulation pathways (Supplementary Figure 18). Overall, WGCNA identified distinct co-expression modules associated with proliferation, immune and stromal biology, transcriptional programs associated with low-confidence CUPPA predictions, and liver-associated metabolic activity. Together, these modules highlight coordinated gene-expression patterns across CUP samples that capture complementary aspects of tumor biology, and revealing similar expression patterns across CUP patients.

Discussion

In this study, we interrogated and integrated whole-genome and transcriptomic sequencing data to characterize the genomic landscape, tumor microenvironment (TME), and potential therapeutic vulnerabilities of CUP. Using WGS data from 283 patients, we identified mutational processes, recurrent driver alterations, structural variations (SVs), and putative genetic immune escape (GIE) events. In a subset of 79 patients with matched RNA-seq data, transcriptomic subtyping revealed distinct immune-enriched/depleted, fibrotic, and proliferative TME states.

Together, these diverse findings further emphasized the marked heterogeneity of CUP and support the integration of genomic and transcriptomic data, in addition to tissue-of-origin (ToO) prediction, to better understand the biology of CUP.

Two predominant hypotheses have been proposed to explain CUP biology [54]. The first proposes that CUP represents metastasis arising from a conventional primary tumor that either cannot be distinguished from metastatic lesions or remains clinically occult, potentially due to early dissemination, dormancy or regression. The second proposes that CUP is an independent entity lacking a conventional primary site counterpart. Several observations in our data support the occult primary hypothesis in a substantial proportion of cases. High-confidence CUPPA predictions were obtained for 58% of tumors, and many of these shared genomic characteristics with cancers with known primaries. For example, tobacco-and UV-associated mutational signatures were linked to lung-and skin-like genomic profiles; TMPRSS2-ERG and FGFR2 fusions were detected in tumors with concordant CUPPA predictions; the enrichment of squamous/basaloid histology in the UV-signature group provided clinicopathological support for a cutaneous origin. However, the remaining tumors assigned as "inconclusive" CUPPA predictions should not be interpreted as solid evidence for an independent CUP entity, as current classifiers are limited by the number and constellation of reference categories and features on which they are trained. Additionally, previous work has shown that, no matter whether DNA-, RNA-or methylation-based, ToO approaches are only able to resolve a subset of clinicopathologically unresolved CUPs [10, 55]. Moreover, mutations in epigenetic modifiers such as ARID1A, SMARCA4, and ARID2 could shift the distribution of somatic mutations in these cancers and reduce the reliability of RNA-or methylation-based ToO detection. Nevertheless, a previous multi-lesion CUP case study identified a high number of fully shared mutations across all 15 metastases, found remarkably similar expression profiles, and observed distinct "galaxy"pattern evolutionary trajectories in individual CUPs [16]. Taken together, the characterized profiles suggested an occult primary in many cases but did not establish a separate biological CUP entity for the remaining tumors, especially those with low-confidence ToO prediction.

The transcriptomic analyses added another layer of heterogeneity. We identified immuneenriched/depleted, fibrotic, and proliferative states, and these TME states were not significantly associated with other clinical or genomic features. However, weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) revealed significant associations between gene modules, histology, and genomics. The blue module, enriched for cell-cycle and mitotic processes, was also associated with TP53 alterations, higher TMB, and neuroendocrine characteristics. The proliferation-associated modules identified across our CUP cohort were conceptually consistent with experimental CUP models in which stem-like "agnospheres" showed growth-factorindependent proliferation, rapid multi-organ dissemination, and MAP kinase/MYC axis dependence [14]. Of note, transcriptomic analysis was also partially influenced by biopsy site, particularly through liver-associated metabolic programs. These signals may reflect true metastatic-niche adaptation, contamination from normal tissue, or both. Our transcriptomic analysis can not distinguish these possibilities. But other studies have described site-specific effects in metastatic breast [56] and pancreatic cancer [57].

To investigate which genomic mechanisms might enable immune escape in CUPs, we interrogated putative GIE alterations. They were present in 35% of tumors, most often affecting the HLA-I locus, followed by interferon-γ signaling and antigen-presentation pathways. Notably, GIE alterations did not define the transcriptomic TME subtypes, suggesting that GIE and the overall immune state are not equivalent. Similarly, although 61% of TMB-high tumors with RNAseq were immune-enriched, a substantial fractionwere not. This indicates that, for CUP, high TMB does not necessarily correspond to an immune-infiltrated TME, warranting caution when applying only TMB as a biomarker for immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy in CUP.

In this study, 64% of patients harbored at least one actionable alteration. This supports the use of comprehensive molecular profiling as a way of generating new treatment options beyond empiric chemotherapy. A recent WGS study that included an overlapping Dutch CUP population similarly reported potentially actionable alterations in 73% of CUP patients and demonstrated diagnostic and treatment utility of WGS [58]. However, the applicability of these targetable alterations is uncertain because many alteration-drug associations depend on the specific combination of alteration plus tumor type. Therefore, further clinical trials are needed to expand the scope of tumor-agnostic alterations and therapies in CUP.

Several limitations should be mentioned. Biopsy site and tumor purity affected bulk transcriptomes, so they cannot fully separate malignant cells from local immune, stromal, and normal tissue components. The cohort also lacked a systematic non-CUP metastatic comparator, limiting research on CUP-specific biology. Finally, the authors inferred actionability from retrospective molecular evidence rather than prospectively testing it.

Future studies should first specifically investigate CUP tumors that lack high-confidence ToO predictions. Matched multi-lesion samples and single-cell or spatial profiling could be used to distinguish tumor-intrinsic signals from environmental adaptation to, or by the metastatic tissue environment. Additionally, methylation profiling may provide complementary information to genomic and transcriptomic features, and cell-free (cfDNA) methylation has already shown feasibility for ToO prediction in CUP [10]. In current inconclusive CUPs, particularly when samples could be collected over time, these approaches would further help determine whether diagnostic uncertainty reflects an unusual ToO or biological changes that occur during tumor progression and metastasis.

Conclusions

Our integrated genomic and transcriptomic analysis provides a comprehensive, detailed view of the molecular landscape of CUP, revealing the molecular heterogeneity across CUP tumors. While many CUP cases exhibit recognizable mutational features of established cancer types. transcriptomic profiling identified distinct immune, stromal, and proliferative states that are not fully captured by genomic subgroups or ToO predictions. These findings demonstrate that ToO assignment alone does not fully reflect the biological complexity of CUP. Integrating genomic, transcriptomic, and epigenomic data may further refine biological stratification and identify therapeutic vulnerabilities, while prospective studies are needed to establish clinical utility of multi-omics profiling.

Figure 2. Mutational signatures reveal five distinct genomic subgroups in CUP (A) Hierarchical clustering of COSMIC mutational signatures identified five genomic subgroups across the CUP cohort, with the 283 samples ordered horizontally.

Figure 3.

Figure 3. Recurrent driver alterations, fusion events, and immune-escape mechanisms in CUP (A) Landscape of driver genes that are statistically significant using dN/dS analysis and GISTIC2.0 in ≥5% of tumors that are ordered horizontally. Gene labels include the number of altered cases and their corresponding percentages. Outer boxes represent copy number changes, inner boxes represent small mutations or Structural Variants, boxes marked with a G highlight germline variants, and an asterisk (*) marks hotspot mutations. (B) Presence of known oncogenic gene fusions detected across the cohort. (C) High-confidence CUPPA tissue-of-origin predictions for each sample. (D) Genetic immune escape alterations across six biological pathways, including HLA-I locus disruption, antigen presentation, interferon-γ signaling, immune checkpoint genes, epigenetic escape, and co-stimulation pathways.

Figure 4.

Figure 4. Transcriptomic profiling defines five immune-stromal subtypes in CUP (A) Louvain clustering of 29 functional gene expression signatures (Fges) identified five transcriptomic subtypes: immune-enriched/fibrotic (IE/F), immune-enriched/non-fibrotic (IE), fibrotic (F), immune-depleted/tumor proliferated (D/P), and immune-depleted (D) for 79 samples ordered on the x-axis. (B) Heatmap of anti-tumor immune cells, pro-tumor immune cells, angiogenesis/fibrosis, and malignant cell property signatures across the five subtypes. (C) Expression levels of nine immune-checkpoint genes. (D) Neuroendocrine-related gene signature expression levels. (E) Biopsy site for each sample. (F) Tumor Mutational Burden (TMB) status. (G) COSMIC multinational signature cluster assignment.
