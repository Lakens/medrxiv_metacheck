What we found

We found 14 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background | Coronary microvascular dysfunction (CMD) is a common cause of ischemic symptoms in patients with angina and non-obstructive coronary arteries (ANOCA), predominantly women. Neutrophils may contribute to CMD via vascular inflammation and endothelial injury. We therefore investigated neutrophil and coronary endothelial cells in women with ANOCA.

In this cross-sectional study, we collected blood samples from ANOCA patients (n=123, 84% women) undergoing coronary function testing. Complete blood counts (CELL-DYN, n=123) and neutrophil activation (AQUIOS, n=76) were assessed. Isolated neutrophils were profiled using single-cell RNA-seq (n=11). Guidewirederived coronary endothelial cells (n=7) isolated during coronary function testing were cultured to passage 2 for bulk RNA-seq.

Results | Of 103 women with ANOCA, 23 (22%) were diagnosed with CMD (CFR<2.5). These women showed higher NT-proBNP than women without CMD (234 [136-416] pg/ml vs. 112 [79-200] pg/ml, p<0.001). Neutrophil counts, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio and systemic immune-inflammation index were higher among women with a lower coronary flow reserve (CFR). The proportion of activated neutrophils (CD62L dim ) was inversely associated with CFR at baseline. Single-cell RNA-seq identified four neutrophil clusters, including interferon (IFN)-activated and immature neutrophils. The IFN-activated cluster was expanded in patients with lower CFR and its proportion was inversely associated with surface CD62L expression. Immature neutrophils showed reduced translational and increased inflammatory gene expression in patients with low CFR. Coronary endothelial cells from patients with increased CD62L dim neutrophils showed higher proliferative/metabolic and lower IFN, complement and coagulation pathway activity.

Conclusion | Reduced CFR in women with ANOCA is associated with neutrophil activation, expansion of an IFNactivated neutrophil population, and altered immature neutrophil transcriptional programs. Neutrophil activation is accompanied by distinct endothelial signatures. These findings implicate neutrophil-endothelial interactions in CMD and identify IFN-associated neutrophil states as targets for mechanistic investigation.

Introduction

Angina with non-obstructive coronary arteries (ANOCA) is a highly prevalent yet clinically challenging condition among patients undergoing coronary angiography, particularly among women 1, 2 . A central mechanism underlying ANOCA is coronary microvascular dysfunction (CMD), which is characterized by impaired regulation of coronary microvascular function, resulting in an inadequate increase in myocardial blood supply in response to myocardial demand 3 . Importantly, CMD is associated with high angina burden and adverse cardiovascular outcomes, including an increased risk of heart failure with preserved ejection fraction 4, 5 . Despite this burden, effective and mechanism-guided treatments remain limited 6, 7 . Elucidating the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms is therefore crucial to identify potential therapeutic targets and improve the management of patients with CMD.

Although the mechanisms underlying CMD remain incompletely understood, both coronary endothelial dysfunction and systemic low-grade inflammation have been implicated in its pathophysiology 8, 9 . For instance, circulating IL-6 and TNF- have been associated with reduced coronary flow reserve (CFR) in obese patients without clinical heart disease 10 . In addition, microvascular diseases are highly prevalent in chronic inflammatory diseases, including rheumatoid arthritis and systemic lupus erythematosus [11] [12] [13] [14] , both of which disproportionately affect women.

Within these diseases, neutrophils play a central role [15] [16] [17] . They are key effector cells of the innate immune system and may link systemic inflammation to coronary microvascular impairment, as hypothesized previously in ANOCA 18 . Upon activation, neutrophils adhere to the endothelium and release reactive oxygen species, proteases, and inflammatory mediators, which can disrupt the endothelial barrier and impair nitric oxide mediated vasodilation. Neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) may further contribute to endothelial injury by promoting endothelial activation, inducing oxidative stress and creating a prothrombotic environment 19, 20 . Beyond their established role in inflammatory and autoimmune diseases, neutrophil activation has also been implicated in coronary plaque erosion 21 , a pathology that is also characterized by sex-differences 22 . These mechanisms provide a biological rationale for investigating the role of neutrophils in CMD.

. Given the involvement of neutrophils in several female-predominant diseases, we investigated neutrophil abundance, activation and transcriptional states, together with coronary endothelial cell transcriptional profiles, in relation to CMD and invasively measured CFR in women with ANOCA.

.

Methods 1. Study design and population

Blood sampling and isolation of coronary-catheterization derived endothelial cells was performed at the University Medical Center Utrecht as part of the ENDOTYPE biobank (TCbio 24-203). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to the coronary function test. Participation in the Netherlands Registry of Invasive Coronary Vasomotor Function Testing (NL-CFT, 21-473) 23 was a prerequisite for inclusion in the ENDOTYPE biobank, which enabled linkage of the biological samples to the corresponding clinical and procedural data. The ENDOTYPE biobank was approved by the Biobank Committee operating under the responsibility of the Medical Ethical Committee of the University Medical Center Utrecht. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Data supporting the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

For the present study, patients with obstructive coronary artery disease, as identified prior to the coronary function test and patients with incomplete CFR measurements were excluded.

2. Diagnostic coronary function testing

After exclusion of functionally significant epicardial coronary artery disease (non-hyperemic pressure ratio >0.89 or fractional flow reserve >0.80), ANOCA patients underwent invasive coronary function testing in the left anterior descending artery, including vasospasm provocation testing and coronary vasodilatory capacity assessment. The procedure has been described in detail previously 24 . Intracoronary acetylcholine provocation testing was used to assess coronary vasospasm, using incremental acetylcholine doses of 20, 100 and 200mg. Spasm diagnosis was based on the Coronary Vasomotor Disorders International Study Group criteria 25 : epicardial spasm required reproducible angina, ischemic electrocardiographic changes, and >90% epicardial vasoconstriction; microvascular spasm required recognizable anginal symptoms and ischemic electrocardiographic changes without >90% epicardial vasoconstriction. Tests with symptoms or ischemic electrocardiographic changes alone were considered inconclusive (or equivocal).

Coronary vasodilatory capacity was assessed using continuous thermodilution, using a pressure-and temperature-sensitive sensor equipped guidewire (PressureWire X, Abbott Vascular, Santa Clara, USA) together with a RayFlow infusion catheter (Hexacath, Paris, France) positioned in the proximal left anterior descending artery, as described previously 24 . Absolute coronary blood flow was quantified under resting and hyperemic conditions during saline infusion at 10 and 20 mL/min, respectively.

Measurements were obtained using dedicated software (CoroVentis, Uppsala, Sweden) based on the continuous thermodilution principle. Absolute microvascular resistance was calculated as distal coronary pressure divided by absolute coronary blood flow. Coronary flow reserve (CFR) was defined as the ratio of hyperemic to resting absolute coronary blood flow and microvascular resistance reserve was calculated as previously described 26 . For the present study, a diagnosis of CMD was defined as an absolute CFR <2.5, consistent with established diagnostic thresholds for impaired vasodilatory capacity 27.

3. Blood withdrawal

Blood samples were collected from ANOCA patients during the coronary function test, before any intracoronary measurements were performed. First, systemic blood samples were obtained from the radial or femoral arterial sheath immediately after vascular access was established. Second, local blood samples were collected from the guiding catheter after engagement of the left anterior descending artery.

Both blood samples were collected following heparin administration to minimize procedural influences.

4. Clinical biomarker measurements

Clinical biomarkers, including total cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, triglycerides, creatinine and N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP), were measured retrospectively in plasma samples obtained from systemically collected EDTA-anticoagulated blood. Blood samples were centrifuged at 1,850g for 10 minutes at room temperature with brake (Hettich centrifuge). Afterwards, the plasma was stored at -80°C and was thawed once before clinical biomarker measurements.

. Total cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, triglycerides and creatinine were measured using the Atellica CI analyzer (Siemens Healthineers, Forchheim, Germany) and NT-proBNP was measured using the Atellica IM immunoassay analyzer (Siemens Healthineers). Lipid parameters and creatinine were quantified using enzymatic assays with photometric detection. NT-proBNP was quantified using a fully automated, two-site sandwich immunoassay based on direct chemiluminescence.

5. Routine hematological complete blood cell counts

Hematological complete blood cell counts were routinely measured in systemic and local EDTAanticoagulated blood samples using the CELL-DYN Sapphire hematology analyzer (Abbott Diagnostics, Abbott Park, IL, USA). Available measurements for all included patients were retrieved from the Utrecht Patient Orientated Database (UPOD) 28 . Parameters included red blood cell indices (hemoglobin, hematocrit, red blood cell count, mean corpuscular volume, mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration and red cell distribution width), platelet count, total leukocyte count, and differential leukocyte counts (neutrophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, eosinophils and basophils). The neutrophil-tolymphocyte ratio (NLR), monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR), and systemic immune-inflammation index (SII, platelet x neutrophil/lymphocyte ratio) were calculated from absolute counts.

6. AQUIOS flow cytometry and data analysis

Immediately after collection, systemic and local blood samples collected in sodium-heparin tubes, were loaded onto the AQUIOS CL 'Load & Go' flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, Miami, FL, USA) 29 . This platform fully automates sample preparation and flow cytometric analysis, including sample mixing, pipetting and staining with antibodies. After a 15-minute incubation, red blood cells were lysed by adding 335 ml of lysing reagent A. Next, 100 ml of lysing reagent B was added to stop the lysis and preserve leukocyte integrity. The prepared samples were then aspirated for analysis. Absolute leukocyte counts were determined by electronic volume measurement.

To assess neutrophil surface marker expression, a custom antibody panel was used, consisting of CD64-PC7 (clone 22, Beckman Coulter, Miami, FL, USA), CD16-FITC (clone 3G8, Beckman Coulter), CD11b-PE (clone Bear1, Beckman Coulter), CD62L-ECD (clone DREG56, Beckman Coulter) and CD10-PC5 (clone ALB1, Beckman Coulter). To assess neutrophil responsiveness, each sample was measured both unstimulated and after stimulation with the bacterial/mitochondrial-derived stimulus Nformyl-norleucyl-leucyl-phenalalanine (fNLF, BioCat GmbH, Heidelberg, Germany) at a final concentration of 10 -5 M. fNLF is a bacterial formyl-peptide receptor agonist that stimulates neutrophil activation, including degranulation and the mobilization of adhesion molecules 30.

For detailed analysis, data files were exported from the AQUIOS CL flow cytometer and analyzed using FlowJo software (Tree Star Inc. Ashland, OR, USA). Granulocytes were gated based on forward and side scatter, followed by neutrophil identification based on CD16 and CD11b co-expression. The number of cells/L was calculated by multiplying the gated events with a correction factor of 232558.139535.

The median fluorescent intensity (MFI) of the different markers was determined within this neutrophil gate. Neutrophil phenotypes were classified by quadrant gating based on CD16 and CD62L expression, as previously described 31 . The gating strategy for CD16/CD62L-based phenotyping is shown in Supplemental Figure S1.

7. Neutrophil isolation and single-cell library preparation

Immediately after blood collection, polymorphonuclear neutrophils were isolated by layering of EDTAanticoagulated systemic whole blood onto of Polymorph prep™ (Axis-Shield, CAT#1114683; 1:1 v/v), followed by immediate centrifugation at 500g for 35 min at room temperature, as described in detail before 21 . Leftover red blood cells were removed via hypotonic lysis using 10 mL of ice-cold dH₂O for 10-30 seconds, immediately neutralized with 10 mL of 2xPBS (Sigma Aldrich, CAT#D8537), and centrifuged at 400g for 5 minutes at 4°C. For single-cell RNA sequencing, a 100 μL aliquot of the purified PMN suspension (diluted with 1x PBS to 3.5 × 10⁵ cells/mL) was processed according to the GEXSCOPE Single-Cell RNA Library for NEO Handbook 32.

8. Library QC and sequencing of single-cell RNA libraries of isolated neutrophils

The library concentration and fragment size distribution were assessed using a Qubit High Sensitivity dsDNA kit on a Qubit 4 fluorometer (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and the TapeStation High Sensitivity D1000 tape (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). The libraries were pooled in an equimolar fashion and sent to GeneWiz (Azenta Life Sciences, Leipzig, Germany) to be sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq X to an average depth of 30,000 reads/cell in a 2x150 bp manner. The BCL files were demultiplexed to fastq files on Illumina's BaseSpace platform. Libraries were excluded from downstream analysis when sequencing quality control indicated a low median UMI count per cell, consistent with low sample or cDNA quality.

9. Single-cell data analysis

Raw sequencing fastq files were processed into gene expression matrices using CeleScope™ version 2.11.3 (Singleron Biotechnologies, Cologne, Germany, https://github.com/singleron-RD/CeleScope).

Fastq file barcode chemistry determination and barcode correction were performed before being demultiplexed and gene counts generated using the STARSolo single-cell pipeline with STAR version 2.7.11a. The human reference genome (GRCh38) was used for mapping the reads and annotation was done with Ensembl version 99. Cell calling was based on bimodal modeling using the EmptyDrops algorithm. Samples were additionally excluded from downstream analysis if their gene-barcode matrices were dominated by near-empty droplets, with the majority of nominally called cells failing minimum UMI/gene-count thresholds upon reanalysis. Doublet detection and standard per-cell quality-control filtering (based on library size, gene number and mitochondrial and hemoglobin content) were performed per sample prior to merging the individual patient libraries into a single dataset for downstream integration. Non-neutrophil populations were excluded based on marker gene expression. Data were normalized using SCTransform (glmGamPoi), followed by principal component analysis (30 PCs), batch correction using Harmony, and shared nearest-neighbor clustering. A clustering resolution of 0.1, yielding four clusters, was selected based on mean silhouette width. Clusters were annotated using canonical markers, differential gene expression analysis (Wilcoxon rank-sum test; log2 fold change threshold 0.25) and pathway enrichment (clusterProfiler; GO Biological Process). To assess concordance with an independent human neutrophil reference atlas, per-cluster module scores were calculated (AddModuleScore, Seurat) using neutrophilspecific gene signatures derived from a large single-cell atlas of human neutrophils 33.

Transcriptional relationships among clusters were further characterized using trajectory and pseudotime analysis. Trajectory inference was performed using Slingshot 34 . The trajectory was rooted in the immature/banded cluster, based on its high ribosomal gene content, indicating a less mature neutrophil state. Pseudotime values were extracted separately for each lineage. Cells not assigned to a given lineage received no pseudotime value for that lineage and were excluded from the corresponding analysis. To visualize gene expression dynamics along each trajectory, the top four marker genes per cluster were identified. For each gene and each lineage, expression (SCT-normalized) was fitted against pseudotime using local polynomial regression (loess, span=0.3) across 200 evenly spaced points spanning that lineage's pseudotime range. The smoothed values were z-scored per gene within each lineage. To test whether this lineage bias was already detectable within the immature cluster itself, composite module scores representing the interferon-stimulated and aged transcriptional programs were computed. These were derived from genes differentially expressed between these two clusters (FindMarkers, Wilcoxon rank-sum test, log2 fold-change threshold 0.25, adjusted p<0.05), with genes enriched in each cluster scored separately (AddModuleScore). The difference between the two scores (IFN-vs-Aged score) was calculated per cell. Within the immature neutrophil cluster, this score was related to continuous CFR using linear mixed-effects model (lme4), with patient included as a random effect to account for multiple cells per patient and compared against an intercept-only null model by likelihood ratio test.

10. Endothelial cell isolation and bulk RNA sequencing

Endothelial cells were isolated from catheterization material obtained during the diagnostic coronary function test and cultured as described previously 24 . In brief, guidewires were cut into ±5 cm segments and transported on ice in complete endothelial cell medium. To release adherent endothelial cells, the segments were rotated for 10 minutes at 4°C, after which the segments were removed from the medium. Cells were pelleted by centrifugation, resuspended in complete endothelial cell medium and seeded onto a gelatin-coated 12-well plate. Cultures were maintained at 37°C and 5% CO2 with medium refreshed three times weekly. At passage 2, endothelial cells were harvested for bulk RNA sequencing, which was performed as described previously 24.

11. Statistical analysis

Normally distributed continuous variables, as assessed by the Shapiro-Wilk test, are presented as mean ± SD, while non-normally distributed variables are shown as median [IQR]. Categorical variables are reported as number (%). Group comparisons (patients with vs. without a CMD diagnosis, defined as CFR<2.5) were performed using Welch's t-test or the Mann-Whitney U test, as appropriate, and the chisquared or Fisher's exact test for categorical variables. Associations between continuous variables were assessed using Spearman's rank correlation, with age-adjusted estimates obtained from linear regression models. Right-skewed variables (NT-proBNP, NLR, MLR, SII) were log-transformed prior to inclusion in linear regression models. When appropriate, continuous predictors were standardized to obtain effect estimates per SD with 95% confidence interval.

Differential gene expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 on patient-level aggregated (pseudobullk) counts for both the neutrophil single-cell RNA sequencing clusters and the coronary catheterization-derived endothelial cells. For neutrophil clusters, genes were retained for analysis if at least 3 patients had 5 counts and models were adjusted for mean nFeature and cell number per patient.

For endothelial cells, DESeq2 modeled baseline CD62L MFI, measured by AQUIOS flow cytometry as continuous predictor (z-scored to obtain effect estimates per SD) adjusting for age. Genes were retained if at least 3 samples had 10 counts and log2 fold-change estimates were shrunk using the apeglm method. Across both analyses, genes with adjusted p<0.05 were considered significant, and volcano plots were generated using the EnhancedVolcano package. Gene set enrichment analysis was performed using fgsea on genes ranked by the DESeq2 Wald statistic, testing Hallmark gene sets. Pathways with Benjamini-Hochberg-adjusted p<0.05 were considered significant. For the present study, all tests were two-sided, with p<0.05 considered statistically significant unless otherwise specified. Analyses were performed in R (version 4.5.1).

Results

1. Study population

In total, 128 ANOCA patients undergoing coronary function testing consented to participate in the ENDOTYPE biobank for withdrawal of both systemic and local coronary blood. Four patients (3%) were excluded due to obstructive coronary artery disease detected during the procedure, and in one patient (1%), coronary flow reserve could not be measured due to technical difficulties. Ultimately, blood samples were available from 123 ANOCA patients, of whom 84% were women. Given the focus on women in the present study, baseline characteristics of the women are presented in Table 1, while corresponding data of men are provided in Supplemental Table S1.

Among the women in the study, the mean age was 58 ± 10 years. Cardiovascular risk factors were common, including hypertension (47%) and hypercholesterolemia (45%), while 7% had diabetes mellitus and 17% were diagnosed with an auto-immune disease. Acetylcholine spasm provocation testing resulted in a diagnosis of epicardial spasm in 24 women (24%), microvascular spasm in 31 women (30%), and combined epicardial and microvascular spasm in 10 women (10%). An inconclusive spasm test (not meeting the COVADIS criteria) was observed in 28 women (27%), while 9 women (9%) had a negative spasm test. Twenty-three women (22%) had a CFR < 2.5 and were clinically diagnosed with CMD, while 80 women (78%) had a CFR  2.5. Median MRR was significantly lower in women with a CMD diagnosis (2.4 [2.2-2.7]) compared to women without a CMD diagnosis (3.7 [3.3-4.2]) (p<0.001). Baseline demographic characteristics, cardiovascular risk factors, biomarker profiles, comorbidities, and medication use did not differ significantly between women without CMD and with CMD (all p>0.10), with the exception of age, which showed a trend towards a higher age in women with CMD than those without CMD (62 ± 11 years vs. 57 ± 10 years, p=0.078) (Table 1).

Women with CMD are known to have a higher risk for developing heart failure 4 . Therefore, we measured plasma NT-proBNP concentrations. NT-proBNP concentrations were higher in women with CMD compared to those without CMD (234 [136-416] vs. 112 [79-200] pg/mL, p<0.001), and this remained significant after adjustment for age (= -0.154, p<0.001). In addition, NT-proBNP inversely correlated with CFR (rho =-0.24, p=0.023) also when adjusted for age (= -0.154, p=0.038).

2. Systemic neutrophil abundance and neutrophil-related inflammatory indices are inversely associated with CFR in women

First, to study the circulating neutrophil profile of women with ANOCA in this cohort, we assessed systemic complete blood counts (CELL-DYN, n=103) and flow-cytometry derived neutrophil counts (AQUIOS, n=67) in this population. CELL-DYN-derived absolute neutrophil counts were not different between women with and without a CMD diagnosis (4.30 [3.70 -6.06] vs. 4.29 [3.44 -5.17] x10 9 cells/L, p =0.621). However, neutrophil count showed an inverse association with CFR after age adjustment (= -0.217, p=0.022) (Figure 1A). AQUIOS-derived neutrophil counts showed a similar inverse association with CFR, although not statistically significant (rho =0.163, p=0.188; age-adjusted  =-0.204, p=0.082) (Figure 1B).

Clinically, systemic neutrophil-related inflammatory indices, such as the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) and systemic immune-inflammation index (SII, platelet x neutrophil/lymphocyte ratio) have been associated with coronary microvascular function, although findings have been inconsistent [35] [36] [37] [38] . We therefore studied the NLR and the SII. The NLR (log-transformed) showed no difference between women with and without CMD (2.07 [1.74-4.02] vs. 2.26 [1.60-3.02], p=0.539) but was inversely associated with CFR (rho = -0.199, p=0.044; = -0.206, p=0.029) (Figure 1C). Similarly, the SII (logtransformed) showed no difference between women with CMD and without CMD (614.6 [356.5 -1025.6] vs. 532.9 [384.9 -786.2], p=0.408), but a significant inverse correlation with CFR (rho =-0.223, p=0.023; age-adjusted  =-0.252, p=0.007) (Figure 1D).

In local blood samples, collected sequentially during the same catheterization procedure, women (n=94) with a lower CFR similarly had higher values for all three CELL-DYN indices and AQUIOS neutrophil counts, consistent with findings in systemic blood, although the effects were generally weaker (Supplemental Table S2 and Supplemental Figure S2). In contrast to the pattern observed in women, in the smaller cohort of men (CELL-DYN, n=20; AQUIOS, n=9), neutrophil-count parameters showed trends towards positive associations with CFR. However, these trends did not reach statistical significance in either systemic or local blood (Supplemental Table S3 and Supplemental Figure S3) Beyond neutrophil-related measures, eosinophil counts in women showed positive associations with CFR in both systemic and local blood, although the systemic association was no longer significant after age adjustment. In contrast, the association in local blood remained significant after adjustment. Local hemoglobin levels also differed between women with and without CMD, but not after age adjustment (Supplemental Table S2).

Together, these findings suggest that in women with ANOCA, higher systemic neutrophil abundance and neutrophil-related inflammatory indices are associated with a lower CFR, independent of age. Next, we examined whether neutrophil characteristics beyond cell abundance, including cellular activation and phenotype, were associated with CFR.

3. Flow cytometry characterization of neutrophils links activated CD62L dim neutrophils and CFR

To test the hypothesis that women with a low CFR have more activated neutrophils compared to women with a normal CFR, we tested neutrophil surface marker expression. We used flow cytometry (AQUIOS, n=67) to measure a panel of neutrophil activation markers, including CD62L, CD64, CD10, CD16, and CD11b. Both neutrophils isolated from systemic blood at baseline and after ex vivo stimulation with fNLF were characterized. CD62L is constitutively expressed on resting neutrophils and is rapidly shed from the cell surface upon activation 39 . We therefore used an established cutoff (as described in the Methods section) to quantify the proportion of CD62L bright and CD62L dim neutrophils, the latter reflecting a more activated neutrophil phenotype.

At baseline, a higher percentage of CD62L dim neutrophils was associated with lower CFR (rho = -0.189, p=0.126; age-adjusted =-0.274 per SD, p=0.018) (Figure 2A and B). Consistent with this, the MFI of CD62L showed the same trend towards a positive association with CFR (rho = 0.173, p=0.160; ageadjusted  = 0.196 per SD, p=0.094). The MFIs of CD64, CD10, CD16 and CD11b showed no association with CFR (all p>0.50) (Figure 2A).

To test whether this higher neutrophil activation in women with low CFR reflected a difference in baseline state or a difference in activation capacity, we assessed the percentage of CD62L dim neutrophils after ex vivo stimulation with the bacterial peptide fNLF. The percentage of CD62L dim neutrophils was no longer associated with CFR after stimulation (Figure 2C). This suggests that the association with CFR may reflect a baseline activation state of circulating neutrophils rather than differences in their response to stimulation.

In local coronary blood, the percentage of CD62L dim neutrophils showed a similar, although weaker association with CFR (Supplemental Figure S2). In men (n=9), the association in local blood was in the opposite direction compared to women and reached statistical significance. In systemic blood, no significant association was observed, and the direction was inconsistent between unadjusted and ageadjusted models (Supplemental Figure S3).

Overall, lower CFR in women was associated with a higher percentage of CD62L dim neutrophils at baseline, which suggests a link between reduced CFR and an altered neutrophil activation state.

4. Single-cell RNA sequencing reveals an interferon-activated neutrophil cluster associated with CMD and a reduced CFR

To characterize the transcriptional heterogeneity of circulating neutrophils, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing of isolated neutrophils from ANOCA patients (n=16, 12 women and 4 men). After quality control, neutrophils from 11 ANOCA patients (8 women and 3 men) contributed to a total of 55,915 cells that were analyzed. After exclusion of contaminating cell populations, the final dataset comprised 53,216 neutrophils from 11 patients (Supplemental Table S4), with a median of 4,747 cells per patient and 416 detected genes per cell.

Unsupervised clustering of the neutrophils identified four transcriptionally distinct circulating neutrophil clusters (Figure 3A). Based on marker gene expression and pathway enrichment, these clusters were annotated as 1) An immature cluster (RPSA, RPS8, RPL22, RPS12), 2) A mature cluster (MMP9, RFLNB, PADI4, S100A12), 3) An aged cluster (SLPI, PI3, SIGLEC10, ADGRE2) and 4) An interferon (IFN)-activated cluster (RSAD2, ISG15, HERC5, IFIT1) (Figure 3B, Supplemental Table S5). Each neutrophil cluster was defined by a distinct transcriptional program (Figure 3C, Supplemental Table S6). The immature cluster was enriched for pathways involved in protein synthesis, consistent with a less mature neutrophil state. The mature cluster was enriched for leukocyte activation and chemotaxis, the aged cluster for ferroptosis and innate immune signaling, and the IFNactivated cluster for antiviral/interferon-response pathways. Comparison with an independent human neutrophil reference atlas 33 , showed concordant signatures for the mature, aged and IFN-activated cluster, but not for the immature cluster, which is instead characterized by high ribosomal gene expression rather than classic granule genes (Supplemental Figure S4).

The proportion of IFN-activated neutrophils was more than doubled in patients with a CMD diagnosis compared to those without a CMD diagnosis (median 17.1% vs. 6.3%, p=0.048, adj. p=0.0036) (Figure 3D). This was also true for the relation with CFR which proved to be inverse with CFR (rho=-0.74, p=0.013; β=-0.65, adj. p=0.087) (Figure 3E). The proportions of the other three neutrophil clusters were similar between patients with CMD and without CMD (all p>0.53).

Given that surface CD62L shedding is a well-established marker of neutrophil activation 39 , we examined whether the IFN-activated neutrophil state was associated with this activation phenotype by relating its proportion to neutrophil surface CD62L MFI in the same patients. We found that the proportion of IFNactivated neutrophils was inversely correlated with CD62L MFI (n=10, rho=-0.68, p=0.030; β=-0.67, adj. p=0.024) (Figure 3F) in ANOCA patients. In addition, SELL transcript expression (the gene encoding CD62L), was positively correlated with the proportion of IFN-activated neutrophils (n=11, rho = 0.74, p=0.013; β=0.77, adj. p=0.0083)) and negatively correlated with CFR (rho = -0.78, p=0.007; β=-0.71, adj. p=0.051) (Figure 3G). Together, these findings identify an IFN-activated neutrophil cluster that characterized both CMD and a lower CFR, and that was related to the shedding of CD62L from the cell surface. These findings were consistent in the women-only analysis, with similar trends for the associations between the IFN-activated cluster and CMD diagnosis, CFR, CD62L MFI and SELL expression (Supplemental Figure S5).

To assess whether the four clusters represent independent states or a transcriptional continuum, we performed pseudotime trajectory analysis, setting the immature neutrophil cluster as a starting point. This identified two trajectories originating from the immature cluster and passing through the mature cluster, one terminating in the IFN-activated cluster and the other in the aged cluster (Figure 3H and 3I). Within the lineage terminating in IFN-activated neutrophils, we identified that pseudotime position along the trajectory towards the IFN-activated fate (trajectory 2, Figure 3H) was further advanced in patients with a lower CFR (rho = -0.64, p=0.033) (Figure 3J). This supports that both the proportion of the IFN-activated neutrophils is higher and neutrophils overall are further along the IFN trajectory in patients with a lower CFR.

5. Immature neutrophils exhibit a distinct translational gene signature associated with low CFR suggestive of altered maturation

To investigate whether CFR-associated transcriptional changes were specific to particular neutrophil clusters, we performed cluster-specific pseudobulk differential expression analyses, modelling CFR as a continuous variable. We did not observe significant differentially expressed genes in the mature, the IFN-activated, or aged neutrophil clusters. In contrast, the immature cluster exhibited a distinct transcriptional signature associated with CFR. This comprised 140 differentially expressed genes (adjusted p <0.05, corrected for sequencing depth and cell number). This signature was dominated by genes encoding ribosomal proteins and translation machinery (e.g., RPL9, RPL32, RPL6, RPS4X, RPL13, RPS17) (Figure 4A, Supplemental Table S8). Gene set enrichment analysis showed that pathways indicating proliferation and biosynthesis (e.g., MYC targets, E2F targets, and oxidative phosphorylation) were lower in immature neutrophils from patients with a lower CFR measurement.

Pathways reflecting cytokine-driven inflammatory signaling (e.g., IFN response and TNFα signaling) were higher in immature neutrophils from patients with a lower CFR measurement (Figure 4B, Supplemental Table S9). Consistent with this inflammatory phenotype, immature neutrophils from patients with a lower CFR also showed higher expression of CXCR1 and CXCR2, which encode receptors for CXCL6, a chemokine that we recently found to be more abundant in the plasma of ANOCA patients with a lower CFR (Supplemental Figure S6) (Kregting, BHCM et al., unpublished data, 2026).

. A parallel analysis using CMD diagnosis (CFR<2.5) as a binary variable showed similar results, with 83 of the 140 CFR-associated genes also significantly differentially expressed by CMD status (Supplemental Table S9 and S10). A sensitivity analysis restricted to women yielded a highly similar signature across both comparisons (Supplemental Table S11-S14). Thus, in addition to the higher percentage of IFN-activated neutrophils, lower CFR was associated with a distinct transcriptional phenotype in immature neutrophils marked by lower expression in ribosomal/translational and proliferative gene expression alongside increased inflammatory signaling. As IFN response signaling was enriched in immature neutrophils from patients with a lower CFR, we hypothesized that this reflects an early skewing towards the IFN-activated phenotype. We therefore asked whether immature neutrophils from patients with low CFR already resemble the IFN-activated cluster. Within the immature neutrophils, transcriptional profiles were leaning towards an IFN-activated (relative to an aged) phenotype in patients with a lower CFR (p=0.024, mixed-effects model accounting for multiple cells per patient) (Figure 4C, Supplemental Table S15), suggesting that immature neutrophils in patients with lower CFR are already transcriptionally primed towards an IFN-activated state.

6. Lower CD62L MFI on neutrophil surface is linked to altered endothelial transcriptional programs

In ANOCA patients, the IFN-activated neutrophil cluster we identified was associated with lower surface expression of CD62L, which is known to be relevant for neutrophil adhesion and migration 39.

We hypothesized that a lower CD62L MFI might be linked to endothelial cell states. To study this, we isolated coronary endothelial cells from catheterization material used during the diagnostic coronary function test in these patients (n=7, all women, Supplemental Table S16), as described before 24 . We performed bulk RNA sequencing and differential gene expression analysis using CD62L MFI as measured by the AQUIOS as a continuous variable.

We identified 31 genes that significantly increased (Log2FC <-0.25; e.g., MT1X, MT2A, LDLR, MT1E, RSPO3, HMGCS1, CRIP1, FASN, ERAP2, ECI2) and 161 genes that significantly decreased (Log2FC>0.25; e.g., CCL14, TWIST2, SNED1, CLU, APOD, CXCL16, CORO2B, ACTA2, TSPAN15, AQP1) with a lower CD62L MFI (adjusted p <0.05) (Figure 5A, Supplemental Table S17). Among . these, LDLR encodes the LDL receptor, mediating cellular cholesterol uptake 40 , while TWIST2 and ACTA2 are established markers of endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition 41, 42 . Gene set enrichment analysis (Hallmark) revealed significant positive enrichment of proliferative and metabolic pathways with a lower CD62L MFI (all adjusted p<0.05). Conversely, pathways reflecting IFN, complement and coagulation signaling, together with epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition and inflammatory/chemokine signaling, were negatively enriched with a lower CD62L MFI (all adjusted p<0.05) (Figure 5B, Supplemental Table S18). These findings suggest that a lower CD62L MFI is associated with a shift in endothelial gene expression, characterized by reduced expression of genes and pathways characteristic of an immune-competent, quiescent endothelial phenotype and increased expression of proliferative and metabolic genes.

Discussion

In this study, we characterized the circulating neutrophil phenotype in women with CMD and lower CFR using multiple complementary approaches. Women with coronary microvascular dysfunction (CMD) and lower coronary flow reserve (CFR) showed a distinct circulating neutrophil phenotype.

Lower CFR was associated with higher neutrophil counts and inflammatory indices (NLR, SII), more activated CD62L dim neutrophils, and a larger interferon (IFN)-activated neutrophil cluster identified by single-cell RNA sequencing. Immature neutrophils from these patients had reduced ribosomal and translational gene expression and were already skewed toward the IFN-activated profile, which may point to altered neutrophil maturation. In patient-derived endothelial cells, lower CD62L expression on circulating neutrophils was linked to a shift from an immune-supportive state toward greater proliferative and metabolic activity, suggesting these neutrophils may affect endothelial biology.

The pathophysiology of CMD remains incompletely understood. Previous studies on microvascular diseases have primarily focused on circulating neutrophil counts and NETosis 18, 19, 35, 43 , providing limited insight into neutrophil activation and transcriptional heterogeneity. By combining complete blood counts, flow cytometric activation phenotyping and single-cell transcriptomics, our study provides a multidimensional characterization of circulating neutrophils in relation to CMD. Importantly, these analyses were performed in a well-phenotyped clinical cohort with invasively measured CFR, enabling assessment of the relationship between neutrophil phenotype and an objective, quantitative measure of coronary microvascular function.

The female predominance of CMD raises the question whether sex-specific immune biology contributes to the neutrophil phenotype observed in our study. Indeed, neutrophils from healthy young women show stronger type I IFN signatures than those from men, despite similar circulating IFN activity and IFN receptor expression. This difference appears to reflect enhanced intracellular signaling, including greater nuclear localization of IFN-responsive transcription factors, rather than greater IFN exposure. The female-biased response was linked to estrogen and was absent before puberty, suggesting that sex hormones may shape neutrophil responsiveness 44 . Complementing these observations, a recent singlecell atlas of human neutrophils identified a higher proportion of neutrophils expressing combined type I and II IFN-associated programs in healthy female donors 33 . Together, these observations suggest that the neutrophil phenotype observed in our CMD cohort reflects, in part, an amplification of pre-existing sex differences in neutrophil IFN responsiveness. Related evidence of sex-specific IFN-associated pathways in cardiovascular disease, including heart failure with preserved ejection fraction 45 and other female-predominant conditions 46, 47 , further supports the potential relevance of sex-dependent IFN signaling in shaping immune phenotypes and contributing to disease susceptibility. This raises the possibility that these differences precede clinical disease, although longitudinal studies are needed to establish their contribution to CMD.

Importantly, our findings suggest that this IFN-biased phenotype may emerge already early in neutrophil maturation. Our pseudotime analysis indicated that immature neutrophils from patients with lower CFR were already transcriptionally skewed towards an IFN-activated state. Consistent with this, the association between CD62L dim neutrophils and CFR observed at baseline was no longer evident following ex vivo fNLF stimulation. Although stimulation may alter CD62L surface expression and obscure baseline differences, this finding suggests that lower CFR is associated with a pre-existing neutrophil state that is not simply explained by an enhanced acute response to stimulation.

Endothelial dysfunction contributes to impaired coronary microvascular regulation in CMD 3 . The proportion of IFN-activated neutrophils identified in our study was associated with a reduced surface CD62L, pointing to altered neutrophil-endothelial interactions. CD62L mediates initial neutrophil rolling, and its shedding accompanies activation and the transition to firm adhesion 39 . Despite lower surface CD62L, SELL transcript expression was higher with increasing proportions of IFN-activated neutrophils, potentially reflecting ongoing transcription alongside protein shedding. However, our data cannot establish whether shedding is ongoing or whether these neutrophils interact directly with the endothelium. In our endothelial cell data, we found that lower neutrophil CD62L MFI was associated with increased endothelial proliferative and metabolic activity, consistent with a shift away from a quiescent state. This may be consistent with evidence that type I IFN signaling can promote endothelial activation and leukocyte recruitment, reduce nitric oxide bioavailability, impair endothelial repair and promote NET formation and thrombosis [48] [49] [50] . IFN-activated neutrophils may therefore influence endothelial function through altered adhesion or inflammatory signaling, potentially contributing to microvascular dysfunction. However, the direction of this relationship remains unclear: the neutrophil phenotype may contribute to endothelial dysfunction, arise in response to pre-existing abnormalities, or reflect a shared underlying process. These findings raise the possibility that IFN-associated neutrophil activation may be a therapeutic target in CMD, although further functional studies are needed to establish its causal role and therapeutic relevance.

Future work should build on our associative findings in several directions. Longitudinal studies are needed to determine whether this neutrophil phenotype precedes the development of CMD or arises as a consequence of reduced CFR. Some of our data suggests that, at least in part, this phenotype might be encoded already in immature neutrophil stages. Functional studies are needed to establish whether CD62L shedding and the IFN-activated neutrophil phenotype causally affect neutrophil-endothelial adhesion and coronary microvascular function. In addition, the higher CXCR1 and CXCR2 expression in immature neutrophils from patients with a lower CFR, together with our parallel observation of elevated plasma CXCL6 in patients with a lower CFR, suggests a candidate signaling axis that merits further mechanistic investigation (Kregting, BHCM et al., unpublished data, 2026). It also remains unclear whether this circulating phenotype preferentially affects the coronary microcirculation or instead, reflects a more generalized marker of systemic microvascular vulnerability. Examining whether specific features of the coronary endothelium, or its hemodynamic environment confer susceptibility, would help clarify this. Finally, direct measurements of circulating IFN levels and sex-hormones would allow the proposed estrogen-driven mechanism of neutrophil IFN predisposition to be tested directly, rather than inferred from prior literature in healthy cohorts.

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes conclusions about causality, as discussed above. Second, this was a single-center study, which may limit generalizability.

Third, the number of men included for single-cell RNA sequencing and the supplementary subgroup analyses was small, limiting statistical power for these analyses.

In conclusion, this study provides the first detailed phenotypic characterization of circulating neutrophils in patients with CMD. We identified a circulating neutrophil phenotype marked by an increase in IFN-activated neutrophils, an IFN-enriched immature neutrophil phenotype and CD62L shedding, with potential relevance to coronary endothelial function. These findings provide pathophysiological insight into a condition that disproportionately affects women, positioning neutrophil activation as a candidate contributor to CMD and underscoring the importance of sex-specific research in cardiovascular disease.
