Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Antibiotics are among the most widely prescribed drugs, and they reliably disrupt the commensal gut microbiota. Large epidemiological studies link antibiotic exposure to type 2 diabetes (T2D) risk, yet smaller randomized intervention trials have failed to find this connection. Whether this discordance reflects a true lack of signal or subpopulation-dependent responses to antibiotics remains unclear. Here, across three independent cohorts (N=4,122), we show that recent antibiotic use may reshape host physiology in specific dietary, microbial, and metabolic contexts. Recent antibiotic use was associated with reduced gut microbiome richness, greater Bacteroides and pathobiont dominance, lower predicted butyrate production, and altered levels of 34 blood metabolites, including several microbe-host co-metabolites, such as hippurate and isoursodeoxycholic acid, while the blood proteome was unchanged. Diet and microbiome composition influenced the magnitude of these effects: preference for liquor was associated with steeper diversity loss, and the presence of specific bacterial genera either dampened or amplified antibiotic-associated blood metabolite differences. Clinical chemistries did not differ, on average, across antibiotic treament groups, affirming the intervention trials. However, recent antibiotic users with T2D showed elevated methylmalonic acid, homocysteine, and alkaline phosphatase, suggesting B-vitamin deficiency. Furthermore, antibiotic users with poorer metabolic health (e.g., blood metabolomics-inferredBMI > measured BMI) showed lower circulating 25-hydroxyvitamin D. Overall, metabolic disruption associated with recent antibiotic use appears to be related to vitamin deficiencies and is concentrated in metabolically vulnerable subgroups, arguing for further research into context-aware prescribing and targeted cotherapies, like higher fiber diets, probiotics, or supplements.

Introduction

Fecal metagenomic and 16S amplicon sequencing analyses have revealed that antibiotic treatment can promote microbiome phenotypes mirroring those of individuals with cardiometabolic disease or T2D 1, 2 . Similarly, antibiotic exposure has been linked to increased risk of developing Type 2 Diabetes (T2D) in epidemiological studies that leverage either electronic health records [3] [4] [5] [6] [7] [8] [9] [10] or environmental monitoring 11, 12 . These results are, perhaps, unsurprising, given that the gut microbiota is known to contribute to host energy balance 13, 14 and has been widely associated with a range of obesity-related phenotypes 13, [15] [16] [17] . However, epidemiological studies are limited by unmeasured confounders and lack the causal certainty of a randomized intervention trial. Most epidemiological studies linking antibiotic use with T2D risk show a positive dose-response association, yet adjustment for confounding variables appears to ablate this association. For example, a nested case-control study from Alberta's Tomorrow Project found no association between antibiotic use and T2D after controlling for self-reported clinical and lifestyle measures (e.g., socioeconomic status, body mass index (BMI), leisure time physical activity and overall diet quality) that are not always captured in other observational cohorts 5 . This finding is consistent with smaller, randomized, placebo-controlled intervention studies that have not found lasting impacts on host metabolism following antibiotic treatment 18, 19 . In aggregate, the existing literature suggests that there is a need to collect more extensive molecular phenotype data in addition to other lifestyle and dietary metadata in antibiotic users, as the putative metabolic risk of antibiotic treatment may vary with population-or individual-level variables 5, [20] [21] [22] [23] . Antibiotic-induced perturbations of the gut microbiota include transient declines in total bacterial biomass, changes in community composition, and incomplete ecological recovery over time [24] [25] [26] . Indeed, a recent epidemiological study linked gut microbiome samples from three Swedish cohorts to the Swedish National Prescribed Drug Register and detected persistent ecological effects of certain antibiotic classes, such as fluoroquinolones, up to eight years post-treatment. 2 The ecological resistance and resilience of the gut microbiota to antibiotic treatment can vary across individuals and appears to be related to baseline microbiome composition, dietary intake, and host behavior [27] [28] [29] . Prior work from our group and from others has found an intimate connection between human gut microbiome composition and the abundances of circulating metabolites in blood [30] [31] [32] , which aligns with antibiotic treatment studies showing how antibiotics can affect the blood metabolome. For example, a recent study demonstrated that antibiotic use strongly impacts the fecal metabolome and modestly influences plasma metabolites 33, 34 . Likewise, lower serum levels of five metabolites-all of which can involve bacterial metabolism [35] [36] [37] -were reported in 20 hospitalized patients who received antibiotics, compared to five hospitalized controls 38 . Many interventional studies have investigated the impact of various antibiotic classes on the commensal gut microbiota in humans and rodents 18, 19, [39] [40] [41] . However, these studies often have small sample sizes, limiting their statistical power and generalizability. In contrast, for many large, cross-sectional cohort studies that collect stool and blood samples for multi-omic analyses, recent antibiotic use is treated as an exclusion criterion, rather than as an exposure of interest. Baldanzi et al. clearly demonstrated the value of linking prescription drug information to microbiome data in order to learn about the long-term consequences of antibiotics on the gut microbiome, but they lacked paired host molecular phenotype data 2 . The broader consequences of recent antibiotic use on host physiology, as assessed through dense multiomic measurements and clinical chemistries, across thousands of individuals with different dietary, lifestyle, and gut microbiota contexts, remain poorly understood. Here, we used data from 4,122 individuals across three large, deeply phenotyped populations to characterize the context-dependent impacts of recent antibiotic use on the host molecular phenotype by integrating gut microbiome, blood metabolome, blood proteome, clinical chemistry, and questionnaire data on diet, demographics, and behavior.

Results

Study design and cohort composition

We used data from two United States-based cohorts and one European cohort: Arivale (n = 2,666) 42 , Hispanic Community Health Study/Study of Latinos (SOL; n = 866) 43 , and MetaCardis (MC; n = 590) 1, 44 , respectively. The Arivale cohort consists primarily of non-Hispanic, white adults who were formerly subscribers of the Arivale Scientific Wellness program. 45, 46 Individuals in Arivale for whom antibiotic usage information was available were eligible for analysis (n = 3,155), resulting in a cohort of 2,666 after excluding participants with missing covariate data (Fig. S1, methods). We validated our Arivale findings using data from HCHS/SOL and MetaCardis. The HCHS/SOL cohort consists of 16,415 Hispanic adults (USborn and non-US-born) residing in the US; analyses were restricted to those with antibiotic usage and multi-omic data (N=866) whose clinical data were collected within 60 days of the stool sample. Finally, the MC cohort includes European individuals across the spectrum of cardiometabolic disease. For this study, we analyzed MC cohort data of individuals without heart disease and available fecal metagenomes, blood metabolomes, antibiotic use data (N=590). Antibiotic use metadata were operationalized differently across the three cohorts; Arivale and HCHS/SOL use time since last antibiotic use with cutoffs at 12 or 6 months (Fig. 1A, Table S1), while MC counted the number of antibiotic courses in the five years prior to sampling. Importantly, antibiotic recency groups represent broad windows, so time since last antibiotic use is unresolved within each group (e.g., within the 3-month group, an individual sampled 10 days after antibiotic use is grouped with one sampled 90 days after). Moreover, our exposure includes the association for both the antibiotic and the treated condition, which we cannot distinguish in this study. Antibiotic use within the past three months or within the past twelve months was reported by 428 (16.1%) and 593 (22.2%) Arivale participants, respectively, with the remaining 1,645 (61.7%) reporting no antibiotic use in the last year. In HCHS/SOL, 136 (15.0%) reported use within three months and 72 (8.3%) within the previous 6 months. Because analyses were restricted to participants whose samples were collected within 30 days (Arivale) or 60 days (SOL) of antibiotic recency ascertainment, per-assay sample sizes are smaller than the full cohorts and vary by data type (Fig. 1A).

We used multivariable linear regression to assess cross-sectional variation in host molecular and microbiome features associated with recent antibiotic use. Models for the discovery cohort (Arivale) were adjusted for age, sex, BMI, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), the first five principal components of genetic ancestry, and cholesterol-lowering and blood sugarlowering medication use ("full model"). Microbiome or clinical chemistry vendors were added as covariates, where applicable. For HCHS/SOL models, covariates included age, sex, study center, BMI, and eGFR. Birthplace was an additional covariate in microbiome models only. MC models were adjusted for gender, age, BMI, microbial load, patient group, study center, antilipid medication use, and antidiabetic medication use. Recent antibiotic use is associated with changes in the structure and function of the gut microbiome Using linear regression models, we tested the association of antibiotic recency with three alpha diversity measures representing richness (observed features), dominance (Berger-Parker index), and a hybrid metric that takes both richness and evenness (inverse dominance) measures into account (Shannon index) 47 . Both the 3-month and 12-month groups exhibited significantly fewer observed features and lower Shannon diversity than those without antibiotic use (two-tailed t-test, Benjamini-Hochberg [BH] q < 0.1). In contrast, we observed a significant positive relationship between the Berger-Parker index and the recency of antibiotic use (β3 = 0.05, BH q = 4.88 x 10 -12 ; β12 = 0.02 BH q = 0.005), indicating that more recent antibiotic use is associated with decreased richness and increased dominance (Fig. 1B). In the HCHS/SOL cohort, estimates for all three metrics were directionally consistent with the Arivale findings, with reduced observed features and Shannon diversity in addition to increased Berger-Parker dominance in both recency groups relative to no reported use. However, in the HCHS/SOL cohort, only observed features in the six month group reached significance (β = -4.11, SE = 1.80, BH q = 0.07; Fig. 1C). Shannon diversity was similarly reduced in both the 3-(β = -0.09, SE = 0.05, BH q = 0.15) and 6-month groups (β = -0.11, SE = 0.06, BH q = 0.10).

Next, to examine how antibiotic use may influence interindividual variation in the gut microbiome, we performed principal coordinate analysis using a Bray-Curtis dissimilarity matrix computed from a rarefied ASV-level abundance matrix (21,869 reads per sample) in the Arivale cohort (Fig. 1D). PERMANOVA and PERMDISP revealed that the community structure and dispersion across the three antibiotic recency groups was significantly different (p-value = 0.0001; 9,999 permutations for each analysis). Follow-up pairwise comparisons confirmed that each group was significantly different from the others (BH q < 0.1; 9,999 permutations for all comparisons). For a subset of 791 individuals in the Arivale cohort, a second stool sample was available at least 60 days after the first stool sample (mean time between samples: 434 days). Antibiotic recency was defined relative to the first stool sample (Fig. 1A & 1B) and held fixed for both timepoints; it describes how recently antibiotics had been used at baseline, not continued use between samples. We assessed the longitudinal change in gut microbiome alpha diversity using linear mixed-effects models (Fig. 1E). Using a likelihood ratio test (LRT) of models with and without the antibiotic recency and time interaction term, we determined that the trajectories of observed features and Shannon index differed by antibiotic recency group (BH q = 2.6 x 10 -4 and 9.9 x 10 -3 , respectively). Estimated marginal slopes for observed features were monotonically ordered by recency (3 months: 1.33 genera per 100 days, 95% CI 0.70-1.96; 12 months: 0.46, -0.03-0.96; None: -0.08, -0.37-0.22), with the 3-month group differing from those reporting no recent use (Tukey-adjusted p = 1.0 x 10). Having established that recent antibiotic use is associated with measurable impacts on alpha and beta diversity measures, we sought to investigate whether we could detect potential functional differences in the gut microbiome of recent antibiotic users. We used microbial community-scale metabolite models (MCMMs) 48 to predict short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) production in Arivale participants, using microbiome data and fiber intake estimates from dietary questionnaires as constraints 49 . We tested the Arivale antibiotic recency groups for differences in predicted butyrate and propionate production (Fig. 1F), finding that the gut microbiomes of individuals in the 3-month group were predicted to produce less butyrate (β = -1.46, BH q = 0.001) and propionate (β = -0.81, BH q = 7.4 x 10 -6 ). Given that we were working with 16S amplicon sequencing data in the Arivale cohort, we used Picrust2 50, 51 to generate carbohydrate-active enzyme (CAZyme) family gene abundance predictions (Fig. 1G), finding that both the 3-and 12-month groups harbor fewer predicted CAZymes (two-tailed t-test, p < 0.05). Moreover, we leveraged HCHS/SOL stool shotgun metagenomic data to further investigate the CAZyme repertoire following antibiotic use. In these samples, CAZyme annotations were generated using Cayman 52 . Fewer unique CAZyme families were observed in both 3-and the 6-month groups, compared to the group reporting no antibiotic use within the past 6 months (Fig. 1H; two-tailed t-test, p < 0.05), and the overall structure of the CAZyme repertoire was significantly different between the antibiotic recency groups (Fig. S2, PERMANOVA, R² =0.004, p = 0.002). Finally, we observed an enrichment of Bacteroides-dominated enterotypes in antibiotic users across both Arivale and HCHS/SOL cohorts (Fig. S3). In Arivale, the strongest enrichment was for the Bac2 enterotype (one of the four enterotypes assigned to Arivale participants, using a Dirichlet Multinomial Mixture model 53 , which was enriched in the genus Bacteroides and depleted in butyrate producers) in individuals reporting antibiotic use in the last 3 months (OR = 2.89, BH q = 6.96 x 10 -13 , two-sided Wald test) and in the last 12 months (OR = 1.75, BH q = 4.04 x 10 -05 , two-sided Wald test). This was accompanied by a concomitant decrease in the proportion of individuals assigned to the Ruminococcaceae (Rum) enterotype in recent antibiotic users (OR3 = 0.48, BH q = 1.23 x 10 -06 ; OR12 = 0.54, BH q = 9.62 x 10 -06 , two-sided Wald tests).

Genus-level associations with antibiotic recency

Having detected high-level structural differences in the gut microbiome of recent antibiotic users, we next investigated variation in gut microbiome composition using two approaches. First, we investigated potential changes in centered log ratio (CLR) transformed relative abundances (Fig. 2, inner heatmap) by constructing individual linear regression models for all genera with prevalence above 10% in the Arivale cohort (n = 188, Table S2). In line with previous studies of antibiotic use in human cohorts 18, 54 , we observed increased abundances of Enterococcus, Veillonella, and Escherichia/Shigella in individuals who reported recent antibiotic use (BH q < 0.1). However, many of these findings were significant only in the 3-month group, indicating that antibiotic-associated differences in the abundances of gut bacterial genera were dampened over longer recovery times. Additionally, we observed an increase in the relative abundance of Sellimonas (β3 = 0.73, two-tailed t-test, BH q = 5.46 x 10 -08 ), which has recently been proposed as a marker of post-disturbance recovery in the gut 55 . Because individuals with recent antibiotic use have decreased gut microbiome richness, some genera may be below the limit of detection or have failed to recover in these individuals. Thus, we also used logistic regression models to identify genera (n = 209, Table S3) with higher or lower odds of being detected in the gut microbiome of antibiotic users (Fig. 2, outer heatmap). Most genera were associated with decreased odds of detection in the gut microbiome of recent antibiotic users (OR < 1), consistent with reduced richness following antibiotic exposure. A minority, however, showed the opposite trend: 17 genera had increased odds of detection in at least one of the two recent antibiotic use groups, predominantly low-prevalence genera. Citrobacter, for instance, was detected in 21/319 (6.6%) of participants reporting antibiotic use within the last three months and 25/429 (5.8%) within twelve months, compared to 27/1219 (2.2%) of non-users (OR3 = 2.89, BH q = 0.001; OR12 = 2.58, BH q = 0.006). For a small subset of genera, there were inconsistent trends between the logistic and linear regressions. Certain genera were associated with increased odds of detection in recent antibiotic users and, when present, were more abundant than in non-users-like Sellimonas, for example. However, genera such as Gastranaerophilales and Parasutterella had lower odds of being detected among recent antibiotic users but then showed an increase in relative abundance in antibiotic users when they were present (Fig. 2).

Recent antibiotic use impacts the blood metabolome, but not the blood proteome

We next investigated whether or not we could detect variation in the blood metabolome and proteome associated with recent antibiotic use. Starting with the Arivale cohort, we built individual linear regression models for metabolites with over 70% prevalence (n = 969, Table S4), finding 32 metabolites with altered abundance in the 3-month group (Fig. 3A, two-tailed ttests, BH q < 0.1). Only 5 were altered in the 12-month group, reflecting similar recovery trends observed in the gut microbiome diversity and composition. Two unannotated metabolites (X-12283 and X-17351) were significantly different in the 12-month group but not in the 3-month group, suggesting a temporal association or unmeasured group-specific differences. We refer to the union of these two sets (n = 34 metabolites) as antibiotic-responsive metabolites. In most cases, metabolites were significantly reduced in abundance in recent antibiotic users compared with the non-users. These antibiotic-responsive metabolites include host-microbe cometabolites, and the majority were shown to be more closely associated with the gut microbiome than with host genetics in a prior study 30 . Finally, we assessed whether recent antibiotic use is associated with differences in the blood proteome. After applying a 70% prevalence threshold, we tested 274 proteins (adjusting for the full set of covariates), finding no significant differences in the levels of these circulating proteins between antibiotic users and non-users (Table S5). To assess the robustness of the metabolite associations, we next pursued validation in two external cohorts, constructing individual linear regression models for only the 34 antibioticresponsive metabolites, if present in the external cohort. In the HCHS/SOL cohort, 13 of these metabolites, including several amino acid and fatty acid derivatives, were statistically significantly different in the recent antibiotic users, and the directionality was consistent (BH q < 0.1). We next validated these results in the MC cohort 1 . Because antibiotic use in the previous 5 years is enumerated in MC, we used two approaches for validation in this cohort: binarizing the variable to assess the impact of using any number of courses over the previous 5 years (most similar to Arivale), and using the total number of courses as a continuous variable. Unsurprisingly, given the longer antibiotic recency time window, fewer metabolites were statistically significantly altered in the MC cohort (N=3, BH q < 0.1). In particular, differences in cinnamoylglycine, 4-hydroxyhippurate, and X-12544 levels were statistically significant and directionally consistent among antibiotic users in all 3 cohorts.

Antibiotic-responsive blood metabolites show variable associations with metabolic health status

We next assessed the association between the 34 antibiotic-responsive metabolites and T2D phenotypes (Fig. 3B, Table S6). Blood sugar-lowering medication use was included as a covariate in the Arivale models, as medication intake can account for a substantial fraction of apparent disease-metabolome associations 1 . Examining this coefficient, 12 of the 34 antibioticresponsive metabolites were higher in Arivale participants reporting blood sugar-lowering medication use (including branched-chain amino acids catabolites and monohydroxy fatty acids), while indolepropionate was lower (BH q < 0.1). Because MC includes individuals along the cardiometabolic spectrum, we also examined the patient group coefficient contrasting T2D against healthy individuals, adjusted for antidiabetic medication use. Individuals with T2D in MC had lower levels of hippurate, 3-phenylpropionate, and cinnamoylglycine, consistent with prior reports associating lower hippurate with metabolic syndrome 56, 57 and of reduced 3phenylpropionate and cinnamoylglycine with T2D 58 . We next compiled results from three published studies investigating the relationship between circulating metabolites and glycemic phenotypes in diverse cohorts [59] [60] [61] . Yousri et al. built linear regression models for 826 metabolites and prevalent T2D in a Qatari cohort, validating the results with data from the Qatar Biobank 59 . Wu et al. pursued serum metabolite associations with impaired fasting glucose, impaired glucose tolerance, and combined glucose intolerance/T2D in two independent Swedish cohorts 60 . Finally, Li et al. examined 469 metabolites in relation to incident T2D across ten population-based cohorts (including SOL) of initially T2D-free participants followed for up to 26 years 61 . Thus, effect estimates are not comparable (betas [Yousri et al.], odds ratios [Wu et al.], and hazard ratios [Li et al.], respectively; Fig. 3B), but the directions of association can be compared across these designs. Of the three studies, the linear regression models built by Yousri and colleagues most closely resemble the Arivale models, and the direction of association between the antibiotic-responsive metabolites and prevalent T2D is also largely consistent with blood sugar medication use associations in Arivale. Findings from the latter two studies are sparse because the regressions were limited to results observed in both discovery and validation cohorts (Wu et al.) or metabolites successfully harmonized across the 10 cohorts (Li et al.). Notably, compiling these prior associations lets us visualize divergence between antibiotic-responsive and glucose intolerance-associated metabolites. That is, for five metabolites-isoursodeoxycholate, indolepropionate, hippurate, 3-phenylpropionate, and cinnamoylglycine-the direction of association with phenotypes along the glucose-dysregulation spectrum is concordant with recent antibiotic use across at least 2 patient groups or studies. Meanwhile, 14 metabolites, such as alpha-ketobutyrate and 4-methyl-2-oxopentanoate, were discordant: depleted in recent antibiotic users yet positively associated with glucose dysregulation. The remaining 15 metabolites had no clear association with glucose dysregulation phenotypes. Metabolites positively associated with metabolic health, such as cinnamoylglycine 58 , hippurate 56 , and indolepropionate 62 , were depleted in recent antibiotic users. However, branched-chain amino acid catabolites-which have been positively associated with T2D 63 -were also decreased in antibiotic users, suggesting heterogeneous or context-dependent associations of antibiotic exposure on microbial metabolites relevant to host metabolic homeostasis.

Moderation effects by diet and gut microbiome composition reveal context dependencies

To explore context-dependent associations with recent antibiotic use, we investigated diet as a potential moderator of the effect of antibiotics on gut microbiome diversity in the Arivale cohort (Fig. 4A, Table S7). Arivale participants completed health questionnaires, including limited food frequency questionnaires, which assessed habitual diet. Most dietary items were sparsely populated in our cohort, and additional variables were excluded during preprocessing, leaving 9 dietary variables for analysis (Methods; Table S7). Focusing on these variables, we binarized all dietary features that capture intake frequency to balance group sizes across categories, and we tested the three alpha diversity metrics presented above (Fig. 1) for moderation effects. Moderation effects were only found for the number of observed features, where a preference for liquor (Fig. 4B) or an intake of at least one alcoholic drink per day (Fig. 4C) was associated with lower gut microbiome richness in the 3-and 12-month groups, respectively (interaction term BH q < 0.1, two-tailed t-test). The 3-month group also displayed interesting trends for daily intake of sugary drinks and fruit: consumption of at least one serving of sugary drink per day may be associated with lower gut microbiome richness, whereas consumption of at least one serving of fruit per day may be associated with increased gut microbiome richness (Fig. 4C, 0.1 < BH q < 0.2). Next, we wondered whether gut microbiome composition could moderate the effect of antibiotic treatment on the blood metabolome. For these regressions, we used binarized microbial presence or absence (n = 209 taxa, Table S8) as the moderator and built individual moderation models for each antibiotic-responsive metabolite and microbe pair (n = 7106 models; Fig. 4D). We also binarized antibiotic use recency due to the sparsity of these data types (e.g., for some microbe-metabolite pairs, there were no observations in one of the 3-or 12-month groups). We found moderation effects for ten metabolites: cinnamoylglycine, 3-phenylpropionate (hydrocinnamate), hippurate, 2-piperidinone, glutarate (C5-DC), hydantoin-5-propionate, 3-(3-hydroxyphenyl)propionate, 3-(4-hydroxyphenyl)lactate, 4-methyl-2-oxopentanoate, and the unannotated metabolite X-12544 (interaction term BH q-value < 0.1, two-tailed t-tests). Some metabolites possessed few microbial moderators (Fig. 4E, Fig. S4). Lachnospira, for instance, was associated with a smaller decrease in 4-methyl-2-oxopentanoate levels in those reporting antibiotic use in the last year, whereas the presence of Lachnospiraceae NC2004 group was associated with a larger decrease in 3-(3-hydroxyphenyl)propionate in the antibiotic-treated group. In contrast, we identified over five potential microbial moderators for cinnamoylglycine, 3phenylpropionate (hydrocinnamate), hippurate, 2-piperidinone, and glutarate (C5-DC). In most cases, microbial moderators reduced the effect of recent antibiotic use on the blood metabolome (i.e., 'dampeners'; Fig. 4F). We observed instances where the antibiotic-metabolite association reverses direction in the presence of a particular genus (i.e., 'modifiers'; Fig. 5F and Fig S4). Finally, the presence of certain genera, such as Dialister and Veillonella, was associated with a greater change in blood metabolite abundance in recent antibiotic users compared to non-users (i.e., 'amplifiers').

Context-dependent associations between recent antibiotic use and clinical chemistries

Prior studies investigating the effect of antibiotic use on long-term metabolic health outcomes have produced mixed results 23 . To explore this inconsistency in the Arivale cohort, we next constructed covariate-adjusted linear regression models for 94 clinical chemistry outcomes. We found no significant differences in clinical chemistries between recent antibiotic users and nonusers (Table S9), consistent with prior results from smaller placebo-controlled randomized trials 18, 19 . We next performed moderation analyses to explore potential subpopulation heterogeneity. First, we constructed linear regression models for all 94 chemistries as done previously, but adding a history of T2D (none: n = 42, 12-month: n = 24, and 3-month: n = 18) as a moderator (Table S9). We found significantly higher levels of methylmalonic acid, homocysteine, and alkaline phosphatase in individuals with T2D having antibiotic use in the last three months (Fig. 5A, two-tailed t-test, BH q < 0.1). Given the variable associations of individuals with a history of T2D, we next turned to previous work in the Arivale cohort that established metabolomics-inferred measures of BMI (metBMI) as more sensitive to lifestyle changes and metabolic heterogeneity 64 . We wondered whether we could use metBMI to detect subtle differences in host metabolic health following antibiotic use. Recent antibiotic users were excluded from the original metBMI study, so we applied the beta coefficients from their metBMI model to generate new metBMI predictions for antibiotic users in the Arivale cohort. To assess metabolic heterogeneity, we calculated metBMI error as the difference between predicted (metBMI) and measured BMI, and applied an interaction with the antibiotic recency variable (Table S10). We tested 94 clinical chemistries and found two that passed our significance threshold (Type II ANOVA, interaction term; BH q < 0.1): absolute basophils and 25-hydroxyvitamin D, 25(OH)D. The association between absolute basophils and metBMI error diverged between those with antibiotic use in the previous three months (positive association) versus use in the prior twelve months (negative association). For 25(OH)D, the metBMI-moderated antibiotic recency coefficients were negative and monotonic. In other words, as metBMI error increases-suggesting worse metabolic health than expected using measured BMI alone-25(OH)D levels decrease more strongly among more recent antibiotic users. This association was robust to further adjustment for vitamin D supplementation and season, and removing two outliers with levels of 25(OH)D greater than 150 mg/dL. Finally, we performed a large, unbiased screen of antibiotic-moderated associations between blood metabolite abundances (n = 969) and clinical chemistry outcomes (n = 94). Here, our goal was to learn more about how recent antibiotic use (binarized) might change the relationship between blood metabolites and clinical chemistries (Fig. 5F, Table S11). After applying a global (BH q < 0.1) and within-family (BH q < 0.1) FDR correction, we filtered our results by requiring hits to exhibit monotonic antibiotic recency trends, survive dropping influential points, and remain significant with robust regression. We found 20 associations where antibiotic use moderated the effect of metabolite abundance on clinical chemistries (Fig. 5G). Encouragingly, we observed a positive association between features detected in both the metabolomics and clinical chemistry panels (e.g., glucose and bilirubin). Except for the association between LDL medium particles and 1-stearoyl-2-dihomo-linolenoyl-GPI (18:0/20:3n3 or n6)*), moderation by recent antibiotic use exhibited two patterns. Most often, the metabolite-clinical chemistry association exhibited opposing directions between recent antibiotic users and non-users. For example, the association between the metabolites tryptophan and 3-formylindole with blood arsenic levels is negative in those without recent antibiotic use, whereas this association is positive in recent antibiotic users. Further, in recent antibiotic users, abundances of alphaketobutyrate, 3-methyl-2-oxobutyrate, and 1-stearoyl-2-docosapentaenoyl-GPE (18:0/22:5n6)* were positively associated with neutrophil percentage, whereas the association was negative in non-users. We see the opposite pattern for lymphocyte percentage, which is unsurprising given that neutrophil and lymphocyte percentages are compositional measures normalized to the total number of white blood cells. Incidentally, moderation of absolute neutrophil or lymphocyte counts did not reach statistical significance, though the coefficients were directionally consistent. Finally, in select cases, the metabolite-chemistry association was directionally consistent, though the magnitude of the beta coefficient varied in recent antibiotic users. We observed this pattern in related molecules, such as the association between bilirubin and X-21796, which was recently hypothesized to be a bilirubin derivative 65 . Discussion Contrary to epidemiological evidence linking antibiotics to heightened risk of T2D 6,8 , short-term intervention studies have not been able to link antibiotic use to long-term changes in metabolic parameters or hormones, despite showing that antibiotics can alter gut microbiome composition 18, 39, 54 . Here, we leverage three cohorts, encompassing diverse participant demographics, to assess the molecular signatures of recent antibiotic use. We first investigated differences in gut microbiome diversity, function, and community composition associated with recent antibiotic users. As expected, recent antibiotic treatment is associated with decreased gut microbiome richness (Fig. 1B) and altered community structure (Fig. 1D). In Arivale, we observed a clear monotonic relationship between the recency of antibiotic treatment and the magnitude of the association with gut microbiome diversity and composition, suggesting recovery of the gut microbiome over time. Accordingly, in our longitudinal analysis of gut microbiome richness, individuals who had used antibiotics in the 3 months prior to their baseline stool sample experienced a greater positive change in the number of observed genera over time than those reporting no antibiotic use in the past year, with the 12-month group showing an intermediate slope (Fig. 1E). This graded pattern is consistent with the broader observation that antibioticassociated diversity loss is generally followed by rapid recovery within a few weeks of treatment 2, 66 . Furthermore, we found that enterotypes dominated by Bacteroides were enriched in recent antibiotic users, while the enterotype associated with higher levels of butyrate-producing Clostridia was depleted (Fig. S3). Antibiotic enrichment for Bacteroides dominance may have negative implications in the context of aging, given that this genus was previously associated with poorer metabolic side effects in response to statins and with reduced survival in the latest decades of life 67, 68 . We also observed expected functional differences in the gut microbiome of recent antibiotic users, including lower predicted SCFA production (Fig. 1F) and decreased CAZyme richness (Fig. 1G & 1H). Though our analyses were cross-sectional, these antibiotic recency groups may represent windows along a trajectory of gut microbiome recovery. The monotonic trends in CAZyme richness seen in both Arivale and HCHS/SOL were consistent with previous work by Chng et al. 69 , suggesting that the number of carbohydrate-active enzyme (CAZyme) families is positively correlated with community recovery from antibiotic perturbations. We also investigated individual genus-level differences in the gut microbiome of recent antibiotic users (Fig. 2). Genera containing opportunistic enteric pathogens (e.g., Citrobacter and Campylobacter) showed increased odds of detection, consistent with prior studies 1,2 , and we observed increased relative abundance of oral-associated bacteria, such as Veillonella. Tyzzerella 4 was increased in both detection and relative abundance in individuals reporting antibiotic use in the previous 3 months relative to non-users, in line with a previous study associating this taxon with antibiotic use and adiposity in children 70 . Tyzzerella 4 may also mediate the effect of smoking on risk of colorectal high-risk adenoma 71 , highlighting the interconnectedness of host behavior and gut microbiome effectors. For some antibioticresponsive taxa, such as Parasutterella and Gastranaerophilales, we found lower odds of detection in recent antibiotic users, but an increased relative abundance in antibiotic users, relative to non-users, when the taxon was detected. This phenomenon may arise due to stochastic extinction of low-abundance taxa (such as Gastranaerophilales) during antibioticinduced population bottlenecks, which are then able to bloom in the antibiotic-disrupted ecosystem if they avoid local extinction. During our investigation of the blood metabolome following antibiotic treatment, we found 34 metabolites, including several fatty acid and amino acid metabolites, that were significantly different between recent antibiotic users and non-users (Fig. 3A). For nearly all of these metabolites, prior work found that the variance in these molecules was explained solely by the microbiome and not host genetics 30 . However, we saw that 3-hydroxyoctanoic and 3hydroxydecanoic acids were depleted in antibiotic users, even though these metabolites were previously only found to be associated with host genetics in the Arivale cohort 30 . This result is in line with prior work showing that 3-hydroxyoctanoic acid is produced by Parabacteroides goldsteinii in culture 72 , 3-hydroxydecanoic acid is elevated in the bloodstream of germ-free mice 73 , and adds to a growing body of evidence that antibiotic treatment is a useful tool for identifying microbiome-responsive metabolites 34 . Moreover, the majority of antibiotic-responsive metabolites decreased in abundance, in line with antibiotic-mediated disruption of bacterial transformations. For instance, decreased levels of 3-phenylpropionate, hippurate, and cinnamoylglycine could be the result of decreased bacterial phenylalanine metabolism [74] [75] [76] , leading to lower levels of microbe-host co-metabolism 36, 77 . Meanwhile, we detected increased levels of 3-(3-hydroxyphenyl)propionate, along with a concomitant increase in the relative abundance of flavone metabolizing genera, Flavonifactor and Eggerthella 78 . These antibioticassociated differences in the host metabolome may have variable associations with health and physiology (Fig. 3B). For example, several BCAA metabolites, known biomarkers of metabolic diseases 63 , were reduced in abundance following antibiotic use. However, isoursodeoxycholate, which has been associated with post-prandial lipemia and elevated liver enzymes in individuals recovering from bariatric surgery 79 , was more abundant in those who had used antibiotics in the last three months. In contrast to the close association between the microbiome and the antibiotic-responsive metabolite hits, we detected no differences in the blood proteome following recent antibiotic use, consistent with prior work in the Arivale cohort indicating that the microbiome shows weaker coupling to the blood proteome than it does to the blood metabolome 31 . To investigate factors that may lead to variability in the physiological response to antibiotics, we looked at dietary behaviors and gut microbiome composition as potential moderators. We found that a preference for liquor was associated with lower gut microbiome richness in antibiotic users (Fig. 4B). Despite not meeting a significance threshold of q < 0.1, we observed a similar trend of lower richness in antibiotic users consuming at least one sugary drink per day and higher richness in individuals who consumed fruit (Fig. 4C), consistent with observational 80, 81 and in vitro studies 27 . Having detected dietary variables that influence microbiome recovery, we next moved on to investigating the influence of gut microbiome composition on host responses to antibiotic use. Chng et al. describe recovery-associated bacteria that are key to ecological recovery of the gut microbiome through successional processes, and that, importantly, the combined influence and abundance of these species is critical for recovery 69 . In line with this observation, we found several significant genus-antibiotic interaction effects that help to explain variation in blood metabolite levels (Fig. 4D). Specifically, we found that microbial moderators could dampen or amplify antibiotic-associated differences in blood metabolite abundances (Fig. 4E). Commensal microorganisms known to produce beneficial metabolites, such as SCFAs, were often associated with dampening effects, whereas the opposite was true for orally-derived taxa and opportunistic pathobionts. Cinnamoylglycine had 40 candidate microbial moderators (Fig. 5F). This was the highest number of potential moderators detected, in line with cinnamoylglycine's close association with alpha diversity metrics 31, 82 . Moreover, the presence of Enterococcus, previously observed to expand under sugary diets following antibiotic treatment 80 , was associated with a greater loss in blood cinnamoylglycine levels (Fig. 5F), affirming the importance of assessing both host-behavior and microbiome variables. We next investigated whether 94 clinical chemistry measures, including glycohemoglobin A1c, total cholesterol, and C-reactive protein, varied significantly between antibiotic users and nonusers, adjusting for the full set of Arivale covariates (Table S9). On average, there were no covariate-adjusted associations between antibiotic use and clinical chemistries across the entire population, which aligns with results from intervention studies 18, 19 . Suspecting subpopulation heterogeneity, we used both medical history and a metabolomic-inferred BMI metric 64 to evaluate if antibiotic-associated perturbations of clinical chemistry measures differed by metabolic health status (Fig 5A-E). We found increased levels of methylmalonic acid and homocysteine in those with T2D who had taken antibiotics in the past three months-but not in the past 12 months-perhaps indicating a transient, antibiotic-associated vitamin B12 deficiency, but only in individuals with T2D 83 . Vitamin B12 deficiency is a known side effect of metformin treatment 84 , and microbial mechanisms have been hypothesized 1, 85 . Indeed, the gut microbiome may directly provision B12 to the host, although host absorption of B12 in the colon is thought to be limited [86] [87] [88] [89] . Given there were no average differences in clinical chemistries between antibiotic users and non-users in our original model, these findings could point to a short-term disruption of B12 balance that is only detectable in those at greater risk of deficiency. We also observed elevated alkaline phosphatase levels in the blood of recent antibiotic users with T2D, relative to non-users; however, this was not accompanied by a statistically significant difference in gamma-glutamyl transferase or alanine transaminase (i.e., if all three of these markers are elevated, then liver or bile duct injury is indicated), making it difficult to interpret this result. We hypothesized that metBMI error could be leveraged to reveal perturbations in clinical chemistries that were not apparent in our primary analyses of antibiotic recency. Previously, we demonstrated that metBMI is more sensitive to lifestyle changes than traditional measures of BMI and that the error was associated with clinically relevant differences in metabolic health 64 . Four of the 34 antibiotic-responsive metabolites are also metBMI features retained in at least one of the 10 LASSO models used to predict metBMI: 3-(4-hydroxyphenyl)lactate, alphahydroxyisocaproate, hydantoin-5-propionate, and indolepropionate. We found a significant interaction between metBMI error and recent antibiotic use on 25(OH)D levels (25hydroxyvitamin D). Levels of 25(OH)D decreased as metBMI error increased, and this association was most pronounced in those with antibiotic use in the last three months (Fig. 5E), suggesting antibiotic-associated vitamin D depletion is only detectable in individuals with poor metabolic health. Vitamin D supplementation has been used to lower the risk of T2D in various cohorts, though its efficacy varied across populations [90] [91] [92] . Moreover, conventionalization of germ-free mice was associated with increased levels of various vitamin D metabolites, including 25(OH)D 93 . Interestingly, the same authors demonstrated an increase in 25(OH)D following antibiotic administration in vitamin D3-supplemented, germ-free mice 94 , suggesting both indirect (i.e., microbiome-mediated) and direct mechanisms by which antibiotics may influence vitamin D homeostasis. Lastly, we performed a large screen of metabolite-chemistry-antibiotic interaction effects, finding 20 instances where recent antibiotic use may moderate the association between metabolite and a clinical chemistry outcome. We found several instances where recent antibiotic use may be associated with a directional change in the relationship between metabolites and clinical chemistries. For instance, in recent antibiotic users, levels of tryptophan and 3-formylindole (a derivative of tryptophan) show positive associations with blood arsenic levels, though this association is negative in non-users. Antibiotic use has been associated with reduced fecal excretion of arsenic in mouse models 95, 96 , and in a small case-control study of Chinese adults chronically exposed to arsenic via drinking water, levels of tryptophan were negatively associated with arsenic-induced skin lesions 97 . With the exception of one individual (whose blood arsenic was 31 mcg/L), levels of blood arsenic in the Arivale cohort were below the Quest Diagnostics reference range of less than 23 mcg/L 98 . Thus, our findings are not directly comparable to studies on interventional or environmental arsenic exposure. However, these data reveal context-dependent associations between blood arsenic and tryptophan metabolites in individuals with recently perturbed gut microbiomes, in line with a recent study observing lower indolepropionate levels in arsenic-exposed mice with perturbed microbiomes 99 . We also observed a moderation effect for the association between alpha-ketobutyrate and 3-methyl-2oxobutyrate (also known as α-ketoisovalerate) with percentages of lymphocytes and neutrophils (Fig. 5G). BCAA metabolites have been implicated in T-cell activation and signaling 100, 101 , and circulating levels may play a role in connecting immune dysfunction with metabolic dysregulation [102] [103] [104] . In a study investigating associations between whole-blood transcriptome modules and metabolic phenotypes, 3-methyl-2-oxobutyrate levels were positively associated with a neutrophil granule protein module 103 . Interestingly, this module was negatively associated with diverse measures of health: fiber intake, insulin sensitivity, and indolepropionate levels 103 . Taken together, these exploratory results highlight specific host physiological processes that appear to display context-dependent associations in the settings of recent antibiotic perturbations.

Conclusion

In this study, we set out to resolve an apparent contradiction. On the one hand, many epidemiological studies that have focused on large-sized cohorts have indicated that antibiotic use increases T2D risk. On the other hand, several randomized, controlled intervention trials in small cohorts have not detected antibiotic-associated disruptions to host metabolic health 18, 19, 39, 54 . Most recently, Baldanzi et al. have analyzed the metagenomes of 14,979 individuals, identifying lasting changes in response to clindamycin, flucloxacillin and fluoroquinolone use 2 . In many cases, antibiotic-enriched taxa were also associated with markers of cardiometabolic health, consistent with epidemiological studies linking antibiotic use with diabetes risk 4, 6, 8 . We suspected that the contradiction between intervention studies and epidemiology stemmed from latent confounding and context-dependencies across cohorts. To investigate this hypothesis, we assembled multi-omic data from several cohorts, integrating gut microbiome, blood metabolome, blood proteome, clinical chemistry, diet, and lifestyle data to study the molecular fingerprint of antibiotic use across a wide variety of contexts. Overall, when controlling for a wide variety of covariates, we confirmed the interventional study results, showing no effect, on average, of antibiotic use on clinical chemistries. However, we found that individuals who were already metabolically vulnerable did see significant antibiotic-associated changes in clinical markers in the blood, suggesting vitamin defficiencies. Finally, we identified several gut bacterial genera that, when present, appeared to dampen or amplify the metabolomic impact of antibiotic use on the host. Future studies should investigate metabolic perturbation following antibiotic treatment with greater resolution of temporal, host dietary, and host environmental factors in order to inform prescribing standards and co-therapy design.

Study Limitations

Our study has several limitations, notably the lack of complete antibiotic use metadata, including name, class, dosage, and indication. This may be partially mitigated by the recent observation that antibiotic-associated changes to gut microbiota composition tends to be very similar across different broad-spectrum antibiotic classes 2 . In the current study, we cannot compare an individuals' response and recovery to their pre-treatment state, given the lack of a pre-treatment sample. However, gut microbiome composition is largely stable over time 105, 106 , even following antibiotic treatment 66 . Moreover, because individuals take antibiotics in response to a clinical indication, often an infection, our exposure variable necessarily includes that of the treated condition. Finally, we are always limited by unmeasured confounders in observational cohorts, but we were able to control for a long list of relevant covariates across our analyses. Despite these limitations, we successfully detect anticipated differences in gut microbiome diversity and composition following antibiotics (Fig. 1). We identified differences in several microbe-host cometabolites following recent antibiotic treatment that were shown to be antibiotic-responsive in prior work 34 , and we validated our results across several external cohorts.

Methods

Cohorts Arivale

The Arivale cohort consists of individuals aged 18 years or older, primarily from California and Washington, who self-enrolled in a former health wellness company (Arivale, Inc., USA). A detailed description of the cohort is provided in Zubair et al 45 . Individuals who answered the recent antibiotic use question in the medication and health history questionnaires were considered for this study (n = 3,155). When asked about recent antibiotic use, answer choices were as follows: (0) "Within the last 3 months"; (1) "Within the last year"; or (2) "More than a year ago". These choices were mutually exclusive. Thus, individuals who answered "within the last 3 months" do not also appear in the 12-month group. For this study, antibiotic use was coded as a categorical variable with three levels ("none", "twelve", and "three"; reference = "none"). Individuals missing covariate data (age, sex, BMI, medication usage, eGFR, and genetic ancestry) were excluded from this study (n = 489). Subsequent analyses include individuals from this subset of 2,666, provided their biosamples were collected within 30 days of their medication questionnaire. Procedures for this study were reviewed and approved by the Western Institutional Review Board, with institutional review board study numbers 20170658 for the Institute for Systems Biology and 1178906 for Arivale.

Hispanic Community Health Study/Study of Latinos

HCHS/SOL is a prospective community-based cohort study of 16,415 self-identified Hispanic/Latino individuals living in 4 US metropolitan areas (Bronx, NY; Chicago, IL; Miami, FL; and San Diego, CA). A 2-stage area probability sample design was used to recruit participants (aged 18-74 at recruitment) and is described in more detail elsewhere. 107, 108 The HCHS/SOL Gut Origins of Latino Diabetes (GOLD) ancillary study, conducted from 2016-2018, encompasses a subset of 3057 HCHS/SOL participants 43 and is approximately concurrent with the second in-person visit period (2014 to 2017), during which blood draws and clinical assessments were performed. In HCHS/SOL, individuals provided the date of last antibiotic use as month and year. The 15th of the month was imputed to calculate the time between antibiotic use and biosample collection. Individuals with a GOLD stool sample received within 60 days of Visit 2 (when blood is drawn for metabolomics) were included in the present study (n = 866). Antibiotic use was subsequently coded as a categorical variable with three levels ("none", "six", and "three"; reference = "none"). The study was approved by Institutional Review Boards at each field center where all participants gave written informed consent. MetaCardis Cohort The MetaCardis cohort consists of adults from France, Denmark, and Germany enrolled in a prospective cross-sectional study investigating cardiac and metabolic phenotypes and has been described in detail elsewhere 44 . Publicly available data from two studies were merged for analyses conducted in Figures 3A and 3B. Briefly, individual log-transformed serum metabolite data were obtained from Table S12 provided in Fromentin et al. 44 . Individual-level metadata, anti-lipid and anti-diabetic medication use, antibiotic use in the last 5 years, and microbial load data were taken from Forslund et al. 1 Tables S1B, S2B, S2D, and Fromentin et al. Table S10, respectively.

Gut Microbiome Sequencing Arivale

Two external providers performed amplification and library preparation, with 300-bp paired-end MiSeq profiling of the 16S V3 + V4 region (DNAgenotek) or 250-bp paired-end MiSeq profiling of the 16S V4 region (Second Genome). Further analysis was performed using the denoise workflow from mbtools (https://github.com/gibbons-lab/mbtools) that wraps DADA2 109 . Taxonomy assignment was performed using the RDP classifier 110 with the SILVA database (version 132) 111 . Here, 99% of the reads could be classified at the family level, 89% at the genus level, and 32% at the species level. A total of 1,967 individuals (out of 2,666) provided stool samples within 30 days of completing the antibiotic usage question. HCHS/SOL HCHS/SOL participants participating in the GOLD ancillary study were provided with a disposable paper inverted hat stool collection kit (Protocult collection device, ABC Medical Enterprises, Inc.). DNA extraction from fecal samples followed the Earth Microbiome Project protocol 112 and has been described in detail elsewhere 43 . Shotgun sequencing was conducted in the Knight laboratory at the University of California San Diego (UCSD) using Illumina NovaSeq platforms 113 . Prior to the CAZyme analysis, performed separately from the other microbiome analyses, raw shallow shotgun reads were trimmed of adapters and host contamination removed using kneaddata (including trimmomatic 114 , bowtie2 human genome 115 , and fastqc 116 ).

Gut Microbiome Analysis Arivale

For cross-sectional analyses, the "rrarefy" function in Vegan (v2.7.1) 117 was used to generate a count table rarefied to 21,869 reads. This count table was used for diversity calculations, logistic regression analysis (Fig. 2), and microbiome-metabolite moderation analysis (Fig. 4). Three diversity metrics were calculated as follows: (1) Richness: number of observed genera; (2) Dominance (Berger-Parker): number of reads assigned to the most abundant genus in a sample divided by the total number of reads in that sample; (3) Information (Shannon): ∑ , calculated using the "diversity" function in Vegan (v2.7.1). Principal coordinate analysis was performed using an ASV-level Bray-Curtis dissimilarity matrix rarefied to 21,869 reads (using the "avgdist" function in Vegan). Functional prediction (i.e., the number of carbohydrate-accessible enzyme families) from amplicon sequence variants was performed using Picrust2 50, 51 (v.2.6.0), which uses EPA-ng 118 and gappa 119 for placement of phylogenetic reads, castor 120 for hidden-state prediction, and MinPath 121 for pathway inference. Longitudinal assessment of gut microbiome richness was performed as above (albeit rarefied to 21,780 reads) for a subset of individuals who provided stool samples at least 60 days after the first sample (n = 837). Following rarefication, we removed participants with missing sequencing vendor (n=2), missing BMI (n=13), or with unexplained low reads (n=31 [all from one sequencing vendor]) at follow up, leaving 791 participants for the longitudinal analysis. To predict SCFA production, genus-level abundances were used to construct MCMMs using the community-scale metabolic modeling platform, MICOM 48 v0.37.1, as described previously 49 . A presence/absence count table was created from our rarefied count table, where any non-zero count was converted to "1". From here, taxa with prevalence between 3.5% and 97.5% were considered for testing. Taxa observed in only one sequencing vendor were removed (n=4), leaving n=209 taxa for analysis. Prior to the centered log-ratio (CLR) transformation of relative abundances, zero counts were imputed with half of the smallest non-zero observed count across the dataset. Subsequently, imputed values were reverted to missing (NaN) to avoid interpreting them as true observations. Finally, we applied prevalence filtering, retaining only the taxa present in at least 10% of samples.

HCHS/SOL

Of the 866 HCHS/SOL participants, 859 had available data in an OTU table matching the stool sample questionnaire. To exclude low-quality samples, those with <100000 reads were removed (n = 5), leaving 854 participants with antibiotic recency data, stool shallow shotgun metagenomic data, and for which stool samples were returned within 60 days of HCHS/SOL V2 (blood for metabolomics taken at V2). Individuals with missing BMI (n = 3) and eGFR (n = 5) data were removed, leaving n = 846 for microbiome diversity analyses. 2709 species were detected in these samples. These were agglomerated to the genus level for harmonization with Arivale using the "tax_glom" function in the phyloseq R package 122 (v 1.50.0), to 868 genera. Alpha diversity calculations and microbiome analyses were subsequently performed using the same approaches and prevalence cut-offs listed above. For the CAZyme analysis in HCHS/SOL using Cayman 52 , the human-gut database was downloaded from Zenodo (https://zenodo.org/records/10473258?preview_file=gene_catalogues.zip) and indexed with BWA, and the human-gut CAZyme annotation file also downloaded for use with the Cayman tool. Clean paired reads along with any orphaned reads were then processed using the Cayman profile function. For this analysis, raw reads were available from 822 participants (646 with no antibiotic use in the past 6 months, 73 with use 3-6 months prior to stool sample, and 135 within 0-3 months). CAZyme richness was calculated by counting the number of uniquely observed CAZyme families within each sample, provided that the abundance was > 1 RPKM 52.

Enterotype Analysis

Dirichlet Multinomial Mixture (DMM) modeling 53 was performed on rarefied genus-level count tables for both Arivale and HCHS/SOL cohorts. To select the optimal number of enterotype clusters, we used the Bayesian information criterion (BIC) which resulted in 4 enterotypes for Arivale and 3 for SOL. Logistic regression models were used to test enrichment of Bacteroides enterotypes among antibiotic users. In Arivale, this was specified by testing for "Bac2" versus any other enterotype, given the health relevance of this specific enterotype. In HCHS/SOL, the 3 and 6 month groups were combined to a single antibiotic use group, and "Bac" versus any other enterotype was tested.

Plasma and Serum Metabolomics

Metabolomic data for the cohorts included in Figure 3A were generated by Metabolon Inc. (Durham, NC) using methods described in detail elsewhere 44, 123 . Untargeted metabolomics was performed on either plasma (Arivale) or serum (HCHS/SOL, MetaCardis) samples. Briefly, all measurements were taken using Waters ACQUITY ultraperformance liquid chromatography (UPLC) and a Thermo Scientific Q-Exactive high-resolution/accuracy mass spectrometer interfaced with a heated electrospray ionization (HESI-II) source and an Orbitrap mass analyzer operated at 35,000 mass resolution. Four sample fractions (including one backup) underwent measurement using different approaches. The first aliquot was analyzed under positive-ion conditions by elution from a C18 column using water and methanol with 0.05% perfluoropentanoic acid and 0.1% formic acid. The second aliquot additionally used acetonitrile. The third aliquot was analyzed under negative-ion optimized conditions and gradient eluted from a C18 column using methanol, water, and 6.5 mM ammonium bicarbonate at pH 8.0. The final aliquot was measured using negative ionization following elution from a HILIC column using water, acetonitrile, and 10 mM ammonium formate at pH 10.8. MS analyses utilized MS and data-dependent multistage MS scans with dynamic exclusion at an approximate range of 70-10,000 m/z. Metabolites not meeting annotation criteria but consistently detected in various biofluids are labeled with the prefix 'X' followed by a unique ID. For Arivale, the discovery cohort, metabolites observed in fewer than 30% of samples were excluded, leaving 969 metabolites for analysis. Metabolites were log-transformed and Z-scored prior to analysis. Finally, regression models were fit for each metabolite individually (dependent variable), including antibiotic recency as the predictor of interest and adjusting for all covariates included in the "full model". Results were subsequently validated in two external cohorts: HCHS/SOL and MetaCardis. Here, individual linear regression models were constructed for the 34 antibiotic-responsive metabolites (BH q < 0.1). For HCHS/SOL, each metabolite was regressed on antibiotic recency, with antibiotic recency as the predictor of interest, adjusting for age, sex, BMI, field center, and eGFR. For HCHS/SOL, metabolite data was available for 815 individuals, and three were removed due to missing BMI, leaving 812 participants for analysis. For MC, each metabolite was regressed on the predictor of interest-the total number of antibiotic courses in the last 5 years or binarized antibiotic use-adjusting for age, gender, BMI, microbial load, study center, antidiabetic medication use (encoded as 0 or 1), and antilipid medication use (encoded as 0 or 1). To compare the effect of antibiotics versus glucose intolerance on the 34 antibioticresponsive metabolites, data from MC were re-analyzed as raw data were publicly available, and significant findings from 3 cohorts (Qatari 59 , Swedish 60 , HCHS/SOL 61 ) were compiled and merged to produce Figure 3B. To re-analyze MC data, each metabolite was regressed on health status as the predictor of interest, adjusting for age, gender, BMI, microbial load, study center, antidiabetic medication use, antilipid medication use, and the total number of antibiotic courses in the last 5 years. Significant findings were obtained from Yousri et al. Tables S2, Wu et al. Table S8, and Li et al. Table S2. Compiled data used to create Fig. 3B is provided in Table S6.

Arivale Plasma Proteomics

Plasma protein levels for Arivale were measured using Proseek Inflammation, Cardiovascular II, and Cardiovascular III panels (Olink Biosciences). Data was processed and batch corrected as described previously 124 . Data was matched to antibiotic recency questionnaire data with a threshold of 30 days. We applied a 70 percent prevalence filtering threshold, resulting in 274 proteins for analysis.

Arivale Metabolic BMI Prediction Analysis

Beta coefficients from ten sparse metabolomics-inferred BMI (metBMI) models previously trained on Arivale metabolomic data using LASSO regression 64 were used to generate metBMI predictions for the antibiotic recency cohort (as individuals having used antibiotics in the last 3 months were excluded in Watanabe et al.). Outputs from the ten models were averaged to produce the final metBMI prediction. Correlation between measured and metabolic BMI was assessed using Pearson's correlation. Error was calculated as metBMI minus measured BMI (kilograms/meters 2 ), producing an error term used in subsequent analysis. Finally, individual linear regressions were performed for 94 clinical chemistries, using the following model: chemistry ~ error * abx_recency + BMI:abx_recency + BMI:error + covariates. A BMI:error interaction term was included because metBMI error prediction is known to vary by BMI (for example, underprediction is more common in high BMI ranges). 64 However, the predictor of interest in these analyses is a two-way interaction between error and antibiotic recency.

Arivale Dietary Questionnaire Data

Response rates for self-reported questionnaires in Arivale vary. Analysis of dietary variables was limited to dietary variables with at least 100 responses, yielding a set of 12 dietary variables: diet type (no special diet, vegetarian, diabetic, etc.), alcohol preference (no preference, beer, wine, or liquor), alcohol days per week, alcohol drinks per day, breakfast frequency, snack frequency, cruciferous vegetable frequency, fruits per day, vegetables per day, sugary drinks per day, grains per day, and cups of water per day. Two were removed due to small sample sizes (<10 in at least one cell): diet type and cups of water per day. Subsequently, all dietary variables except alcohol preference were binarized to reflect regular or near-daily consumption. Finally, we retained alcohol drinks per day rather than alcohol days per week, as the two capture largely overlapping information following binarization, leaving 9 dietary variables for analysis.
