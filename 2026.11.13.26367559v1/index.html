Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 5 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

INTRODUCTION

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a leading threat to global health, with six priority pathogens, known collectively as the ESKAPE pathogens (Enterococcus faecium, Staphylococcus aureus, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Acinetobacter baumannii, Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Enterobacter spp.), representing the greatest AMR burden (1, 2). Bronchiectasis is characterised by permanent bronchial dilatation and distortion, resulting from a vicious cycle of infection, inflammation, impaired mucociliary clearance, and airway injury (6, 7). Consequently, this leads to chronic productive cough, copious sputum production, recurrent respiratory exacerbations, and progressive lung damage. (6, 7). Antibiotic susceptibility testing.

Susceptibility profiles were determined for eleven antipseudomonal antibiotics. Ten antibiotics were tested by disc diffusion (Edwards Group, Murrarie, QLD, Australia) (40) using cation-adjusted Mueller-Hinton agar (Becton Dickinson): amikacin (AMK; 30 mg), aztreonam (ATM; 30 mg), cefepime (FEP; 30 mg), ceftazidime (CAZ; 30 mg), ceftolozane/tazobactam (C/T; 30/10 mg), ciprofloxacin (CIP; 5 mg), imipenem (IPM; 10 mg), meropenem (MEM; 10 mg), piperacillin/tazobactam (TZP; 100/10 mg) and tobramycin (TOB; 10 mg). As chemical properties limit its diffusion through agar (41), colistin (CST) susceptibility was determined by broth microdilution. Isolates were classified as susceptible, susceptible (increased exposure), or resistant using the EUCAST v 16.1 2026 breakpoints (42) for AMK, ATM, FEP, C/T, CIP, IPM or MEM, and using the CLSI M100 35 th edition (43) for CAZ and TZP. The AMR-sensitive control strain PAO1 (LMG 12228) was used as a control strain for all testing to confirm antibiotic integrity.

Definitions of AMR, MDR, XDR, and pan-drug resistance were based on the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control and US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention guidance (13). Briefly, isolates were classified as AMR when resistant towards 1-2 antimicrobial categories, MDR when resistant to at least one agent in three or more antimicrobial categories, XDR when resistant to at least one agent in all except one or two antimicrobial categories, and pan-drug resistant when resistant towards all agents in all categories (13).

Whole-genome sequencing. Genomic DNA was extracted from purified isolates by transferring a ~10 μL loopful of agar-cultured cells into 200 μL lysis buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 2 mM sodium EDTA, and 1.2% Triton X-100 [Sigma-Aldrich]), followed by bead beating in DNase-free 2 mL O-ring tubes (SSIBio, Lod, CA, USA) containing ~100 μL of 0.1 and 0.5 mm zirconia beads (Daintree Scientific, St Helens, TAS, Australia) using a Precellys Evolution Touch Homogeniser (Bertin Technologies, Montigny-le-Bretonneux, France) at 2 x 7500 rpm for 60 sec (with a 30 sec pause between cycles). DNA was purified using the 'Gram-positive Bacteria' protocol of the DNeasy Blood and Tissue kit (QIAGEN, Clayton, VIC, Australia), commencing at the Buffer AL and Proteinase K addition step. Extracted DNA was quantified using a Qubit 4 fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Scoresby, VIC, Australia). WGS was performed using Illumina NovaSeq X to generate 150bp paired-end reads at ~100x depth (AZENTA, Suzhou, China and Macrogen, Geumcheon-gu, Seoul, Korea).

Bioinformatic analyses.

Following genome quality filtering and assessment using Trimmomatic v0.39 (44) and FastQC v12.1 (45), species identification was conducted using Kraken 2 v2.17.1 (46). AMR-conferring determinants were identified using ARDaP v2.3.2 (25, 26). For variant analysis, SPANDx v4.2 (47) was used to first quality-filter reads and then detect single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and insertions/deletions (indels). SPANDxgenerated SNP matrices were used to construct phylogenetic trees using FastTree v2.2.0 (46); trees were subsequently visualised using iTOL v7.4.2 (48). Genome assembly was undertaken using Microbial Genome Assembler Pipeline v3.5 (49), with PAO1 (GenBank ref: AE004091.2) included as the scaffolding reference. Assembled contigs were then aligned against PAO1 and annotated with Prokka v1.15.6 (50). In silico multilocus sequence typing (MLST) was performed on assembled genomes using the P. aeruginosa PubMLST database (available at https://pubmlst.org/organisms/pseudomonas-aeruginosa), with alleles and STs assigned via the embedded BIGSdb software (51). Recombination was examined using Gubbins v3.4.1 (52). All assembled genomes were uploaded to GenBank (BioProject ID PRJNA1325058) and P. aeruginosa PubMLST databases, and Illumina reads were uploaded to the NCBI SRA database (BioProject IDs PRJNA1330417 and PRJNA1325058).

Publicly available P. aeruginosa genomes from international bronchiectasis datasets.

Data were downloaded for 4,575 P. aeruginosa isolates from eight published (27) (28) (29) (30) (31) (32) (33) (34) and two unpublished (BioProject: PRJNA1419033 and PRJNA1376148) studies. International isolates were from the UK (n=185, 4.0%), Germany (n=117, 2.5%), Spain (n=46, 1.0%), China (n=31, 0.6%), and USA (n=5, 0.1%), with most of unknown origin (n=4201, 91.8%). These data were integrated with our 137 P. aeruginosa bronchiectasis isolates and bioinformatically analysed as described above.

RESULTS

Demographic data.

Our cohort represents the typical bronchiectasis population, with elderly, female, non-smokers being most common, reflecting findings from the Australian Bronchiectasis Registry (8,53) (Table 1). Average participant age was 67.8 ± 14.0 years with most participants being female (n=44, 74.6%). Average BMI was borderline normaloverweight at 25.7kg/m 2 ± 8.0 (15 -65kg/m 2 ), and most were never smokers (n=44, 74.6%).

As per ATS/ERS 2005 guidelines (54), average lung function was classified as moderate, with an average FEV 1 of 60.3 ± 22.5% predicted. Of those with Bronchiectasis Severity Scores (BSI) available, most (n=35/53, 66.0%) had severe BSI (i.e. score >9/20), with 24.5% (n= 13/53) having a BSI score ≥15/20. Bronchiectasis aetiology was predominantly postinfective (36.2%), followed by idiopathic (12.1%), mercury associated (8.6%), and other subcategories (43%) (Table 1). There were no statistically significant differences between male and female demographic variables based on age, smoking status, BMI, BSI score, or FEV 1 (p>0.05).

MLST.

Fifty-seven sequence types (ST) were identified in our dataset (Table S3), including seven novel STs (ST5013, ST5014, ST5015, ST6238, ST6239, ST6243, ST6966). The highrisk international clone ST274 (n=9, 6.6%) and ST2128 (n=8, 5.8%) were most common, followed by ST253 (n=7, 5.1%), ST655 (n=7, 5.1%), ST170 (n=5, 3.6%), and the common global clone ST155 (n=5, 3.6%). Although less frequent, other notable STs included the high-risk international clones ST395 (n=1, 0.7%), ST244 (n=1, 0.7%), ST277 (n=1, 0.7%), ST298 (n=1, 0.7%), and common global clones ST782 (n=2, 1.4%), ST27 (n=1, 0.7%), and ST260 (n=1, 0.7%) (Figure 1).

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing.

AMR rates were high, with 75.9% (n=104/137) isolates being resistant to at least one clinically relevant antibiotic. Specifically, 46.7% (n=64/137) were AMR, 19.7% (n=27/137) were MDR, and 9.5% (n=13/137) were XDR (i.e. resistant to all except one or two categories (13,42)), with the remaining isolates (24.1%; n=33/137) sensitive to all tested antimicrobials (Table S3; Figure 2). No pan-drug resistant isolates were observed. CIP resistance was most common (n=88, 64.2%), followed by FEP (n=61, 44.5%), C/T (n=40, 29.2%), ATM (n=29, 21.2%), CAZ (n=27, 19.7%), IPM (n=26, 19.0%), MEM (n=21, 15.3%), AMK (n=16, 11.7%), TOB (n=14, 10.2%), TZP (n=12, 8.8%), and CST (n=1, 0.7%) resistance (Figure 2). AMR/MDR/XDR classification was not associated with lung function severity (p=0.783); however, AMR evolved over time for several participants (Table S3). Phenotypically, 56.2% (n=77/137) isolates displayed a mucoid phenotype (Table S3).

In silico AMR profiling. Several known and novel AMR determinants were identified in silico in our cohort (Table 2). AMR determinants correlated well with phenotypic resistance, with genomic drivers of AMR identified in most resistant isolates.

β-lactam resistance determinants were heterogenous in nature, with numerous mutation combinations responsible for conferring resistance (Table S2). Mutations typically occurred in genes controlling AmpC regulation (ampR, dacB) and structure (ampC), penicillin-binding protein production (PBPs; ftsI, dacB), outer-membrane porin structure (oprD), or efflux pump regulation (mexR, mexT, nalD). Missense mutations in the transcriptional regulatory gene, ampR, were seen in 7.3% (n=10/137) of isolates (Table S4), most of which were classified as MDR or XDR (Table S3). We also identified four nonsense oprD variants affecting residues 1-82 (Table S3). Nine (6.5%) isolates were resistant to all four β-lactam classes tested (carbapenems, cephalosporins, penicillins, monobactams), all of which had multiple mutations across several β-lactam-associated genes. For example, SCHI0181. S.68, which was resistant to all β-lactams, including carbapenems and cephalosporins (Table 2), encoded deleterious mutations in ftsI (likely reducing β-lactam affinity to PBP3), nalD (likely causing efflux pump upregulation), oprD (likely reducing cell permeability), and ampR (likely resulting in ampC upregulation) (Table S3).

To evaluate the efficacy of β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations, we compared TZP resistance rates against C/T, a newer antimicrobial specifically engineered to evade AmpC hydrolysis and efflux (55, 56). Unexpectedly, C/T resistance (29.2%, n=40/137) was over three-fold higher than TZP resistance (8.8%, n=12/137), a trend that remained even when accounting for multiple isolates from individual participants (37.3%, n=22/59 participants vs. 10.1%, n=6/59 participants). Interestingly, TZP resistance was significantly associated with XDR status (p= <0.001). In contrast, C/T resistance was not significantly associated with active antibiotic administration, resistance to other compounds, or AMR/MDR/XDR status, suggesting the presence of intrinsic resistance with distinct AMR mechanisms. Although we observed specific ampR and/or ampC variants in some C/T resistant isolates, the AMR driver mutation remained unknown for most isolates.

We unexpectedly identified a CST-resistant P. aeruginosa isolate, SCHI0273. S.1, from P43, who was receiving long-term nebulised CST at the time of sputum collection. To facilitate comparative genomic analysis, we attempted to retrieve CST sensitive strains from this participant's sputum; however, all >70 subcultured clones were CST resistant, indicating a likely fixed population. Closer inspection of SNP and indel variants identified two missense mutations affecting PmrB (Val136Met) and PhoQ (Gln263His) in SCHI0273. S.1 (Table S3). These mutations were not identified in strains obtained from any other participant.

Within-host evolution. Among our longitudinal cases, phylogenomic reconstruction revealed a strong signal of within-host evolution in participants who were infected for at least 6 months (n=13/13 [100%]). Within-host genetic distances ranged from 8 to 1646 accumulated SNP differences (Table S3), with an average of 1.16 substitutions per million base pairs/year, excluding two outliers (Figure 4).

In most cases, AMR rates did not worsen over time, consistent with the initial isolates often already possessing high AMR levels, and in some cases (P06, P39, P45), isolates became more susceptible over time. Of the participants who developed worsening AMR, we identified several chromosomal AMR determinants associated with acquired phenotypic resistance. The most common AMR determinants in our longitudinal cohort were GyrA Thr83Ile/Val and Asp87Asn/Tyr (CIP resistance), AmpR Arg86Cys and Asp135Asn (cephalosporin resistance), and nonsense OprD (carbapenem resistance), occurring in two or more participants each.

In P09, >1,000 SNPs accumulated in the latter isolates over a nearly nine-year period, including mutations affecting MexT (Gln80fs) and GyrA (Thr83Ile) that drove MDR development (Table S3). Additionally, all 2018 isolates retrieved from P09 underwent genome reduction, losing a total of 56 S3, Table S5). Furthermore, twenty-eight strains encoded the PA5438 (GavR) Ser275_Arg277del variant, which has been associated with chronic P. aeruginosa infection establishment (32).

Multilineage infections (co-infection with two or more genetically distinct isolates) were detected in 12.5% (n=4/32) of participants (Figure 3; Table S3). In addition to multilineage infections, comparative genomic analysis identified that two longitudinal infection cases, P05 and P09, harboured isolates that likely changed STs within-host due to single missense variants in MutL (Arg264Cys) and AroE (Lys218Arg), respectively, becoming single-locus variants (Table S3).

Hypermutation.

A common phenomenon in CF-derived P. aeruginosa is hypermutator emergence, especially in chronic infections (36-60% incidence) (57) (58) (59). Hypermutators lack DNA repair mechanisms, typically due to high-consequence defects in mutS and mutL DNA mismatch repair systems, resulting in up to 1000-fold higher spontaneous mutation rate compared to wild-type strains (57) (58) (59). Hypermutation has also been reported in the P. aeruginosa-positive bronchiectasis population, albeit less frequently (~9%) than in CF (27).

We similarly found that 13.1% (n=18) of our isolates encoded high-consequence mutS (n=10; 7.3%) or mutL (n=8; 5.8%) mutations (P04: MutL Glu205*, P30: MutS Leu771fs, P31: MutS Cys295fs, P40: MutL Ile475_Arg479del, P41: MutL Ala347fs, P46: MutL Thr402del, P47: MutS Gly422fs, P49: MutS Thr115fs, P56: MutS Trp851*. Table S3). When assessing hypermutated longitudinal isolates spanning at least six months, we observed an average rate of 24.5 substitutions per million base pairs/year compared with the average of 1.16 substitutions per million base pairs per year in non-hypermutated strains (i.e. a 21x higher mutation rate; Table S3). In four participants encoding high-consequence MutS (Gly422fs, Trp851*) or MutL (Glu205*, Thr402del) variants, only a single isolate was retrieved, hampering confirmation of their hypermutation status.

We also observed elevated mutation rates in strains lacking high-consequence MutS and MutL variants. P25, who presented with a persistent infection over the sampled 5-month timeframe, harboured two latter strains, SCHI0221. S.4 and SCHI0221. S.8, that had unusually accumulated >2,000 mutations, yet no hypermutation variants were identified (Table S3; Figure 4). Gubbins analysis confirmed a single recombination event in these latter strains, involving ~55kb of the P. aeruginosa chromosome (SCHI0221. S.8 coordinates 5814054 to 5815135 [42 SNPs], 6100492 to 6121243 [658 SNPs] and 6130266 to 6163717 [1306 SNPs]), likely donated from an unsampled isolate co-infecting this participant. Similarly, P09 exhibited very high mutational burden over an 8.7-year timeframe (Figure 4); although no recombination or high-consequence hypermutation variants were identified (Table S3), a missense MutS Asp552Asn variant may explain the elevated SNP rate in these isolates.

Global isolate analysis.

Bioinformatic analysis of all 4,575 publicly available bronchiectasisderived P. aeruginosa genomes identified numerous known AMR determinants, with mutations in mexB (36.7%, n=1680/4575), gyrA (31.1%, n=1422/4575) and mexA (11.0%, n=504/4575) being most frequent (Table S4). This global AMR determinant prevalence was mostly consistent with our Australian cohort (Table S4). No significant differences were identified for the well-characterised GyrA AMR determinants Asp87His/Asn/Tyr (11.0% [our cohort] vs. 7.3% [global dataset]; p= 0.179), and Thr83Ile (24.1% vs. 21.5%; p= 0.503) Interestingly, our cohort had significantly higher prevalences of AmpC Val239Ala (4.4% vs 1.0%, p <0.001), AmpR Asp135Asn (5.1% vs 2.3%, p=0.029) and ArgJ loss (13.9% vs. 0.7%; p= <0.001), all of which encode resistance towards penicillins and/or cephalosporins.

Five MGEs were identified in the global isolate genomes, all rare: two aminoglycoside modifying enzyme genes aac(6')-Ib-cr (n=7/4575, 0.15%) and ant(2'')-Ia (n=12/4575, 0.26%), two β-lactamase genes bla oxa-10 (n=11/4575, 0.24%) and bla per-1 (n=17/4575, 0.37%) and a plasmid-mediated quinolone resistance gene qnrb19 (n=3/4575, 0.07%). No MGEs were identified in our local dataset. Phylogenomic inclusion of our Australian isolates within the global dataset showed no distinct clustering based on geography, with Australian isolates interspersed among the global collection (Figure 5). However, country of origin data was unpublished for most global isolates (n=4201, 91.8%) (28), confounding our analysis. The isolates with available geographic origin data were from the UK (n=185, 4.0%), Germany (n=117, 2.5%), Spain (n=46, 1.0%), China (n=31, 0.6%), and USA (n=5, 0.1%). Using the available metadata, there was no strong evidence of phylogeographic clustering, suggesting a highly panmictic population (Figure 5).

DISCUSSION

Numerous studies have investigated AMR P. aeruginosa in CF, bloodstream infections, and nosocomial infections. However, research detailing the prevalence and genomic epidemiology of AMR P. aeruginosa in bronchiectasis is limited, with a noticeable dearth of data from Australia. To address this gap, we investigated AMR prevalence, underlying genetic mechanisms, population structure, and within-host evolutionary dynamics of P. aeruginosa isolates from a contemporary Australian bronchiectasis cohort.

Approximately three-quarters (75.9%) of our Australian P. aeruginosa isolates were resistant to at least one clinically relevant antibiotic, with 46.7% AMR, 19.7% MDR, and 9.5% XDR.

Fortunately, no pan-drug resistant isolates were identified. Our MDR incidence was high compared to several prior bronchiectasis studies, including a retrospective observational UK study (11%; n=155 participants) (22), a recent Chinese study by Xu et al. (16.3%; n=306) (20), and a small Spanish study by Cabrera et al. (16%; n=43 isolates) (21), but slightly lower than a Chinese study by Gao et al. (23.1%; n=147 isolates) (23). Compared to the Cabrera and Xu studies, we found considerably higher AMR rates towards CIP (64.2% vs. 44.2%, 39.2%), FEP (44.5% vs. 7.0%, 10.8%), C/T (29.2% vs. 0.0%, N/A), ATM (21.2% vs. 7.0%, N/A), and MEM (15.3% vs. 9.3%, 4.6%) respectively, with our AMR towards TZP (8.8%) lower than Xu (11.8%) but higher than Cabrera (4.7%) (20, 21). In contrast, we observed lower AMR rates for IPM (19.0% vs 32.6%), AMK (11.7% vs 18.6%), and TOB (10.2% vs Our high CIP resistance rate reflects its widespread use in Australia as the sole oral therapy option for exacerbations and its status as a first-line therapy recommendation according to multiple international respiratory guidelines (60) (61) (62) (63) (64). However, our rate was substantially higher than other bronchiectasis studies (range: 13-44%) and even Australian CF cohorts (46.0%) (15, (20) (21) (22) (23). We found that CIP resistance was driven by chromosomal gyrA or gyrB mutations rather than acquisition of an aminoglycoside modification enzyme, consistent with other bronchiectasis studies (20, 65). Given the high prevalence of these AMR determinants in our local bronchiectasis population, the introduction of routine CIP AMR genotyping into clinical practice ( 66 Although comprehensive AMR surveillance data targeting Australian bronchiectasis cohorts is limited, a large study of 934 Australian CF isolates found a 35% MDR rate (15), supporting the notion of increased AMR rates in CF compared to bronchiectasis cohorts (67).

Nevertheless, the AMR and MDR rates observed in our cohort remain concerningly high compared to broader national averages. Our MDR rate of 19.7% is six-fold higher than the 3.2% reported MDR rate for bloodstream infections described by the Australian Group on Antimicrobial Resistance (AGAR) 2023 report (68, 69), and is markedly higher than the national average P. aeruginosa AMR rate (< 7% for all five antimicrobials tested) reported in the Antimicrobial Use and Resistance in Australia (AURA) 2023 report (70). Additionally, the 2023 AGAR report found a significantly higher 30-day all-cause mortality rate for MDR (46.2%) compared to non-MDR (18.2%) P. aeruginosa infections, raising concerns for respiratory cohorts. Our findings highlight that bronchiectasis is an important and distinct patient cohort that experiences higher contemporary AMR rates than is currently appreciated.

CST is a critically important last-line antibiotic for P. aeruginosa treatment, as it typically remains effective against otherwise untreatable infections due to its unique mechanism of action (71,72). Although global CST resistance remains low, rates have steadily increased from 2% in 2006-2010 to 5% in 2020-2023 (73). Most previous P. aeruginosa bronchiectasis studies have either not assessed CST resistance or have not identified any resistant strains (20) (21) (22) (23) (24), except for Xiao et al., who identified a single CST resistant (n= 1/66) isolate in China (65). The PROMIS-I and PROMIS-II randomised control trials, which investigated CST nebuliser efficacy in bronchiectasis across 12 countries, including Australia, reported modest P. aeruginosa CST resistance rates (2.4% and 9.0%, respectively, in the CST group vs. 1.3% and 0.9%, respectively, in the placebo group); however, the geographic origin of these CST resistant isolates was not reported (74). Furthermore, despite its critical importance, Australia's national registries (AURA, CF Data Registry, and the Australian Bronchiectasis Registry) do not routinely report CST resistance rates (53, 70, 75, 76).

To the best of our knowledge, our study reports the first documented CST-resistant P. aeruginosa isolate (SCHI0273. S.1) isolated from an Australian bronchiectasis cohort. The isolate was recovered from an elderly participant on long-term nebulised CST with severely impaired lung function (FEV 1 : 39.5%) and a severe BSI score of 14. CST resistance is predominantly driven via activation of two-component systems, frequently PmrAB or PhoPQ, which mediates the addition of positively charged moieties to lipid A, preventing the initial electrostatic interactions of CST (71,72,77,78). We identified a missense mutation in pmrB (Val136Met), which was recently identified in an experimental CST evolution study (79), and a phoQ mutation (Gln263His), reported once prior in a hospitalised patient with a CST-resistant P. aeruginosa infection of unspecified origin (80). Although this novel combination of mutations is strongly suspected to confer CST resistance, functional verification is required to confirm their role.

Β-lactams are a cornerstone of P. aeruginosa treatment in BE cohorts, acting as the primary intravenous option in cases of oral CIP therapy failure. It is therefore unsurprising we identified numerous mutations associated with β-lactam resistance, many deleteriously impacting OprD, PBPs, and efflux pump regulators (Table 2, Table S2). Under baseline conditions, AmpR represses ampC transcription; however, deleterious variants can abrogate its repressor function, leading to greatly increased ampC expression and β-lactam hydrolysing capacity (81, 82). Although ampR mutations are frequently described in CF isolates (58,81) they are reportedly rare in bronchiectasis cohorts, with Hilliam and colleagues reporting a 1% ampR mutation rate (27). Similarly, AmpC Val239Ala, identified in six (4.4%) of our bronchiectasis isolates, has been previously reported in CF cohorts, and is associated with CAZ and TZP resistance (83). Interestingly, our cohort had higher prevalences of AmpC Val239Ala (4.4% vs 1.0%, p <0.001) and AmpR Asp135Asn (5.1% vs 2.2%, p=0.029) determinants compared with the global bronchiectasis isolates, indicating a potential overrepresentation of AmpR/AmpC-driven β-lactam resistance in Australian bronchiectasis cases that deserves further exploration.

In addition to ampC upregulation, cell permeability alterations play an important role in conferring β-lactam resistance. As such, we found three nonsense OprD variants (Phe36fs, Trp65*, Gln79fs) that occurred between residues 1-82, affecting the β-barrel structural region (Table 3) and rendering the cell impermeable to carbapenems (84). Additional deleterious OprD AMR determinants (Gln166fs, Phe308fs, Arg342fs, Trp417*, Ala420fs) were also identified in our cohort. In all cases, isolates with these mutations were resistant to imipenem, and most were resistant to meropenem, consistent with the known role of certain OprD variants to confer carbapenem resistance (25). Similarly, other bronchiectasis studies have identified numerous OprD variants responsible for IPM resistance, rather than acquired carbapenemases, highlighting the crucial importance of OprD mutation in conferring carbapenem AMR in P. aeruginosa (20, 21). In strains resistant to all four tested β-lactam classes (carbapenems, cephalosporins, penicillins, monobactams), multiple mutations affecting ftsI, nalD, oprD, and ampR were present; all likely contribute to this MDR phenotype (85) (86) (87).

Comparison of AMR rates towards the β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations, TZP and C/T, identified three-fold higher C/T resistance, despite all participants being C/T treatment-naïve prior to our study. Among the TZP resistant isolates, we identified a significant association with XDR status (p=<0.001), which likely reflects higher TZP use to treat severe infections with recalcitrant strains (63). It is less clear why we observed high C/T resistance rates in our cohort given that this phenotype was not associated with AMR/MDR/XDR status (p>0.05), prior or active C/T use, or cross-resistance. One possibility is that some Australian strains possess intrinsic C/T resistance, encoded by geographically distinct yet undefined AMR resistance mechanisms. Another more likely explanation is that current EUCAST disc diffusion criteria for C/T (< 23mm for AMR strains (42)) lack sufficient stringency for AMR strains, as most of our resistant isolates measured between 20-22mm (n= 29/40). Further research is clearly required to identify the AMR determinants driving baseline C/T resistance, and to more effectively categorise disc diffusion criteria for this newer antibiotic.

No high-risk MGEs were identified in our cohort, such as metallo-β-lactamases (e.g. bla VIM or bla IMP carbapenemases), extended spectrum β-lactamases, or mobile CST resistance (mcr) genes. However, given the recent 2024 Australian CARAlert Annual Report found a 29.3% increase in carbapenemase-producing P. aeruginosa 2023 to 2024, the lack of MGEs in this cohort is likely temporary (88). Nevertheless, our current results further support our prior observation that Australian P. aeruginosa strains evolve AMR predominantly via chromosomal mutations, rather than acquisition of AMR-encoding MGEs (25).

Global P. aeruginosa epidemiology is largely driven by the dissemination of international high-risk clones. These STs are distinguished by their high MDR rates, environmental persistence, frequent hospital transmission, and successful lung colonisation (89). In our cohort, we identified five high-risk clones (Table S3), with ST274 being the most prevalent (n=9, 6.6%), along with several common Australian lineages, including ST155 (n=5/137, 3.6%), ST782/AUST-08 (n=2/137, 1.5%), and ST260 (n=1/137, 0.7%), but no high-risk Australian CF clones such as ST649/AUST-01, ST266/AUST-02, or ST801/AUST-06 (90).

The high ST274 rate in our population is concerning as this ST is also common in people with CF, where it is associated with high AMR rates (89) (90) (91) (92). Xu et al. also found ST274 (n= 16/306, 5.2%) to be the most common ST in bronchiectasis (20). ST274 has been reported previously in Germany and two UK studies; however, these bronchiectasis studies found greater proportions of other international clones that were either absent (ST17 [Clone C]), or less prevalent in our study (ST253 [n=7/137, 5.1%], and ST395 [n=1/137, 0.7%]) (27, 93, 94). ST395 has not been reported in Australian CF populations, only bronchiectasis cohorts; therefore, a potential association between ST395 and bronchiectasis may exist, although this requires further validation.

On a phylogenomic scale, we found no distinct geographic clustering when comparing our Australian isolates to the global dataset (Figure 5). This panmictic population structure has been noted in prior studies investigating both clinical and environmental P. aeruginosa from similar geographical regions (25, 95), and other bronchiectasis studies (20, 27, 65), demonstrating that P. aeruginosa clones are highly transmissible across geographic boundaries.

In people with bronchiectasis, P. aeruginosa lung infections can persist for years, even decades, despite aggressive antimicrobial therapy (27). Our phylogenomic analysis confirmed persistence (i.e. infection with the same strain for ≥6 months) in 13 participants, with the longest example being at least 9 years (Table S3). In two cases, we observed substantially higher mutation rates; specifically, in P25 this elevated rate was attributed to a 50kb recombination event with an unsampled P. aeruginosa strain, resulting in >2,000 SNP differences over just a six-month period (Figure 4). In P09, latter strains accumulated ~1,000 mutations over an 8.7-year period (SNP accumulation rate of 19.0 substitutions per million base pairs/year), noticeably higher than the average 1.16 substitutions per million base pairs/year for the remaining 11 participants (Figure 4). No high-consequence MutL, MutS, or UvrD variants were identified in P09 isolates, with the latter strains encoding MutS Asp552Asn; however, we deem this variant as speculative given that no other MutS missense variants (e.g. Gly31Ser, Ala187Val, Ile579Val) were definitively associated with hypermutation. In contrast, 9/59 (15.3%) of participants (P04, P30, P31, P40, P41, P46, P47, P49, P56) encoded high-consequence MutS or MutL variants, indicating that these participants harboured hypermutable P. aeruginosa (Table S3). Hypermutation and recombination thus represent key adaptative strategies adopted by P. aeruginosa in bronchiectasis airways to increase genetic heterogeneity, in turn accelerating the transition towards chronic persistence and potential AMR acquisition.

Alongside hypermutation, we observed several convergent within-host evolutionary signatures in persistent isolates, consistent with the transition to a chronic, sessile, biofilmdriven lifestyle (Table S3, Table S4). Deleteriously affected loci comprised those associated with motility/chemotaxis (aer, chpA, chpE, cupA2-5, cupB3/5/6, cupC1/2, fleS, fleR, flgK, flgL, flhA, fliC, fliI, pilA, pilB, pilD, pilG, pilJ, pilM, pilN, pilP, pilQ, pilV, pilW) lipopolysaccharide synthesis or remodelling (arnD, arnT, gmd, galU, lpxO2, migA, pagL, rmlA, rmd, waaC, waaL, wapR, wbpM, wbpY, wbpZ) loci, virulence and quorum sensing (exsA, exoS, exoT, exoY, lasA, lasI, lasR, mvfR, pscB, pscP, pqsA, pqsB, vreA/I/R, xcpR), iron and haem acquisition (fiuA, fpvB, fpvI, fpvR, hemO, hasD, hasR, pchD, pchE, pchF, tonB2), and biofilm exopolysaccharide/mucoidy production (algG, algL, algU, mucA, mucD, pelB, pelD, pelE, pelG, pslH, pslL, rpoS, tpbB, vfr, wspR, yfiR) (Table S3, Table S4). These hallmark adaptations are consistent with the switch to chronic persistence in the hostile airway environment previously reported in P. aeruginosa and other bacterial respiratory pathogens (96). Such mutations provide highly favourable conditions for P. aeruginosa by enhancing immune evasion (especially via reduced immunogenicity), rewiring metabolic pathways to reduce energy-expensive cellular processes, and passively increasing antimicrobial and bacteriophage resistance via the production of highly protective, mucoidrich biofilms and altered or absent lipopolysaccharide moieties. In addition, we identified 28 strains encoding the PA5438 (GavR) Ser275_Arg277del variant, which is strongly associated with the transition from acute to chronic P. aeruginosa infection (32). Taken together, these substantial genomic adaptations drive a high rate of chronic persistence that closely mirrors CF cohorts, ultimately establishing a formidable barrier to successful immunological and antimicrobial eradication.

Our study has several limitations. First, despite our isolates being collected from multiple tertiary centres, they were collected from the same subtropical geographical region (Southeast for a subset of participants, leaving gaps in our understanding of parallel within-host 568 evolution pathways, transient infections with different lineages, and additional transmission 569 and population dynamics. Third, functional validation of novel AMR determinants, including 570 those encoding CST or C/T resistance, was outside of scope for this work, but represent an 571 important avenue for future research given the importance of these antibiotics in 572 contemporary P. aeruginosa treatment regimens. 573

CONCLUSION

Our findings shed important light on contemporary AMR prevalence, genetic diversity, and within-host dynamics of P. aeruginosa from an Australian bronchiectasis cohort. We found higher than anticipated rates of AMR/MDR/XDR, including the first reported CST resistant isolate in our region. In all cases, AMR was conferred by chromosomal mutation rather than MGE acquisition. The detection of persistent infections, within-host adaptive evolution, and multi-lineage co-infections further highlights the dynamic and heterogeneous nature of P. aeruginosa in the bronchiectatic airway. Our findings emphasise the need for accurate AST and genomic sequencing of P. aeruginosa from bronchiectasis airways to assist with the development of personalised treatment strategies.
