To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 3 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. This module retrieves information from repositories.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Introduction

Vocabulary is among the most stable indices of verbal ability in the adult neuropsychological repertoire (Yuspeh et al., 1998). It resists the decline seen in speeded and fluid measures, permitting the estimation of premorbid ability and improving the classification of impairment when it is included in demographic correction (Lezak et al., 2012).

Vocabulary tests can be broadly divided into three formats: (1) fixed-form tests in which all participants receive the same set of items; (2) terminating fixed-form tests in which all participants receive the same predetermined sequence that halts based on discontinuation rules; and (3) adaptive tests in which item difficulty changes dynamically as a function of the participant's responses. Most clinical vocabulary measures use one of the first two approaches. Vocabulary tests incorporate four major response formats: (1) Verbal definitions scored by the examiner (e.g., WAIS); (2) Pronunciation of irregular words, scored by the examiner (e.g., NART, ANART, TOPF); (3) Picture-word matching scored objectively (PPVT, NIH toolbox); and (4) Synonym selection, scored objectively.

For example, a fixed-form verbal test with stopping rules, such as the WAIS Vocabulary subtest (Wechsler et al., 2008), moves a participant through thirty items from a designated start point and stops after three consecutive failures. Several features limit the precision of this approach. First, response scoring depends partly on subjective judgment and may therefore be influenced by the concordance of the participant's response style, dialect, and strategy with that of the examiner. Second, while items may be reliably ordered by global difficulty at a population level, ordering may not hold for the individual words at a particular difficulty level in the test because an individual's word knowledge is influenced by education, occupation, reading experience, culture, and idiosyncratic exposures. Third, scores reflect response variability as well as underlying vocabulary knowledge: an anomalous failure on an easy item affects the score even when it provides little information about the boundary of the individual's vocabulary. Finally, fixed-sequence testing is inefficient, administering items below the participant's ability level, while undersampling vocabulary around the critical boundary -at and above the estimated vocabulary level.

Irregular-word reading tests such as the National Adult Reading Test (Nelson & Willison, 1982), the AmNART (Grober et al., 1991) and the Test of Premorbid Functioning [TOPF] (Wechsler, 2009) require individuals to read aloud irregular words with their pronunciation evaluated by the examiner. Irregular-word pronunciation tests measure familiarity with the conventional pronunciation of words rather than their semantic representations. Consequently, a native speaker may have encountered a word often enough to pronounce it correctly without knowing its precise meaning. Conversely, an individual-particularly a non-native English speaker-may understand a written word while being unfamiliar with its conventional English pronunciation. Pronunciation-based scoring may also be influenced by a participant's accent or dialect. Moreover, ceiling effects are common, limiting sensitivity in individuals with large vocabularies (van der Linde & Bright, 2024).

Thus, performance on tests such as the NART shares about 50% of variance (r = 0.75) with WAIS vocabulary scores (Uttl, 2002).

Picture-vocabulary tests such as the Peabody Picture Vocabulary Test (Dunn & Dunn, 2007) and NIH Toolbox Picture Vocabulary Test (Gershon et al., 2014) require participants to match a picture to a word and thus provide an efficient, objectively scored measure of receptive vocabulary. The NIH Toolbox Picture Vocabulary Test is a well-calibrated example: its 602-word bank was fit with a one-parameter Rasch model on 4,703 participants (including 1,513 adults) tested through 21 overlapping forms of 40 to 60 items, with at least 200 responses per item (Gershon et al., 2014). Importantly, it is administered adaptively, stopping at 25 items or when the standard error of the ability estimate falls below 0.3, and retests with an intraclass correlation of .80 and a convergent validity of r = .80 against the PPVT-4 (Dunn & Dunn, 2007). However, picture vocabulary word selection is constrained by the imageability requirement, with high concreteness words constituting about 20% of large word corpora (Brysbaert et al., 2014). Because every target and distractor must be represented unambiguously by an image, picture-vocabulary tests necessarily limit the representation of abstract, relational, and semantically nuanced vocabulary.

Synonym matching tests require participants to select synonyms or phrases most closely matching the meaning of a word. For example, the Shipley vocabulary test is a self-administered test of 40 words where participants select the closest synonym from four alternatives (Kaya et al., 2012). More recent vocabulary tests using synonym selection have incorporated larger corpora. For example, the Vocabulary Size Test (Nation & Beglar, 2007) orders items purely by word frequency band (ten items per 1,000-word-family band over fourteen bands of the British National Corpus) with no empirical difficulty estimates and no adaptive rule. LexTALE (Lemhöfer & Broersma, 2012) uses word/non-word discriminations, selecting 60 items from a 240-item pool using classical percent-correct values and item-total correlations obtained from a pilot of eighteen participants, and then deliberately did not order them by difficulty, arranging them instead so that no more than five words or nonwords appeared consecutively. Recently, the NIH Mobile Toolbox group developed a Word Meaning test (Young et al., 2024) incorporating more than 1,000 words using norming procedures similar to those used to develop the Picture Vocabulary test. The Word Meaning test properties are compared with those of the CCAB vocabulary test in the discussion below.

The CCAB vocabulary test uses a 4-choice synonym selection test based on a difficulty ordering derived from aggregated response data from the widely used, publicly accessible online vocabulary game FreeRice.com, in which players selected word synonyms. We categorized 600 ranked words into 60 levels, edited the synonym alternatives, and reordered the list with Rasch procedures against pilot data in 60 participants.

Method

Participants

There were 2,181 English-speaking adults who completed the vocabulary test between May 2021 and September 2026. The CCAB Spanish-language vocabulary test uses a separate word bank and was excluded. Relatively complete questionnaire and demographic data were available for 2,176 participants: age 18 to 89 years (M = 51.9, SD = 17.4); 57.4% of the 2,142 who reported sex were women; mean education was 15.4 years (SD = 2.4). Race/ethnicity was coded as a mutually exclusive variable; of the 2,175 reporting race there were 367 Hispanic/Latino participants of any race (the coding used in the CCAB C-model), 709 non-Hispanic (NH) Whites, 475 NH Blacks, 388 NH Asians, and 236 NH "other", including mixed race. Age-band sizes were 291 at 18-29, 505 at 30-44, 526 at 45-59, 659 at 60-74, and 195 at 75-89. Three hundred participants (13.8%) reported that English was not spoken in their home while growing up. Testing was telemedical: participants were tested in their homes by an experienced proctor who monitored performance through a builtin web interface. All participants gave written informed consent under WIRB protocol 20201196. The vocabulary test is one subtest of the California Cognitive Assessment Battery (Woods et al., 2024); a companion report describes the battery's scoring models which include the vocabulary score as a covariate in the comprehensive C-model correction of the other tests (Woods et al., 2026). Table 1 summarizes the sample.

The word bank

The 600-word bank, with 10 words at each of 60 difficulty levels, was derived from aggregated response data from the online vocabulary game FreeRice.com, in which players answered four-alternative synonym questions. The list was pruned, edited, and reordered with Rasch procedures against pilot CCAB data and edited prior to use with the present sample. Each item presents a target word with four response alternatives, one of which is a synonymous word or short phrase.

The adaptive vocabulary test

Participants responded by screen touch with 24 words presented to every participant. There was no time limit, and administration was self-paced. The task, including instructions, required a median of 227 s (IQR 200-258 s); the interval from the first stimulus to the last response had a median of 173 s (IQR 146-203 s).

Difficulty was adapted with a 1-up/1-down staircase: a correct response moved the participant to a harder level and an error to an easier one. Participants began at level 16 with step sizes of 4, 4, 3, 3, 2, 2, and 1, with the step size decreasing after successive performance reversals (a change from correct to incorrect or vice versa). These large initial step sizes generally brought participants near their final difficulty level within 10-18 trials (Figure 1). Duplicate word selection at a repeated level (e.g., level 34 in Figure 1) was avoided by sampling words at that level without replacement. Scoring

Two vocabulary scores were computed. The default was the QuickSIN estimate, the Spearman-Kärber arithmetic used to score the QuickSIN speech-in-noise test (Killion et al., 2004). The proportion correct was computed at each difficulty level visited, so that all 24 trials contributed, and the score was the Spearman-Kärber estimate of the level at which the proportion correct crosses 0.5. With the levels visited ordered L₁ < … < Lₖ and pᵢ the proportion correct at Lᵢ, the proportion was taken as p₀ = 1 at L₀ < L₁ -1 and as pₖ₊₁ = 0 at Lₖ₊₁ > Lₖ + 1, and the score is Σᵢ (pᵢ -pᵢ₊₁)(Lᵢ + Lᵢ₊₁)/2 for i = 0 … k, the weighted estimate of the 50%-correct level. An alternative score, the mean of the last four difficulty levels, was closely related (r = 0.98). Observed ranges were 3.75 to 57.75 for the QuickSIN estimate and 1.00 to 59.75 for the mean of the last four. Throughout this report, "difficulty level" refers to a word's position in the bank and "vocabulary score" to a participant's estimate on the same 1-60 scale. Both scores were also compared with the model-based ability estimate from the Rasch calibration described below.

Accuracy, the proportion of the 24 items answered correctly, reflected how quickly the adaptive staircase settled rather than where it settled. Response times were summarized as the mean of log response time over responded trials, for correct trials and for all trials, and as the coefficient of variation of the raw times.

Demographic screen and models

Twenty one candidate demographic correlates were screened: age, education in years, gender, Latino ethnicity, daily medication count, a socioeconomic composite, computer use, weekly hours of reading, parental education for each parent and their mean, highest and most recent household income, subjective social standing (a 0-1 self-rating), number of rooms in the household, marital status (married vs. not), whether English was spoken in the home while growing up (coded as English as a second language, ESL, when it was not), family history of dementia, and the GAD-7, GDS, and Cognitive Failures Questionnaire scales. Selfreported race and Hispanic ethnicity were combined into one mutually exclusive race/ethnicity variable -Hispanic/Latino of any race, then non-Hispanic (NH) White, Black, Asian, and other or mixed race -the coding used in the CCAB C-model (Woods et al., 2026), and analyzed as group means and as model indicators rather than as a screened correlate. Of the 367 Hispanic participants, 256 recorded their race as other. Education in years was coded from the categorical education item by the standard CCAB mapping; the four education categories used in the C-model (high school or less, some college or associate's degree, bachelor's degree, graduate degree) were examined as an alternative specification. Age-squared was orthogonalized against age.

Two regression models of the vocabulary score were compared. The first was the common age-educationgender (AEG) adjustment in clinical use. The second, the comprehensive model, was selected from the 21 demographic and questionnaire candidates by iteratively removing the predictor with the smallest standardized coefficient until all remaining |β| ≥ 0.10, on the 1,420 participants with all candidate predictors. This retained eight terms. Most recent household income (β = -0.12, zero-order r = +0.18) was then dropped because its negative weight arose only in the presence of highest household income, leaving seven terms: age, education, reading hours, computer use, ESL, parental education, and highest household income. After selection, the seven-term model was refitted in the 1,564 participants with complete data on the seven predictors. Each model was fitted with and without race/ethnicity indicators (NH Black, Hispanic, NH Asian, and NH other, against NH White). Residual scores were compared across the five groups, and the proportion of each group falling in the bottom 7% of residuals in the entire population, the CCAB low-score flag, was tabulated. The comprehensive model was checked by 10-fold cross-validation and by bootstrap estimates of coefficient stability.

Rasch calibration of the word bank

A dichotomous Rasch model was fitted to the trial-level responses of all 2,181 participants to estimate each word's difficulty independently of its assigned level. The model is P(correct) = 1 / (1 + exp(-(θ -b))), with θ the examinee's ability and b the word's difficulty on a common logit scale. It was fitted by marginal maximum likelihood with an EM algorithm over a 71-node quadrature, using each participant's first session (52,224 responses). The current operational bank contains 600 words. Eleven retired items that appeared transiently during a 2022-2023 bank revision (121 responses in all) were excluded from calibration and scoring; of the 600 current-bank words, 593 were administered at least once and seven, at levels 2, 3, and 59, were never sampled. A weak empirical-Bayes prior, b ∼ N(α + β × assigned level, τ²) with the hyperparameters estimated from words answered at least 50 times, kept sparsely sampled words finite and pulled them toward the difficulty typical of their assigned level. Every session was then scored on the frozen word difficulties with Warm's weighted likelihood estimate of ability (Warm, 1989). The within-level spread of difficulty was summarized as the SD of the calibrated difficulties of well-sampled words around their assigned level's mean, corrected for sampling variance, and expressed in levels by dividing by the slope of difficulty of assigned levels.

To test whether the word difficulties were the same across race/ethnicity groups, the model was refitted separately to the NH White, NH Black, NH Asian, Hispanic, and NH other participants (the mutually exclusive coding above). Each group's scale was linked to the NH White scale by equating the mean difficulty of the words answered at least 30 times in both groups, and differential item functioning was computed for each of those words as the group minus NH White difficulty on the linked scale, with a false-discovery-rate correction over words within each group. The SD of the differential item functioning expected from estimation error alone was the root mean square of the standard errors of the paired difficulty estimates. Because the items are fouralternative, the calibration was repeated as a sensitivity analysis with a fixed lower asymptote of 0.25, P = 0.25 + 0.75 × [1 + exp(-(θ -b))]⁻¹, and the two solutions were compared on item ordering, person scores, and retest reliability.

As an external criterion, the calibrated difficulties and the assigned levels were compared with the word prevalence norms of Brysbaert et al. (2019), the proportion of 221,268 respondents in a lexical-decision task who reported knowing each of 61,855 English lemmas, expressed on a probit scale.

Reliability

Retests occurred at 1 day (n = 100) and 12 months (n = 307) for different participants. Retest reliability was computed as the Pearson correlation and as the intraclass correlation for absolute agreement from a two-way random-effects model, ICC(A,1), with 95% confidence intervals (McGraw & Wong, 1996). Dependent correlations measured in the same participants were compared with Steiger's test.

Reliability

One-day retest reliability was 0.83 for the QuickSIN score and 0.82 for the mean of the last four (n = 100); at twelve months the corresponding values were 0.81 and 0.79 (n = 307). Because mean change between sessions was negligible intraclass correlations were identical to the Pearson values to two decimals: ICC(A,1) = 0.83 (95% CI 0.75-0.88) and 0.81 (0.76-0.84) for the QuickSIN score at one day and twelve months. The twelve-month ICC is numerically similar to the .80 ICC reported for the NIH Toolbox picture vocabulary test over a 7-to-21-day interval.

Practice effects were negligible, due to the large size of the item bank: with 600 words and 24 items, repeat exposure to the same word was uncommon. The vocabulary score changed by +0.19 levels at one day (d = +0.03) and +0.41 levels at twelve months (d = +0.05); neither differed from zero. Mean response time shortened by about 9% at one day and 2% at twelve months, presumably reflecting increased familiarity with the interface.

Rasch calibration of the word bank

Because adaptive routing determines which examinees see which words, the raw percentage correct for any item is a poor measure of its difficulty. Difficulty levels 16, 20, 24, 28, and 32 were the ascending steps most examinees passed through, and their words scored 73 to 87% correct, against 54 to 70% at levels between those (i.e., that were selected following higher-level failures); a level-16 word was administered to 2,142 participants and a level-17 word to only 172. In Rasch terms the two levels were only 0.37 logits apart, about two levels' worth of difficulty, so the accuracy gap between them (87.1% vs 59.8%) reflected the performance of different participants, not inherent item difficulty. The assigned difficulty levels were broadly right and locally wrong (Figure 4). Assigned level and estimated difficulty correlated ρ = 0.95 among the 316 words administered at least 50 times, and ρ = 0.99 across all 600 current-bank words, the latter measure inflated by forcing the 195 words answered fewer than 20 times to their assigned levels. Ranking all words by calibrated difficulty and reassigning them ten per level, the median absolute displacement across all well-sampled words, including those that did not move, was 2 levels. Overall 40% of well-sampled words moved three or more levels, 16% moved five or more, and 4 moved ten or more. Person separation reliability was 0.93, with a mean standard error of ability of 0.48 logits. Adding the 25% guessing floor to the model changed the scale but not the ordering: it stretched the bank range from 11.2 to 13.5 logits, but the two sets of difficulties correlated r = 0.999 across the 316 well-sampled words, 99.7% of words fell within one level of the same calibrated position, person estimates correlated r = 0.997, and twelvemonth retest reliability was 0.84 under both models. Because the guessing-adjusted model produced essentially identical item ordering, person ranking, and retest reliability, the simpler Rasch model was retained for the primary analyses.

The slope of calibrated difficulty on assigned level was 0.18 logits per level among well-sampled words. But words sharing an assigned level were spread 0.53 logits around their level's mean. Thus, within a single level the words ranged over about 2.9 levels' worth of difficulty. This has practical consequences: two words drawn from adjacent levels therefore differed by a mean of 0.18 logits of calibrated difficulty (SD 0.75), meaning that a one-level step actually raised the difficulty level only 60% of the time (a two-level step raised difficulty 69% of the time, and a four-level step 84%). Thus, the staircase's smallest steps were close to a coin flip in their difficulty adjustment.

Rescoring every session on the calibrated scale left the ranking of examinees almost unchanged (r = 0.99 with both level-based scores) but improved retest reliability: at twelve months the ability estimate retested at 0.84 (ICC 0.84, 95% CI 0.80-0.87) against 0.81 for the QuickSIN score (Steiger z = 4.2, p < .001).

Fit was good: mean infit 0.90 (SD 0.10) and mean outfit 0.87 (SD 0.21) among words answered at least 30 times, with 12 words flagged as overfitting (infit and outfit both < 0.7) and 4 as underfitting (infit > 1.3 or outfit > 1.5). The largest displacements were words answered correctly more often than their assigned level implied. For example, meme, assigned level 39 on a list assembled before the word entered general use was answered correctly by 97% of those who saw it, moving it down twelve levels.

Word difficulty and word prevalence

Of the 593 administered words, 499 appear in the prevalence norms of Brysbaert et al. (2019); the 94 that did not are rare words concentrated at the hard end of the bank. Across the matched words, calibrated difficulty correlated ρ = -0.85 with prevalence and the assigned level ρ = -0.87; among the 285 well-sampled words, where the range is narrower, the correlations were -0.65 and -0.71. The proportion of respondents responding correctly fell from 1.00 at level 1 to 0.97 at level 16, 0.72 at level 31, 0.58 at level 41, and 0.31 at level 56. Prevalence added nothing to the prediction of calibrated difficulty once the assigned level was known (standardized β = 0.01 for prevalence against 0.95 for level), and within assigned levels it was unrelated to the calibrated scatter (r = 0.01). The within-level spread of difficulty therefore likely reflected properties of the synonym-choice items as well as word knowledge.

Discussion

A 24-item adaptive test requiring four minutes produced a vocabulary score that retested at 0.83 over one day and 0.81 over one year, spanned the ability range without floor or ceiling effects, showed no practice effect, and was higher at older ages. The score was most strongly associated with education, then reading habits, age, and the indices of family and household circumstance, and non-English upbringing. A comprehensive demographic model substantially attenuated but did not eliminate the differences between NH White participants and the Hispanic, Asian, other, and Black groups; all four adjusted coefficients remained significant, the Black-White difference the largest. Rasch calibration and independent word prevalence norms strongly supported the assigned difficulty levels while showing that the resolution of a level-based score is approximately 2-3 levels.

Demographic predictors of vocabulary

Education was the strongest single correlate and was not merely a proxy for reading: each survived control for the other and for age. Parental education retained a partial correlation of 0.24 once the participant's own education was removed, reflecting the independent effect of family background. That highest household income predicted better than most recent household income (0.27 against 0.18) suggesting that scores reflect accumulated exposure to language over a lifetime rather than present circumstances. Participants who did not speak English in the home while growing up scored 5.2 levels lower (d = -0.57), a difference that persisted with education and age controlled (partial r = -0.26), and carried the largest weight in the comprehensive model. This is consistent with the bilingual lexical deficit documented by meta-analysis: bilingual speakers know fewer words in their second languages than monolingual speakers, with an effect of about half a standard deviation on vocabulary tests (Bylund et al., 2023). Marital status and family history of dementia correlated with the score only because both were age-related. There was no sex difference (d = 0.007, 95% CI -0.08 to +0.09). This is consistent with studies of other English-language vocabulary and premorbid-ability measures, including the NAART, Shipley Vocabulary, WAIS-IV Vocabulary, and the English NIH Toolbox Picture Vocabulary Test, which generally report negligible male-female differences.

Vocabulary scores were approximately 0.15 levels higher per year of age across the cross-sectional adult sample, within every education stratum, though at different rates (0.09, 0.10 and 0.18 levels per year). The magnitude agrees with large web-based estimates of vocabulary growth across adulthood: Brysbaert et al. (2016) tested 221,268 participants and estimated the gain at roughly one new lemma every two days between twenty and sixty.

Race and demographic adjustment

We examined race/ethnicity as a social/demographic grouping variable, not a biological construct. Race/ethnic differences were among the largest seen with any CCAB test, and were attenuated, but not eliminated, with the comprehensive model: with ESL, parental education, and income in the model, the adjusted group coefficients were -0.27 to -0.38 SD for Asian, Hispanic, and Other groups, and -0.76 SD for NH Black participants, all significant. The pattern is the one described in the literature on quality of education: Manly et al. (2002) showed that reading level, a proxy for the quality rather than the quantity of schooling, attenuated Black-White differences on neuropsychological tests that years of education did not, and Manly et al. (2004) extended the finding to acculturation. Years of schooling, parental education, and income are coarse proxies for the lifetime exposure to the written and spoken lexicon that a synonym test measures. There was little evidence that the group differences arose from systematic differential item functioning: difficulties estimated separately in the NH Black, NH Asian, and Hispanic groups agreed closely with the NH White calibration (r = 0.96) across the adequately sampled portion of the bank (Table 6). Whether to add race/ethnicity indicators, which equalize group means by construction and reduce the low-score flag ratio from 8.0:1 to 2.3:1, is a scoring-policy decision rather than a statistical one.

List ordering and the staircase

The CCAB list was ordered by aggregated play on a web game rather than by expert judgment or formal calibration. Rasch calibration showed that the ordering closely paralleled a formal calibration. Minor differences reflected usage drift: Roivainen (2014) traced Wordsum, WAIS, and WISC vocabulary items through Google Ngrams and found that usage frequency correlated .38 to .73 with item difficulty. Here the largest displacements in CCAB vocabulary items (e.g., meme, aphasia) reflected more common usage. The rank order of vocabulary items is nonetheless durable over decades (Dorius & Alwin, 2016; Malhorta et al., 2007) and the agreement of the assigned levels with the Brysbaert et al. (2019) prevalence norms (ρ = -0.87), collected from a different population using a different method, shows that the inherited ordering tracks word knowledge in English, and not only performance in this sample.

The adaptive staircase used a 1-up/1-down rule with equal steps in the two directions, which converges on the 50%-correct point (Levitt, 1971). Accuracy over the final four trials (0.55) confirmed this-with a 25% chance of a correct guess, 0.55 corresponds to knowing about 40% of the words at the final level, and the 0.50 convergence point to knowing a third-and accuracy over all 24 trials was higher (0.64) only because the sampling began with words which were below the final level of most subjects. The convergence point does not affect the QuickSIN score, which is computed from the proportion correct at every level visited rather than from where the track ended. In these data that score reproduced the model-based Rasch ability estimate at r = 0.99.

The within-level spread of difficulty has a practical implication for the resolution of level-based scores. A step of one level raised difficulty only 60% of the time, and a step of two levels 69% of the time, so the final adjustments of the staircase, which the mean of the last four averages, were close to a coin flip. The effective resolution on the level scale was therefore 2-3 levels. The Rasch ability estimate, which carries its own standard error (0.48 logits, about 2.6 levels), makes this explicit, and its higher twelve-month retest reliability (0.84 against 0.81) shows that part of the level-based score's retest error is attributable to the ordering rather than to the examinee.

WAIS and CCAB approaches

The WAIS-IV Vocabulary test is subjectively scored and requires 7-15 minutes for administration. It administers thirty items in a fixed sequence, starting at item 5 for standard administration and discontinuing after three consecutive scores of zero. Test length is therefore determined by performance: a strong examinee receives most of the list and a weak one stops early. This introduces dependence on local item ordering around the discontinuation point; after three consecutive zero scores, more difficult items are not sampled while in some cases an examinee who would have answered more difficult items correctly never sees them.

In the CCAB vocabulary test every participant receives 24 items; test length is fixed, and performance determines which difficulty levels are tested. The adaptive staircase settles near the participant's threshold quickly and thus, in contrast to the WAIS approach, samples words above and below the participant's ability level extensively. Administration time is predictable: a median of 227 s with an interquartile range of 200 to 258 s.

Comparisons with other adaptive vocabulary tests

Two useful comparison classes are calibrated adaptive vocabulary tests and large crowdsourced vocabulary studies. The NIH Toolbox picture vocabulary test is a fully calibrated adaptive test. Its bank of 602 Raschcalibrated words is close to the size of the CCAB stimulus set; its administered length of at most 25 items is near the CCAB 24-trial structure; and its 7-to-21-day retest intraclass correlation of .80 is close to the CCAB twelve-month ICC of 0.81 and one-day ICC of 0.83. The core difference between the two tests lies in the provenance of the word difficulties: the NIH assignments came from 4,703 participants (1,513 adults) and at least 200 responses per item under a documented Rasch calibration, whereas the CCAB assignments came from aggregated web play and were calibrated here against 2,181 adults.

Another NIH Toolbox variant, the Mobile Toolbox Word Meaning test (Young et al., 2024) more closely resembles the CCAB vocabulary test. The Word Meaning Test is a five-alternative synonym test whose 1,363word bank was Rasch-calibrated on 7,525 online participants, retested at an intraclass correlation of 0.65 over two weeks. Because items answered correctly by fewer than 15% or more than 85% of the calibration sample were discarded, its bank has a much narrower difficulty range than CCAB vocabulary, spanning 4.3 logits against 11.2 for the CCAB bank. The adaptive rules also differ. Word Meaning begins every examinee at an ability estimate of zero, re-estimates ability after each response, and selects the next item to give a 0.675 probability of a correct answer, stopping after 15 to 25 items when the standard error falls to 0.50. The CCAB staircase begins at level 16 and adapts level rapidly in fixed steps, reducing step size after successive reversals. For an examinee who answers the first four items correctly, the CCAB staircase rises 16 levels, about 2.9 logits, whereas the Word Meaning test using an item-response estimate with a standard normal prior rises about 1 logit in decelerating steps. Thus, the CCAB staircase reaches difficult test items in fewer trials, often overshooting the participant's final level (Figure 1). Because the Word Meaning selection rule is deterministic given the responses, examinees with the same early response pattern receive the same words unless an exposure-control procedure is applied. The CCAB test draws each word at random from the ten words at each level, so that identical level paths yield different words and a retest rarely repeats words (e.g., average two or three of the 24 words). Word Meaning reports its score as the Rasch ability estimate; the CCAB test reports a level score and, from the present calibration, is virtually identical to a Rasch ability estimate. The reported Word Meaning two-week retest intraclass correlation was 0.65 in 141 participants, against 0.83 at one day and 0.81 at twelve months for the CCAB level score and 0.84 at both intervals for the ability estimate. The demographic profiles of the CCAB and Word Meaning test are similar, both show significant correlations with age and education.

Limitations

The sample was a volunteer telemedical sample, not a census-matched standardization sample, and its education distribution (M = 15.4 years) is above the nationwide population mean. Some demographic questions were unanswered: the parental-education, income, and social-standing items were unavailable in about 25% of participants, so the comprehensive model was fitted on 1,564 participants. Race was selfreported into four broad categories. The twenty-one demographic variables in Table 3 were not corrected for multiple comparisons, and the variables at the bottom of that table should be treated as hypotheses. The empirical-Bayes prior pulled sparsely sampled words toward the difficulty expected from their assigned levels, inflating agreement across the full bank, so the correlation among the 316 well-sampled words is the more informative estimate; 195 words at the two ends of the bank were too sparsely sampled for stable data-driven difficulty estimation; 94 words, mostly at the hard end, are absent from the prevalence norms. The invariance analyses could compare only 91-102 of the 600 words per race/ethnicity group, those answered at least 30 times in both the group and the NH White participants, and were confined to levels 16-40; their power to detect differential item functioning smaller than about 0.8 logits in a single word was low, so while they rule out a general reordering, small item-specific effects may have been missed. Because the age analyses were crosssectional, the observed age gradient likely reflects cohort as well as within-person aging effects. Concurrent validity against an established individually administered vocabulary or premorbid-ability measure was not available in this cohort and remains to be established.

Conclusion

The CCAB adaptive vocabulary test measured verbal ability in four minutes with a one-day retest reliability of 0.83, a twelve-month reliability of 0.81, no practice effect, and negligible floor and ceiling effects across the adult span. The vocabulary score tracked education (r = 0.43), hours reading (r = 0.39), age (r = 0.29), household income, parental education, and English-language background, but not sex. A seven-term comprehensive model explained 32% of the variance (cross-validated R² = .31) against 17% for ageeducation-gender adjustment alone, and attenuated but did not eliminate racial/ethnic differences: adjusted differences from NH White participants were 0.27-0.38 SD for Hispanic, Asian, and other participants and 0.76 SD for Black participants. A Rasch calibration of the word bank showed that the ordering largely inherited from aggregated FreeRice response data agreed with estimated difficulty at ρ = 0.95 among well-sampled words, but that the within-level spread of about 0.53 logits limits the resolution of a level-based score to 2-3 levels. Rescoring on the calibrated scale raised twelve-month retest reliability from 0.81 to 0.84. Independent word prevalence norms agreed with the calibrated difficulties at ρ = -0.85.
