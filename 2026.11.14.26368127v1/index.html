Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Whose deposit is each link? Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Introduction

Looking up from a distant object and back to a line of print requires the eyes to change their relative angle (vergence) and hold the new alignment while taking in visual information. In Parkinson's disease (PD), abnormal basal ganglia activity may disrupt this process, directly or indirectly, altering how and when a vergence begins (latency), whether there is a slow smooth vergence or it is associated with rapid saccades (motor strategy), and how steadily the eyes maintain their position after vergence (gaze holding) (Almer et al., 2012).

Motor Strategy and Latency.

A shift in viewing depth can occur through vergence alone or in combination with a disconjugate saccade, rapidly reducing vergence error. Basal ganglia output and subthalamic nucleus (STN) activity may influence when a depth shift begins, whether a saccade is recruited, and when and how much it contributes. These effects can dissociate. More frequent saccades may not improve accuracy, and a larger saccadic contribution may leave final vergence gain unchanged (Busettini and Mays, 2005 , Quinet et al., 2020 , Zee et al., 1992).

Primate physiology identifies several routes for this control. Neurons in and around the supraoculomotor area (SOA) encode vergence velocity, angle, or both (Mays, 1984 , Mays et al., 1986 , Zhang et al., 1991). The STN may influence saccade initiation through the substantia nigra pars reticulata, whose inhibitory output regulates the superior colliculus (Hikosaka and Wurtz, 1985, Van Horn et al., 2013). Saccadic assistance allows different movement combinations to achieve the same fixation depth (Busettini and Mays, 2005). Collicular projections to the SOA and pontocerebellar circuits involving the nucleus reticularis tegmenti pontis (NRTP) may coordinate saccades with vergence (Bostan et al., 2010 , Gamlin and Clarke, 1995 , Glickstein et al., 1990 , May et al., 2021 , Stanton et al., 1988 , Takeichi et al., 2005). Cerebellar fastigial and interposed nuclei project to the superior colliculus, midbrain near-response regions, and central mesencephalic reticular formation (cMRF) (Bohlen et al., 2021 , May et al., 1990 , May et al., 1992). In the cMRF, saccadevergence burst neurons encode vergence velocity during disconjugate saccades. The saccadic contribution to a depth shift depends on the interaction between the burst and the remaining vergence error (Busettini and Mays, 2005, Quinet et al., 2020).

STN-region deep brain stimulation (DBS) provides an opportunity to probe these circuits in humans.

We hypothesized that stimulation alters vergence latency and saccade recruitment through modulation of frontal-basal ganglia signaling in the associative STN and possible antidromic recruitment of nearby cerebellothalamic fibers. Altered cerebellar output could change when the saccadic burst arrives relative to the remaining vergence error and how much it contributes to binocular alignment.

Gaze Holding and Vergence Neural Integration.

Maintaining alignment after a depth shift requires a vergence neural integrator that converts brief velocity commands into sustained position signals. The velocity and angle signals encoded in and around the SOA make this region a candidate substrate (Mays, 1984 , Mays et al., 1986 , Quinet et al., 2025). Fastigial and posterior interposed projections could allow the cerebellum to tune these sustained signals (Bohlen et al., 2021). Cerebellothalamic fibers near the field of Forel are accessible to STN-region DBS (Gallay et al., and cerebellar projections to the thalamus adjacent to the fields of Forel, limbic, accessory, and sensorimotor regions of the subhthalamus, and caudal parts of the thalamus. Connectomic analysis was performed using the HCP 1065 atlas to objectively examine the VTA overlap with number of white-matter tracks, including fields of Forel, cerebellothalamic projections, rubrospinal tracts, and cortico-pontine pathways.

Statistics Outcomes analysis

We compared convergence and divergence during DBS on and off in PD participants, retaining paired visits and determining eligibility separately for each outcome and direction. Outcomes included latency, first, intermediate, and final gain, gaze holding, and movement strategy. Gain was signed relative to demand, with unity indicating the requested amplitude. Absolute gain errors were calculated per take as |gain -1| and multiplied by demand to obtain angular errors. Subsequent correction was initial minus final error.

Behavioral analyses

Measurements were matched within participant, visit, direction, and nominal demand. Takes were averaged, using medians for latency, followed by equal averaging across matched demands and visits. Two-sided sign-flip tests assessed participant-level on-minus-off changes; participant bootstrap resampling provided confidence intervals. Permutation tests assessed strategy distributions, and paired slope comparisons assessed demand dependence. Trial-level mixed models adjusted for stimulation state, direction, their interaction, and demand, with participant and nested-visit random intercepts and participant-clustered intervals.

Anatomical associations and prediction

Ward clustering examined standardized behavioral changes, with silhouette scores and omission analyses assessing cluster structure. Anatomical associations were tested using Spearman correlations with 29,999 permutations and Benjamini-Hochberg correction. Eligible variables required at least 12 observations and sufficient variation.

Elastic-net models evaluated 42 anatomical predictors using outer leave-one-participant-out prediction and inner fivefold tuning, with preprocessing restricted to training folds. Prediction errors were compared with the training-cohort mean. Follow-up models examined associative STN coverage and convergence latency, and fasciculus subthalamicus activation and movement strategy.

Results

Clinical and ophthalmic characteristics

The paired DBS vergence analysis included 23 PD participants. Mean age was 68.2 ± 9.3 years, mean disease duration was 12.1 ± 6.7 years, and 22 of 23 participants were men. The mean motor score measured with Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale part 3 (UPDRS-III) was 36.3 ± 13.2 with DBS off and 14.2 ± 6.1 with DBS on. Mean daily levodopa intake was 769 ± 628 mg/day.

With DBS on, distance cover testing showed orthophoria in 19 of 23 participants (82.6%), whereas near cover testing documented exotropia or intermittent exotropia in 20 of 23 (87.0%). Visual acuity was 20/25 or better in 87.0% of left eyes and 78.3% of right eyes. Median measurable stereopsis was 100 arcsec (interquartile range, 60-100 arcsec). In participants with paired clinical convergence measurements, the mean on-minus-off change was -2.2 ± 6.9 for Royal Airforce (RAF) ruler distance (n = 17) and -4.5 ± 9.2 for distance to pen (n = 20). Effects of DBS on vergence

We quantified the within-participant effect of STN DBS on vergence in 23 PD participants. We examined initiation, early and final responses, movement strategy (saccade vergence combination), and post-vergence gaze holding (Fig 1A-F).

DBS reduced variability during post-vergence gaze holding

Participant-weighted averages showed that STN DBS improved gaze holding in convergence as well as divergence positions (Table 2). Variance, the measure of gaze holding, decreased from 0.005 to 0.003 during convergence (37.0% reduction, paired p = 0.007, Fig 1F) and from 0.004 to 0.002 during divergence (29.4% reduction, paired p = 0.028, Fig 1F). Across the 23 participants with data in both directions, the overall reduction was 33.3% with equal weighting of convergence and divergence (participant-bootstrap 95% CI, 13.7%-49.3% reduction, paired p ≈ 0.008, Table 2).

Final gain, first gain, and latency showed no clear average group-level change

Vergence responses often comprised a vergence movement and an accompanying disconjugate saccade, with either component occurring first. First vergence gain describes the initial vergence component, and final gain describes the alignment reached after the depth shift. First vergence gain increased in both directions (convergence, +0.10, paired p = 0.26; divergence, +0.04, paired p = 0.47, Fig 1B). The final gain was similar between stimulation states for convergence (paired p = 0.99) and increased for divergence (0.56 to 0.63, paired p = 0.40, Fig 1A).

Mean latency was shorter with DBS on in both directions, with uncertain paired estimates (Table 2). Convergence latency changed from 378 to 286 ms (-92 ms, paired p = 0.52, Fig 1E), and divergence latency from 280 to 204 ms (-76 ms, paired p = 0.38, Fig 1E).

DBS reduced initial convergence error

To assess the accuracy of the initial vergence response, we calculated initial angular error for each trial as the absolute difference between the desired vergence movement and the amplitude of the first vergence movement. This measure depicts how far the first movement landed from the target, regardless of whether it undershot or overshot. At the group level, STN DBS significantly reduced convergence initial angular error (DBS off 5.84°, DBS on: 4.45°, Fig 1C). For divergence, the reduction in initial angular error was smaller (Off: 5.51°; On:4.99°, Fig 1C), suggesting a weaker improvement in early divergence accuracy than in convergence (Table 2). Endpoint accuracy was quantified as absolute final-gain error. During convergence, this error decreased from 0.665 to 0.524 (-0.141, paired p = 0.07, Fig 1D). The paired comparison for divergence gave p = 0.59 (Table 2).

Strategy distributions showed no clear group-level DBS effect

Depth shifts often included a disconjugate saccade in addition to vergence. The combined response remained the predominant strategy in both DBS states, accounting for 84.3% and 84.6% of convergence responses, 83.0% and 82.2% of divergence responses, and 85.5% and 84.2% overall (OFF and ON, respectively; convergence p = 0.9, divergence p = 0.8, Table 3).

DBS effects did not differ significantly between convergence and divergence

Within-participant comparisons of DBS on-off changes showed no significant interaction with movement direction for any primary outcome (Table 4). The strongest evidence for an interaction was for final gain (convergence minus divergence, -0.10, p = 0.06). Interaction p values were 0.96 for first gain, 0.38 for absolute final-gain error, 0.46 for latency, 0.36 for gaze holding, and 0.71 for movement strategy distribution.

DBS did not significantly alter dependence of vergence behavior on target demand

To test whether DBS altered the scaling of vergence with stimulus amplitude, we compared off-state and on-state slopes across matched low, intermediate, and high demands within each participant. Across participants, DBS did not significantly change the demand dependence of final gain, first gain, or latency. For convergence, the mean slope change was +0.021 gain units/degree for final gain (paired p = 0.32), +0.020 gain units/degree for first gain (paired p = 0.32), and -34 ms/degree for latency (paired p = 0.44). For divergence, corresponding changes were +0.012 gain units/degree (paired p = 0.41), +0.016 gain units/degree (paired p = 0.21), and +2 ms/degree (paired p = 0.95). The available data therefore did not establish that DBS altered the demand dependence of gain or latency.

Group averages concealed heterogeneous individual responses

The small average on-off changes in gain, final accuracy, and strategy reflected cancellation among markedly different individual responses. In several participants, changes in gain magnitude, endpoint accuracy, timing, early accuracy, and movement strategy moved independently, emphasizing that these dimensions should not be conflated. For example, PD19 showed one of the clearest beneficial responses: final gain increased by 0.35 during convergence and 0.80 during divergence, while absolute final-gain error decreased by 0.85 and 0.50, respectively, and latency shortened in both directions. In contrast, PD29 and PD31 became substantially more accurate in both directions despite decreases in convergence gain (-0.73 and -0.65, respectively), consistent with correction of excessive off-state responses. At the opposite extreme, PD18 showed large increases in final gain (+1.31 during convergence and +1.05 during divergence) but became less accurate, with absolute final-gain error increasing by 0.58 and 0.84. Thus, increased gain could accompany either improvement or deterioration in endpoint performance, depending on the participant's baseline state.

The initial-angular-error analysis reinforced this point. DBS-related increases in first movement magnitude did not necessarily imply better early accuracy; rather, the change was beneficial only when it moved the participant closer to the requested amplitude. The largest convergence improvements in initial angular error were seen in PD17 (-3.65°), PD31 (-2.71°), PD12 (-2.57°), PD47 (-2.18°), and PD41 (-2.14°). For divergence, the largest improvements were observed in PD30 (-4.38°), PD31 (-2.78°), PD19 (-2.44°), PD12 (-1.54°), and PD13 (-1.16°). However, some participants worsened despite the overall convergence effect, including PD42 for convergence (+1.21°), PD22 (+0.78°), and PD24 (+0.29°), and PD47 for divergence (+1.38°), PD10 (+0.94°), and PD11 (+0.76°).

Other dissociations were also evident. PD10 and PD28 showed marked shortening of convergence latency (approximately 580 ms and 490 ms, respectively), yet divergence endpoint accuracy worsened by 0.32 and 0.44, indicating that faster initiation and better endpoint accuracy were separable. PD12 and PD30 showed large increases in first divergence gain (+0.64 and +0.75) with much smaller and opposite-signed changes in final divergence accuracy (+0.16 and -0.10), suggesting that DBS altered the initial motor response more than the eventual endpoint. Meanwhile, PD17, PD32, and PD41 showed pronounced shifts toward more negative holding in both directions, particularly during convergence, accompanied by some improvement in final accuracy.

Given the marked individual heterogeneity, we asked whether participants could be partitioned into response subgroups. Hierarchical clustering was performed in all participants with complete profiles using eight standardized features: on-off changes in final-gain error, first gain, latency, and holding, each measured for convergence and divergence.

A small subgroup consisting of PD29 and PD31 emerged consistently. In the best-performing twocluster solution, these two participants separated from the remaining with a silhouette value of 0.33, reflecting modest but detectable structure. Both showed large reductions in final-gain error in both directions together with lower first convergence gain, suggesting a similar pattern of DBS-related correction of excessive OFF-state responses. A three-cluster solution additionally identified a possible gaze holding-shift group (PD17, PD24, PD32, PD41, PD48), but separation was weak (silhouette 0.15), and a four-cluster solution mainly isolated PD10 because of marked divergencelatency change (silhouette 0.18). Cluster assignments remained reasonably stable when one participant was removed, but were only moderately robust to omission of individual features (median adjusted Rand index 0.55).

Computational modeling of pathway activation, volume of tissue activated, and associations with vergence behavior

The heterogeneous effects of STN DBS on vergence may reflect differential engagement of neural circuits governing distinct aspects of binocular motor control. Variability in stimulation location, volume of tissue activated, and recruitment of specific white matter pathways may selectively influence vergence initiation, execution, accuracy, gaze holding, and saccade-assisted strategies. This anatomical variability provides an opportunity to investigate the circuit-level mechanisms underlying these behaviors. Accordingly, we examined associations between modeled activation of STN subregions, peri-subthalamic territories, and white matter pathways and DBS-induced changes in vergence function (Fig 2A,B). Across analyses, each behavioral outcome was defined as the withinparticipant on-minus-off change. Negative values indicated improvement for latency and absoluteerror measures. The top exploratory anatomy-behavior associations are summarized in Table 5, and the multivariable modeling results in Table 6.

Broad anatomical screen identifies candidate associations for convergence latency and divergence strategy

Across the full anatomical screen, the strongest association was observed between greater right associative STN coverage and larger DBS-on shortening of convergence latency (Spearman ρ = -0.70, permutation p = 0.001, n = 19; Table 5). Leave-one-out analyses showed that the correlation with convergence-latency change remained consistently strong (ρ range, -0.78 to -0.65), and the relationship persisted after accounting for total VTA volume (partial rank ρ = -0.62). Greater right associative STN coverage was also associated with both larger right-sided stimulation volumes (ρ = +0.67) and longer OFF-state convergence latency (ρ = +0.58). When both right VTA volume and OFF-state latency were accounted for, the correlation weakened to ρ = -0.43, suggesting that part of the observed effect may reflect greater room for improvement in participants who were slower at baseline. This association was notably weaker for divergence latency, and a direct comparison using each participant's convergence-change minus divergence-change score also yielded only a weak relationship (Table 5).

Although group-level strategy frequency changed little (Table 3), individual divergence strategy changes were associated with modeled left-sided stimulation anatomy (Table 5). Greater overlap with the left Field of Forel (ρ = -0.75, unadjusted p = 0.00036) and left ventro-oralis anterior (ρ = -0.72, unadjusted p = 0.00077) accompanied larger reductions in combined saccade-plus-vergence responses.

Associations with endpoint error

Greater stimulation overlap with the right ventro-oralis posterior was associated with larger convergence endpoint errors with DBS ON, both in degrees (ρ = 0.61, p = 0.0073) and relative to target demand (absolute final-gain error, ρ = 0.59, p = 0.0096). Greater overlap with the right nucleus reticulatus polaris showed a similar pattern for divergence (angular error, ρ = 0.58, p = 0.012, and absolute final-gain error, ρ = 0.53, p = 0.021) (Table 5). Associations with initial angular error were weaker and did not reach nominal significance. Total VTA volume showed weaker associations than regional overlap, with its largest correlations observed for the two divergence endpoint error measures. None of the accuracy associations survived correction for multiple comparisons (minimum q = 0.647).

First vergence gain showed weak and exploratory anatomical associations

Greater left Field of Forel overlap showed the strongest association with a DBS-related increase in signed first gain for divergence (ρ = +0.55, p = 0.015, n = 19; Table 5). The correlation was stable in leave-one-out analyses (ρ = +0.51 to +0.63) but did not survive correction for multiple comparisons (q = 0.93). Neither modeled fiber activation nor total stimulation volume showed a clear association with first gain in either direction.

Greater overlap was only weakly associated with a reduction in absolute initial error (|first gain -1|, ρ = -0.22, p = 0.372) and correlated with lower off-state first divergence gain (ρ = -0.55). This exploratory finding therefore points to a larger initial divergence response with DBS, potentially dependent on baseline gain, with little evidence of improved initial accuracy.

Multivariable prediction was strongest for divergence strategy

We used elastic-net models to test whether combinations of anatomical features improved prediction beyond individual associations. The 42 candidate predictors included hemisphere-specific fiber activation, regional stimulation coverage, and left and right VTA. In nested cross-validation, we predicted each participant's DBS on-off change from models trained on the remaining participants. The mean change in the training set served as the benchmark (Table 6).

Prediction improved most for divergence strategy, particularly the proportion of trials combining saccades and vergence. Greater left Field of Forel coverage was a leading correlate of reduced use of this strategy. Anatomical models reduced squared prediction error by 26.7-35.0% relative to the benchmark, although the advantage diminished substantially after influential participants were excluded. Under the original strategy classification, greater right sensorimotor STN coverage predicted more frequent use of the vergence-plus-saccade category, whereas greater left associative STN coverage, left fasciculus subthalamicus activation, and right internal capsule coverage each predicted less frequent use. All four predictors were selected in 18 of 19 training sets, with modest improvement in prediction for held-out participants.

In exploratory regression, each 1-SD increase in left VTA volume was associated with an 11.1percentage-point increase in wrong-directed divergence responses (95% CI, +1.5 to +20.6), although statistical evidence for the bilateral VTA model as a whole was limited (p = 0.061). Adding left fasciculus subthalamicus and left cerebellothalamic pathway activation to bilateral VTA volumes improved prediction of changes in combined saccade-plus-vergence responses. Root mean squared error (RMSE) was 15.3 percentage points with the pathways included, compared with 25.3 for volumes alone and 22.2 for the training-mean benchmark. This advantage persisted when influential participants were excluded one at a time, suggesting that pathway engagement added predictive information beyond stimulation volume within this cohort. Because strategy proportions sum to 100%, a decrease in one category corresponds to an increase in one or more others.

Elastic-net models did not meaningfully improve prediction of latency, gain, accuracy, or gaze holding. Despite the strong univariable association with right associative STN coverage, convergence latency RMSE was 465 ms versus 463 ms for the benchmark. For divergence latency, squared prediction error increased by 13.0%. Squared error for final divergence gain decreased by only 0.15%, while prediction of absolute final-gain error worsened in both directions. For gaze holding, squared error increased by 33.4% for convergence and 3.8% for divergence.

Elastic net improved none of the six accuracy outcomes: initial angular error, endpoint angular error, and absolute final-gain error for each direction. In separate regressions accounting for DBS off-state error, adding bilateral VTA volumes yielded only a small improvement for divergence absolute finalgain error (RMSE, 0.401 to 0.394). Adding left fasciculus subthalamicus and left cerebellothalamic pathway activation increased held-out prediction error for all six accuracy outcomes compared with using OFF-state error alone (Table 6).

Anatomical models explained effects on gaze-holding

The behavioral analyses showed a clear average DBS ON-OFF effect on gaze holding in convergence or divergence position. Gaze holding variance decreased with DBS ON in 12 out of 19 participants (Group 1) and remained unchanged or increased in seven (Group 2). Group assignment was based on the ON-minus-OFF change in holding variance, with convergence and divergence weighted equally within each participant. For each anatomical measure, we averaged the left and right percentages. The cumulative score summed the bilateral averages for Field of Forel coverage, cerebellothalamic pathway activation. The specific rationale for choosing these pathways was that its coverage with VTA would retrogradely modulate the cerebellar outflow, influencing the fidelity of vergence neural integrator, affecting gaze holding in convergence or divergence state.

The median cumulative stimulation score was 13.48 in Group 1 and 9.31 in Group 2 (rank-biserial effect size = 0.48, exact permutation p = 0.100, adjusted q = 0.201). Median exposure was numerically higher in Group 1 for all three components. Bilateral cerebellothalamic pathway activation showed the largest rank separation between groups (rank-biserial effect size = 0.49, p = 0.071, q = 0.201). All 19 participants had some modeled Field of Forel coverage on at least one side. Across participants, the correlation between the cumulative score and the magnitude of change in holding variance was weak (Spearman ρ = -0.23, p = 0.351). Thus, participants with improved holding had higher median cumulative exposure, but the group difference was statistically inconclusive.

Stimulation anatomy distinguishes exploratory patterns of gaze holding and vergence initiation

To examine whether stimulation anatomy distinguished patterns of gaze stability, we grouped ON-OFF changes in gaze-holding variance together with cumulative exposure of field of Forel and cerebellothalamic projections. Convergence and divergence observations were included together, with separate analyses for each hemisphere (Fig 3A,B; Table 7). The higher right-sided exposure cluster showed reduced gaze-holding variance, whereas the lower-exposure cluster showed little average change. Mean cumulative exposures were 29.67 and 4.38, with corresponding variance changes of -0.00513 and +0.00074 deg². The between-cluster difference was -0.00586 deg² (95% CI, -0.00870 to -0.00302; nominal p = .00040; BH-adjusted q = .00079). A similar pattern occurred for left-sided stimulation. Mean cumulative exposures were 86.74 and 13.51, with variance changes of -0.00462 and -0.00141 deg², respectively (difference, -0.00321 deg²; 95% CI, -0.00577 to -0.00065; nominal p = .017; q = .017). Thus, greater cumulative exposure characterized clusters with steadier post-vergence gaze holding.

We next examined whether cumulative exposure and associative STN coverage distinguished changes in movement initiation (Fig 3C-F; Table 7). The higher left cumulative-exposure cluster showed a mean latency reduction of 387 ms, compared with a 2-ms increase in the lower-exposure cluster (difference, -389 ms; 95% CI, -597 to -181; nominal p = 0.00098; q = 0.0020). The corresponding right-sided comparison showed no clear latency difference (-50 ms; 95% CI, -258 to +158; p = 0.617). Greater right associative STN coverage characterized a cluster with shorter latency during DBS on: latency decreased by 143 ms in the higher-coverage cluster and increased by 40 ms in the lower-coverage cluster (difference, -183 ms; 95% CI, -307 to -60; nominal p = 0.0060; q = 0.0072). Left associative STN comparisons were not estimated because the higher-coverage cluster contained only one participant. Comparisons accounted for repeated observations within participants. Because clustering used the same outcomes subsequently compared, and exclusions depended on response values, these statistics describe the selected clusters and do not independently confirm stimulation-behavior associations.

Associations of stimulation anatomy with clinically measured motor and ophthalmological responses

Of the 24 participants in the paired DBS vergence cohort, 19 had imaging and paired motor scores, 14 had paired RAF ruler measurements, and 17 had paired distance-to-pen measurements. Clinical changes were calculated as DBS on-minus-off. Negative changes indicate lower motor scores or shorter convergence distances with stimulation. Associations with modeled fiber activation, regional overlap of the VTA, and overall VTA volume were evaluated using Spearman rank correlations and two-sided permutation tests.

Greater right cerebellothalamic activation was associated with a larger reduction in motor score with DBS on (ρ = -0.52, unadjusted p = 0.025). The right reticular tract measure yielded the same rank correlation. Greater left ventro-oralis posterior overlap was associated with a smaller reduction or an increase in motor score (ρ = +0.56, p = 0.014). After adjustment for off-state motor score and total VTA volume, the partial rank correlations were -0.35 for right cerebellothalamic pathway activation and +0.49 for left ventro-oralis posterior overlap. Overall VTA volumes showed little association with motor score change (|ρ| ≤ 0.11, all p ≥ 0.662).

Greater right frontal corticopontine tract activation was associated with a longer RAF ruler distance (ρ = +0.55, unadjusted p = .045). After adjustment for OFF-state RAF ruler distance and total VTA volume, the partial rank correlations were +0.62 and +0.43, respectively. Overall VTA volumes showed weak associations with RAF ruler distance change (|ρ| ≤ 0.19, all p ≥ .501).

For distance to pen, greater overlap with the right substantia nigra region, defined by the combined pars compacta/pars reticulata atlas label, accompanied a larger shortening with DBS ON (ρ = -0.66, unadjusted p = 0.0050). Greater activation of the left corticospinal tract (ρ = -0.55, p = .025) and left cerebellothalamic pathway (ρ = -0.53, p = 0.033) also accompanied shorter distances. After adjustment for OFF-state distance to pen and total VTA volume, the corresponding partial rank correlations were -0.67, -0.60, and -0.57. Among the overall volume measures, left VTA volume showed the largest correlation with distance to pen (ρ = -0.43, p = 0.090).

Discussion

Changing viewing depth requires the brain to establish a new binocular alignment and sustain it after the movement ends. Our findings suggest that STN-region DBS can affect these components differently. Its clearest effect was on post-vergence gaze stability: eye-position variance decreased by approximately one-third across convergence and divergence. The initial convergence response also landed closer to the target, whereas average changes in signed gain, final accuracy, latency, and movement strategy were less consistent. This pattern suggests that a subcortical perturbation can improve the maintenance of binocular alignment with relatively little average change in how the eyes attain it. Exploratory anatomical analyses identified candidate contributions from associative STN territories and the fields of Forel with adjacent cerebellothalamic projections. These observations raise the possibility that stimulation influences movement preparation and saccade-vergence coordination through basal ganglia circuits, while modulation of cerebellar signals reaching midbrain vergence circuitry helps stabilize the attained eye position. The reduction in post-vergence variability provides the strongest behavioral basis for examining this proposed distinction.

Maintaining alignment after a depth shift

STN-region DBS reduced vergence holding variance after both convergence and divergence, with little evidence of a change in final gain. This finding is consistent with improved fidelity of vergence integration. Once the eyes reached a new vergence angle, they fluctuated less around it, even though the average achieved angle changed little. Participants whose gaze holding improved had greater cumulative modeled coverage of the fields of Forel and cerebellothalamic fibers than those whose gaze holding was unchanged. Together, these observations support the hypothesis that retrograde modulation of cerebellar outflow by engaging field of Forel and cerebellothalamic projections would improve the fidelity of vergence integration, expressed behaviorally as a steadier gaze holding after the vergence movement ends.

One possibility is that stimulation of cerebellothalamic axons alters the cerebellar signals that tune midbrain vergence circuitry. Field of Forel and cerebellothalamic track modulation can antidromically modulate deep cerebellar nuclei (Tolbert et al., 1978), and modulation of the fastigial and posterior interposed outputs reach the SOA and adjacent central mesencephalic reticular formation (Bohlen et al., 2021). Although the axons recruited by DBS in our participants belonged to neurons that also influence these midbrain regions remains a speculation, the combined coverage measure identifies a region in which engagement of neighboring or overlapping pathways are associated with better gaze holding. The variance in gaze holding also reflects both the stability of the tonic position command and the effectiveness of visual disparity feedback (Quinet et al., 2025 , Semmlow et al., 1993 , Semmlow et al., 2019).

Initiating a depth shift

The association between greater right associative STN coverage and shorter convergence latency is compatible with modulation of movement preparation or release. Prefrontal inputs to the associative STN provide an anatomical basis for such an effect (Haynes and Haber, 2013). A candidate downstream route runs through the substantia nigra pars reticulata and superior colliculus to midbrain vergence circuitry. Primate experiments link nigral output to the release of saccades, and the rostral superior colliculus carries slow vergence signals and projects to the supraoculomotor area (Hikosaka and Wurtz, 1985 , May et al., 2021 , Van Horn et al., 2013). Stimulation might shorten the time required to disengage fixation, select a response to disparity, or release its first detectable motor component. Latency alone leaves these possibilities open.

Coordinating saccades and vergence

Combined saccade-plus-vergence responses predominated in both stimulation states. Their persistence is consistent with the physiology of three-dimensional gaze shifts, in which saccades transiently facilitate vergence and influence how rapidly the eyes reduce disparity (Busettini and Mays, 2005). The strongest anatomical signal for those individual differences was reduced use of combined responses during divergence with greater left Field of Forel overlap. Left ventro-oralis anterior overlap and left fasciculus subthalamicus recruitment showed associations in the same direction. The proximity of pallidothalamic and cerebellothalamic fibers in this region makes Field of Forel overlap a marker of exposure to the border region (Gallay et al., 2008). It leaves open contributions from pallidothalamic output, pallidal-subthalamic interactions, and adjacent cerebellothalamic fibers.

The modeled stimulation overlap with the campi Foreli, pallidothalamic projections, and cerebellothalamic projections suggest a complementary route to modulate saccade vergence collaboration to achieve gaze shift in depth. Within this anatomy, pallidal output could bias the anticipated movement strategy, including whether a smooth response is likely to suffice and when a saccade should be released if it is recruited. Cerebellar output could calibrate the selected response, including how much of the remaining vergence error the saccade should remove and how the eyes settle afterward (Busettini and Mays, 2005 , Ilinsky et al., 2018 , Sakai et al., 1996 , Takeichi et al., 2005). Cerebellar output, caudal fastigial and posterior interposed nuclei project to distinct parts of the midbrain cMRF (Bohlen et al., 2021 , May et al., 1990 , May et al., 1992); the region relevant to saccades. Saccade-vergence burst neurons in this region discharge in relation to vergence velocity; the rapid vergence contribution depends on the interaction of a saccadic burst with the vergence error remaining when the saccade begins (Busettini and Mays, 2005, Quinet et al., 2020). In this way STN DBS, via recruiting and antidromically modulating cerebellothalamic projection can modulate when the saccadic burst arrives relative to the evolving vergence error, how strongly the burst contributes to the change in binocular alignment (Gallay et al., 2008 , McIntyre et al., 2004).

Implications for sensorimotor control

Binocular near viewing depends on how the brain uses visual cues to align and focus the eyes and then keep them steady. Our findings suggest that STN DBS may influence different parts of this process. Video-oculography showed steadier gaze after a depth shift, with greater combined stimulation coverage of the field of Forel and adjacent cerebellothalamic pathways among participants who improved. The bedside tests probed how well binocular alignment or single vision was maintained as disparity, blur, and proximity cues changed together. Greater left cerebellothalamic activation was associated with a closer pen-tip breakpoint, whereas greater right frontal corticopontine activation and internal capsule overlap were associated with a more distant RAF ruler breakpoint. These exploratory findings raise the possibility that cerebellar outflow helps stabilize an attained vergence position, while frontal signals relayed through the pons and cerebellum may shape how visual cues drive the midbrain near response (Bohlen et al., 2021 , Gallay et al., 2008 , Gamlin and Clarke, 1995 , Gamlin and Yoon, 2000 , Horwood and Riddell, 2009 , Zhang and Gamlin, 1998). The bedside associations did not survive correction for multiple comparisons. A prospective within-participant study pairing targeted stimulation with eye tracking, bedside testing, and reading measures could determine whether steadier gaze also helps patients with Parkinson's disease maintain single vision as viewing demands change. exposure.
