Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module retrieves information from repositories.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) and borderline personality disorder (BPD) frequently co-occur, but their shared genetic susceptibility and directional associations remain unclear.

We analyzed European-ancestry genome-wide association study (GWAS) data for ADHD (38,691 cases; 186,843 controls) and BPD (12,339 cases; 1,041,717 controls). Linkage disequilibrium score regression, conjunctional false discovery rate (conjFDR), bidirectional Mendelian randomization (MR), MR accounting for linkage disequilibrium and sample overlap (MR-LAP), Causal Analysis Using Summary Effect estimates (CAUSE), colocalization, and Sum of Single Effects (SuSiE) fine-mapping assessed genetic sharing and directionality.

Results Genetic correlation was substantial (r_g = 0.733). ConjFDR identified 48 shared loci; 36 (75%) showed concordant directions in both phenotype-specific comparisons. Functional annotation implicated axon guidance, oxidative phosphorylation, and developing midbrain cells. ADHD-to-BPD MR was positive (22 instruments; inverse-variance weighted beta = 0.487, SE = 0.082, p = 3.27 × 10^-9), persisting after outlier removal, MR-LAP correction, and exclusion of two instruments at colocalized loci. Reverse MR was positive (five instruments; beta = 0.303, SE = 0.112, p = 6.83 × 10^-3) but heterogeneous and variant-sensitive. CAUSE favored directional over sharing-only models in both directions (delta ELPD = 6.98 and 5.92, respectively). SuSiE colocalization supported shared signals at rs17576773, rs9969232, and rs4727799.

Conclusions ADHD and BPD exhibit extensive genetic overlap involving 48 shared loci and convergent evidence of directional associations. Findings across complementary methods more consistently support an ADHD-to-BPD relationship, suggesting that shared genetic susceptibility alone may not fully explain their co-occurrence.

INTRODUCTION

Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) and borderline personality disorder (BPD) are distinct psychiatric disorders that frequently co-occur, causing substantial functional and psychosocial impairments. ADHD and BPD affect approximately 1.6-5% and 2.4% of the general population, respectively (Popit et al., 2024; Ramos-Suárez et al., 2026). ADHD is a neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by persistent inattention and/or hyperactivityimpulsivity (Ditrich et al., 2021). Conversely, BPD is characterized by pervasive instability in relationships, self-image, and affect, alongside marked impulsivity (Ditrich et al., 2021). Despite distinct classifications, they share clinically relevant features, particularly impulsivity and emotion dysregulation, complicating differential diagnosis in adulthood (Weiner et al., 2019).

Beyond phenotypic overlap, ADHD and BPD may share vulnerability factors. Genetic predisposition and environmental exposure, such as childhood adversity, have been implicated in both disorders (Distel et al., 2011). Longitudinal studies have shown a higher occurrence of BPD among individuals with childhood ADHD, suggesting that persistent impulsivity and emotion dysregulation contribute to later BPD vulnerability (Tiger et al., 2022). However, clinical overlap makes it difficult to determine whether this association reflects directional developmental pathways or shared liability. Observational studies cannot fully distinguish these possibilities due to potential confounding by unmeasured familial, environmental, and developmental factors.

Genetic methodologies offer a framework for examining the relationship between these disorders.

Both conditions exhibit heritability, and recent genome-wide association studies (GWAS)

demonstrated significant genetic overlap between ADHD and BPD, indicating that their cooccurrence may be partially attributed to shared genetic susceptibility (Streit et al., 2026).

Nevertheless, genetic correlation alone cannot differentiate between shared polygenic architecture, locus-specific overlap, or directional relationships. Conjunctional false discovery rate (conjFDR) analysis identifies loci that are jointly associated by utilizing polygenic overlap (Smeland et al., 2020). In contrast, Mendelian randomization (MR) assesses directional associations, while colocalization analysis determines whether regional associations share a common genetic signal (Burgess et al., 2023). Accordingly, this study aimed to characterize the genetic relationship between ADHD and BPD by examining shared loci and evaluating whether liability to either disorder is directionally associated with the risk of the other. We applied conjFDR to identify jointly associated loci, then conducted bidirectional MR using GWAS summary statistics with sensitivity analyses and Steiger testing. Finally, we integrated locus-specific colocalization and SuSiE fine-mapping to assess shared genetic signals and tested whether MR associations persisted after excluding instruments within these regions.

METHODS

Data source and Selection of genetic instruments

This study adhered to the recommendations for MR investigations as outlined by Burgess et al. (2023) and was based exclusively on publicly-available, de-identified datasets. Ethical approval and informed consent were obtained from the original studies in accordance with their respective institutional requirements. We used summary statistics from the largest GWAS meta-analyses of ADHD and a recent BPD GWAS meta-analysis, both available through the Psychiatric Genomics Consortium. The ADHD statistics provided by Demontis et al. (2023) included 38,691 cases and 186,843 controls of European descent (Supplementary Table 1). The BPD statistics, sourced from Streit et al. (2026), comprised 12,339 cases and 1,041,717 controls of European ancestry (Supplementary Table 2).

Statistical Analysis

We applied linkage disequilibrium score regression (LDSC) to estimate the genome-wide genetic correlation between ADHD and BPD. SNP heritability estimates were converted to liability scale assuming population prevalences of 5% for ADHD and 1.5% for BPD, accounting for effective sample sizes as a balanced case-control sample (effective sample prevalence = 0.5). To assess polygenic enrichment between phenotype pairs, we examined conditional quantile-quantile plots of the p-value distribution for one phenotype given p-value thresholds for another (p < 0.1, p < 0.01, and p < 0.001). To limit inflation, the major histocompatibility complex region was excluded. Shared loci were identified using conjFDR analysis, considering SNPs with conjFDR < 0.05 as shared (Smeland et al., 2020). To evaluate shared-loci consistency, we performed sign-concordance analyses (Karadag et al., 2023) using FinnGen Release 13 (R13) F5_ADHD and F5_EMOPER statistics (Kurki et al., 2023).

Inspection of the BPD discovery GWAS cohort revealed sample overlap from inclusion of FinnGen

Release 8 (R8) F5_EMOPER data. To address this, and because no sufficiently independent BPD GWAS datasets were available, we used MetaSubtract (Nolte et al., 2020 using two-sided binomial tests. Variants that were unavailable, could not be reliably harmonized, or showed discordant effects in either comparison were not considered jointly replicated.

To elucidate the biological mechanisms contributing to the shared genetic architecture of ADHD and BPD, conjFDR-loci were functionally characterized and mapped to putative genes using Functional Mapping and Annotation of Genome-Wide Association Studies (FUMA; Watanabe et al., 2017). These genes were then examined through functional enrichment analyses of Gene Ontology terms and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes pathways, as well as analyses of tissue-and cell type-specific expression.

Directional relationships between the two traits were explored using a MR framework. In MR statistics, genetic instruments were selected from the corresponding GWAS at genome-wide significance (p < 5 × 10^-8). Variants were clumped using the European subset of the 1000 Genomes Project Phase 3 as the LD reference, with r^2 < 0.001 within a 10-Mb window. Only variants with adequate strength (F > 10) were retained.

Primary MR analysis used inverse-variance weighted multiplicative random-effects models (IVW) to account for between-instrument heterogeneity, confirmed in fixed-effects analyses (Cochran's Q p < 0.05 in both directions). We also used the weighted-median estimator, which can provide consistent estimates if up to 50% of the instrument weight comes from invalid instruments (Bowden et al., 2016). Heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran's Q and I^2 statistics (Burgess et al., 2023).

We performed leave-one-out analyses (LOO), MR-Egger, MR pleiotropy residual sum and outlier (MR-PRESSO; Verbanck et al., 2018), and radial-MR. Steiger directionality testing across multiple plausible prevalence scenarios for ADHD and BPD assessed whether instruments explained more phenotypic variance in the designated exposure than outcome (Burgess et al., 2023). MR-LAP addressed potential bias from sample overlap, weak instruments, and winner's curse (Mounier & Kutalik, 2023). Furthermore, Causal Analysis Using Summary Effect estimates (CAUSE; Morrison et al., 2020) was performed to distinguish causal effects from correlated horizontal pleiotropy.

We also investigated whether MR findings reflected local genetic architecture using Bayesian colocalization with the coloc framework (Giambartolomei et al., 2014). All instruments were tested bidirectionally across genomic regions (+/-500 kb) centered on lead SNPs using effect estimates, variances, allele frequencies, and sample-size information from GWAS summary statistics.

To assess the statistical sensitivity of the bidirectional MR analyses and contextualize differences in instrument strength and availability between the two directions, we calculated the minimum detectable effects (MDEs) corresponding to 80% power at a two-sided significance threshold of alpha = 0.05. Using SNP-level associations, fixed-effects inverse-variance weighted (IVW) standard errors were calculated and adjusted for heterogeneity under a multiplicative randomeffects model using max(1, Q/[k-1]), where Q is Cochran's heterogeneity statistic and k the number of instruments. Log-odds MDEs used (z_0.975 + z_0.80) x SE, were exponentiated and interpreted as conditional detection thresholds. Genome-wide significance was defined as p < 5 × 10^-8 for instrument selection. For MR analyses, statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p < 0.05. The MR-PRESSO global test and Steiger directionality test were evaluated at p < 0.05. For colocalization analyses, posterior probabilities rather than p-value thresholds were used, with PP.H4 > 0.80 considered strong evidence of colocalization. A detailed description of the statistical pipeline and software packages used is provided in the supplementary methods section.

RESULTS

Genome-wide genetic correlation between ADHD and BPD

Cross-trait genetic enrichment

Conditional Q-Q plots revealed bidirectional cross-trait enrichment between ADHD and BPD. In both directions, more stringent thresholds for the conditioning phenotype corresponded to leftward deviations from the null expectation, indicating shared associations (Supplementary Figure S1 panels A and C). Conditional fold-enrichment analyses showed increasing enrichment across conditioning thresholds, corroborating enrichment (Supplementary Figure S1 panels B and D).

ConjFDR analysis evidenced 48 shared loci (Figure 1). To assess consistency, we evaluated 48 SNPs using estimates from the ADHD discovery GWAS, the BPD GWAS following the MetaSubtract procedure, and FinnGen R13 F5_ADHD. Of the 48 loci, 37 (77.1%) showed concordant directions between the ADHD discovery GWAS and FinnGen R13 F5_ADHD (twosided exact binomial p = 2.22 × 10^-4); 40 of 48 loci (83.3%) showed concordant directions between the FinnGen R8-depleted Streit BPD GWAS and FinnGen R13 F5_EMOPER (two-sided exact binomial p = 3.31 × 10^-6). Concordance in both phenotype-specific comparisons was observed for 36 of the 48 lead variants (75.0%), representing the intersection of the two concordant sets.

Functional Annotation

Functional mapping of the 48 shared ADHD-BPD loci (Supplementary Data Sheets 1-7) identified 629 mapped genes, corresponding to 430 unique Entrez genes after deduplication. 131 candidate SNPs had CADD scores exceeding 12.37, indicating predicted deleteriousness. Among 198 significantly enriched gene sets (adjusted p < 5.0 × 10^-2), four KEGG/Hallmark pathways were identified. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) Axon guidance showed the strongest statistical evidence of enrichment among these pathways, with 10 overlapping genes among 127 pathway genes (4.42-fold enrichment; p = 9.47 × 10^-5; adjusted p = 1.76 × 10^-2). KEGG Glycosaminoglycan degradation included 4 of 19 genes and showed the largest relative enrichment (11.82-fold; p = 3.11 × 10^-4; adjusted p = 2.89 × 10^-2), although based on a fourgene overlap. KEGG Huntington's disease included 10 of 164 genes, and Hallmark Oxidative Phosphorylation included 11 of 195 genes. Genes contributing to Axon guidance and Huntington's disease enrichments were distributed across seven loci, whereas Glycosaminoglycan degradation mapped to a single region, indicating that this signal was locus-driven rather than supported by convergence across independent loci. Hallmark Oxidative Phosphorylation genes were distributed across eight loci, with two mapping to locus 9 (Figure 1). Cell-type signature analysis identified 2.91-fold enrichment for the MANNO_MIDBRAIN_NEUROTYPES_HPROGFPL signature (16 of 309 genes; adjusted p = 2.48 × 10^-2), with overlapping genes distributed across 11 loci (Figure 1).

Bidirectional Mendelian Randomization

Using the full BPD GWAS dataset, 1,428 ADHD variants reached genome-wide significance (p < 5 × 10^-8). After LD clumping, 26 independent variants were retained as strong candidate genetic instruments (F-statistics: 29.85-60.13; mean, 38.89; median, 34.85; Supplementary table 5). Of the 26 instruments, 22 were directly available and could be harmonized with the BPD GWAS (Supplementary table 6). Multiplicative random-effects IVW analysis showed a positive association between genetic liability to ADHD and BPD (figure 2, panel A; p = 3.3 × 10^-9). Heterogeneity existed among SNP-specific estimates (Q = 51.09; df = 21; p = 2.57 × 10^-4; I^2 = 58.9%), but 17 of 22 Wald ratio estimates were directionally concordant (Supplementary Figure 2). MR-Egger regression yielded a positive but imprecise slope (Figure 2, panel A; p = 0.151). The MR-Egger intercept did not provide statistically significant evidence of directional horizontal pleiotropy (intercept = -0.0009, SE = 0.0219, p = 9.67 × 10^-1; Supplementary Figure 3). LOO indicated that the ADHD-to-BPD association was not driven by any single genetic instrument (Supplementary Radial MR (Supplementary Figure 6) yielded consistent estimates (t = 5.916; p = 7.15 × 10^-6; Figure 2, panel A). After excluding three outliers (rs9969232, rs2886697, and rs10875612), the association remained significant across 19 instruments (t = 7.320; p = 8.48 × 10^-7; Figure 2, panel A) and residual heterogeneity became non-significant (Q = 22.73, df = 18, p = 2.01 × 10^-1, I^2 = 20.81%). Consistent with the Radial MR findings, MR-PRESSO detected global pleiotropy (Global Test RSSobs = 56.32; p = 1.4 × 10^-3) and identified one outlier (rs9969232). After outlier correction, the association remained significant (t = 6.008; p = 7.11 × 10^-6; Figure 2, panel A), with no significant difference between raw and corrected estimates (distortion test p = 3.94 × 10^-1).

Reversely, five of the six identified BPD instruments were analyzed (Supplementary Tables 7 and 8). Genetic liability to BPD (Figure 2, panel B) was positively associated with ADHD using multiplicative random-effects IVW (Z = 2.705; p = 6.83 × 10^-3) and weighted median estimators (Z = 3.101; p = 1.93 × 10^-3). Substantial between-instrument heterogeneity was observed (Q = 20.24, df = 4, p = 4.47 × 10^-4; I^2 = 80.2%). The MR-Egger intercept did not differ significantly from zero (intercept = -0.251, SE = 0.151; t = -1.662, df = 3; p = 1.95 × 10^-1; Supplementary Figure 8). However, the MR-Egger slope was highly imprecise (t = 1.831, df = 3; p = 1.64 × 10^-1; Figure 2, panel B), and its interpretation was limited by the small number of instruments. However, also in BPD->ADHD direction, LOO indicated that the association was not driven by any single genetic instrument (Supplementary figure 10).

MR-PRESSO identified rs4727799 as an outlier (global test p = 1 × 10^-2); its exclusion attenuated the association, which remained positive (Figure 2, panel B; p = 4.95 × 10^-2) with significant distortion compared with the uncorrected estimate (p < 1 × 10^-4). Radial MR using modified second-order weights showed a non-significant positive association between genetic liability to BPD and ADHD across five instruments (t = 2.704, df = 4; p = 5.39 × 10^-2; Figure 2, panel B), with significant heterogeneity (Q = 16.68, df = 4, p = 2.23 × 10^-3; I^2 = 76.0%). After excluding two outliers (rs4727799, rs6549383), the association was significant across three retained instruments (t = 6.735, df = 2; p = 2.13 × 10^-2; Supplementary Figure 11), with non-significant residual heterogeneity (Q = 0.57, df = 2, p = 7.54 × 10^-1; I^2 = 0%). These findings support a reverse-direction association, though limited by few instruments and post-hoc outlier exclusion.

To evaluate sample overlap and winner's curse, we applied MR-LAP. For ADHD->BPD (26 instruments; Supplementary Table 9), the observed IVW showed a positive association (Figure 2 panel C, p = 3.50 × 10^-11). After bias correction, the association remained significant (Figure 2 panel D, p = 8.37 × 10^-11), with a significant difference between estimates (p = 1.36 × 10^-9). The MR-Egger intercept showed no directional pleiotropy (p = 0.133). For BPD->ADHD (5 instruments; Supplementary Table 10), both observed (p = 7.0 × 10^-3) and corrected estimates (p = 8.0 × 10^-3) were significant. The MR-Egger intercept was not significant (p = 3.7 × 10^-1), although its interpretability was limited by the small number of instruments. Comparison with conventional MR instruments demonstrated substantial concordance in both directions.

Across 20 combinations of assumed BPD and ADHD population prevalences, Steiger analyses continued to support the intended orientation of the instruments in both directions (Supplementary Tables 11 and 12).

CAUSE analysis

To distinguish directional causal effects from correlated horizontal pleiotropy, we applied CAUSE.

After harmonizing ADHD and BPD GWAS datasets, 4,729,831 variants were available. Exposureassociated variants (P < 1 × 10^-3) were LD-pruned at r^2 < 0.1 using the CAUSE LD reference panel, yielding 2,135 variants for ADHD-to-BPD and 1,619 for BPD-to-ADHD. The correlation between estimation errors was rho = 0.181 and was incorporated into both models.

In the ADHD-to-BPD direction, both the sharing model (delta ELPD [sharing -null] = 52.49, SE = 8.60) and the causal model (delta ELPD [causal -null] = 59.47, SE = 9.74) outperformed the null model. The causal model fit better than the sharing model (delta ELPD [causal -sharing] = 6.98, SE = 1.33, P = 7.3 × 10^-8), with posterior median gamma = 0.27 (95% CrI, 0.21-0.32).

In the BPD-to-ADHD direction, both models outperformed the null model (sharing: delta ELPD = 29.46, SE = 6.53; causal: delta ELPD = 35.37, SE = 7.82). The causal model again fit better than the sharing model (delta ELPD [causal -sharing] = 5.92, SE = 1.54, P = 6.2 × 10^-5), with posterior median gamma = 0.16 (95% CrI, 0.12-0.20).

Sharing-only models estimated eta = 0.35 (95% CrI, 0.28-0.45) and q = 0.65 (95% CrI, 0.48-0.81) for ADHD-to-BPD, and eta = 0.26 (95% CrI, 0.18-0.37) and q = 0.51 (95% CrI, 0.31-0.71) for BPD-to-ADHD. Although the sharing-only models indicated substantial correlated pleiotropy, the causal model provided better predictive performance in both directions, suggesting that directional effects accounted for variation beyond that explained by correlated horizontal pleiotropy alone.

Colocalization analysis

In the ADHD->BPD direction, coloc-ABF showed strongest evidence of a shared association signal at rs9969232 (PP.H4 = 0.990), rs17576773 (PP.H4 = 0.972) and rs2582895 (PP.H4 = 0.875), with additional support at rs11596214 (PP.H4 = 0.616) and rs7506904 (PP.H4 = 0.515). In contrast, rs17718444 and rs76284431 supported distinct signals (Supplementary Table 13). SuSiE-based fine-mapping identified 25 credible sets across 19 loci: 21 for ADHD and four for BPD. Fifteen loci yielded ADHD-only credible sets, whereas four (rs17718444, rs17576773, rs9969232, and rs76284431) yielded sets for both traits (Supplementary Table 14). Two ADHD loci, rs7844069 and rs2582895, contained two credible sets each; posterior coverage ranged from 95.2% to 99.5%.

Among the four loci evaluable by coloc-SuSiE, two confirmed shared signals. The chromosome 4 locus indexed by rs17576773 yielded PP.H4 = 0.972, with 17 ADHD-associated and four BPDassociated variants, including three shared variants. The chromosome 7 locus indexed by rs9969232 showed PP.H4 = 0.989, with 46 and 37 variants in the ADHD and BPD credible sets, including 30 shared variants. At chromosome 4, rs72678864 had the highest BPD posterior inclusion probability among shared variants (PIP = 0.486; ADHD PIP = 0.050). At chromosome 7, rs9969232 showed PIPs of 0.372 for ADHD and 0.165 for BPD, whereas rs4727799 showed 0.048 and 0.466; rs4727799 was an instrument in the reverse BPD-to-ADHD MR analysis. In contrast, coloc-SuSiE favored distinct signals at rs17718444 (PP.H3 = 0.989; PP.H4 = 0.0007) and rs76284431 (PP.H3 = 0.976; PP.H4 = 0.0009). The remaining 18 regions could not be evaluated because sets were not identified for both traits (Supplementary Table 14). In the BPD->ADHD analysis, coloc-ABF across five loci showed colocalization at rs4727799 (PP.H4 = 0.990), distinct signals at rs6549383 (PP.H3 = 0.982), and H2 support at three loci (Supplementary Table 15). Coloc-SuSiE confirmed colocalization at rs4727799 (PP.H4 = 0.985), whereas the signals at rs6549383 were distinct (PP.H3 = 0.989). Similar to the ADHD-to-BPD direction, the remaining regions could not be formally evaluated using coloc-SuSiE due to the absence of identified credible sets for both traits (Supplementary Table 16).

Sensitivity analyses excluding lead SNPs from colocalized loci

To assess whether bidirectional MR estimates were influenced by variants at loci colocalized in the SuSiE analyses, we repeated the analyses after excluding corresponding lead SNPs. Excluding rs9969232 and rs17576773 did not materially alter the ADHD->BPD association. The IVW and weighted median estimates remained positive and statistically significant. Additional radial-MR analyses identified nominal outliers, but exclusion of these variants also retained a positive association with reduced heterogeneity (Supplementary tables 17-18). In the BPD->ADHD direction, exclusion of rs4727799 provided estimations consistent with the primary analysis, with no evidence of heterogeneity. However, these results were cautiously interpreted, given the limited number of instruments.

Minimum Detectable effect analysis

At 80% power and a two-sided significance threshold of alpha = 0.05, the minimum detectable odds ratio (MDE), calculated using multiplicative random-effects inverse-variance weighted (IVW) standard errors, was 1.259 for ADHD->BPD with 22 genetic instruments, compared with an observed IVW odds ratio (OR) of 1.628. Following sequential instrument exclusions, the MDE decreased to 1.230 with 20 instruments and 1.220 with 18 instruments, while the corresponding observed ORs were 1.518 and 1.509, respectively. In the reverse direction (BPD->ADHD), the conditional MDE was 1.369 with five instruments, compared with an observed IVW OR of 1.354, in the presence of substantial between-instrument heterogeneity (Cochran's Q = 20.24, df = 4, p = 4.47 × 10^-4). After excluding rs4727799, the MDE decreased to 1.186 with four instruments, while the observed IVW OR was 1.215 and residual heterogeneity was no longer statistically significant (Q = 3.48, df = 3, p = 3.23 × 10^-1).

DISCUSSION

This study combined conjFDR and bidirectional MR to examine the shared polygenic architecture and possible directional genetic associations between ADHD and BPD. We identified 48 loci associated with both disorders. Analyses suggested a positive association between genetic liability to ADHD and BPD, while evidence for the reverse direction was less precise and more sensitive to individual variants.

The primary multiplicative random-effects IVW analysis supported an association between ADHD and BPD, with directionally consistent results across sensitivity analyses. Although the instruments showed heterogeneity, the MR-Egger intercept provided no evidence of directional horizontal pleiotropy, and LOO indicated that no single instrument drove the estimate. The association remained positive after excluding radial-MR outliers and eliminating heterogeneity. MR-PRESSO detected pleiotropy but yielded a positive outlier-corrected estimate. MR-LAP also supported a positive ADHD->BPD association after adjusting for sample overlap, winner's curse, and weakinstrument bias. The corrected estimate remained statistically significant and exceeded the observed standardized IVW estimate, suggesting these biases do not fully explain the association. Steiger analyses also supported the expected direction, with instruments explaining more variance in ADHD than in BPD. However, we cannot completely exclude correlated pleiotropy: ADHD-related variants might influence BPD through related factors such as impulsivity, emotion dysregulation, or broader mental health issues rather than directly through ADHD. Violations of instrument independence due to population differences, selective mating, or genetic confounding are also possible, and the Steiger-test results do not definitively establish causal direction.

Colocalization analyses identified two ADHD instrument loci, rs9969232 and rs17576773, with evidence of shared ADHD->BPD association signals in both coloc-ABF and coloc-SuSiE analyses.

After excluding these variants, the IVW association remained positive, suggesting the estimate was not entirely attributable to these two SNPs.

The reverse BPD->ADHD analysis requires cautious interpretation. Although IVW and weighted median analyses indicated a positive association, only five instruments were available with substantial heterogeneity. The association was influenced by rs4727799, a chromosome 7 variant showing colocalization between ADHD and BPD; removing it reduced association magnitude and heterogeneity, although the estimate remained positive. Similarly, radial-MR analyses identified rs4727799 and rs6549383 as outliers; after exclusion, the association remained positive but relied on only three instruments.

The reverse-direction result is less stable than the ADHD->BPD result and may reflect the shared genetic architecture at the chromosome 7 locus together with limited instrument precision. Given limited instrument number and sensitivity to individual variants, the reverse-direction findings do not establish a direct developmental pathway from BPD to ADHD and may partly reflect shared genetic liability underlying dimensional traits such as impulsivity and emotional dysregulation.

CAUSE analyses offered a complementary genome-wide assessment of the genetic overlap between ADHD and BPD. In both directions the causal model fit the data significantly better than the sharing-only model, suggesting correlated horizontal pleiotropy alone did not fully account for the observed association patterns under CAUSE assumptions. The estimated directional effect was more pronounced from ADHD to BPD than from BPD to ADHD; however, differences in GWAS characteristics prevent direct comparisons of effect sizes. Additionally, directional-model support in the BPD-to-ADHD analysis contrasted with the greater sensitivity to individual instruments in conventional MR, with only five BPD instruments. This discrepancy likely arises from instrument selection: CAUSE employs genome-wide variants (p < 1 × 10^-3), whereas MR uses genome-wide significant instruments (p < 5 × 10^-8). The CAUSE findings suggest directional effects at the genome-wide level, but the limited number of BPD instruments restricts the precision of conventional MR in the reverse direction.

The conjFDR-identified shared loci provide a further perspective on the ADHD-BPD relationship.

Functional annotation implicated pathways related to axon guidance, oxidative phosphorylation, and neurodevelopmentally relevant cell type signatures. The axon-guidance and oxidative phosphorylation findings involved genes distributed across multiple loci, which is more consistent with broader biological convergence than enrichment driven by a single region. In contrast, glycosaminoglycan degradation enrichment was attributable to genes from one locus and should be interpreted as locus-specific rather than as evidence of a general pathway-level mechanism. These annotations are hypothesis-generating and do not establish the causal genes, affected tissues, or molecular mechanisms underlying shared associations. The chromosome 7 region containing FOXP2 represents a particularly notable shared locus. Colocalization and fine-mapping analyses supported a shared association signal in this region, although the posterior inclusion probabilities differed between ADHD and BPD across individual variants. FOXP2 has established roles in neurodevelopmental and corticostriatal processes (Vargha-Khadem et al., 2005), making it a biologically plausible candidate in the context of ADHD and BPD-related phenotypes.

Nevertheless, the current analyses do not establish FOXP2 as the causal gene, and the variants identified may affect other genes or regulatory elements within the region. Similarly, chromatininteraction mapping has implicated MET; however, the interaction evidence available was obtained from mesenchymal stem cells rather than neural tissue. Consequently, any suggested role of MET in the ADHD-BPD association should be considered provisional, despite its previous implication in neurodevelopmental processes (Campbell et al., 2006; Ahirwar et al., 2025).

This study has several limitations. First, MR estimates rely on assumptions that cannot be fully verified, including the absence of horizontal pleiotropy and confounding pathways. In this context, developmental pleiotropy (e.g., shared effects on impulsivity or executive functions) is plausible and may violate the exclusion restriction even after colocalization-based exclusion. Although the sensitivity analyses, CAUSE, MR-LAP, and targeted removal of colocalized loci addressed several potential sources of bias, none could conclusively establish causality. The small but statistically significant cross-trait LDSC intercept (0.026, SE = 0.007) suggests modest correlated estimation error between the discovery GWAS, potentially reflecting sample overlap or other sources of covariance. Although MR-LAP and FinnGen R8 depletion addressed sample-overlap concerns in complementary analyses, residual bias cannot be entirely excluded, warranting caution in causal interpretation without negating the consistency of the observed genetic associations. Second, the BPD GWAS yielded fewer genome-wide significant genetic instruments than the ADHD GWAS, constraining the power and precision of the BPD-to-ADHD Mendelian randomization analysis.

Minimum detectable effect analyses indicated that the BPD-to-ADHD direction was only powered to detect relatively large effects (OR > 1.37 with five instruments), raising the possibility a true smaller effect was overlooked. Third, the use of European-ancestry GWAS limits generalizability to other populations. Fourth, the BPD phenotype and the FinnGen emotionally unstable personality disorder phenotype may not be fully interchangeable, despite their conceptual overlap. Finally, the sign-concordance analysis supports directional consistency of the shared loci across datasets but does not constitute independent replication of the full conjFDR or MR analyses.

These findings may have clinical relevance. Shared genetic susceptibility may partly explain why ADHD and BPD frequently co-occur and why difficulties in impulsivity and emotion regulation can complicate their differential diagnosis. The stronger and more consistent ADHD-to-BPD findings are compatible with the hypothesis that genetic liability to ADHD is associated with increased BPD risk. Whether this reflects a developmental pathway in which ADHD-related traits precede BPD features, or shared liability to dimensional traits such as impulsivity, requires validation in longitudinal cohorts with repeated phenotyping. This does not imply that ADHD inevitably leads to BPD or that genetic risk establishes individual-level clinical progression. Rather, our results may reinforce the clinical relevance of monitoring emotional dysregulation, impulsivity, interpersonal difficulties, and emerging borderline personality features during adolescence and early adulthood among individuals with ADHD. Future studies using larger and more diverse BPD GWAS, longitudinal cohorts, and functional genomic data are needed to clarify the biological mechanisms underlying these shared and potentially directional genetic relationships.
