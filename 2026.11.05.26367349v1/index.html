Files that do not belong in the repository

We left out 1 file that software creates by itself, not the authors: editor settings (1). They hold nothing a reader can use, but they clutter the repository, and software can mistake them for real files (for example a ._ copy of a .csv for data).

Remove these: Operating system files: macOS adds a __MACOSX folder and a ._ copy of every file it puts in a zip, and .DS_Store files to folders; Windows adds Thumbs.db and desktop.ini. On a Mac, create zips from the command line with zip -r -X, or remove them afterwards with zip -d archive.zip "__MACOSX/*" "*.DS_Store". RStudio session files (.Rproj.user/) hold open tabs and unsaved edits; R history (.Rhistory, .Rapp.history) lists the commands typed in a session and can hold file paths and passwords; Jupyter checkpoints (.ipynb_checkpoints/) are automatic copies of notebooks; compiled Python files (__pycache__/, .pyc) are recreated when the code runs; open-document lock files (~$...docx, .~lock...#) are left while a document is open; editor backups (file~, .bak, .swp) and temporary files (.tmp, .temp, an interrupted save of R’s workspace, .RDataTmp) are copies a program made by itself.

Keep only on purpose: version control folders (.git, .svn, .hg) hold the project’s history, which can include earlier versions of files and data that was later removed; editor settings (.idea, .vscode) can hold paths on your own computer. A .gitignore based on GitHub’s templates (https://github.com/github/gitignore) keeps them out of a repository.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Objectives: To evaluate gout management in England before and after publication of a national clinical guideline by the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) and assess the feasibility of using routinely collected health data to monitor guideline implementation at scale.

Setting: Primary care and hospital admission data, with the approval of NHS England.

Participants: 22,618,945 individuals aged 18 to 110 years registered with general practices in England contributing data to the NHS OpenSAFELY Data Analytics Service between July 1, 2016, and June 30, 2025.

Intervention: Publication of NICE guidance for the diagnosis and management of gout in June 2022.

Main outcome measures: Trends in evidence-based measures for gout management, including new prescription of urate-lowering therapy (ULT) and attainment of a target serum urate below 360 micromol/L. Changes in temporal trends after guideline publication were estimated using interrupted time-series models.

Results: Of 245,445 individuals newly diagnosed with gout who had available follow-up, 87,885 (35.8%) were prescribed ULT within 12 months of diagnosis. ULT initiation increased from 26.6% in July 2016 to 43.0% in June 2025; however, the rate of increase slowed after publication of the NICE guideline (change in trend: 0.76 percentage-point decrease per year; 95% CI 0.35 to 1.17; p<0.001). Among 108,380 people initiating ULT at any point after diagnosis, 35,865 (33.1%) had at least one recorded serum urate level below target within 12 months of initiation. Urate target attainment improved modestly following publication of the NICE guideline (change in trend: 1.25 percentage-point increase per year; 95% CI 0.64 to 1.86; p<0.001), although sensitivity analyses suggested that was a continuation of an existing trend rather than a guideline-specific effect. Younger adults and males were more likely to be prescribed ULT but less likely to achieve urate targets than older adults and females.

Conclusions: Substantial gaps remain between observed gout management and NICE guideline recommendations, particularly for ULT initiation and serum urate target attainment. Guideline publication was not associated with marked improvements beyond existing temporal trends, highlighting the need for implementation strategies to improve gout care. Importantly, routinely collected health data can be used to monitor guideline implementation at scale and identify priorities for improvement.

Introduction

Gout is a common and debilitating form of inflammatory arthritis caused by the deposition of monosodium urate crystals in the joints and other tissues. 1 The crystal burden in gout can be reduced through sustained urate-lowering therapy (ULT, e.g. allopurinol or febuxostat), titrated to achieve a serum urate concentration below 360 micromol/L, or below 300 micromol/L in people with tophaceous gout. [2] [3] [4] Treat-to-target ULT can prevent recurrent gout flares and irreversible joint damage, promote resolution of tophi, and improve quality of life. 1 Increasing evidence from observational studies also suggests that treat-to-target ULT is associated with a lower risk of major adverse cardiovascular events and progression of chronic kidney disease (CKD). 5, 6 Despite ULT being available for over 60 years, earlier population-based studies from the UK and many other countries had shown persistently low rates of ULT initiation and serum urate target attainment. [7] [8] [9] Recognising the need for improvement, the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) published its first guideline on the diagnosis and management of gout in June 2022. 2 NICE guidance is of particular relevance to primary care clinicians, where most people with gout are managed in the UK. 2 NICE recommendations include offering ULT to people with gout who have multiple or troublesome flares, CKD stages 3 to 5, diuretic therapy, chronic gouty arthritis or tophaceous gout; discussing the option of ULT with people with gout who are not within these groups; titrating the dose of ULT to achieve a serum urate target; and consideration of prophylactic use of colchicine, NSAIDs or corticosteroids during ULT initiation and titration. 2 It remains unknown whether these recommendations have led to improvements in gout care. This reflects broader challenges in evaluating the implementation of guidelines across healthcare systems. Current approaches rely heavily on local clinical audit and manual data collection, which is resource intensive and limits the ability to identify unwarranted variation at a national level. 10, 11 Access to electronic health records in primary care has the potential to overcome many of these limitations. The NHS OpenSAFELY Data Analytics Service, a Trusted Research Environment run by NHS England, 12 provides secure access to these data, creating an opportunity for continuous monitoring of guideline implementation and quality of care across a wide range of clinical conditions.

Our objective was to use data in OpenSAFELY to evaluate gout management in England before and after publication of NICE guidance, assess variation across demographic groups, and demonstrate a scalable approach to monitoring guideline implementation using routinely collected health data.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a population-level, observational cohort study using the NHS OpenSAFELY Data Analytics Service. This service provides secure access to full, coded GP records and linked hospital admission data for patients registered with general practices in England using SystmOne (TPP) software, representing approximately 40% of the population of England. 12 There is substantial variation in the regional coverage of SystmOne practices across England, although their registered patients are representative of the general population of England with respect to age, sex, socioeconomic status, ethnicity and causes of death. 13 With support from the Royal College of General Practitioners and the British Medical Association, the Department of Health and Social Care expanded the use of OpenSAFELY for research beyond COVID-19 in 2025. 14, 15 The reference population for this study consisted of all individuals aged 18 to 110 years who were registered for at least 12 months with SystmOne general practices in England at any point between 1 July 2016 and 30 June 2025. Individuals with type 1 opt-outs recorded with their general practice were excluded, indicating that they did not consent to personal data being shared with organisations outside their direct care (approximately 3% of the population of England in 2017). 16, 17

Case definition

Incident gout diagnoses were defined as individuals from the reference population who had an index diagnostic code for gout in their primary care record or a hospitalisation with a primary discharge diagnosis of gout during the study period (see Supplementary Appendix for codelists). 18 Individuals were required to have at least 12 months of continuous practice registration before their index diagnosis to minimise the likelihood of misclassifying prevalent cases as incident. Individuals who had received a prescription for ULT more than 30 days before their index diagnostic code for gout were deemed not to be incident diagnoses.

Outcomes

Outcomes were selected a priori to reflect NICE recommendations for gout management that were measurable using data available in OpenSAFELY, as follows: the proportion of individuals with index gout diagnoses who: i) had a serum urate level recorded within 2 months before or after their index diagnosis, provided this was before ULT initiation; ii) were prescribed ULT (allopurinol or febuxostat) within 12 months of diagnosis; iii) had at least one recorded risk factor indicating a need to be offered ULT (recurrent gout flares, CKD stages 3 to 5, diuretic use, chronic gouty arthritis or tophaceous gout) 2 and who were prescribed ULT within 12 months of risk factor onset (or within 12 months of gout diagnosis if risk factors were present at diagnosis); iv) received allopurinol versus febuxostat as their first uratelowering medication; v) were initially prescribed higher dose allopurinol (300 mg tablets) or febuxostat (120 mg tablets) as their first ULT prescription; vi) were prescribed febuxostat with pre-existing major cardiovascular disease (coronary heart disease, stroke or transient ischaemic attack); vii) were prescribed colchicine, NSAIDs or corticosteroids on the same day as their first ULT prescription, with a further prescription 3 months later (used as a proxy for gout flare prophylaxis); viii) were still prescribed ULT 12 months after their initial ULT prescription; ix) had at least one serum urate level, or two or more serum urate levels, recorded within 12 months of initiating ULT; x) had a recorded serum urate concentration below 360 micromol/L within 12 months of ULT initiation; xi) had a repeat serum urate level recorded 6 to 12 months after obtaining a serum urate level below 360 micromol/L; xii) remained below 360 micromol/L on the first repeat serum urate measurement 6 to 12 months after target attainment; xiii) had prescriptions issued for colchicine, NSAIDs or corticosteroids on the same day as a recorded gout flare (see Supplementary Appendix for further details on outcome definitions).

Statistical analysis

Baseline sociodemographic characteristics and comorbidities were summarised for the reference population at cohort entry and at the time of diagnosis for individuals with gout. Monthly trends in outcome attainment were presented for all individuals with index gout diagnoses, and separately by sex, age group (18-39 years; 40-59 years; 60-79 years; and 80 years and over), socioeconomic status (measured using quintiles of Index of Multiple Deprivation [IMD], ranging from quintile 1 [most deprived] to quintile 5 [least deprived]), and region (categorised into 9 Nomenclature of Territorial Units for Statistics Level 1 regions of England). 12 Single group interrupted time-series analysis (ITSA) was performed to estimate changes in trends in monthly outcome attainment following publication of the NICE gout management guideline in June 2022. 19 Annualised trends before and after guideline publication, expressed as percentage-point change per year, were presented alongside the difference in trends (post versus pre-guideline) and the percentage step change occurring in June 2022. Autocorrelation between monthly observations was accounted for using Newey-West standard errors with a bandwidth of 5 months. 19 Residual autocorrelation was assessed using the Cumby-Huizinga test for autocorrelation, 20 with robustness examined using alternative lag specifications. Sensitivity analyses included adjustment for calendar-month effects to account for seasonality, and for the COVID-19 pandemic using an indicator for March 2020 to February 2022.

To explore the potential impact of delays in guideline implementation, sensitivity ITSAs were performed using an alternative intervention point of 12 months after NICE guideline publication (June 2023), comparing differences in trends with the primary analysis. Additionally, an intervention point of 12 months before NICE guideline publication (June 2021) was utilised as a negative control to investigate whether changes in trends began before guideline publication. Data management was conducted using Python 3.14, with statistical analyses performed in Stata version 19 and R version 4.5. Analysis code and codelists are available online (https://github.com/opensafely/nice_gout).

To meet statistical disclosure control requirements, all frequency counts were rounded to the nearest five, and non-zero counts fewer than eight were suppressed.

Results

Study population and baseline characteristics

From 22,618,945 eligible adults in the OpenSAFELY reference population, there were 262,925 newly recorded gout diagnoses between 1 July 2016 and 30 June 2025. The mean age at diagnosis was 60.7 years (standard deviation [SD] 16.3 years), compared with a mean age of 46.4 years (SD 18.8 years) in the reference population at cohort entry. Individuals with gout were more likely to be male than the reference population (73.9% vs. 49.6%, respectively); more likely to be of White ethnicity (89.2% vs. 79.5%); less likely to be of Asian ethnicity (5.5% vs. 8.3%), Black ethnicity (1.7% vs. 3.1%), mixed ethnicity (0.7% vs. 1.5%), or Chinese or other ethnicity (1.2% vs. 3.0%); and more likely to be from the least deprived socioeconomic quintile (20.1% vs. 17.7%). Individuals with gout were more likely to have comorbidities at baseline than the reference population, including CKD stages 3 to 5 (23.6% vs. 5.1%), hypertension (42.9% vs. 16.3%), obesity (45.9% vs. 25.5%), type 2 diabetes mellitus (14.3% vs. 5.8%), coronary heart disease (12.0% vs. 3.7%), previous stroke or transient ischaemic attack (5.4% vs. 2.1%), or diuretic use (25.3% vs. 6.0%) (Supplementary Table S1).

Serum urate measurement at diagnosis

Serum urate levels were recorded at diagnosis in 154,585 (58.8%) individuals with gout, with a mean concentration of 464 micromol/L (SD 98.7). The proportion of individuals with baseline serum urate measurements decreased during the early COVID-pandemic, from 57.9% in January 2020 to 27.5% in April 2020, before returning to pre-pandemic trends by August 2020 (Supplementary Figure S1). Individuals 80 years and above were less likely to have baseline serum urate measurements than younger adults (49.8% in individuals aged ≥80 years; 59.8% in 18-39 years; 59.8% in 40-59 years; and 60.7% in 60-79 years; Supplementary Figure S2). Females were more likely than males to have baseline serum urate levels (61.2% vs. 57.9%; Supplementary Figure S3). Trends were comparable by socioeconomic status and region (Supplementary Figures S4 and S5).

New urate-lowering therapy prescription

Of 262,925 people with incident gout, 245,445 (93.4%) remained continuously registered for at least 12 months after diagnosis. Among these individuals, 87,885 (35.8%) were prescribed ULT within 12 months of diagnosis. ULT initiation increased from 26.6% among individuals diagnosed in July 2016 to 43.0% among those diagnosed in June 2025. A reduction in the rate of increase in ULT initiation was observed after NICE guidance was published (trend before NICE guideline: 1.97 percentage-point increase per year; trend after: 1.21 percentage-point increase per year; trend difference: 0.76 percentage-point decrease per year, 95% CI 0.35 to 1.17, p<0.001) (Supplementary Figure S6). 138,790 (52.8%) individuals with gout had at least one recorded risk factor at diagnosis or during available follow-up for which NICE specifically recommends that ULT should be offered to patients (recurrent flares; CKD stages 3 to 5, diuretic use, chronic gouty arthritis or tophaceous gout). Of these individuals, 60,580 (43.6%) were prescribed ULT within 12 months of risk factor onset, or within 12 months of gout diagnosis if risk factors were present at diagnosis. By month of risk factor onset, ULT initiation increased from 32.4% in July 2016 to 48.7% in June 2025. The rate of increase slowed after NICE guidance was published: trend before NICE guideline: 1.90 percentage-point increase per year; trend after: 0.82 percentage-point increase per year; trend difference: 1.07 percentage-point decrease per year, 95% CI 0.47 to 1.67, p=0.001 (Figure 1A).

Older adults were less likely to be prescribed ULT than younger age groups (Figure 1B and Supplementary Figure S7), while males were more likely than females to be initiated on ULT (Supplementary Figure S8). Trends in ULT initiation were comparable by socioeconomic status (Supplementary Figure S9). There was divergence in ULT initiation between regions of England, with rates of ULT initiation declining in London towards the end of the study period (Supplementary Figure S10). Of 118,555 individuals initiating ULT, 13,135 (11.1%) were prescribed colchicine, NSAIDs or corticosteroids at ULT initiation and again 3 months later, used as a proxy for gout flare prophylaxis. Trends in co-prescription of prophylaxis remained broadly stable over the study period (Supplementary Figures S14 to S17).

Of 108,380 individuals initiating ULT who had available follow-up, 79,125 (73.0%) were still prescribed ULT 12 months after their initial prescription. There were no significant temporal changes in ULT persistence after publication of NICE guidance (Supplementary Figure S18). Adults aged 18 to 39 years were substantially less likely to remain prescribed ULT after 12 months compared with older age groups (56.8% in individuals aged 18-39 years; 70.5% in 40-59 years; 79.1% in 60-79 years; and 77.5% in ≥80 years; Figure 3). Trends in ULT persistence were comparable by sex and socioeconomic status (Supplementary Figures S19 and S20), with lower rates of ULT persistence in London relative to other regions (Supplementary Figure S21). The proportion of individuals initiated on allopurinol who remained on allopurinol at 12 months was higher than for febuxostat (71.9% vs. 50.6%, respectively; Supplementary Figure S22), although the number of people prescribed febuxostat was substantially lower than allopurinol with more variation on a month-by-month basis.

Urate monitoring and target attainment

Of 108,380 individuals with 12 months of available follow-up after initiating ULT, 73,235 (67.6%) had at least one serum urate measurement during this period, while 35,560 (32.8%) had two or more urate levels recorded. Post-ULT urate monitoring decreased temporarily during the early pandemic, followed by an increase after the NICE guideline (Supplementary Figure S23), which was attenuated after adjustment for the effects of the COVID-19 pandemic (Supplementary Table S2).

Among 108,380 individuals initiating ULT, 35,865 (33.1%) had at least one recorded serum urate level below 360 micromol/L within 12 months of initiation. Target attainment increased modestly towards the end of the study period, from 29.6% in July 2016 to 36.9% in June 2025, with a significant increase after publication of the NICE guideline (trend before NICE guideline: 0.03 percentage-point decrease per year; trend after: 1.22 percentage-point increase per year; trend difference: 1.25 percentage-point increase per year, 95% CI 0.64 to 1.86, p<0.001) (Figure 4A). When restricted to the 73,235 individuals with at least one recorded serum urate measurement within 12 months of ULT initiation, target attainment was 49.0%, with a comparable post-guideline increase (Supplementary Figure S24).

Females were substantially more likely to achieve target than males (45.0% vs. 29.6%, respectively; Figure 4B). Younger adults were less likely to achieve target than older adults (18.0% in individuals aged 18-39 years; 29.8% in 40-59 years; 40.2% in 60-79 years; and 35.7% in ≥80 years; Supplementary Figure S25). Individuals from the most deprived socioeconomic quintile were less likely to achieve target than those from the least deprived quintile (28.8% vs. 37.3%, respectively; Supplementary Figure S26). Urate target attainment was broadly comparable by region (Supplementary Figure S27).

Of 48,155 individuals achieving target at any point after commencing ULT who had available follow-up, 12,060 (25.0%) had a repeat serum urate level recorded within 6 to 12 months of urate target attainment, with modest improvement after publication of the NICE guideline (Supplementary Figure S28A). Of those with a repeat urate measurement, 9,185 (76.2%) remained below 360 micromol/L (Supplementary Figure S28B). Sensitivity analyses

Results for the primary ITSA models were consistent across alternative Newey-West lag specifications and following adjustment for seasonality (Supplementary Table S2). Estimates for baseline serum urate testing and urate monitoring after ULT initiation were attenuated after accounting for reductions in testing during the pandemic (Supplementary Table S2).

ITSA models with alternative intervention dates were used to assess the sensitivity of findings to intervention timing. Moving the intervention date to June 2023, to allow for delayed guideline implementation, produced a similar magnitude but non-significant slowing in ULT initiation compared with the primary model (Supplementary Figure S30A). Moving the intervention date to June 2021, as a negative control, produced a smaller, non-significant change, suggesting that the change in trend in ULT initiation became more apparent around the time of guideline publication (Supplementary Figure S30B).

For urate target attainment within 12 months of ULT initiation, moving the intervention date to June 2023 produced a larger post-intervention increase than in the primary model, consistent with improvement continuing over time (Supplementary Figure S31A). Moving the intervention date to June 2021 produced a similar increase to the primary model, suggesting that the improving trend in urate target attainment was already underway before guideline publication and could not be clearly attributed to the NICE guideline (Supplementary Figure S31B).

Discussion

In this nationwide study from England, we showed that routinely collected health data in OpenSAFELY can be used to monitor the implementation of a clinical guideline at scale. Despite improvements in some aspects of gout care, including greater use of low starting doses when initiating ULT, substantial gaps in management remain evident. By June 2025, only 43% of individuals with gout were prescribed ULT within 12 months of diagnosis, and only 33% of those initiating ULT achieved target serum urate levels. There was little evidence of guideline-specific effects on these trends, highlighting the need for targeted strategies to translate evidence-based recommendations into routine care.

Earlier studies from the UK and several other countries have highlighted suboptimal gout management. [7] [8] [9] [22] [23] [24] [25] We previously showed that gout management guidelines from the British Society for Rheumatology (BSR 2017) and European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR 2016) were not associated with significant improvements in ULT initiation or urate target attainment in the UK. 7 However, these guidelines were developed by specialist societies, whereas most patients with gout in the UK are managed in primary care. Publication of the NICE guideline in 2022 therefore represented an important opportunity to improve gout care, given the central role of NICE guidance in setting standards for clinical practice across the NHS. 2 As with many long-term conditions, gout lacks a national quality improvement programme in the UK through which guideline implementation can be monitored. In this study, we demonstrated that routinely collected health data can help address this gap. ULT initiation increased gradually over the study period but remained below 50% in 2025. Rather than accelerating following publication of the NICE guideline, the rate of improvement in ULT prescribing slowed. This should not, however, be interpreted as evidence that the guideline adversely affected gout care. It is plausible that these changes would have occurred irrespective of guideline publication. Indeed, the post-intervention period coincided with recovery from the COVID-19 pandemic and sustained pressure on healthcare services, which are likely to have impacted on the management of long-term conditions such as gout.

Although improvements in serum urate target attainment were observed following publication of the NICE guideline, the magnitude of increase was relatively small, equivalent to approximately 3.8 additional people per 100 achieving target over three years. Sensitivity analyses suggested that the improving trend had begun before guideline publication, but despite this, target attainment remained low in June 2025, at only 37%. Achieving urate targets is central to preventing gout flares and complications associated with gout, 5, 6, 26 and is possible for the vast majority of patients when a treat-to-target approach is followed. 26 Our findings suggest that treat-to-target therapy is still not being implemented for the majority of gout patients in the UK.

We were also able to identify variation in gout care across population groups. Females were less likely than males to be started on ULT but more likely to achieve serum urate targets once treated, while individuals from more deprived areas were less likely to achieve target than those from less deprived areas. Younger adults were more likely than older adults to be initiated on ULT but substantially less likely to achieve target, which may in part reflect their lower likelihood of remaining prescribed ULT at 12 months. Similar age-related differences in treatment persistence have been reported for type 2 diabetes in UK primary care. 27 Identifying these differences using routinely collected data may help target interventions to groups with the greatest unmet need. For younger adults, this could include support to maintain long-term ULT use and reduce the risk of recurrent gout flares and longer-term complications of inadequately treated gout, including progressive joint damage, CKD and cardiovascular disease. 5, 6, 26 We showed that ULT prescribing remains dominated by allopurinol, despite NICE recommending either drug as first-line treatment. 2 This recommendation differs from BSR and other gout guidelines, and was informed by updated economic analyses reflecting the reduced cost of febuxostat. 28 We found that persistence at 12 months was lower among patients initiating febuxostat than allopurinol, although the small number of patients receiving febuxostat warrants caution. There may also have been clinical reasons for the selection of febuxostat, such as previous use of allopurinol in secondary care that were not captured in our data.

An aspect of gout care that improved considerably over the study period was prescribing safety. There were large reductions in the proportion of patients starting ULT at higher doses, which increases the risk of adverse cutaneous reactions. Febuxostat prescribing in patients with major cardiovascular disease also declined from 2019 onwards, coinciding with warnings from the Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency (MHRA) advising against its use in patients with pre-existing major cardiovascular disease unless no suitable alternatives were available. 29 Following publication of the FAST trial in 2020, 30 the MHRA softened this advice in May 2023 to recommend cautious use in this population. 31 Despite this revision, we found that febuxostat prescribing in people with cardiovascular disease continued to decline, suggesting a sustained influence of the initial safety warning.

There are several potential explanations for why publication of a NICE guideline has not yet been associated with substantial improvements in gout care beyond existing trends. The guideline was published during a period of health service recovery following the COVID-19 pandemic, and the post-intervention period in this study may have been too short to capture the full effects of implementation. However, the lack of substantial improvement beyond existing trends is consistent with previous studies highlighting challenges in improving gout care. [7] [8] [9] [22] [23] [24] [25] Implementing treat-to-target ULT requires repeated blood tests and coordinated dose titration, which need to be delivered alongside competing and increasing demands in primary care. 32 Unlike long-term conditions such as diabetes, gout is absent from the Quality and Outcomes Framework in the UK, providing little financial incentive to systematically identify patients who remain undertreated. Previous studies have also highlighted multiple barriers to effective gout management, including stigma around the diagnosis and an excessive focus on dietary modification. 33 Implementation strategies are therefore essential to improving gout care. Education should promote a shared understanding of gout as a long-term condition requiring sustained uratelowering treatment. Nurse-led treat-to-target care has been shown to be highly effective in trial settings, 26 although the intensity of follow-up presents challenges for delivery within stretched healthcare services. Digital approaches to supporting implementation are particularly attractive in UK primary care, where electronic health records are used almost universally. Automated recall systems for serum urate testing, clinical decision support templates and prescribing prompts, and dashboards identifying patients who have not commenced ULT or remain above target could support delivery of treat-to-target care while reducing the need for repeated manual review.

Importantly, our study demonstrates how routinely collected data can support near real-time monitoring of guideline implementation and care quality. Once quality metrics have been defined and computed, they can be updated regularly without repeated manual data collection. 34 For organisations such as NICE, this approach could enable more routine monitoring of guideline implementation, helping to identify gaps or variation in care and inform where further action may be required. This, in turn, allows more time to be directed towards improving care rather than measuring it, while data-enabled trials could be used to evaluate quality improvement interventions in real-world practice. 35 Our openly shared, adaptable code provides a basis for applying this approach across many other diseases and guidelines.

Several limitations should also be considered. Some aspects of gout management, such as patient education, are challenging to ascertain from electronic health records. We therefore focused on outcomes that could be captured more consistently, including ULT initiation and urate target attainment; however, we could not determine whether ULT was offered to patients but declined, and prescribing records do not confirm medication adherence. Prescriptions and laboratory tests recorded exclusively in secondary care were unavailable, although most individuals with gout in England are managed in primary care. Similarly, the use of non-prescription medications, such as over-the-counter ibuprofen would not be captured when assessing flare treatment. Some analyses were limited by small numbers, which precluded subgroup analyses by ethnicity. Interrupted time-series analysis cannot distinguish guideline effects from concurrent changes in healthcare delivery, including lasting effects of the COVID-19 pandemic. Some diagnostic misclassification is also likely in studies using coded health data, although previous studies have demonstrated good validity of gout coding in UK primary care data. 18, 36 Finally, as our study was conducted in a single health system, the findings may not be directly generalisable beyond the NHS in England.

In conclusion, despite improvements in some aspects of gout care, substantial gaps remain between clinical practice and NICE guideline recommendations, particularly for ULT initiation and serum urate target attainment. Guideline publication was not associated with marked improvements beyond existing temporal trends, highlighting the need for implementation strategies to improve gout care. Importantly, our approach provides an adaptable framework for monitoring quality of care and guideline implementation at scale using routinely collected data.
