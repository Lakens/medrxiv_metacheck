How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Abstract

Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) infects over 90% of adults worldwide, causing infectious mononucleosis (IM) in adolescents and young adults and contributing to multiple malignancies. Despite its global burden, population-level transmission modeling of EBV remains limited. We present the first population-level agent-based model (ABM) of EBV transmission. The model is implemented with 10,000 agents and calibrated to age-specific seroprevalence data from the US National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey. Two complementary calibration methods are evaluated, each with its own distinct advantages: Approximate Bayesian Computation (ABC) and Bayesian Optimization with Expected Quantile Improvement (BO-EQI). ABC directly yields an approximate distribution of transmission parameters for propagation through vaccination scenarios. BO-EQI uses a limited simulation budget to identify well-fitting parameter vectors, particularly as the number of fitted parameters increases. We estimate parameter uncertainty at each resolution in a separate Gaussian-kernel posterior analysis, which requires additional simulations and an explicit model for discrepancies between simulated and observed prevalence. As an illustrative application, we simulate the population-level effect of hypothetical EBV vaccines over a 100-year projection horizon. Similar to a previous modeling study, our results suggest that vaccine durability is at least as critical as efficacy, and that a vaccine with direct protection against only EBV may persistently shift IM burden to older ages, an effect our longer simulation horizon reveals to be a durable equilibrium shift rather than a transient cohort phenomenon. These findings highlight the importance of careful vaccine target product profile design; in particular, our results suggest that dual efficacy against both EBV infection and IM progression represents the most promising achievable target profile, and should be a core design objective for next-generation EBV vaccines.

1 Introduction

Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) is one of the most widespread human pathogens, with seroprevalence exceeding 90% in adults worldwide 1, 2 . Primary infection usually occurs in childhood, where it is often asymptomatic, but acquisition during adolescence or early adulthood frequently results in infectious mononucleosis (IM) 3 . Following infection, commonly through saliva 4 , EBV establishes latency within B cells and can periodically reactivate, leading to viral shedding and continued opportunities for transmission 1 . Beyond illness, EBV has been shown to result in several malignancies, including multiple sclerosis (MS), Hodgkin's lymphoma (HL), nasopharyngeal cancer and gastric cancer [5] [6] [7] . Despite its global burden, the transmission dynamics of EBV is not as thoroughly studied as those of other common viruses like influenza. The difficulty arises from asymptomatic infections, heterogeneous shedding, and the challenge of measuring interpersonal contacts relevant for transmission.

Over the decades, attempts to control EBV infection and its sequelae have focused on managing symptoms of IM, suppressing viral replication, and preventing EBV-driven malignancies. Antiviral therapies such as acyclovir, ganciclovir, and valganciclovir have been trialed, primarily targeting lytic replication [8] [9] [10] . Lytic replication, or the lytic cycle, is a viral reproduction process where a virus infects a host cell, replicates its genetic material, and ultimately destroys the host cell to release new viral particles. While these drugs can reduce viral shedding in the short term, clinical studies have shown little benefit in altering the course of infectious mononucleosis or preventing latency, since EBV persists as an episome within B cells 11 . Similarly, corticosteroids have been used to manage severe IM complications such as airway obstruction, but they are not recommended for routine treatment due to limited benefit and potential risks 12 . These results highlight the challenge of intervening after primary infection has occurred.

Vaccination against EBV has long been considered an attractive preventive strategy, particularly given its contribution to both acute morbidity and long-term cancer risk. The most advanced vaccine candidate to date was a recombinant glycoprotein (gp) 350 subunit vaccine developed by GlaxoSmithKline Biologicals (Rixensart, Belgium), which was tested in a large phase 2 trial. This vaccine reduced the incidence of IM by 78% (95% CI [1.0% -96.0%]) but did not prevent EBV infection itself 13 . More recently, efforts have shifted toward vaccines targeting multiple viral antigens, including those involved in viral entry into epithelial and B cells 14 . Advances in nanoparticle platforms and mRNA technologies have renewed interest in EBV vaccine development, with several candidates currently in preclinical or early clinical stages 15, 16 . These approaches aim not only to reduce IM but also to mitigate downstream risks of EBV-associated cancers and autoimmune diseases.

Mathematical modeling offers a valuable framework, allowing exploration of how age structure, infection outcomes, and behavioral factors shape population-level patterns of EBV spread. Such models also provide tools to test hypothetical scenarios such as vaccination programs, which are increasingly under consideration given the morbidity associated with IM and EBV's role in cancer risk. Previous research on EBV transmission modeling has employed diverse approaches to understand infection dynamics and inform public health strategies. Goscé et al. (2019) 17 represents the most comprehensive population-level mathematical model of EBV transmission to date, explicitly incorporating age structure and vaccination scenarios. Calibrated to seroprevalence data from England, the compartmental model showed that vaccine duration of protection has a greater impact on EBV and infectious mononucleosis (IM) prevalence than efficacy. Short-lived immunity deferred infection into adolescence, where IM risk is highest, while longer-lasting protection reduced both transmission and disease burden. The study also noted that vaccinating younger age groups could unintentionally delay infection to later ages, thereby increasing the likelihood of IM.

Exploring infection dynamics in a different way, Shapiro et al. (2008) 18 created an agent-based simulation of EBV infection within a host, enabling exploration of infection parameters. The agents of this model are EBV virus, B cells and T lymphocytes. Addressing age-related risk directly, Huynh and Adler (2012) 19 applied an age-dependent framework to explain why infectious mononucleosis (IM) peaks in adolescence, focusing on in host dynamics rather than interventions. A joint statistical model linking Danish seroprevalence with IM incidence was employed by Rostgaard et al. (2019) 20 to estimate age-and sex-specific infection hazards and attack rates. In Balfour et al. (2013) 3 , U.S. NHANES (The National Health and Nutrition Examination Surveys) data is used to estimate the age-specific prevalence of EBV infection in individuals aged 6-19 years. It identifies demographic and socioeconomic factors associated with earlier EBV acquisition, providing updated population-level insights into infection timing in the United States. The agent based model we develop also uses this data.

In this study, we develop a flexible, long-horizon agent-based model of EBV transmission and IM development. It is the first agent-based model, and only the second population-level transmission model 17 of EBV. The first model, a compartmental model, was parametrized using data from England. The agent-based model we develop is calibrated with data from the United States. An agent-based model was chosen over a compartmental one in order to facilitate the simulation of heterogeneous populations, and to allow the tracking of complex individual health state histories (e.g. age at EBV infection and IM onset) without requiring a combinatorial explosion in the number of compartments. Being able to resolve these histories in detail is critical to the future addition of EBV-associated sequelae, e.g. MS. We also use this model as a testbed for comparing two calibration strategies for stochastic agent-based models: Approximate Bayesian Computation (ABC) and Bayesian Optimization with Expected Quantile Improvement (BO-EQI). We distinguish point calibration from uncertainty quantification. ABC directly supplies a joint parameter sample for the vaccination projections. BO-EQI directs simulations toward finding a well-fitting point under a fixed evaluation budget. We additionally examine parameter uncertainty through a separate importance-sampled posterior; its intervals are not an output of BO-EQI itself. Finally, we use the calibrated model to examine illustrative 2/28 hypothetical vaccine scenarios selected to be comparable with previous EBV modeling work.

2.1 The agent-based model and synthetic population

We developed an agent-based model implemented in R to study the transmission of Epstein-Barr Virus (EBV) and the occurrence of Infectious Mononucleosis (IM) within a synthetic population. At initialization, the model generates a population of agents distributed across age and sex-assigned-at-birth to reflect the demographic structure of the target setting, with some agents seeded as EBV-infected. The demographic structure of the model is informed by population birth rates 21 and age-stratified mortality rates 22 . Social mixing patterns are defined by an age-structured social contact matrix of the USA 23 , as implemented in the Bernadette R package 24.

Transmission-related parameters capture the risk of EBV infection and of developing IM at the time of infection. The probability of EBV transmission per infectious contact is calibrated using as the target the age-specific prevalence of EBV within the US population 3 , estimated using data from the US National Health and Nutrition Examination (NHANES) survey. Calibration is performed using two methods: Approximate Bayesian Computation (ABC) and Bayesian Optimization (BO). Further details are provided in Section 3. The probability that a new EBV infection leads to Infectious Mononucleosis is also stratified by age 20 . Where vaccination is available, the information provided includes the age groups eligible for vaccination, the annual probability of vaccination in those groups, and the efficacy of the vaccine. In the base case, vaccination is not implemented. Hypothetical vaccine strategies are explored in our analysis (see Section 5 below).

A simulated scenario starts with an initial population (here, 10,000 agents distributed according to present-day US demography), a start date, and a duration. Agent attributes are summarized in Table 1 and model parameters in Table 2. Time advances in discrete steps; through experimentation, we found that a step size of 28 days adequately resolves the (relatively slow) EBV transmission dynamics. At each step, agents undergo deterministic changes (aging, waning of protection if vaccinated), and depending on their current state, may probabilistically undergo transitions to vaccinated, infected (EBV or EBV+IM), or death. This structure allows the simulation to capture how demographic processes, infection risk, and vaccination strategies shape EBV prevalence and IM incidence over time. Upon exposure, susceptible individuals may acquire EBV infection. If infection occurs, an age-dependent probability governs whether the agent develops IM. Thus, an agent's health state can be (i) EBV-seronegative; (ii) EBV-seropositive without IM at primary infection; or (iii) EBV-seropositive with IM at primary infection.

3.1 Approximate Bayesian Computation

ABC is a likelihood-free method for Bayesian inference. In cases where the likelihood function is too complicated to formulate, ABC enables us to approximate the parameter of interest through multiple simulations. Parameters are sampled from the prior distribution, and for each draw, simulated data is generated. Both simulated and observed data are transformed into summary statistics for comparison by some distance measure. We implement the ABC-Rejection method, taking into consideration the confidence interval of the observation. The method of Latin Hypercube Sampling (LHS) is applied to generate parameters θ i j within specified prior ranges, where1 ≤ i ≤ m is the size of the prior set and j = 1, . . . , n is the number of age groups.

The calibration age groups used to define these parameters are (0 -5, 6 -14, 15+), which are distinct from the five age groups used to summarize model prevalence for comparison with the Balfour et al. data. This grouping reflects an assumption that individuals within these broader age ranges have similar patterns of close social contact and saliva exposure, such as contact through caregiving, shared objects, or intimate interactions, which influence EBV transmission. Broader and finer age stratifications were considered, but the three-group specification was selected as a practical balance between capturing age-related differences in transmission and limiting the number of age-specific transmission probabilities estimated from relatively sparse age-specific prevalence data. In section 4, a sensitivity experiment is conducted using the more scalable Expected Quantile Improvement method, with the number of age-specific transmission parameters varied from one to seven. Model outputs are consistently summarized as age-specific prevalence for the five age groups reported by Balfour et al.

To generate results from the m × n parameter matrix, we complete simulations that cover a 100 year period. For each scenario the mean prevalence within the age groups (6 -8, 9 -11, 12 -14, 15 -17, 18 -19) are calculated as our summary statistics, to be compared to the intervals of prevalence for matching age 3 . The running of prior simulations is perfectly parallelizable since there is zero communication needed between simulations. Hence, performance scales linearly with the number of processors. The simulations were completed on a single workstation 7/28 equipped with a 12th Gen Intel Core i5-12500 CPU, with 6 physical cores and 12 logical processors, and 16 GB RAM. Using this hardware, 10,000 prior simulations were completed in approximately four hours.

4.4 EBV probability of infection calibration: BO-EQI

We first conducted calibration experiments with a single per-contact infection probability parameter. The framework was subsequently extended to include up to 7 age-stratified infection probability parameters, resulting in a higherdimensional calibration problem. We apply both ABC and BO-EQI at each resolution with the same bounds and 700-evaluation budget to compare the effects of parameter resolution. ABC shares the 140 initial evaluations with BO-EQI and adds 560 Latin hypercube draws from the same uniform prior. It follows the tolerance-selection rule in Section 3, searching fractions from 0.0001 to 0.20 in increments of 0.0001 and requiring at least five accepted draws. The accepted-sample mean is compared with the BO-EQI point estimate. Each fitted point is evaluated with 100 ABM validation runs, independent of calibration, using common 12/28 seed indices across settings. Table 3 reports their mean discrepancies, Monte Carlo standard errors, and paired 95% intervals for the ABC-minus-BO-EQI difference.

All calibrations start from the same initial population of 10,000 agents, generated with seed 20260917. They use 28-day steps over 100 years, demographic rebalancing every 0.25 years, and prevalence averaged over outputs stored every 26 steps within ten years of the last stored output. The preceding approximately 90 years are warmup; this duration alone does not establish equilibrium. The simulations are run on one Intel Core i9-13900KF workstation with 32 logical processors and 64 GiB RAM (nominal clock approximately 3.0 GHz). Table 3 compares the two methods at the same budget of 700 ABM evaluations. For three to seven parameters, the BO-EQI point estimate has a lower mean validation discrepancy than the ABC accepted-sample mean, and the paired 95% interval for the difference excludes zero. For one and two parameters, the two means differ by less than 0.1 and the interval contains zero, so the fits are equivalent at these resolutions.

Table 4 reports the resulting parameter estimates for the data of Epstein-Barr seroprevalence among US children 3 . BO-EQI returns one parameter vector and says nothing about how well the data pin it down, and ABC accepts too few draws at this budget (Table 3) to give intervals, so we estimate parameter uncertainty separately by asking which parameter values are compatible with the survey. A parameter value is compatible to the extent that its simulated prevalence falls within the survey confidence limits. Since ρ(θ ) measures the distance from the observed prevalence in units of survey standard errors, we give each parameter value the weight exp{-ρ(θ ) 2 /2}: it equals one for an exact match, about 0.6 when the simulation is one standard error away in a single age group, and about 0.14 at two standard errors. This is the likelihood that follows from treating the five Z k (θ ) as independent standard normal errors 29 . Combined with the uniform prior π on [l, u] and averaged over ABM randomness, it gives the posterior

This posterior weights every parameter value continuously, whereas the ABC procedure in Section 3 either accepts or rejects a draw, and it does not use the GP. The posterior cannot be evaluated on a grid, so we sample it. We draw parameter values, run the ABM once at each, and weight the run by exp{-ρ 2 /2}. Drawing uniformly from [l, u] would spend most runs where the weight is close to zero, so we draw from a distribution q concentrated where earlier training simulations found small ρ and correct for this preference by dividing each weight by q(θ j ). The resulting importance weights w j = π(θ j ) exp{-ρ(θ j ) 2 /2}/q(θ j ) make the weighted sample represent the posterior above 30 . Here q is a mixture of a Student-t component fitted to the training simulations and a uniform component over [l, u], and it is held fixed during the 145,000 production runs. The intervals in Table 4 are the weighted 2.5% and 97.5% quantiles of these runs. They describe which parameter values the data support and are not centred on either point estimate. At every resolution the weighted sample carries at least as much information as 400 equally weighted draws, and no single draw holds more than 2% of the total weight. To determine whether the ABC and BO-EQI parameter estimates produce different epidemiological predictions, we compare their five age-specific prevalence outcomes using the 100 common-seed validation runs at each parameter resolution. We regard an absolute mean paired difference smaller than the standard deviation across the 100 validation runs at the BO-EQI estimate as being within the ABM noise floor. A difference comparable to or larger than this standard deviation is at least as large as the variation between individual runs, whereas a paired 95% Monte Carlo interval containing zero indicates that its direction is not stable against simulation noise.

5 Results

5.1 Vaccination Scenarios

As an illustrative application of the calibrated ABM, we examined several hypothetical EBV vaccination scenarios. These were not intended to provide an exhaustive evaluation of EBV vaccine target product profiles, nor to represent any specific vaccine candidates. They were, however, chosen to be broadly comparable to the ones previously explored by Goscé et al. 17

15/28

Given that three parameter strata had been shown sufficient to provide a reasonable fit for our homogeneous population (see Figures 7 and 8), we used the ABC version of the calibration; this provided us with a joint posterior distribution of parameters of size 297. Each scenario was then run as a set of 297 simulations drawn from the posterior. In all scenarios, vaccination was introduced into a population of 10,000 agents that had evolved for 100 years before intervention, thus allowing it to reach a stable epidemiological state. The impact of vaccination was then simulated over the subsequent 100 years and compared with a no-vaccination baseline. Vaccination was handled as a one-time opportunity upon an agent reaching the eligible age, using an uptake of 80% across all scenarios.

We considered three vaccination ages (1, 5, or 11 years), two vaccine efficacies (80% or 100%), and either lifelong protection or waning protection. In the waning scenario, efficacy was assumed to decline exponentially such that, 15 years after vaccination, it remained at 80% of its initial value. Under the exponential decay formulation (Equation 1), this corresponds to a half-life of approximately 46.6 years.

In most scenarios, the vaccine was implemented as being efficacious only against EBV, as in the model of Goscé et al.; no direct efficacy against IM was modeled. However, we also considered a set of scenarios with a vaccine having dual efficacies of 80% against both EBV and IM, both with a 15-year duration of protection. Results are shown in figures Figures 9 to 14. Since IM is an acute (rather than lifelong) condition with highly variable duration, and to align with how real-world IM burden is usually reported, we show the incidence rather than the prevalence for IM.

We observe that the relative impact of vaccine efficacy versus duration changes with age group: Below age 25, increasing efficacy from 80% to 100% achieves roughly the same reduction after 100 years as increasing duration of protection from 15 years to lifetime, whereas in the 25+ group, increase in duration has the greater effect. We also see that for a given efficacy and duration, earlier vaccination achieves greater EBV reduction. In the case of lifetime protection, the reason is simple: Earlier vaccination means fewer years exposed to infection before becoming protected. However, in the case of waning protection, there is a competing effect: Earlier vaccination means earlier waning. Across our scenarios, the former effect is evidently stronger than the latter.

More diverse behavior becomes apparent when we look at vaccine impact on the burden of IM. The "perfect vaccine" scenario (100% efficacy, lifetime protection) remains relatively straightforward; IM incidence is reduced across all age groups below 25, and above 25, where the incidence is already extremely low, it has negligible effect. Furthermore, all scenarios achieved significant reduction in IM incidence in the highest-incidence 12-18 age group, and waning-protection scenarios showed the strongest impact when vaccination took place just before, at age 11, thus minimizing the effect of waning. However, above age 18, the picture becomes more complex. When vaccinating at age 1, all but the perfect vaccine and dual-efficacy scenarios actually increased the IM incidence in the 19-24 age group, by deferring infection to higher ages. Waning further exacerbated this effect by leaving older ages progressively less protected. This effect weakened with increasing vaccination age; when vaccinating at age 11, all but the 80%/15yr scenario reduced incidence relative to the baseline. However, above age 25, all except the perfect vaccine scenario eventually produced elevated IM incidence. Throughout, the dual-efficacy scenario achieved the second-best protection after the perfect vaccine scenario.

5.2 Sensitivity of IM Assumption

We examine the sensitivity of our assumption that the probability of developing infectious mononucleosis (IM) following primary EBV infection, p im , remains constant from age 29 onward. The impact of this assumption is most prominent in the IM incidence of the 25+ age group. For vaccination at age one, we consider two alternative assumptions for p im beyond age 29: a linear decline beginning at age 30 and reaching zero at age 100, and an extreme case in which p im is set to zero from age 30 onward (Figure 15). Under the original assumption of constant p im , IM incidence increases substantially above the baseline in most vaccination scenarios, except for the non-waning vaccine with 100% efficacy. Introducing a linear decline in p im reduces the magnitude of these increases, although the overall trends remain similar. In contrast, setting p im to zero from age 30 onward results in only marginal increases in IM incidence relative to the baseline, with considerably smaller differences between vaccination scenarios. These findings indicate that the projected increase in IM incidence among older individuals is sensitive to the assumed probability of developing IM following primary EBV infection at older ages. 5.3 Sensitivity of θ 1

There is no well-established nationally representative estimate of EBV prevalence for children aged 0 -5 years in the United States. As a result, the corresponding probability of EBV infection per meaningful contact, θ 1 , is not calibrated directly to an age-specific target, but is instead informed by the overall fit of the simulated prevalence profile to the observed age groups. This relies on the assumption that adequately reproducing the downstream age-specific prevalence pattern across all ages provides sufficient information to estimate transmission occurring in the youngest age group. In Figure 16, we divide the baseline scenario into three, based on the input parameter θ 1 in the posterior and investigate the impact of the different range of θ 1 on IM incidence. The scenario labeled "Top 1/3 of baseline" are the baseline results from the highest θ 1 values, "Last 1/3 of baseline" are from the lowest θ 1 values, and "Mid 1/3 of baseline" are the middle range of values. The corresponding θ 2 and θ 3 values of θ 1 remain consistent. This consistency is important to ensure that θ 1 , θ 2 and θ 3 are collectively a good fit to the observed EBV prevalence data. In the age range 0-5, all baseline categories overlap. When θ 1 is low, θ 2 compensates with relatively higher values to ensure a suitable fit to the overall observed data. Hence in the age group 6-14, the higher EBV incidence results in higher IM incidence for the lowest θ 1 range, compared to the highest θ 1 range.

6 Discussion

In this paper, we developed an agent-based model (ABM) of Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) transmission that tracks infection dynamics within a synthetic population over many decades. By incorporating demographic structure, contact patterns, and age-dependent infection processes, the model provides a flexible framework for examining EBV epidemiology beyond standard compartmental approaches. Calibration to age-specific prevalence data from the United States allowed the simulations to be broadly aligned with available empirical evidence. To our knowledge, this is only the second transmission model of EBV, and the first agent-based model. It is also the first model calibrated to the United States.

A key focus of this work was the comparison of two calibration strategies for estimating the probability of EBV infection per meaningful contact. Using Approximate Bayesian Computation (ABC), we showed how a large ensemble of simulations can be used to obtain a posterior distribution of infection probabilities that provides a good fit to observed prevalence patterns. This likelihood-free approach offers a practical way to account for stochasticity and uncertainty in the model, and naturally yields probability distributions for calibrated parameters. However, there are computational limitations of ABC, particularly as the number of calibrated parameters or the dimensionality of the problem increases, at which point the approach becomes computationally intractable.

24/28

In the present work, only three transmission parameters required calibration, corresponding to the three age strata in the Balfour et al. data. Had we instead stratified the population by race/ethnicity group as Balfour et al. suggest -given that EBV prevalence varies substantially across such groups 3 -we would have required nine parameters simultaneously, at which point ABC becomes prohibitively expensive. This represents both the natural use case for BO-EQI and an important direction for future work.

To address the scalability limitations of ABC, we explored Bayesian Optimization with Expected Quantile Improvement (BO-EQI) as an alternative calibration strategy. By formulating calibration as an optimization problem, BO-EQI was able to identify infection probability estimates that reproduce the observed data well while requiring substantially fewer model evaluations. A tradeoff is that BO-EQI yields parameter point estimates rather than probability distributions. At the three-parameter level, BO-EQI and ABC yielded closely comparable results, validating both approaches. At higher dimensionalities, BO-EQI remained tractable where ABC did not, demonstrating its practical value for more biologically detailed parameterizations of heterogeneous populations.

Our vaccination scenario results confirm and extend the findings of Goscé et al. 17 . Consistent with their compartmental model, we find that vaccine durability is at least as important as efficacy in driving reductions in EBV prevalence, and that earlier vaccination achieves greater reduction in EBV burden across the population. We also replicate their observation that vaccination can unintentionally shift the IM burden to older ages. Our results go further, however, in revealing that this is not merely a transient cohort effect. Over Goscé et al.'s 27-year horizon, it was not possible to determine whether elevated IM incidence at older ages would eventually fade away, or whether it represented a durable shift in the endemic equilibrium. Our 100-year projections show clearly that it is the latter: even a century after vaccination introduction, scenarios with waning protection continue to exhibit elevated IM incidence at higher ages relative to the unvaccinated baseline. This persistence reflects the ongoing recycling of susceptible individuals as vaccine protection wanes in each successive cohort.

Our results also illuminate a fundamental vaccine design constraint. The very property that makes infant vaccination most effective for EBV control -intervening before first exposure -simultaneously maximizes the age-deferral effect on IM. Vaccinating at age 1 produces the greatest reduction in EBV prevalence across all age groups, but also produces the greatest increase in IM incidence at higher ages. This tradeoff weakens but does not disappear as vaccination age increases: even at age 11, waning-protection scenarios eventually produce elevated IM incidence above age 25. The implication is that optimizing a vaccine solely for EBV infection prevention, without regard to what happens to deferred infections, risks a net increase in IM morbidity at the population level.

This observation motivated our dual-efficacy scenario, and connects directly to the history of EBV vaccine development. The most clinically advanced candidate to date, the gp350/AS04 subunit vaccine 13 , demonstrated approximately 78% efficacy against IM but no efficacy against EBV infection itself. Conversely, a vaccine with efficacy only against EBV infection reduces transmission and prevalence but does not protect breakthrough-infected individuals from developing IM. Our dual-efficacy scenario is, to our knowledge, the first modelling analysis to quantify what happens when both components act in tandem. The results are clear: across all vaccination ages, the dual-efficacy profile consistently achieves the second-best IM outcomes after the biologically implausible "perfect" vaccine, substantially outperforming EBV-only vaccination at ages 19 and above. This finding argues that dual efficacy against both EBV infection and IM progression should be treated as a core component of future EBV vaccine target product profiles. The recent shift toward mRNA and nanoparticle platforms 15, 16 , which offer greater design flexibility than earlier subunit approaches, makes this goal increasingly realistic.

7 Limitations and Recommendations

Several limitations of the present analysis warrant acknowledgement. First, the probabilities of developing infectious mononucleosis (IM) following primary EBV infection were estimated from Danish registry data covering ages up to 29 years 20 . Beyond that age, we assume that these probabilities remain constant at their age-29 values. In subsection 5.2, we investigate the impact of declining this probability. The magnitude of the increase in IM incidence in the 25+ age group is lower if the probability is assumed to decrease. However, whether the probability of IM upon primary EBV infection remains high, declines, or approaches zero in middle age is not well established empirically, particularly in the hypothetical setting of vaccination-deferred infection.

25/28

The probability of EBV infection per meaningful contact is calibrated to observed data from the US NHANES for ages 6-19. This data is used to calibrate the probabilities across the entire population based on how well the parameters fit to the available observed data. The sensitivity of the parameter representing the 0-5 age group is explored in subsection 5.3. Nationally representative seroprevalence studies in children aged 0-5 years could better constrain early-life transmission parameters. Also, as noted above, the pooling of all race/ethnicity groups into a single calibration may limit results. Balfour et al. 3 document variations in EBV seroprevalence across racial and ethnic groups in the United States, and a calibration that treated these groups separately would have different transmission parameters (a higher number of parameters) which could provide population dynamics closer to the real world for analysis.

In addition to that, our model does not capture the intermittent and highly variable 31 nature of EBV shedding by infected individuals. We abstract infectiousness as a constant average value. Other biological complexities that may influence EBV transmission and disease outcomes include vital strain diversity, co-infections and variation in mucosal immunity. Excluding these mechanisms may limit the model's ability to capture heterogeneity in susceptibility and infectiousness.

Finally, although ABC provides posterior distributions that capture uncertainty, it has high computational cost which increases as the number of calibrated parameters and the population size grows, limiting its practicality for models with higher stratification. BO-EQI addresses this computational challenge and remains tractable at higher dimensions, but at the cost of returning point estimates rather than probability distributions of parameters.

Despite these limitations, this study provides both a methodological framework and substantive epidemiological findings that are relevant to EBV vaccine development. The ABM architecture, with its ability to track complete individual infection histories, is particularly well-suited to the addition of downstream sequelae such as multiple sclerosis and Hodgkin's lymphoma in future work, outcomes for which the age at primary EBV infection, and its interaction with vaccination, may be of direct clinical importance. For vaccine development and public health planning, model findings could also be translated into clearer target product profiles that distinguish vaccines designed primarily to prevent EBV infection from those that additionally reduce the risk of infectious mononucleosis, including explicit consideration of efficacy, durability of protection, and age at vaccination.
