Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 4 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

The Replication Check module compares the reference list against studies in the FLoRA (FORRT Library of Replication Attempts) database based on the DOI. The module requires that the reference has a DOI: the DOI in the paper’s reference list, or one looked up with add_bib_match() before the module runs (see ref_table()). References without a DOI are not checked.

Abstract

Background: Older adults with high inherited Alzheimer's disease (AD) susceptibility can still be cognitively normal (CN). An overall risk estimate alone does not show the cognitive and genetic contributions within a forecast. Objective: To quantify clinical and genetic contributions to cognitive prognosis. Methods: PRISM-AD combined nonlinear clinical functions and a polygenic risk score (PRS) term in 4,435 European-ancestry participants. Participant-level internal validation evaluated first recorded mild cognitive impairment (MCI) after CN in 908 participants and AD dementia after MCI in 714, within three or five years. Separately fitted comparators used identical inputs. Post hoc clinical-course comparisons used regression with robust standard errors and Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) correction. Results: PRISM-AD using current assessments achieved average precision of 0.2996 and 0.4094 for three-and five-year CN-to-MCI, with estimates close to logistic regression. No PRISM-AD PRS increment met BH q < 0.05. Three-year first-MCI forecasts characterized risk contributions in 893 initially CN participants. A retrospective comparison over available follow-up included 95 participants with later AD and 701 with CN at their last diagnostic assessment. Modeled genetic factors were higher in later AD in nine of ten shared cognitive-risk deciles. After adjustment for modeled cognitive risk and fold, the genetic-factor ratio was 1.135 (nominal 95% CI, 1.073 to 1.201, BH q < 0.001). Conclusions: Higher genetic contributions were associated with later AD at comparable modeled cognitive risk. These exploratory profiles require independent validation before use in cognitive follow-up or prevention research.

Introduction

Cognitively normal (CN) older adults vary in their risk of later impairment, motivating efforts to identify those who may warrant closer cognitive follow-up or evaluation for prevention research. Clinical stage also defines the setting for Alzheimer's disease (AD) care: lecanemab slowed decline in early symptomatic AD, 1 while amyloid and tau biomarkers support biological diagnosis and blood-based assays improve pathology detection in symptomatic settings. 2, 3 Within this clinical course, forecasting first recorded mild cognitive impairment (MCI) after CN addresses the emergence of recognized impairment, whereas forecasting AD dementia after MCI addresses progression once impairment is present. The latter course remains uncertain because MCI has heterogeneous causes and can revert to CN. 4, 5 Cognitive assessment provides clinical information for these forecasts. The Alzheimer's Disease Sequencing Project (ADSP) Phenotype Harmonization Consortium (ADSP-PHC, hereafter PHC) harmonizes memory, executive function, language and visuospatial scores across neuropsychological batteries. 6 PHC studies have characterized trajectories around incident AD, 7 and National Alzheimer's Coordinating Center (NACC) data have supported cognitive-status prediction. 8 In the Anti-Amyloid Treatment in Asymptomatic Alzheimer's (A4) and Longitudinal Evaluation of Amyloid Risk and Neurodegeneration (LEARN) studies, cognitive concerns and computerized testing combined with plasma p-tau217 helped predict decline. 9 Inherited susceptibility provides a different type of information. Genomewide association studies (GWAS) implicate amyloid and tau pathways, lipid processing and immunity in AD susceptibility. 10 A polygenic risk score (PRS) summarizes associated variation without measuring pathway activity or pathological burden. The existing ten-fold participant assignment (seed 42) kept each person's visits together in folds 0-9. Folds 1-9 supplied development data. Folds 1, 2 and 3 each served once for validation while the other eight development folds supplied training. Fold 0 was excluded from this stage-transition analysis but had been used in earlier project development, so it was not an untouched test set. Preprocessing and censoring models used training participants only. This design comprised three rounds . of participant-level internal validation within a ten-fold assignment. Evaluation used no independent external cohort. Sample sizes followed from the available eligible linked records, without a prospectively recruited target sample.

Two clinical transitions, two horizons, three validation folds and eight models yielded 96 fits. Models were trained using repeated eligible visits. Primary evaluation used the first eligible visit for each transition in participants excluded from that model's training set. Analyses using all eligible visits or participant subgroups were secondary. No additional model was trained solely on each participant's first eligible visit. Settings were fixed for these fits, with no hyperparameter search, validationbased early stopping or checkpoint selection. Predicted probabilities were not recalibrated. Results from these folds were available during earlier analysis development. Specifications were held fixed for the corrected-PRS reconstruction after that exploration. Accounting for incomplete follow-up

To account for incomplete follow-up, we estimated the probability of continued observation using reverse Kaplan-Meier curves from all eligible training visits, stratified by study and age bands below 70, 70 to below 80, and 80 to below 85 years. Strata with fewer than 50 visits used their study curve, with a pooled fallback if a study was absent. Both target events and direct-AD competing events contributed as non-censoring observations when fitting the reverse Kaplan-Meier curves. Visits with a target or competing event recorded within the prediction window were weighted by the inverse probability of continued observation just before the event.

For visits observed through the horizon without an event, the probability just before . the horizon was used in the same weighting rule. Visits with unknown horizon outcomes contributed to the training censoring model but received zero weight in the classification loss and evaluation. Weights were capped at 10, but no known training or validation weights required clipping. Validity depends on sufficient observation probability and conditional independence of censoring within the specified strata. Age was included in both the predictors and the censoring strata, but performance was evaluated across the eligible age distribution rather than standardized to a fixed age.

Observation weights could not be supplied directly to TabPFN. To provide a common training sample, observations with known outcomes were sampled with replacement in proportion to their censoring weights, using a fixed seed. Seven models used this resample, whose size was the smaller of the number of observations with known outcomes and 20,000. All models using the resample received the same observations in the same order with the same labels. Observation weights were not applied again during fitting. A separate HGB model incorporating prior assessments was trained on all observations with known outcomes, with censoring weights applied directly. Validation used all eligible validation observations with their censoring weights and did not involve resampling. Training weights did not equalize each participant's total contribution across repeated visits.

Quantifying clinical and genetic contributions to predictions

PRISM-AD used cognitive measurements, clinical context and PRS to estimate the probability p of a specified transition within the prediction horizon. Separate functions for memory, executive function, language, visuospatial performance and their available-domain mean summed to the cognitive-test risk component C. The linear PRS term defined G, whereas the intercept and remaining clinical, interaction and optional history terms formed R (Fig. 1a): . logit ( p )= C + G + R.

The logistic function converts the summed log odds to the event probability p. C is the contribution assigned to measured cognition on the model's risk scale, not the cognitive test score itself. With G and R fixed, higher C increases predicted odds and probability, and lower C reduces them. G has the same directional interpretation when C and R are held fixed. These signed components are on the log-odds scale, and their exponentials multiply to give the complete predicted odds. Zero is a model reference, rather than a threshold for normal cognition or absence of inherited susceptibility.

Each clinical measurement or indicator was represented by a small neural network and a linear term, with an additional piecewise-linear function defined by training-data quantiles where eligible. Prespecified clinical interactions captured joint associations among selected measurements, while PRS entered only through its separate coefficient. The coefficient was constrained to be non-negative, although G could be negative because PRS was standardized relative to the training distribution.

Architecture dimensions, the complete interaction list and the threshold functions are given in Supplementary Methods and Supplementary Table 1.

To incorporate earlier cognitive assessments, we added their summary measures to the clinical predictors and used one Transformer layer to represent the ordered prior measurements (Fig. 1b). Measurements were pooled within domains to produce a bounded history contribution T, which was zero when no earlier assessment was available. Models using current assessments omitted this pathway. The Supplementary Methods section describes the representation of earlier cognitive assessments, handling of missing domain measurements, domain weights and severity-dependent scaling. Comparator settings and the inference-only correction for reproducible stochastic preprocessing are documented in Supplementary Methods. That correction changed neither the training observations nor model parameters.

Education in older high-genetic-risk participants

The exploratory education analysis used the broader post-QC EA genotyped population with linked PHC phenotypes, independently of model-validation eligibility.

CN and AD participants were in the same previously defined high-PRS interval (approximately 0.474-1.135) and were at least 75 years old at their last diagnostic assessment. The interval retained the earlier GRPa-PRS outlier-trimming definition, whose upper bound was anchored at the 97.5th percentile of the original CN reference distribution. 12 This definition selects a bounded subgroup and excludes scores above its upper limit. Scores above the upper bound were outside this selected phenotype but remained eligible for prediction and the unrestricted profiles.

The interval was not retuned against the present clinical courses. Its original . unrounded bounds were applied inclusively, and training-set PRS standardization did not redefine group membership.

CN participants with any prior recorded AD were excluded. Education in years was taken from the last diagnostic assessment, with negative values treated as missing.

Ages reported as 90+ met descriptive eligibility but were not treated as exact ages in adjusted models. A sensitivity analysis required the final recorded visit itself to document the same CN or AD diagnosis.

The CN-minus-AD education difference was estimated by ordinary least squares with HC3 robust t-based intervals, adjusting for age, age squared, sex, study, continuous PRS and PC1-5. Education was not imputed, and current cognition was not included as a covariate. Complete-case analyses required adequate group representation within each retained study. The full selection flow, high-PRS interval definition and criteria for estimating adjusted comparisons are given in Supplementary Methods and Supplementary Table 9.

Amyloid PET during normal cognition

Amyloid positivity was taken from the PHC-supplied PHC_AMYLOID_STATUS field, with only codes 0 and 1 treated as known. According to its data dictionary, this classification uses thresholds on GAAIN summary cortical standardized uptake value ratio (SUVR) normalized to whole cerebellum. Centiloids are supplied conversions of the same summary measure. No common Centiloid positivity threshold was imposed or estimated in this analysis. Tracer-specific conversions and positivity thresholds are described in the PHC MRI-free PET methods documentation.

Two additional comparisons required CN-stage amyloid PET in both diagnostic groups: one included all amyloid statuses, and the other was restricted to amyloid . positivity. Scans required passing image and timing quality control, finite amyloidburden measurements on the Centiloid scale, normal cognition documented in both PET and paired cognitive records, no preceding MCI or AD, and a scan-to-cognition age difference of at most 90 days. The earliest eligible scan was selected before filtering on positivity. The age 75 threshold applied to the last documented cognitive diagnosis, not to the PET scan. AD cases required a dated first AD diagnosis after joint availability of the CN scan and cognition. PET-subset education came from the paired CN visit, and planned adjustment also included tracer and the interval from that visit to the last documented cognitive diagnosis. Neither PET subset met the same sample-size and study-representation requirements, so no adjusted effects were estimated. Benjamini-Hochberg correction covered all four planned education comparisons, assigning p = 1 to non-estimable tests. The analyses do not estimate an educational intervention effect or preservation at matched pathological burden.

Genetic susceptibility and amyloid burden during normal cognition

Using the existing one-scan-per-person PET selection, we examined continuous Centiloid burden in participants whose last documented cognitive diagnosis was CN at age 75 years or older. A sensitivity analysis used the broader all-age, last-known-CN sample without redefining the older high-PRS group with normal cognition at the last diagnostic assessment. The high-PRS subgroup retained the previously defined score interval. PRS was scaled by its standard deviation in the genotyped population, so slopes represent the Centiloid difference per one standard-deviation increase in that population. The main ordinary least-squares models adjusted for PET age, sex, PC1-5, study and tracer, with HC3 robust t-based intervals and covariates restricted to information available at or before the scan. Sensitivity analyses required normal cognition documented at the final recorded visit, restricted the all-age high-PRS group to those with at least three years of follow-up with known cognitive status, or additionally adjusted for the post-PET interval to the last documented cognitive diagnosis. The small follow-up-restricted subgroup omitted tracer to avoid a singleton high-leverage category. Post-PET follow-up duration was not included in the main model because it was determined after imaging. Each participant contributed one CN-stage observation per model, and AD-stage scans were excluded. The small group later diagnosed with AD after a CN-stage scan was summarized descriptively. Prompted by review of earlier exploratory material, these analyses were neither prespecified confirmatory tests nor independent replication, and no multiplicity-adjusted claim was made (Supplementary Table 10).

Results

Cognitive stage defined three-and five-year prognostic settings

The linked cohort comprised 4,435 participants from ADNI, NACC, ROS and MAP.

We examined first recorded MCI after CN and AD dementia after MCI separately, each within three and five years of an eligible visit (Fig. 1). The CN-to-MCI analysis included 2,759 participants contributing 14,351 eligible visits, and the MCI-to-AD analysis included 2,082 participants contributing 5,692 visits. A total of 406 participants contributed to both analyses at eligible visits. Each participant's visits were assigned to the same fold. Internal validation evaluated three development folds in turn, with each excluded from that round's training data. A separate fold was excluded from the present analysis (Methods). Evaluation at the first eligible visit included 908 CN participants and 714 MCI participants (Fig. 1d and Table 1).

Reconstructing diagnostic histories and recorded outcomes for all 20,043 eligible visits identified no entry with an earlier disqualifying diagnosis. Four CN participants later had AD recorded and then returned to CN or MCI at their last diagnostic assessment. They remained eligible for first-MCI prediction and were excluded only from the retrospective clinical-course profiles (Supplementary Methods).

The two transitions differed markedly in frequency. At the first eligible visit, the censoring-weighted fraction with first MCI after CN was 8.62% by three years and 15.34% by five years, whereas the corresponding fractions with AD after MCI were 35.19% and 47.88% (Table 1). First MCI was recorded for 78 of 801 CN participants with a determinable three-year outcome and 124 of 658 with a determinable five-year outcome. Participant characteristics and predictor missingness at the first eligible visit are reported for the development population and evaluated subset in Supplementary Table 11. AP depends on event frequency, so its values cannot be compared directly between the CN and MCI starting groups (Fig. 2a). Age distributions and follow-up availability are shown in Supplementary Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 12.

PRISM-AD predicted three-and five-year clinical transitions

The primary first-MCI comparison evaluated participants who were CN at prediction, using measurements from that visit and the same clinical and genetic inputs for PRISM-AD and the separately fitted comparators. Models incorporating prior assessments were evaluated separately. Current-assessment PRISM-AD achieved censoring-weighted AP of 0.2996 and 0.4094 for first MCI within three and five years, respectively (Fig. 2a and Table 2). Logistic regression achieved 0.2951 and 0.4091.

Corresponding PRISM-AD AUROCs were 0.7332 and 0.7708.

For first-MCI prediction, PRISM-AD minus logistic regression AP differences were 0.0046 (95% CI, -0.0341 to 0.0340) at three years and 0.0003 (-0.0279 to 0.0304) at . five years. Differences against TabPFN using current assessments were 0.0403 (-0.0190 to 0.0892) and 0.0289 (-0.0309 to 0.0872). These paired intervals quantify uncertainty in the first-MCI AP comparisons with logistic regression and TabPFN (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Table 5).

For AD dementia after MCI, PRISM-AD achieved AP of 0.7105 within three years and 0.8189 within five years. At five years, logistic regression reached 0.8146. PRISM-AD exceeded TabPFN using current assessments by 0.0812 AP (nominal 95% CI, 0.0385 to 0.1229, BH q = 0.0252). This was the only current-assessment AP comparison to meet BH q < 0.05 within the family of 28 first-visit comparisons, which also included models incorporating prior assessments. The comparison is specific to these clinical transitions, forecast horizons and fitted comparators.

Calibration-bin summaries showed discrepancies between predicted and observed risks in some ranges, with limited support in several bins (Fig. 2e,f and Supplementary Table 3). First-MCI calibration slopes were 0.826 (nominal 95% CI, 0.582-1.069) at three years and 0.868 (0.667-1.070) at five years. Corresponding MCI-to-AD slopes were 0.894 (0.714-1.073) and 0.844 (0.680-1.009). All intervals included 1, and no slope departure met the BH threshold. These global diagnostics do not establish calibration throughout the risk range.

Repeated training under the same computational conditions reproduced parameters and predictions exactly. First-visit three-year CN AP ranged from 0.2160 to 0.3975 across validation folds, illustrating variation between evaluated participant groups.

Optimization trajectories are shown in Supplementary Fig. 1.

PRS increments varied by horizon and metric for first-MCI risk ranking

Matched refits tested whether PRS added predictive information beyond the same clinical and cognitive measurements. Five-year first-MCI AUROC changed from 0.7502 without PRS to 0.7708, a paired difference of 0.0206 (0.0055 to 0.0359) (nominal 95% CI). The three-year difference was 0.0026 (-0.0124 to 0.0165). For MCI-to-AD, AUROC differences were 0.0046 (0.0001 to 0.0090) at three years and 0.0041 (-0.0002 to 0.0088) at five years (Fig. 2c, Supplementary Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table 2).

Of the eight PRISM-AD tests across tasks, horizons and metrics, two reached nominal P < 0.05 and none met BH q < 0.05. The five-year CN-to-MCI AUROC increment had nominal P = 0.0152 and BH q = 0.1216, and the three-year MCI-to-AD AUROC increment had nominal P = 0.0362 and BH q = 0.1448. These exploratory tests concern added PRS information, rather than whether fitted genetic terms differ between clinical-course groups (Supplementary Table 4).

First-MCI AP differences after adding PRS were -0.0023 (-0.0281 to 0.0233) at three years and -0.0013 (-0.0262 to 0.0346) at five years (Fig. 2d). All four model families and both forecast windows are shown in Supplementary Fig. 3.

Improvements in ranking do not by themselves establish better probability calibration or clinical benefit.

Current assessments supported prediction with sparse cognitive histories

Earlier measurements were available before the first eligible visit for only 64 of 2,759 CN participants (2.3%), compared with 446 of 2,082 MCI participants (21.4%). For CN-to-MCI, models incorporating earlier assessments achieved AP of 0.2916 and 0.3989, compared with 0.2996 and 0.4094 using current assessments. At three . years, the history-minus-current AP difference was -0.0080 (nominal P = 0.5968), and at five years it was -0.0105 (nominal P = 0.4562). Table 2 and Supplementary Table 5 report all paired comparisons of models incorporating prior assessments, including the HGB sensitivity using observation weights directly.

The two horizons were fitted independently. Their predictions therefore do not define a joint cumulative-risk trajectory. For PRISM-AD using current assessments, threeyear risk exceeded five-year risk at 53 eligible CN validation visits and 29 MCI visits.

Visit spacing also limits the timing resolution of recorded impairment.

Contributions described distinct later clinical courses

Across all 24 PRISM-AD fits with PRS, the recorded terms summed to the held-out predicted log odds. With the remaining terms fixed, a higher cognitive or genetic contribution increases the predicted odds and probability of the specified clinical transition. The broader profile analysis included 893 adults assessed while CN: 701 with normal cognition at their last diagnostic assessment, 97 with MCI at that assessment and 95 later diagnosed with AD. The cognitive term describes the contribution of test performance to predicted first-MCI risk, and subsequent diagnoses define the retrospective clinical-course groups. quintiles within each fitted fold. The PRS term increases with PRS by specification.

The cognitive contribution need not decrease as the genetic contribution increases, because total risk and the other clinical terms vary across participants. Fold-adjusted omnibus tests gave BH q values from 0.4539 to 0.6816 across the four displayed groups (Supplementary Table 4). The marginal distributions overlapped across clinical courses, but participants later diagnosed with AD tended to have higher cognitive and genetic contributions than those with normal cognition at their last diagnostic assessment (rank-biserial r = 0.359 and 0.317, respectively, both BH q < 0.001, Fig. 3e,f and Supplementary Table 4F). The rank tests compare contributions between clinical courses, while the omnibus tests compare PRS quintiles within each course. The marginal comparisons do not hold C constant or adjust for age, study or follow-up, so they address a different question from the analysis at comparable C below.

Preserved cognition at high PRS illustrated contrasting risk contributions

The targeted comparison included 65 high-PRS last-CN participants, 528 other CN participants without recorded AD and 95 participants later diagnosed with AD (Figs. 4a,b and 5h). Both non-AD groups required at least three years of diagnostic followup and normal cognition at their last diagnostic assessment. Intermediate MCI followed by CN was allowed. The AD comparator was not PRS-restricted. Groups were defined by PRS and clinical course, rather than predicted risk.

The adjusted analysis included 64 high-PRS last-CN participants, 495 other CN participants and 63 participants later diagnosed with AD in studies with adequate group representation. The cognitive-test contribution in high-PRS last-CN participants was lower than in later AD, with adjusted high-minus-AD differences of -0.421 (95% CI, -0.696 to -0.146) at three years and -0.444 (95% CI, -0.728 to -0.159) at five years (BH q = 0.0133 and 0.0122, respectively, Fig. 4a). The model therefore assigned a lower first-MCI risk contribution to their cognitive measurements than to those of the later-AD group. The PRS term was higher by 0.195 and 0.272 log-odds units. This genetic contrast partly reflects high-PRS selection and the model's nonnegative PRS coefficient.

. Against other CN participants, cognitive-contribution differences were -0.014 (95% CI, -0.210 to 0.183) at three years and -0.006 (95% CI, -0.196 to 0.183) at five years. The high-PRS last-CN group nevertheless had higher total predicted log odds: 0.348 (95% CI, 0.111 to 0.585) and 0.490 (95% CI, 0.299 to 0.681), respectively (BH q = 0.0171 and < 0.001, Fig. 4b and Supplementary Table 8). Thus, a lower cognitive contribution than in later AD coexisted with higher total predicted risk than in other CN participants. This pattern describes conditional forecasts and does not establish that cognition biologically offsets genetic susceptibility.

Cognitive functions related test performance to predicted risk

Across the fitted models, higher memory scores and higher available-domain mean scores contributed less predicted first-MCI risk over the common training-supported range. For memory, high-minus-low contrasts ranged from -1.364 to -1.017 log-odds units at three years and from -0.973 to -0.724 at five years. Corresponding contrasts for mean cognition ranged from -0.633 to -0.467 and from -0.626 to -0.207, respectively. These main functions exclude interactions and describe fitted associations rather than within-person trajectories (Fig. 4c,d,f and Supplementary Table 6).

Age, education and the individual cognitive domains contributed distinct fitted functions (Fig. 4c-f). Education terms describe prognosis conditional on the measured cognitive state and the other inputs. They address a different question from the separate comparison of educational attainment between older CN and AD participants. Neither the fitted function nor the between-group education association identifies a causal schooling effect.

Greater education accompanied preserved cognition at high genetic risk

In a separate genotyped population at high PRS and age 75 or older, 411 participants had normal cognition at their last diagnostic assessment and 751 had AD. Biomarker-based diagnosis and prognosis could provide additional information on the recorded clinical transitions examined here. 8, 30, 31, 3, 9, 32 Participants with high PRS and normal cognition at their last diagnostic assessment had lower cognitive contributions than those later diagnosed with AD, yet higher overall predicted risk than other CN participants. The targeted CN subgroup was selected for high PRS, whereas the AD comparator was unrestricted, so the genetic contrast partly reflects group selection. A lower cognitive term alongside a higher genetic term describes a prediction profile without establishing biological compensation. Higher memory scores were generally assigned lower first-MCI risk contributions, which may reflect preserved function, premorbid ability or less impairment before clinical diagnosis. The present analyses cannot distinguish these explanations, and correlations among cognitive domains prevent linking individual terms to isolated neural deficits. Allowing intermediate MCI followed by CN also makes the interpretation of preserved cognition dependent on follow-up and outcome definition. 19, 20 In the separate older high-PRS population, CN participants had approximately one more year of education than participants with AD after covariate adjustment, including when diagnosis was confirmed at the final visit. This association is consistent with the use of education as a proxy for cognitive reserve. 15 Pathologyadjusted studies have associated education more consistently with cognitive level than slower decline, depending on tau burden and outcome definition. 16, 33 In the . prognosis model, the positive education term estimated risk conditional on measured cognition and the other inputs, addressing a different question from the comparison of educational attainment. Conditioning on cognition can alter this association, 34 and neither analysis identifies a causal schooling effect. Amyloid PET extended our earlier GRPa-PRS work 12 by showing that 56 of 97 selected high-PRS CN participants were amyloid-positive. The education association and PET findings provide complementary evidence on reserve-related characteristics and measurable AD-related pathology in participants with preserved cognition. The median PET-to-last-CN interval of 0.22 years, small clinical-course groups and unequal study representation limit inference about sustained cognition despite pathology. Longitudinal cognition, amyloid, tau and neurodegeneration measurements could distinguish lower pathology from better function at a given burden. 17, 35 The clinical value of these profiles depends on whether they improve decisions about cognitive assessment and follow-up. Prospective evaluation could compare selection using cognitive and genetic contributions with selection using the complete threeand five-year first-MCI estimate or cognition alone, including participant stratification for prevention research. With assessment capacity held fixed, relevant outcomes include incident-MCI yield, time to recognition, assessment burden and false-positive referrals. Conclusions

PRISM-AD quantifies cognitive and genetic contributions within the same three-and five-year first-MCI forecasts. In exploratory analyses of adults assessed while CN, those later diagnosed with AD had a higher modeled genetic factor than those with normal cognition at their last diagnostic assessment after accounting for modeled cognitive risk (adjusted ratio, 1.135). These internally derived profiles relate the cognitive and genetic information within a forecast to later clinical course. The findings support prospective evaluation of the profiles for cognitive follow-up and participant stratification in prevention research. Independent external validation is needed before clinical use.

Discussion

PRISM-AD made cognitive and genetic contributions explicit within first-MCI forecasts and used the same fitted terms to examine subsequent clinical course in adults assessed while CN. At comparable modeled cognitive risk, participants later . diagnosed with AD had higher genetic contributions than those with normal cognition at their last diagnostic assessment. Within each fold, G is the training-standardized PRS multiplied by a non-negative fitted coefficient. Its contrast therefore expresses the original score in units specific to that fitted model and provides no independent genetic signal. Fold indicators account for mean shifts but do not align the fitted genetic coefficient scales. The within-fold and raw-PRS checks examined this scale dependence while retaining the exploratory interpretation. The genetic difference remained positive after demographic, study and ancestry adjustment, but this association addresses a different question from the predictive value of adding PRS. Models fitted with and without PRS assessed changes in prediction performance, whereas the C-stratified comparison examined fitted genetic contributions across later clinical courses. None of the matched PRISM-AD PRS increments met the BH threshold, so the contribution contrasts do not establish improved prediction from adding PRS. Comparable C does not require identical domain scores or equal overall predicted risk. Likewise, the near-zero correlation between G and C describes how the terms vary together across participants and can coexist with genetic differences between clinical courses at comparable C. The separate five-year AD-status analysis also supported a genetic difference over a common outcome window. Its exploratory comparison used participants with and without recorded AD through five years, so its reference group differed from the original last-CN group. Building on established additive modeling and explainable AD prediction, 21,22 PRISM-AD uses the fitted clinical and genetic terms both to estimate risk and to describe individual forecasts. Its primary first-MCI AP estimates were close to logistic regression, while five-year MCI-to-AD AP exceeded TabPFN using current was evaluated by participant-level internal validation across four development cohorts.

Performance in an independent external cohort or a new care setting remains untested. Prognostic analyses were restricted to EA participants with linked genotypes and follow-up with known cognitive status, limiting applicability to other ancestry groups and routine clinical care. Prior model exploration may make performance estimates optimistic, and overlapping training samples limit their independence. Continued decreases in training loss alongside modest late-epoch increases in validation loss for models incorporating prior assessments indicate possible overfitting (Supplementary Fig.1).

The five-year and timeto-event checks do not remove informative ascertainment or uncertainty in censoring weights and fitted C. Global nonproportional hazards also limit the Cox workingmodel summary. Clinical use requires independent external validation, calibration and prospective evaluation of decision benefit.
