To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 5 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Immune dysregulation is implicated in major depressive disorder (MDD), but its links to clinical features and in vivo brain phenotypes remain uncertain. We examined ten plasma biomarkers in 36 unmedicated MDD patients and 48 control participants using high-sensitivity immune profiling at baseline, alongside PET/MR imaging of brain serotonin transporter (SERT) binding and regional structural measures. Patients underwent three months of antidepressant treatment and were followed up with MR imaging. Glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) was higher in MDD patients (β = 0.66, 95% CI 0.22 -1.11), and interleukin-33 (IL-33) correlated with subcortical volumes across all participants (β = 0.22, 95% CI 0.07 -0.38). No marker correlated with SERT binding or cortical thickness after correction, and neither SERT binding nor brain structural measures differed by diagnosis. In the longitudinal analysis, interleukin-13 (IL-13) was associated with greater antidepressant response during first-line treatment with escitalopram (β = 1.09, 95% CI 0.56 -1.61). IL-15 and IL-13 showed nominal associations with hippocampal volume increase after treatment. These findings support astroglial involvement in MDD and link peripheral immune variation to subcortical brain structure. IL-13 is a candidate prognostic marker of response to selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors. Its tentative association with longitudinal changes in hippocampal volume suggests that immune processes in MDD may be related to both the capacity for clinical improvement and brain structural plasticity during treatment.

Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a clinically and biologically heterogeneous disorder for which current diagnostic categories provide little information about underlying pathophysiology or treatment outcome. This heterogeneity has constrained biomarker discovery and may explain why inflammatory, neuroimaging, and molecular measures frequently distinguish patients from healthy individuals at the group level, yet rarely achieve individual-level specificity (Abi-Dargham et al., 2023). Next to the assessment of group differences in biomarkers of MDD, mapping relationships between immune profiles, clinical, and neurobiological measures may clarify how different pathophysiological processes interlock in depression and lead to the development of more effective stratification methods for targeted interventions.

Substantial evidence implicates immune dysregulation in MDD. Meta-analyses have identified higher circulating C-reactive protein (CRP), interleukin (IL)-6, tumor necrosis factor (TNF), and related inflammatory mediators in depression (Köhler et al., 2018; Osimo et al., 2020). Additionally, translocator protein positron emission tomography (PET) studies show altered neuroimmune activity in corticolimbic regions (De Picker et al., 2023; Eggerstorfer et al., 2022). Longitudinal studies further suggest bidirectional associations between inflammation and depression: higher CRP and IL-6 predict subsequent depressive symptoms, while depression may itself contribute to later increases in these markers (Colasanto et al., 2020; Mac Giollabhui et al., 2021).

Immune alterations may therefore contribute to depressive pathophysiology while also being reinforced by the course and consequences of illness.

Immune involvement is unlikely to be uniform across MDD. Inflammatory markers have been associated more consistently with anhedonia, fatigue, psychomotor slowing, sleep disturbance, and appetite change than with overall symptom severity (Kofod et al., 2022; Milaneschi et al., 2021). This has led to proposals for an inflammation-related subtype characterized by elevated inflammatory activity and prominent neurovegetative and reward-related symptoms (Jha et al., 2025; Miller, 2025).

Inflammation may also contribute to variation in antidepressant outcomes. Higher baseline inflammatory activity has been associated with poorer response to standard antidepressants (Bufalino et al., 2013; Carvalho et al., 2013). But findings vary across markers and treatments, and changes in inflammatory markers do not necessarily follow symptom improvement (Kofod et al., 2022). Whether peripheral markers provide information about subsequent treatment outcome beyond their association with diagnostic status or baseline symptom severity therefore remains unresolved.

Understanding how circulating markers relate to brain biology is another key challenge.

Beyond their inflammatory roles, cytokines can modulate synaptic function, with effects that vary across cellular targets and neural circuits (Salvador et al., 2021; Wheeler and Quintana, 2025). Peripheral immune signals can influence the central nervous system via endothelial and vagal pathways (Huerta et al., 2025; Jin et al., 2024), changes in the blood-brain barrier (Huang et al., 2021; Tyagi and Bartley, 2026), immune-cell trafficking (Li et al., 2018; Thomson et al., 2020), kynurenine metabolism (Paul et al., 2022; Savitz, 2020), oxidative and nitrosative stress (di Penta et al., 2013), and shifts in glial activity (Miller and Raison, 2016; Shen et al., 2026; Upthegrove et al., 2025).

Serotonergic regulation may represent one interface between immune activity and antidepressant response, but direct human evidence linking cytokines to in vivo SERT availability remains limited. Cytokines may influence serotonergic signaling through tryptophan-kynurenine metabolism, SERT regulation, and neuroplastic pathways (Savitz, 2020; Zhu et al., 2006), while antidepressant treatment has been associated with changes in immune activity (Bleibel et al., 2025; Köhler et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2019).

By contrast, peripheral inflammation has been more consistently associated with structural variation in cortical and limbic regions, including the hippocampus, which is sensitive to inflammatory and stress-related processes and may undergo structural changes during antidepressant treatment (Cordero et al., 2026; Evans et al., 2024; Gryglewski et al., 2021; Popovic et al., 2026; Schmaal et al., 2016). However, immune markers, SERT availability, brain structure, and treatment-related structural change have rarely been examined together.

In the present study, we combined high-sensitivity profiling of circulating immune and glial markers with prospective assessment of antidepressant response, [ 11 C]DASB PET, and structural MRI in a thoroughly characterized case-control cohort. By evaluating immune associations across complementary clinical and neurobiological phenotypes, we aimed to determine whether immune markers show generalized associations across MDD-related phenotypes or more selective relationships with diagnosis, treatment outcome, serotonergic transmission, or brain structure.

Methods and materials

Participants and study design

Analyses included 36 patients and 48 healthy controls (Table 1), who were partially used in previous studies by our group (Gryglewski et al., 2019; Klöbl et al., 2025 Klöbl et al., , 2020;; Seiger et al., 2021; Silberbauer et al., 2022; Zadka et al., 2025). All participants were non-smokers. MDD patients were free from antidepressant treatment for at least three months before inclusion and had no comorbid psychiatric or somatic illnesses. Patients were recruited at the Department of Psychiatry and Psychotherapy at the Medical (Klöbl et al., 2025 (Klöbl et al., , 2020;; Seiger et al., 2021; Silberbauer et al., 2022; Zadka et al., 2025). All participants gave written informed consent, and the study was approved by the local ethics committee, conducted according to the Declaration of Helsinki, and registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT02711215).

Blood sampling and single-molecule array

Venous blood samples were collected during PET/MR scans. Plasma was separated by centrifugation at 3,506 × g for 4 minutes and stored at -80°C until analysis. Samples were transported on dry ice and gently thawed on ice immediately before measurement. Concentrations of inflammatory and neuroglial markers, including interleukins (IL-5, IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, IL-13, IL-15, IL-17A, IL-22, IL-33), TNF, and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) were measured using single-molecule array technology on the SR-X Analyzer platform (Simoa Quanterix, Lexington, MA, USA) (Rissin et al., 2010). All analyses were performed by an investigator blinded to clinical data, following the manufacturer's instructions. Samples and calibrators were equilibrated to room temperature, diluted 1:4 in sample diluent, and measured in duplicate in 96-well plates.

Detector reagent and paramagnetic beads were added sequentially to each well, followed by incubation at 30°C and 800rpm for 30 minutes on a Simoa microplate incubator. After automated washing with the Simoa microplate washer, streptavidin βgalactosidase was added, and plates were incubated again at 30°C and 800rpm for 10 minutes. A final wash was followed by drying the plates for 10 minutes before transferring them to the Quanterix SR-X Analyzer for readout. All assay kits and consumables used are detailed in the supplement and were from the same lot where applicable. To reduce inter-assay variability, all samples from a single participant were processed on the same plate. Duplicate measurements underwent quality control as described in the supplementary methods. IL-17A was designated as an exploratory binary detectable marker because its detection rate was below 80% during replicate quality control.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed in R 4.5.3. Continuous variables were inspected using histograms and summary statistics, and skewed variables were log-transformed where appropriate. Immune markers and imaging measures were standardized before analysis. Continuous covariates were mean-centered within the corresponding analysis dataset.

Associations of plasma marker levels were evaluated across six prespecified analysis domains spanning three broader outcome categories: A) Clinical characteristics (MDD diagnosis, antidepressant response); B) Brain serotonergic transmission (baseline PET serotonin transporter binding potential); and C) Brain structure (baseline subcortical volume, baseline cortical thickness, and longitudinal hippocampal change with treatment). Models were fitted separately for each immune marker using marker-specific complete cases. Linear mixed-effects models with participant-specific random intercepts were used for analysis of repeated (regional or longitudinal) measurements.

A) Associations with MDD diagnosis were evaluated using linear regression with immune marker as the outcome and diagnostic group as the predictor. Antidepressant response was assessed using linear mixed-effects models of MADRS scores measured at baseline and after six weeks of treatment. Models included timepoint, immune marker level, and their interaction. Exploratory analyses applied the same approach to BDI and HDRS17. B) Associations of plasma markers with baseline SERT BPP were examined across six predefined PET regions with high SERT availability: anterior cingulate cortex, amygdala, hippocampus, striatum, thalamus, and midbrain. Linear mixed-effects models included immune marker concentration, region (repeated), and diagnostic group, and estimated the average marker association across regions in SERT BPP. C) Baseline structural MRI was analyzed for subcortical volume and cortical thickness.

Subcortical volume analyses included the caudate, amygdala, hippocampus, and nucleus accumbens, based on previous evidence implicating frontostriatal and limbic structural alterations in MDD (Brandl et al., 2022; Serrarens et al., 2025). Left-and right-hemisphere volumes were summed for bilaterally defined regions. Cortical analyses included the rostral and caudal anterior cingulate, insula, and medial orbitofrontal cortex, based on large-scale evidence of depression-related alterations in cingulate, insular, and orbitofrontal thickness (Opel et al., 2019; Schmaal et al., 2017).

For each cortical region, bilateral thickness was calculated as the surface area weighted mean of left-and right-hemisphere estimates. Subcortical volumes and cortical thickness measures were standardized for each region, and mixed-effects models for both structural domains included immune marker concentration, region, and diagnostic group, with estimated total intracranial volume (eTIV) included in the subcortical models. Exploratory region × marker interaction models tested for regional heterogeneity. Longitudinal hippocampal analyses were available for MDD patients.

Bilateral hippocampal volumes were analyzed in their original units (mm 3 ). Primary models included timepoint, immune marker concentration, their interaction, and eTIV, retaining participants with at least one hippocampal measurement. Sensitivity models included complete-pair models. Exploratory Spearman rank correlation assessed the association between six-week MADRS reduction and hippocampal-volume change.

For all domains, the effect of potentially influential covariates was assessed by adjusting for age, sex, BMI, and Global Physical Activity Questionnaire (GPAQ) total score. Benjamini-Hochberg correction was applied within each domain and globally across all 60 marker-domain tests. For cross-domain visualization, two-sided p-values were converted to signed z-scores according to effect direction (Figure 1B).

Exploratory analyses examined routine blood parameters across domains and their correlations with immune markers, adjusted for diagnostic group (Supplementary Figure S4). Parameters included CRP, fibrinogen, total leukocytes, and absolute neutrophil, eosinophil, lymphocyte, monocyte, and platelet counts.

Results

Clinical characteristics

The case-control immune profiling cohort comprised 84 participants, including 36 patients with major depressive disorder (MDD) and 48 healthy controls (Table 1).

Group differences in age, sex, and BMI were negligible. Due to modality-specific data availability, sample sizes varied across analyses (Supplementary Table S3).

GFAP concentrations were higher in MDD patients than in healthy controls (β = 0.66, 95% CI 0.22 to 1.10, pFDR,domain = 0.04, Figure 2A), although the association did not survive global correction (pFDR,global = 0.1). TNF, IL-6, and IL-8 were nominally higher in MDD, whereas IL-33 was nominally lower.

Higher baseline IL-13 was associated with a greater improvement in depressive symptoms over six weeks of SSRI treatment. Each 1-SD increase in IL-13 corresponded to an additional 6.22-point reduction in MADRS score (β = 6.22, 95% CI 3.22 to 9.22, standardized β = 1.09, pFDR,global = 0.01; Figure 2B). The association persisted after adjustment for covariates (β = 6.09, 95% CI 1.96 to 10.2).

Exploratory analyses using HDRS17 (β = 4.04, 95% CI 1.71 to 6.37, p = 0.001) and BDI (β = 3.97, 95% CI 0.98 to 6.95, p = 0.01) showed concordant IL-13 associations.

IL-15 was nominally associated with greater MADRS improvement (β = 3.29, 95% CI 0.27 to 6.31, p = 0.03).

No plasma marker was significantly associated with baseline symptom severity (all p ≥ 0.05).

Total leukocyte (β = 0.62, p < 0.01) and absolute lymphocyte counts (β = 0.64, p < 0.01) were higher in MDD patients, and these associations persisted after covariate adjustment (Figure 2A). No routine blood parameter was associated with antidepressant response, SERT binding, subcortical volume, cortical thickness, or hippocampal volume change with treatment (all p ≥ 0.1; Supplementary Table S10).

Brain serotonergic transmission

No plasma marker was associated with in vivo SERT binding after FDR correction, and SERT binding did not differ by diagnosis (all p ≥ 0.45), as previously reported for this sample (Silberbauer et al., 2022). TNF showed a nominal inverse association with SERT binding across regions (β = -0.27, 95% CI -0.48 to -0.07, p = 0.01; Figure 2C).

Adding sex as a covariate attenuated the estimate by 69% and adjustment for BMI by 26%, while other covariates had negligible influence.

Brain structure

Baseline subcortical volume and cortical thickness did not differ significantly by diagnosis. Higher IL-33 was associated with greater baseline subcortical volume across the caudate, amygdala, hippocampus, and nucleus accumbens (β = 0.22, 95% CI 0.07 to 0.38, pFDR,domain < 0.05; Figure 2D), without evidence that the association varied between regions (region × IL-33 interaction p > 0.22). This association was attenuated after controlling for covariates. (β = 0.16, 95% CI -0.00 to 0.32, p = 0.05).

Higher IL-15 was nominally associated with lower cortical thickness across the rostral and caudal anterior cingulate, insula, and medial orbitofrontal cortex (β = -0.23, 95% CI -0.39 to -0.06, p = 0.01, pFDR,domain = 0.07). In the covariate-adjusted model, age alone reduced the IL-15 estimate by 61.4% (β = -0.05, p = 0.62) and was independently associated with lower cortical thickness (β = -0.05, 95% CI -0.07 to -0.03, p < 0.001).

Furthermore, higher IL-15 and IL-13 were nominally associated with greater longitudinal change in hippocampal volume during antidepressant treatment (IL-15: β = 81.64 mm 3 , 95% CI 19.34 to 143.93, p = 0.01; IL-13: β = 64.44 mm 3 , 95% CI 9.60 to 119.28, p = 0.02; Figure 2B). The IL-15 association was not markedly influenced by additional covariates or sensitivity analyses, but covariate adjustment reduced the IL-13 estimate. Moreover, greater six-week MADRS reduction correlated with more positive hippocampal volume change (ρ = 0.41, p = 0.04; Supplementary Figure S5).

Discussion

In this multimodal study, we evaluated associations among high-sensitivity plasma immune markers, clinical characteristics, brain serotonergic transmission, and brain structure in individuals with MDD and healthy controls. Higher baseline IL-13 was associated with greater improvement in depressive symptoms during six weeks of SSRI treatment. This association survived correction across all primary tests, remained evident after covariate adjustment, and was directionally concordant across secondary symptom measures. Higher IL-15 showed a similar but nominal association with treatment response. Moreover, higher baseline IL-13 and IL-15 were both nominally associated with a more positive hippocampal volume change, although these associations did not survive FDR correction. GFAP concentrations were higher in MDD, and IL-33 was associated with greater subcortical volume at the domain-specific FDR level. No plasma marker was significantly associated with SERT binding or cortical thickness after multiple-testing correction. Baseline imaging measures did not differ by diagnosis, and diagnosis did not moderate immune-brain associations.

The higher GFAP concentrations in patients with MDD than in controls are consistent with reports of elevated serum and cerebrospinal GFAP, as well as increased GFAP in astrocyte-derived extracellular vesicles, in MDD and treatment-resistant depression (Michel et al., 2021; Steinacker et al., 2021; S. Xu et al., 2023). Nevertheless, treatment-naïve patients have also shown lower serum GFAP (Hviid et al., 2023), and a recent meta-analysis found no overall case-control difference (Kang et al., 2025).

These inconsistencies may reflect differences in illness characteristics, treatment exposure, assay sensitivity, or the biological compartment examined. Circulating GFAP should also be interpreted as a nonspecific indicator of altered astroglial integrity or turnover rather than as direct evidence of astrogliosis or neurodegeneration.

However, studies linking GFAP to astrocytic mitochondrial regulation and disrupted astrocyte-serotonin signaling to depression-related behavioral and synaptic abnormalities support the role of astroglial dysfunction in MDD (González-Arias et al., 2023; Xiong et al., 2025).

Nominal elevations of TNF, IL-6, and IL-8 were observed alongside increases in leukocyte counts, with both findings largely unchanged after covariate adjustment.

Although these associations did not survive FDR correction, the direction of higher TNF and IL-6 aligns with meta-analytic findings in MDD, whereas evidence for IL-8 has been less consistent (Osimo et al., 2020). Recent work in first-episode, drug-naïve MDD identifies IL-6 rather than a uniform cytokine profile as the most reproducible signal (Zavaleta-Monestel et al., 2026). Lower IL-33 levels are also compatible with studies linking higher IL-33/ST2 concentrations to reduced depression risk. However, the context-dependent actions of this pathway preclude a straightforward protective interpretation (Li et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2023; Nahar et al., 2024). Collectively, these findings point to subtle and heterogeneous astroglial and immune alterations rather than a uniform inflammatory phenotype in MDD.

The strongest evidence concerned antidepressant response: higher IL-13 was associated with greater symptom improvement but not significantly related to baseline severity. IL-15 showed a similar nominal association, attenuated after covariate adjustment. Higher concentrations of both cytokines were also associated with greater increases in hippocampal volume during treatment. Importantly, greater improvement in MADRS was moderately correlated with a more positive change in hippocampal volume. The clinical and structural findings were therefore linked within individuals, rather than merely parallel, suggesting that IL-13 and IL-15 may index a broader treatment-responsiveness phenotype involving both symptomatic and hippocampal adaptation.

Previous findings support this paired signal: responders had higher pretreatment IL-13 and IL-15 concentrations than nonresponders, although neither cytokine alone showed strong discriminatory ability (Y. Xu et al., 2023). IL-13 was the most consistent moderator of depression outcome among 19 inflammatory markers, but its effect was treatment-specific. Higher IL-13 favored combination therapy with escitalopram and bupropion, whereas lower concentrations favored escitalopram monotherapy (Czysz et al., 2021). However, baseline IL-13 did not distinguish responders from nonresponders in other cohorts (Kofod et al., 2022; Schmidt et al., 2016), while metaanalytic evidence linked response to lower baseline IL-8 and greater treatment-related TNF reductions (Kofod et al., 2022; Köhler et al., 2018; Liu et al., 2020; Schmidt et al., 2016). Longitudinal findings were similarly heterogeneous. IL-13 decreased during treatment (Kofod et al., 2022; Schmidt et al., 2016) or shifted toward control levels (He et al., 2024), but its pooled change was nonsignificant (Köhler et al., 2018). IL-15 decreased transiently before returning to baseline in adolescents receiving fluoxetine (Pérez-Sánchez et al., 2018). Thus, the clinical relevance of IL-13 and IL-15 may depend on treatment context and whether baseline concentrations or treatment-related changes are examined.

Despite this clinical heterogeneity, both cytokines nevertheless have biological actions relevant to hippocampal plasticity. IL-13 and its receptors are present at neuronal synapses, where IL-13 can influence AMPA-and NMDA-receptor trafficking and excitatory transmission (Li et al., 2023). Signaling via the related IL-4Rα pathway also contributes to neuronal network homeostasis (Hanuscheck et al., 2022), and alterations in this pathway have been shown to change susceptibility to social stress (He et al., 2026). In models of stroke and traumatic brain injury, IL-13 promotes reparative microglial phenotypes and functional recovery (Chen et al., 2022; Kolosowska et al., 2019; Miao et al., 2020), although its effects are strongly contextdependent and can also aggravate neuronal injury and astrocyte loss (Hong et al., 2022). IL-15 regulates neural stem-cell proliferation and differentiation, while IL-15Rα supports hippocampal GABAergic and serotonergic function (He et al., 2010; Wu et al., 2011 Wu et al., , 2010)). Disruption of IL-15Rα signaling has also been associated with hippocampal microgliosis and astrogliosis, excessive synaptic pruning, and depression-like behavior (Tang et al., 2026). Together with evidence of treatmentrelated hippocampal plasticity and associations between decreasing inflammation and increasing hippocampal volume (Belge et al., 2020; Gryglewski et al., 2021; Phillips et al., 2015), the observed coupling between symptom improvement and changes in hippocampal volume during treatment motivates an immune-neuroplasticity hypothesis in which IL-13 and IL-15 may index biological states permissive of treatment adaptation.

Our PET findings provided little evidence for a relationship between peripheral immune variation and serotonergic transmission. TNF was nominally associated with lower SERT binding across regions, but covariate adjustment substantially reduced this estimate, suggesting possible demographic and metabolic confounding. Its direction also contrasts with the positive association between circulating TNF and brainstem SERT availability in a SPECT study (Krishnadas et al., 2016). Previous studies have linked SERT to IL-1-related cytokines or IL-6, although results have varied by population, region, and whether inflammatory activity was measured peripherally or centrally (Chou et al., 2016; Tsao et al., 2006; Yang et al., 2021). While TNF and IL-1β can experimentally enhance neuronal SERT activity (Zhu et al., 2006), transporter activity observed in cellular models does not directly correspond to in vivo radioligand binding. TNF may also affect serotonergic function independently of SERT by redirecting tryptophan metabolism toward the kynurenine pathway (Haroon et al., 2020; Okamoto et al., 2024). Thus, our data do not support a robust, generalized relationship between peripheral inflammation and SERT binding.

Higher IL-33 was associated with greater volume across the caudate, amygdala, hippocampus, and nucleus accumbens. Experimental studies show that neuronal IL-33/ST2 signaling supports synaptic remodeling in the hippocampus and integration of new neurons through microglial clearance of the extracellular matrix (Nguyen et al., 2020). In systemic-inflammatory and Alzheimer-like models, exogenous IL-33 also promotes reparative microglial responses and limits synaptic dysfunction, demyelination, and apoptosis (Fu et al., 2016; Nedeljkovic et al., 2026). These findings suggest a possible connection between neural tissue health and IL-33. However, peripheral inflammation is generally associated with reduced structural integrity in MDD, including smaller prefrontal grey matter, a thinner entorhinal cortex, and changes in hippocampal and amygdalar volumes (Green et al., 2021; Opel et al., 2019; Valizadeh et al., 2025). Higher IL-15 was nominally associated with lower thickness across the anterior cingulate, insula, and medial orbitofrontal cortex, broadly consistent with previous associations between peripheral inflammation and cortical alterations in MDD (Kakeda et al., 2018; Kang et al., 2024). However, this association appeared largely attributable to age-related confounding.
