Power Analysis Check

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

The probability of hepatitis C virus (HCV) clearance is mediated by an interaction between numerous factors. However, variation in spontaneous HCV clearance was previously modeled using relatively simple approaches with fixed probabilities by sex. Here, we developed a literature-informed clearance algorithm that considers age, sex, interferon lambda 4 (IFNL4) genotype, and infection history to model the probability of HCV clearance in vulnerable populations, such as people who inject drugs (PWID), and in controlled human infection (CHI) models for future HCV vaccines.

We modeled HCV clearance probabilities for three infection-history pathways: first infection, reinfection after spontaneous clearance, and reinfection after direct-acting antiviral (DAA) cure. Logistic and cumulative-hazard equations assigned person-specific clearance probabilities in the first two pathways. The post-DAA pathway used fixed clearance probabilities. We tested the algorithm using HepCEP (Hepatitis C Elimination in PWID), an agent-based simulator.

First-infection clearance probabilities ranged from 0.13 to 0.51 across eight age, sex, and IFNL4 genotype subgroups. Clearance probabilities for reinfection after spontaneous clearance ranged from 0.20 to 0.80 at literature point estimates. Across 100 runs, the mean assigned clearance probabilities were 0.27 for first infections and 0.52 for reinfections after spontaneous clearance, compared with literature inputs of 0.26 and 0.52, respectively. Probabilities varied across subgroups and runs.

Simulations reproduced the algorithm's intended clearance heterogeneity and uncertainty by age, sex, IFNL4 genotype, and infection history, which may support models of HCV elimination in vulnerable populations and CHI model development for future HCV vaccines.

1. Introduction

Despite the increasing availability of curative therapy, hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection remains a major public health burden in the United States, affecting an estimated 2.5-4 million adults with current infection in 2017-2020. 1 Consequently, developing an effective HCV vaccine has become a prominent research priority. 2, 3 Agent-based models (ABMs), which represent individual people and their interactions, have been used to simulate vaccine trials and characterize HCV transmission dynamics. 4, 5 Simulated trials can assess study designs that would be difficult and expensive to test repeatedly in real life. Elimination models in people who inject drugs (PWID) can further estimate the treatment intensity and costs needed to reduce chronic infections. 5, 6 In both applications, spontaneous clearance affects the proportion of infections that become chronic, influencing projected trial outcomes and treatment needs.

Spontaneous clearance of primary infections varies with the characteristics of the infected person. Pooled estimates of spontaneous clearance were 27.9% by 6 months and 37.1% by 24 months after infection. 7 Factors associated with clearance of primary infection include age, sex, HCV genotype, and variants in the host IFNL4 region, historically described as variants near interleukin 28B (IL28B, now IFNL3), a separate neighboring gene. [7] [8] [9] Younger adults and people of female sex are more likely to clear a first infection. 7 Variants in IFNL4 can affect interferonmediated antiviral responses, 10 and their frequencies differ across racial and ethnic populations. [11] [12] [13] [14] Representing IFNL4 genotype allows relevant population variation to be included without treating race or ethnicity itself as a biological cause of clearance. In particular, the CC genotype of rs12979860 is associated with a higher probability of spontaneous clearance than CT or TT. 8, 15 Additionally, people who spontaneously clear an initial HCV infection may clear a later infection more readily and rapidly, with reported clearance proportions of approximately 50%-83% across studies of reinfection. [16] [17] [18] Evidence for factors associated with clearance of reinfection is more limited. In the InC 3 study, consistent with characteristics associated with clearance of primary infections, people of female sex with the CC genotype had a higher rate of spontaneous clearance of reinfection than other participants. 16 HCV reinfections following cure with direct-acting antivirals (DAAs) may also clear spontaneously, although evidence remains limited. One recent study documented spontaneous clearance in one of 11 reinfections after successful DAA treatment, but eight had unknown outcomes, limiting estimation of the clearance probability. 19 Therefore, an algorithm that separately accounts for primary infection, reinfection after spontaneous clearance, and reinfection after DAA cure can assign different probabilities to people with different clearancerelated characteristics. This variation in clearance across different subgroups is especially relevant to controlled human infection (CHI) models proposed for HCV vaccine development. In initial CHI studies, participants would receive a defined dose of virus and undergo scheduled follow-up to characterize the challenge virus and estimate the expected spontaneous clearance rate. 20 These studies would inform the timing of treatment and the design of subsequent CHI vaccine trials. 20 Differences in age, sex, or IFNL4 genotype between initial CHI cohorts and groups subsequently enrolled in CHI vaccine trials could make it difficult to distinguish vaccine-induced clearance from spontaneous clearance. The expected small numbers of people recruited per group for any CHI model would make it difficult to stratify across all combinations of these factors. Although previous modeling studies stratify by sex, 4 few quantify how outcomes vary across combinations of sex, IFNL4 genotype, and age (Table 1). Such estimates could inform CHI model study design, where small sample sizes limit the ability to assess these combinations directly. Increasing the realism of simulations can address this gap in the existing literature by examining how participant characteristics impact spontaneous clearance, which may have utility in determining the optimal length of follow-up for a CHI model prior to a vaccine trial. 5, 20 Our objective was to develop a modular, literature-informed algorithm of spontaneous HCV clearance for use in such simulations. We separately represented first infection, reinfection after spontaneous clearance, and reinfection after DAA cure. We incorporated age, sex, and IFNL4 rs12979860 genotype and represented uncertainty in published estimates across runs. We used the agent-based model HepCEP (Hepatitis C Elimination in PWID) as a testing environment to examine whether the algorithm reproduced the intended person-specific probabilities and runlevel variation. The present study tests the clearance calculation algorithm, and application to CHI model design is a proposed use of its modular structure.

2. Methods

>Methods

2.1. Algorithm overview and evidence sources

Our clearance algorithm uses a simulated person's demographic characteristics and infection and treatment history to assign a probability of spontaneous clearance. It then draws an individual outcome and, separately, a time to clearance or chronic infection (Sections 2.3-2.5). We tested the algorithm in HepCEP, an established ABM of HCV transmission among PWID. [4] [5] [6] HepCEP supplies the population and transmission setting for this evaluation.

The algorithm follows three pathways: first infection, reinfection after spontaneous clearance, and reinfection after DAA cure (Figure 1). An agent with no previous HCV infection enters the first-infection pathway that uses age, sex, and IFNL4 rs12979860 genotype to predict clearance. For reinfection, the pathway depends on whether the most recent infection cleared spontaneously or was cured with DAAs. This separation allows spontaneous clearance and treatment-induced cure to be associated with different subsequent clearance probabilities. Reinfections after spontaneous clearance use age and combined sex-genotype groups to predict clearance, 16 while reinfections after DAA cure use a fixed probability of 0.25 (Section We used published estimates to describe clearance probabilities in our simulations, prioritizing systematic reviews and meta-analyses. For reinfection, we used the InC 3 analysis, which combined data from several cohorts (Table 1). 16 Estimates were selected for their relevance to spontaneous clearance among PWID, their compatibility with age, sex, genotype, and infection history, and the availability of uncertainty bounds. Where joint individual-level evidence was unavailable, we combined estimates from different studies and stated the assumptions involved. 2.2. IFNL4 genotype assignment

We consider the C and T alleles of rs12979860 at the IFNL4 locus (GRCh38, NC_000019.10:g.39248147C>T), where the CC (C/C) genotype is associated with a higher spontaneous clearance rate than the CT (C/T) or TT (T/T) genotypes. 8 Our algorithm assigns each agent a CC or non-CC (CT/TT) genotype group at initialization. Individual genetic data were unavailable for the HepCEP test population, so assignment used probabilities for the agent's race and/or ethnicity within HepCEP's pre-existing categories: Hispanic, non-Hispanic Black, non-Hispanic White, or Other. 6 The assigned CC probabilities were 0.40, 0.16, 0.47, and 0.86, respectively (Table 1). 21, 22 These are observed rs12979860 CC genotype frequencies in gnomAD v4.1.1 genomes for admixed American, African/African American, and non-Finnish European populations. The value for the heterogeneous "Other" category used an East Asian population estimate. We chose the East Asian estimate because its C-allele frequency of 0.93 is the highest among gnomAD genetic ancestry groups. Together with the African/African American group, which has the lowest frequency (0.40), the four mapped groups reflect the full range of allele-frequency diversity across populations. Race and ethnicity only inform genotype assignment and do not enter the clearance equation as separate terms.

2.3. Clearance after first infection

For a first infection, the probability of spontaneous clearance depends on age group, sex, and CC genotype (Table 1). The female-to-male effect is reported as a risk ratio, which cannot be used in a logistic equation as an odds ratio. We therefore solve for the sex coefficient together with the intercept so that the population mean and the female-to-male risk ratio match their published estimates (Table 1). 9, 23 Equation 1 combines these terms with the age and genotype effects.

Equation 1. Probability of spontaneous clearance after first infection

Each indicator I(•) equals 1 when its condition is true and 0 otherwise. At the literature point estimates, the age coefficient is βage=log(0.79), from the odds ratio for age ≥45 versus <45 years, 7 and the genotype coefficient is βCC=log(2.75), from the odds ratio for CC versus CT/TT. 8 The intercept α is chosen to match an overall clearance probability of 0.26, 23 and βfemale is chosen to match a standardized female-to-male risk ratio of 1.61. 9 Each run uses sampled values of these estimates (Section 2.6 and Table 1). Standard bisection searches, which repeatedly halve an interval containing the solution, 24 determine the two unknown coefficients. Implementation details are in Supplementary Methods S1.

Published estimates carry uncertainty, so we allow model inputs to vary between simulation runs. Therefore, the same person can have a higher clearance probability in one simulation run than in another. However, within an individual run, two people with the same age, sex, and CC genotype receive the same clearance probability. In an individual run, a separate random draw determines each person's outcome, so one person may still clear while the other develops persistent infection (Section 2.6).

2.4. Clearance after reinfection

>Clearance after reinfection

2.4.1. Reinfection after spontaneous clearance

For reinfection after spontaneous clearance, the algorithm matches an average clearance probability of 0.52 by 180 days, based on the InC 3 Study. 16 We used InC 3 as the sole source of reinfection-clearance estimates because it pooled individual-level data from nine prospective cohorts and studied predominantly people who inject drugs, making its population relevant to application in HepCEP. It also provided both six-month cumulative clearance after reinfection estimates and reinfection-specific hazard ratios by age, sex, and CC genotype, including a combined sex-genotype contrast (Table 1). 16 Equation 2 converts these into subgroup probabilities using a proportional-hazards approximation.

Equation 2. Probability of clearance after reinfection following spontaneous clearance

Here, H0 is a baseline cumulative hazard over 180 days and HRi is the hazard-ratio combination for an agent. Sacks-Davis et al. reported separate comparisons adjusted for cohort site: age >25 vs. ≤25 years (HR: 1.72), female vs. male (HR: 2.45), CC vs. CT/TT genotype (HR: 2.00), and female CC versus all other participants (HR: 4.16). 16 These were not jointly estimated subgroup effects, so as a modeling approximation, we assign multipliers of 1.00, 2.00, 2.45, and 4.16 to male non-CC, male CC, female non-CC, and female CC agents, respectively. We then multiply each sex-genotype group's value by 1.72 for agents aged >25 years (Table 1).

The two age groups, two sex categories, and two IFNL4 genotype groups produce eight combinations. We weight these combinations by their summed first-infection clearance probabilities, so the weights approximate the composition of people who have cleared a first infection and could enter this pathway. We choose H0 so that the weighted mean probability equals the sampled overall reinfection-clearance estimate. The bisection calculation is given in Supplementary Methods S2.

2.4.2. Reinfection after DAA cure

Age, sex, and IFNL4 genotype affect clearance of earlier infections modeled during simulation and can therefore influence which agents subsequently receive DAA treatment. However, chronic infections present at initialization are assigned using population data as opposed to the clearance algorithm. These infections accounted for an average of 91.2% of first DAA treatments across the 100 simulation runs. Age, sex, and genotype do not modify the post-DAA reinfection clearance probability because available studies have few reinfections, infrequent testing, or unknown outcomes, limiting the clearance estimates that can be transferred to the algorithm. 19, 25 Therefore, we use a fixed probability of 0.25 by one year from a pooled analysis of nine prospective cohorts of primary acute infection (Table 1). 15 This assumption can be revised as evidence accumulates (Section 4.2).

2.5. Time to clearance or chronic infection

The algorithm first draws whether an infection clears and then draws the time to that outcome. Table 1 summarizes the timing inputs for each pathway.

2.5.1. First infection

Each agent's probability of spontaneous clearance after first infection is determined by Equation 1. For infections selected to clear, we then assign a clearance time. The published cumulative clearance estimates are 19.8%, 27.9%, 36.1%, and 37.1% by 3, 6, 12, and 24 months, respectively. 7 To describe timing of HCV clearance, we divide each estimate by the 24month estimate of 37.1%. Thus, among infections selected to clear, 53.4% clear by 3 months (19.8/37.1), 75.2% by 6 months (27.9/37.1), 97.3% by 12 months (36.1/37.1), and 100% by 24 months (37.1/37.1). Just over half therefore clear within three months, and only approximately 2.7% clear between 12 and 24 months. The algorithm draws each clearance time by linearly interpolating between these cumulative values.

For first infections that do not clear spontaneously, the algorithm draws a time to transition to the chronic-infection state from an exponential distribution with a mean of 102 days, using the established HepCEP timing rule (Table 1). 4, 5 This mean applies only to infections pre-selected to not clear.

2.5.2. Reinfection after spontaneous clearance

The InC 3 study reported a median clearance time of 3.0 months with an interquartile range of 2.0-4.4 months for individuals reinfected after spontaneous clearance. 16 We use these data to specify a log-normal distribution with an underlying median of 90 days and a log-scale standard deviation of 0.584, then restrict draws to ≤180 days (Table 1). The restriction is a modeling choice and changes the distribution of accepted times. Reinfections that do not clear are classified as chronic at 180 days.

2.5.3. Reinfection after DAA cure

For reinfection after DAA cure, we use Grebely et al.'s primary-infection data to model both the probability of clearance and its timing (Section 2.4.2). 15 Among participants who cleared during follow-up, 34%, 67%, and 83% had cleared by 3, 6, and 12 months. 15 We restrict this timing distribution to clearance within one year and similarly divide each percentage by 83%. Among post-DAA reinfections selected to clear, the resulting cumulative percentages are therefore 41.0% by 3 months (34/83), 80.7% by 6 months (67/83), and 100% by 12 months (83/83) (Table 1). Times are drawn by linear interpolation between these cumulative values. Post-DAA reinfections that do not clear use an exponential time to chronic infection with a mean of 102 days. 4, 5

2.6. Parameter uncertainty

In the first infection and reinfection after spontaneous clearance pathways, published estimates have uncertainty. At the start of a run, the algorithm draws one value for each uncertain input using the point estimate and its reported 95% confidence interval (Table 1). Overall probabilities are sampled from normal distributions on the logit scale, while risk ratios, odds ratios, and hazard ratios are sampled from normal distributions on the log scale. We then transform the draws back to the original scales, and the coefficients and baseline hazard are chosen to match that run's sampled inputs. All agents share these inputs within the run, and a new run receives a new set. For example, one run may use a higher overall clearance estimate than another, while agents within either run still have different chances according to their characteristics. This variation across runs represents uncertainty in the published evidence. The separate random draw for each infection represents whether that individual infection clears. Literature inputs are sampled independently because their joint uncertainty was not reported. The post-DAA probability and timing parameters are fixed, so uncertainty is not applied in this manner.

2.7. Testing the clearance algorithm

We evaluated the algorithm in a fixed reference cohort (Figure 2) and in full HepCEP transmission simulations. The reference cohort contained 8,000 agents. Calculations at literature point estimates and across 10,000 parameter draws isolated subgroup probabilities and uncertainty without running transmission.

For the full simulations, we ran 100 matched-seed pairs (seeds 42-141). Each pair contained one run using our clearance algorithm and one using a fixed-probability benchmark drawn from established HepCEP settings. [4] [5] [6] This benchmark provides a reference for the numerical evaluation. Both runs used an 800-day horizon, an initial population of 32,000 agents, and the same demographic, network, treatment, burn-in, and other non-clearance settings wherever applicable. Every algorithm run sampled one literature-parameter set. Assigned probabilities and outcomes were recorded at the clearance-decision point without an additional random draw. For infections acute at initialization, clearance decisions are recorded immediately by the algorithm and at acute resolution by the benchmark. The benchmark values are reported with the results in Section 3.3.

Within each run, pathway, and subgroup, probabilities were placed in 30 common bins from 0 to 1 and normalized to sum to one. Agents were grouped by sex and genotype while pooling ages, or by sex and age while pooling genotypes. Bin masses were averaged across the 100 runs, giving each run equal weight. We also calculated the number of recorded clearance decisions and the mean assigned probability within each run, then summarized their means and standard deviations across runs. These summaries include all recorded decisions.

3. Results

>Results

3.1. First-infection clearance probabilities

At the literature point estimates, first-infection clearance probabilities ranged from 0.505 for female CC agents aged <45 years to 0.127 for male non-CC agents aged ≥45 years (Figure 2a). Among female agents aged <45 years, the clearance probability was 0.271 without CC genotypes and 0.505 with CC genotypes. The corresponding male clearance probabilities were 0.155 and 0.336. For age ≥45 years, these values were 0.227 and 0.446 in female agents and 0.127 and 0.285 in male agents, respectively. The central 95% interval was 0.436-0.578 for female CC agents aged <45 years and 0.099-0.161 for male non-CC agents aged ≥45 years (Figure 2a). Although some intervals overlapped, point estimates were consistently higher with CC genotype and female sex and lower in the older age group.

3.2. Reinfection clearance probabilities

After spontaneous clearance, reinfection probabilities covered a wider range (Figure 2b). For agents aged ≤25 years, point-estimate probabilities were 0.201 for male non-CC, 0.362 for male CC, 0.424 for female non-CC, and 0.608 for female CC agents. The age >25 hazard ratio increased these to 0.321, 0.539, 0.612, and 0.800, respectively. Parameter-uncertainty intervals were narrower for first infection than for reinfection after spontaneous clearance. Central 95% intervals extended from 0.050-0.577 in male non-CC agents aged ≤25 years to 0.349-0.998 in female CC agents aged >25 years.

The post-DAA pathway assigned a reinfection clearance probability of 0.25 to every subgroup. No uncertainty interval is shown because this input is fixed.

3.3. Testing in full transmission simulations

The fixed-probability benchmark assigned first-infection clearance probabilities of 0.121 for male agents and 0.346 for female agents (Figure 3a) and a clearance probability of 0.85 for reinfection after spontaneous clearance (Figure 3d). 4, 5 Our benchmark implementation fixes spontaneous clearance following reinfection after DAA cure to zero, differing from the posttreatment behavior described in the published HepCEP model. 6 At literature point estimates, differences between algorithm first-infection probabilities and these reference values ranged from -11.9 percentage points for female non-CC agents aged ≥45 years to +21.5 percentage points for male CC agents aged <45 years. All eight reinfection point estimates were below the benchmark value of 0.85, reflecting the literature mean of 0.52 used by the algorithm.

All 200 simulations in the 100 matched-seed pairs completed. 4. Discussion

>Discussion

4.1. Principal findings and applications

We developed an algorithm of spontaneous HCV clearance that accounts for age, sex, IFNL4 rs12979860 genotype, and three infection histories. Within each run, the algorithm matches the overall probabilities from published estimates, and agents with varying characteristics have different chances of clearance. Testing in 100 HepCEP runs (paired with 100 fixed-probability benchmark simulations) reproduced the intended differences between people and between runs. An infection selected to clear may still be treated, or follow-up may end, before its scheduled clearance time. Among first infections selected to clear spontaneously, 53.4% are scheduled to clear within three months and 75.2% within six months, but only 2.7% are scheduled to clear between 12 and 24 months. For reinfection after DAA cure, 80.7% of infections selected to clear are scheduled to do so within six months and the remaining 19.3% between six and 12 months. Under our first-infection timing distribution, approximately 24.8% of infections selected to clear spontaneously would not yet have cleared at six months. We recently simulated 9 months of follow-up in HCV vaccine efficacy trials to determine whether HCV infection became chronic or cleared spontaneously. 5 Further simulations are needed to determine whether shorter follow-up, with DAA treatment beginning before 9 months after infection, would allow this distinction in CHI models. Earlier treatment could prevent observation of later spontaneous clearance, potentially affecting estimates of vaccine efficacy.

First-infection probabilities varied almost fourfold, from 0.127 to 0.505 across the eight subgroups. This variation supports accounting for age, sex, and IFNL4 genotype when assigning clearance probabilities in individual-based simulations. For reinfection after spontaneous clearance, the InC 3 estimates describe clearance rates over 180 days and include a combined female-CC genotype effect. 16 Equation 2 retains these features and yields probabilities that can be used in individual-based simulations. Male, non-CC agents had low probabilities of clearing a first infection, and if reinfected after spontaneous clearance, they also had low probabilities of clearing again (Figure 2). Male agents aged ≤25 years had lower reinfection-clearance point estimates than those aged >25 years with the same genotype (Figure 2b), reflecting the age hazard ratio used in Equation 2. Its wide confidence interval and the wider reinfection intervals reflect the uncertainty of these estimates.

Realistic clearance probabilities could support CHI model simulations. For a study with one controlled exposure in HCV-naïve participants, the first-infection calculation could use participants' measured genotypes and recruitment characteristics. Small CHI cohorts might preferentially recruit participants with the favorable IFNL4 CC genotype to reduce the risk of persistent infection. This would increase expected spontaneous clearance and could make comparisons with later cohorts misleading if their genotype composition differs. Varying participant composition and clearance assumptions could help assess sample size and interpret clearance at the planned endpoint. 20 These applications remain to be tested.

A next step is to assess how much spontaneous clearance would occur before treatment. Combining our first-infection probabilities and timing distribution gives estimated six-month clearance probabilities of 9.5%-38.0% across the eight host subgroups at the literature point estimates, assuming no treatment before six months. The current model uses the same timing distribution among first infections selected to clear, and estimating subgroup-specific clearance times would require additional data. These estimates could inform the follow-up period before treatment in initial CHI studies. Earlier DAA treatment would shorten the observation period and prevent later spontaneous clearance from being observed. 20 Future CHI model simulations could use more detailed timing data to estimate how many clearances would be missed and how earlier treatment might affect DAA duration and costs.

Notably, some of our timing parameters were also derived from chimpanzee studies and may not generalize to humans. 4 Viral-kinetic profiles based on human data include clearance courses extending beyond six months after primary infection and reinfection. 26 Our algorithm could also be coupled to HepCEP implementations that represent viral kinetics. In a recent HepCEP study, Tatara et al. (2026) incorporated individual viral-load trajectories during acute infection, reinfection, and DAA treatment, with transmission probabilities depending on viral load. 26 Our algorithm could supply the probability of entering a clearing or persistent course, while a viral-kinetics module supplies the trajectory. Such coupling would require profile selection and timing consistent with the assigned outcome. This approach has not been tested here, but it could connect literature-informed clearance heterogeneity to simulations that also represent the changing risk of HCV transmission for an infected individual.

4.2. Limitations

Published estimates were combined across studies with different populations, acute-infection definitions, follow-up schedules, and levels of adjustment. Matching the reported averages aligns the algorithm's clearance probabilities with the literature estimates, but it does not fully account for confounding or other differences between populations. Estimates largely derived from people who inject drugs may not transfer directly to healthy volunteers in CHI models. In the absence of empirical HCV CHI clearance data, this work uses estimates from naturally acquired infection to inform the timing of DAA treatment initiation in future human CHI studies.

The present algorithm assumes HCV genotype 1. This is the most prevalent viral genotype in the United States, 27 and much of the IFNL4 evidence used here concerns genotype 1. 8, [11] [12] [13] [14] Application to other viral genotypes requires appropriate estimates and validation. In particular, the host-genotype effect should not be assumed to capture differences between the viruses used in CHI model studies.

Age is represented by two categories, with different cut points for first infection and reinfection, following the published estimates. IFNL4 genotype assignment in the HepCEP test population uses HepCEP's broad race and ethnicity categories that do not capture all allele-frequency variation. The "Other" category is especially uncertain, and gnomAD v4.1.1 genetic ancestry groups do not correspond exactly to HepCEP's race and ethnicity categories. Measured genotypes would avoid these assignment assumptions.

Literature inputs are sampled independently because joint uncertainty was unavailable. The reinfection algorithm combines separately estimated contrasts into joint subgroup multipliers, although these joint effects were not estimated in the original study. 16 Timing parameters are fixed, and the first-infection timing curve is approximated by rescaling pooled cumulative estimates from a different source than the overall clearance estimate. Truncating the reinfection timing distribution at 180 days also changes its median and quartiles. The post-DAA probability and timing use primary-infection evidence as opposed to evidence specific to reinfection after DAA cure. Finally, we did not evaluate CHI model sample size, vaccine efficacy estimates, elimination targets, or treatment costs.

5. Conclusion

Our literature-informed algorithm represents spontaneous HCV clearance by age, sex, IFNL4 rs12979860 genotype, and infection history. It also accounts for uncertainty in published parameter estimates and variation in whether an individual infection clears. Testing in HepCEP reproduced the intended subgroup and run-level variation and preserved literature-derived population means. Its modular structure allows reinfection pathways to be disabled for future single-exposure CHI model simulations and supports refinement of the post-DAA assumptions.

External validation and study-specific scenarios are needed before using it to guide CHI model design or other intervention decisions.
