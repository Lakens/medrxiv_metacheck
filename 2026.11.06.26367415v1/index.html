Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

p = .502 BSc Midwifery students demonstrated significantly higher knowledge scores than BSc Nursing students (U = 2868.0, p = .002), though the corresponding difference in attitude scores did not reach statistical significance (p = .502).

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Introduction

Infertility, defined clinically as the failure to achieve a pregnancy after 12 months or more of regular unprotected sexual intercourse, is recognized by the World Health Organization (WHO) as a disease of the reproductive system [1]. Globally, infertility affects one in six individuals, with this prevalence strikingly consistent across high-, middle-, and low-income countries (around 16-18%), underscoring that infertility is a universal public health concern [1]. The burden of infertility is particularly acute in sub-Saharan Africa, including Ghana, due to strong pronatalist cultural values. In many African societies, childbearing is not only deeply desired but also socially expected as a fulfilment of marriage and a source of support and status [2, 3].

Consequently, couples who cannot have children may face intense societal pressure and stigma.

In Ghana, epidemiological estimates suggest that roughly 11-12% of women and 15-16% of men of reproductive age experience infertility [4]. Notably, male-factor infertility is a contributing cause in a substantial proportion of cases; however, men are often excluded from the infertility discourse in traditional settings, with blame and social repercussions disproportionately falling on women [3, 5]. This gendered attribution means Ghanaian women suffering infertility can be stigmatized as "barren" and may endure various forms of discrimination and abuse.

Within the socio-cultural context of Ghana, the consequences of infertility for women are severe.

Qualitative studies have documented that women struggling to conceive often experience psychological trauma and social ostracism. For example, a recent study in urban Ghana found that infertile women frequently reported loss of self-esteem, marital instability, social isolation, and instances of physical and emotional abuse as a result of their condition [6]. Such women may be pressured by spouses or in-laws, sometimes leading to polygamy or divorce as the husband seeks children with another partner [6, 7]. Infertility is thus not only a medical issue but also a profound social challenge in Ghana. Many affected couples turn to informal or traditional solutions before seeking biomedical care. Studies have noted that some Ghanaians attribute infertility to spiritual causes or past transgressions, and herbal remedies and spiritual interventions are commonly sought [5]. These beliefs can delay timely medical consultation and contribute to misconceptions about infertility and its treatments.

Healthcare professionals, especially nurses and midwives, have a crucial role in addressing infertility and mitigating its impacts. In Ghana's healthcare system, nurses and midwives are often the first point of contact for women's health issues, including family planning and antenatal care. However, there appears to be a gap in both research and education regarding infertility in the nursing/midwifery curriculum. Globally, reproductive health training for nurses tends to focus on maternal health and contraception, often giving limited attention to infertility and subfertility care [8]. In Ghana, issues of infertility have historically been under-prioritized in public health initiatives compared to problems like high fertility and maternal mortality [9]. As a result, future nurses/midwives may be under-prepared to handle infertility-related cases or to educate the community.

Their position in communities and clinics means nurses and midwives are well-placed to provide education, counselling, and support to couples with fertility problems. Competent, empathetic nursing care can help dispel myths, encourage early referral to specialists, and provide muchneeded psychosocial support to infertile individuals [10]. Conversely, if nurses and midwives themselves lack adequate knowledge or hold prejudiced attitudes about infertility, they may unknowingly reinforce stigma or fail to offer appropriate guidance. Furthermore, if future nurses and midwives hold misconceptions or negative biases, for example believing infertility is always the woman's "fault" or that assisted reproductive technologies are morally unacceptable, such attitudes could adversely affect the quality of care they provide to individuals and couples facing infertility. It is therefore vital that nursing and midwifery education prepares students with sound knowledge of infertility, its causes, prevention, and treatments, and fosters positive, nonjudgmental attitudes toward those affected. Addressing this problem is essential for improving reproductive healthcare services and reducing infertility-related stigma.

The problem that guided this study is the unknown level of knowledge and the nature of attitudes toward infertility among nursing and midwifery students in Ghana. Despite being key stakeholders in reproductive healthcare delivery, student nurses and midwives may lack the knowledge and empathetic orientation necessary to support infertile individuals, and there is insufficient empirical evidence on their preparedness in this regard. A preliminary review of the literature reveals a paucity of empirical research concerning Ghanaian nursing and midwifery students' understanding of infertility. Most existing studies in the region have centered on either general population awareness [11] or the experiences of infertile women [5, 6, 12], rather than on the competencies of healthcare trainees. Without knowing the knowledge gaps and attitudes of student nurses and midwives, educational institutions cannot effectively tailor their curricula or interventions to address those gaps. This research is significant because it addresses this gap in the literature by providing empirical data on the preparedness of nursing and midwifery students to deal with infertility, and the findings can inform educators and policymakers about critical content areas that may need strengthening in the nursing and midwifery curricula.

The aim of this study was therefore to assess the knowledge and attitudes regarding infertility among BSc final-year (Level 400) nursing and midwifery students in four (4) tertiary institutions in Accra, Ghana. The specific objectives of the research were to: (i) assess the level of knowledge about infertility among final-year nursing and midwifery students; (ii) determine the attitudes of final-year nursing and midwifery students toward infertility; (iii) determine how demographic factors relate to knowledge and attitude; and (iv) ascertain the association between students' knowledge level and their attitudes. This study was anchored in the Knowledge-Attitude-Practice (KAP) model, a theoretical framework frequently used in public health and health education research. The KAP model posits a sequential and interrelated progression: individuals first acquire knowledge about a topic, which shapes their attitudes or beliefs, and in turn influences their practices or behaviours; improved knowledge is theorized to lead to more positive attitudes and ultimately to better practices [13]. The model has been widely applied through KAP surveys, structured questionnaires designed to quantitatively assess what respondents know, how they feel, and how they behave with respect to a specific issue [14]. An advantage of the KAP model is that it provides a systematic approach for identifying knowledge gaps, misconceptions, and attitudinal barriers that may hinder effective practice [15]. In the context of infertility, a KAP-based study can reveal whether nursing and midwifery students know key facts, such as the definition of infertility or its common causes, whether they hold biases or stigma toward infertile people, and whether they intend to apply best practices such as making appropriate referrals or offering counselling in the future.

The KAP framework assumes a rational linkage between knowledge gain and behaviour change, a notion that has been both utilized and critiqued in health education literature. While increased knowledge alone does not always guarantee attitude change or improved practice, it is generally a prerequisite for informed action. In this study, the KAP model guided the development of the survey instrument and the interpretation of results. Knowledge items measured the cognitive domain, whereas attitude items gauged the affective domain. Although practice in the strict sense could not be fully assessed in a student population not yet independently practicing, the survey included items on intended practices and self-reported behaviours. By using the KAP model, the study could not only describe knowledge and attitudes but also explored the relationship between these components, for instance whether students with higher knowledge scores also exhibited more positive attitudes toward infertility, aligning with similar studies that have employed this model to investigate health professionals' perspectives on reproductive health topics [16, 17].

Materials and methods

Study design

This research employed a descriptive cross-sectional survey design. A cross-sectional approach was appropriate because the aim was to capture a "snapshot" of the knowledge and attitudes of nursing and midwifery students at a specific point in time.

Population and sampling

The research was conducted in Greater Accra, Ghana, at four universities offering BSc nursing and midwifery programmes These institutions were selected to represent public and private universities that train nurses and midwives, thereby providing a diverse student sample within the region. The survey was administered after obtaining permission from each institution's administration. By covering multiple institutions, the study design enhanced the generalizability of results to similar educational contexts in Ghana, rather than being confined to a single school's culture or curriculum. Accra, as Ghana's national capital, hosts a high concentration of accredited nursing and midwifery institutions spanning public and private ownership, faith-based and secular affiliations, and both generalist and specialist healthcare orientations, providing a diverse and meaningful sample frame even without national geographic dispersion.

The target population comprised all final-year (Level 400) nursing and midwifery students enrolled at the four selected universities. This cohort was targeted because they had completed most of their nursing/midwifery coursework and clinical rotations, including any content on reproductive health, and were therefore expected to have the highest level of knowledge and more formed attitudes, with their views indicative of what they would carry into the workforce imminently.

Using a stratified approach where each institution serving as a distinct stratum, all Level 400 nursing and midwifery students were eligible. A total of 263 students consented and participated.

Measures

Data were collected using a structured self-administered questionnaire developed by the researchers based on the KAP framework and informed by the literature on infertility knowledge and attitudes [11, 16, 17]. The instrument comprised four sections. Section A (Demographics) gathered background information including age, sex, marital status, programme, year of study, and any personal or family experience with infertility. Section B (Knowledge of Infertility) used a mix of question formats to assess factual knowledge concerning definition and duration, prevalence, treatment, and prevention. Section C (Attitudes toward Infertility) measured attitudes using Likert-scale statements derived from previous attitude scales and tailored to the local context, with respondents indicating their level of agreement on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = Strongly Disagree,5 = Strongly Agree). Section D (Practices/Intentions) captured relevant behaviour or intended behaviour, including whether respondents had received formal teaching on infertility, whether they had encountered infertility cases during clinical rotations, their likelihood of participating in future continuing education on fertility, and their perceived role in infertility prevention.

Content validity of the instrument was established through expert review. The draft questionnaire was given to three experts: a senior nursing lecturer knowledgeable in reproductive health, a gynaecologist, and a public health expert, who reviewed the items for relevance, clarity, and cultural appropriateness. Their feedback led to modifications such as rewording ambiguous questions and adding items, including an item on the emotional impact of infertility to reflect empathy. A pre-test (pilot) was conducted with nine (9) nursing students from a different university, not among the four (4) study sites, to check for any difficulties in understanding the questions; pilot respondents completed the survey and were interviewed about any confusing items, and minor adjustments were made accordingly. To ensure reliability, particularly of the attitude scale, Cronbach's alpha for the attitude items from the pilot data was calculated to be .739 and the main study was .850.

Data collection procedure

After ethical clearance and administrative approvals, the researchers coordinated with faculty and class representatives at each university. Primary data were collected electronically using a self-administered online survey hosted on Google Forms. To reach the entire population of Level 400 nursing and midwifery students efficiently across the four (4) institutions, the survey URL was disseminated through official class communication platforms, specifically class WhatsApp groups, with the help of the respective class representatives. The landing page of the Google Form contained a mandatory digital informed consent form outlining the study's purpose, anonymity clauses, and voluntary nature. Participants could only access the questionnaire sections after explicitly consenting to participate. To enforce data integrity and prevent duplicate entries, the platform configuration was restricted to one response per user via Google's authentication control. Data collection remained open for a period between Monday 3 rd November and Wednesday 31 st December 2025, during which periodic follow-up reminders were posted to maximize the response rate.

Data analysis

Data analysis was performed using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS), version 26. Both descriptive and inferential statistics were utilized. Frequencies and percentages were computed for categorical variables, and means with standard deviations for continuous variables.

Individual knowledge items were analysed for the percentage of students answering correctly. A composite knowledge score was computed by assigning 1 point for each correct response and 0 for incorrect or "not sure" responses across all knowledge items; for multiple-response items (B3, B4, B8), 1 point was awarded per scientifically valid option selected, and 1 point deducted for clearly inaccurate responses such as spiritual causes, with "Other, specify" responses reviewed and scored for scientific validity. The maximum total knowledge score was 23, and respondents were categorized into Poor (0-7), Moderate (8-15), and Good (16-23) knowledge levels, following similar scoring models used in reproductive health KAP studies (Mabitsela et al., 2025b). For attitude items, the distribution of agreement responses was examined for each statement, and overall attitude was summarized by the mean item score, ranging from 1 to 5. Bivariate analyses were conducted to compare knowledge and attitude by key demographic factors. The relationship between knowledge and attitudes was assessed using Pearson's correlation coefficient. Chi-square tests were applied to relevant categorical cross-tabulations, and where multiple factors appeared to influence knowledge or attitude, multiple regression analysis was used. All statistical tests were conducted at a 95% confidence level, with a p-value of < .05 considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

This study was reviewed and approved by the Dodowa Health Research Centre Institutional Review Board (DHRCIRB), which served as the primary ethics committee given the lead researchers' affiliation with that institution. Official permission letters were also obtained from the authorities of the four participating universities to allow data collection among their students.

Participation was entirely voluntary, and informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Students were informed that choosing not to participate would have no consequences on their academic standing, and only those who provided informed consent proceeded to fill out the questionnaire. Students could skip any question they were not comfortable with, or withdraw from the survey at any point before submission. Measures were taken to ensure that responses could not be traced back to an individual; the questionnaires did not collect names or student IDs, and all data were handled in aggregate form, with institution names coded rather than 245 explicitly named in results to avoid stigma or unwarranted comparisons. Given the topic's 246 sensitive nature, attention was given to students' psychological comfort, as some students might 247 have personal experiences with infertility in their family that could make certain questions 248 emotionally triggering; it was stated that students were free to leave any uncomfortable question 249 blank. A summary of the findings was shared with the participating institutions to benefit 250 curriculum development, without sensitive or identifying information. Every step of the study 251 was guided by the ethical principles of respect for persons, beneficence, and justice.

Discussion

Knowledge and attitudes toward infertility

This study examined the relationship between knowledge and attitudes toward infertility and reproductive health among final-year nursing and midwifery students in Accra, Ghana. The composite knowledge score (M = 18.25/23, 79.4%) indicates a high overall baseline of factual knowledge, with 85.6% of respondents classified in the "High" knowledge category. This finding diverges from the broader regional literature, in which infertility knowledge among tertiary students and even practicing health professionals has generally been reported as limited.

A research study [11] for instance, found that knowledge about infertility was quite limited among Ghanaian university students outside nursing and medical cohorts, and another study [17] found that over half of Turkish nursing students believed infertility "cannot be treated." The comparatively strong performance of the present sample may reflect the fact that 82.1% of respondents reported having covered infertility as a topic during their training, suggesting that exposure to reproductive health content within the nursing and midwifery curriculum, even where infertility receives only minimal dedicated coverage, as the literature review notes, may be sufficient to produce adequate factual recall on core definitional and aetiological items.

However, the item-level analysis revealed two notable and clinically consequential gaps that the aggregate score conceals. Only 32.7% of respondents correctly identified that approximately half of infertility cases involve male-factor aetiology, and only 36.9% correctly identified the shortened six-month diagnostic threshold recommended for women over 35 years. The first gap is consistent with the broader pattern, documented extensively in the literature review, of malefactor infertility being underemphasized and under-discussed in both lay and clinical discourse in sub-Saharan Africa, with blame and social repercussions disproportionately falling on women [3, 5]. That nearly two-thirds of final-year nursing and midwifery students, who are on the cusp of independent practice, underestimate the male contribution to infertility suggests that this sociocultural bias may be reproduced rather than corrected within formal training, with direct implications for how future providers counsel couples and allocate diagnostic attention. The second gap, concerning the age-adjusted diagnostic window, reflects a more technical clinical knowledge deficit that has direct bearing on the timeliness of referral and management decisions for women approaching the end of their reproductive years.

Attitudes toward infertility were predominantly favourable (M = 3.812/5; 86.3% classified "Favourable"), with particularly strong endorsement of the professional and empathic role of nurses and midwives in supporting infertile couples, and correspondingly low endorsement of stigmatising beliefs such as infertility being a punishment or spiritual sign. This pattern echoes, and arguably exceeds, the moderately positive attitudes among Turkish nursing students [17]. It suggests that, despite the persistence of stigmatising sociocultural narratives in the wider Ghanaian context documented throughout the literature review [2, 9, 19], formal nursing and midwifery education in Accra may be functioning as a partial counterweight to those narratives, equipping students with a professionally oriented, biomedically grounded outlook even where some underlying knowledge gaps persist.

Association between knowledge and attitude

A statistically significant moderate positive correlation was found between composite knowledge and attitude scores (r = .400, p < .001), and knowledge score emerged as the sole significant predictor of attitude in the multiple regression model (B = 0.080, p < .001), after accounting for sex, programme, institution, family infertility experience, and prior infertility education coverage. This finding provides empirical support for the central tenet of the Knowledge-Attitude-Practice (KAP) model underpinning this study, namely that knowledge acquisition is theorized to shape attitudes, which in turn are expected to influence practice [13]. The fact that knowledge remained significant while none of the demographic or institutional variables did suggests that, within this population, cognitive mastery of the subject matter is a more proximal driver of favourable orientation than background characteristics or where a student trained. This is a reassuring finding for curriculum designers: it implies that closing specific knowledge gaps, such as those identified for male-factor aetiology and age-related diagnostic criteria, is a tractable, modifiable lever through which more favourable attitudes might be further reinforced, rather than attitudes being fixed by less malleable factors such as institutional culture or personal background.

At the same time, the regression model explained only 17.8% of the variance in attitude scores, indicating that the majority of variation in students' attitudes is attributable to factors not captured in this study, such as personality, prior exposure to infertile individuals beyond the binary "family experience" variable measured here, religiosity, or broader empathy disposition. This is consistent with the KAP literature's caution that the knowledge-to-attitude linkage, while real, is neither deterministic nor sufficient on its own [15].

Group differences by sex, programme, and institution

Female respondents demonstrated significantly higher knowledge (U = 2800.5, p = .044) and attitude scores (U = 2777.0, p = .040) than male respondents, although the effect sizes were small (r = .221 and .228, respectively) and the male subgroup was considerably smaller (n = 31) than the female subgroup (n = 232), warranting caution in generalizing this disparity. This finding is broadly consistent with earlier study which reported that male nursing students in Turkey displayed more negative attitudes and lower empathy toward infertile individuals than their female counterparts [17], and a very recent study [11] found Ghanaian female university students more likely to hold positive attitudes toward infertility issues than male students. Taken together with the present findings, this cross-cultural consistency suggests a durable pattern, rather than a context-specific artefact, and points to a need for targeted attitudinal and knowledge-reinforcement strategies aimed specifically at male nursing and midwifery trainees, who may otherwise carry comparatively under-formed competencies into a clinical workforce in which they will be expected to counsel both male and female patients on fertility matters.

BSc Midwifery students demonstrated significantly higher knowledge scores than BSc Nursing students (U = 2868.0, p = .002), though the corresponding difference in attitude scores did not reach statistical significance (p = .502). This pattern is plausible given that midwifery curricula in Ghana typically involve more concentrated and applied exposure to reproductive and maternal health content than general nursing curricula, which may translate into stronger factual recall on infertility-specific items without necessarily producing a parallel difference in general attitudinal orientation, since favourable attitudes toward infertile patients may be cultivated through broader professional socialisation common to both programmes rather than through programme-specific content alone.

There were no statistically significant differences in knowledge or attitude scores that were observed across the four participating institutions, despite their differing ownership structures (public versus private), religious affiliations, and institutional histories, as detailed in the methodology. This absence of institutional variation is a noteworthy finding in its own right: it suggests a degree of national consistency in how infertility-related content is delivered across Ghana's nursing and midwifery training landscape, which may reflect shared adherence to Nursing and Midwifery Council curricular standards across institutions of varying type. It also strengthens the generalisability of the present findings beyond any single institutional context.

Similarly, neither personal or familial infertility experience nor self-reported prior coverage of infertility in coursework was significantly associated with knowledge scores, suggesting that direct personal exposure to infertility, in the absence of structured educational content, does not by itself confer the technical knowledge gaps identified in this study; experiential familiarity and formal knowledge acquisition appear to operate through largely independent pathways.

Practice intentions and the demand for further training

Findings from the question on practice and behavioural intentions indicate that final-year students hold strong behavioural intentions consistent with their favourable attitudes: 86.7% of respondents indicated they would be likely or very likely to initiate conversations about fertility with clients in future antenatal or family planning settings, despite only 24.7% having encountered an actual infertility case during their clinical rotations to date. This gap between limited direct clinical exposure and strong intended engagement suggests that students' favourable attitudes and intentions are, at this stage, largely anticipatory rather than experiencetested, reinforcing the importance of structured curricular and clinical-placement interventions, rather than incidental clinical exposure alone, in preparing students for this aspect of practice.

The near-unanimous demand for expanded curriculum content (86.3%) and interest in continuing education on infertility management (90.5%) reinforces this picture and aligns closely with the qualitative themes that emerged from the open-ended responses, namely calls for curriculum enhancement, clinical training, non-judgmental care, community destigmatisation, and psychosocial support, all of which converge on a consistent message that students perceive their current preparation as a foundation requiring further reinforcement rather than as already sufficient.

Conclusion

This study found that final-year nursing and midwifery students across four universities in Accra, Ghana, possessed a generally high level of knowledge and held predominantly favourable attitudes toward infertility and the individuals affected by it. Nonetheless, specific and clinically meaningful knowledge gaps persisted regarding the proportion of infertility cases attributable to male-factor causes and the age-adjusted diagnostic criteria for women over 35 years, gaps that mirror long-documented patterns of gendered blame attribution in the wider Ghanaian sociocultural context. Knowledge and attitude were significantly and positively correlated, and knowledge score was the only significant predictor of attitude among the demographic and institutional variables examined, lending empirical support to the Knowledge-Attitude-Practice framework that guided this study. Female students and BSc Midwifery students demonstrated stronger performance on selected outcomes, while no significant differences emerged across the four participating institutions, suggesting a degree of national consistency in curricular delivery.

Students expressed strong demand for expanded infertility content and continuing professional education, alongside clear behavioural intentions to engage proactively with fertility-related conversations in future practice. These findings support targeted, content-specific curricular reinforcement, particularly around male-factor infertility and age-related diagnostic thresholds, and underscore the value of deliberately structured clinical exposure to infertility care, as concrete, evidence-based steps toward strengthening reproductive health education and reducing infertility-related stigma within Ghana's nursing and midwifery training pipeline.
