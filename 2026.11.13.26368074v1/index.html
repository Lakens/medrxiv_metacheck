Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Abstract

Despite strong evidence supporting low-dose aspirin (LDA) for preventing pre-eclampsia among women at risk, implementation remains suboptimal globally. The prevention pathway encompasses early antenatal care engagement, screening and risk identification, prescribing LDA, and medication uptake. This qualitative study explored barriers and facilitators to LDA adoption by providers (i.e., prescribing) and uptake among women in Nepal.

A qualitative study was conducted with pregnant women, healthcare providers, and maternal and child health stakeholders in Nepal. Data collection and analysis were guided by the Capability, Opportunity, Motivation, and Behavior (COM-B) model. Participants were purposively recruited from three tertiary hospitals and maternal health organizations. Semi-structured interviews explored experiences and perspectives related to antenatal care, such as pre-eclampsia risk screening, LDA prescribing, and uptake. Data were analyzed using rapid qualitative analysis and organized according to the COM-B components.

Seventy-three participants were interviewed, including 36 pregnant women, both at risk and not at risk for pre-eclampsia; 29 healthcare providers, including consultant/senior obstetricians, mid-level doctors, and nurses or midwives; and 8 maternal and child health stakeholders representing government, multilateral, non-governmental, professional, and academic institutions. LDA adoption and uptake were affected by interdependent gaps across the prevention pathway. Delayed antenatal care engagement and inconsistent screening limited timely identification of women at risk, while gaps in national guidance and standardized prescribing protocols contributed to variable provider prescribing practices. Among women prescribed LDA, clear counselling on its purpose and safety and trust in providers supported uptake.

LDA adoption and uptake depend on a functioning prevention pathway in which women engage with antenatal care early enough for timely risk assessment, those at risk are identified through screening, providers prescribe appropriately, and women are supported to use the medication. Strengthening this pathway requires coordinated attention to health-system and provider capacity as well as communication with women, rather than strategies focused narrowly on prescribing or medication use. These findings will inform the development of implementation strategies for Nepal and other lowresource settings seeking to integrate evidence-based preventive interventions into maternal healthcare systems.

Introduction

Hypertensive disorders during pregnancy (HDP) are a leading cause of maternal and fetal morbidity and mortality worldwide, with the greatest burden borne by low-and middle-income countries (LMICs) [1] [2]. Globally, HDP affect an estimated 10-22% of pregnant women, with prevalence rising over recent decades alongside increases in maternal age and the prevalence of pre-existing medical conditions [3]. Pre-eclampsia, one of the most severe forms of HDP, is responsible for approximately 46,000 maternal deaths and 500,000 fetal or newborn deaths annually and contributes substantially to adverse birth outcomes, including preterm birth, fetal growth restriction, stillbirth, and neonatal mortality [4, 5]. In LMICs, where access to timely diagnosis and high-quality obstetric care may be limited, the consequences of pre-eclampsia are particularly severe. In Nepal, pre-eclampsia accounts for 22% of maternal deaths, and is associated with 25% of preterm births and 38% of low-birth-weight infants [6] [7] [8]. Beyond its immediate effects on pregnancy outcomes, pre-eclampsia has important longterm implications for both mother and child. Women who develop pre-eclampsia face elevated lifetime risks of hypertension, kidney disease, metabolic disorders, cardiovascular disease and dementia, while infants born preterm or growth-restricted due to pre-eclampsia are at increased risk of noncommunicable diseases later in life [9, 10]. Given this substantial burden, identifying women at risk and the use of effective preventive interventions are critical, particularly in LMICs where limited capacity for disease management, combined with already constrained health systems, underscores the importance of prevention.

Extensive evidence demonstrates that low-dose aspirin (LDA) reduces the risk of pre-eclampsia among women at moderate-to high-risk, particularly when initiated by 12 weeks' gestation [11] [12] [13] [14] [15] [16]. In addition to its clinical benefits, LDA has been shown to significantly reduce healthcare costs related to pre-eclampsia [17] [18] [19]. Consequently, the World Health Organization (WHO) and numerous international guideline bodies recommend LDA for pre-eclampsia prevention in at-risk pregnancies [15, 20].

First-trimester screening for pre-eclampsia risk during antenatal care (ANC) provides an important opportunity to identify women who may benefit from preventive interventions, including LDA [21, 22]. While women may attend ANC and complete the recommended number of visits [23], delayed initiation of ANC after 12 weeks' gestation can prevent timely risk identification and initiation of LDA within the effective gestational window. Thus, despite strong evidence and global recommendations, the effectiveness of LDA depends on successful implementation across a prevention pathway comprising four behaviors: women's early engagement with ANC, healthcare provider screening and risk assessment, healthcare provider adoption of LDA, and women's uptake of LDA. Provider adoption refers to incorporating appropriate LDA prescribing into clinical practice for women at risk, while uptake refers to women initiating and using LDA. Breakdowns at any stage can limit its impact, with suboptimal implementation widely documented globally, particularly in LMICs [24] [25] [26] [27] [28] [29] [30] [31] [32].

In Nepal, LDA is included on the WHO's list of essential medicines [33] and is widely available [34], but it has not yet been incorporated into the National Safe Motherhood Program guidelines [35, 36] -the country's leading initiative to reduce maternal and fetal morbidity and mortality. As evidence supporting LDA has grown, representatives of the Nepal Ministry of Health and Food Safety (MOHFS) have expressed a need for evidence on the most effective strategies to support its adoption and uptake (personal communications, 2024-2026).

To address this gap, the objective of this study was to identify barriers and facilitators to LDA adoption and uptake across the pre-eclampsia prevention pathway in Nepal, from the perspectives of pregnant women, healthcare providers, and key maternal and child health (MCH) stakeholders. The findings provide context-specific insights that will inform the co-design of implementation strategies and lay the foundation for future implementation efforts in Nepal.

Materials and Methods

Theoretical Framework

This study was guided by the Capability, Opportunity, Motivation, and Behavior (COM-B) model, which proposes that behavior is dependent on capability, opportunity, and sufficiently strong motivation [37]. Capability refers to the physical or psychological capacity to perform a behavior; opportunity to the physical or social factors that enable or constrain behavior; and motivation to reflective or automatic processes that influence the behavior [37].

COM-B was selected for its simplicity and comprehensiveness, enabling consistent analysis of barriers and facilitators to LDA adoption and uptake across multiple actors and stages of the prevention pathway. Although originally developed to understand individual behavior, its conceptual parsimony allows application across multiple health system actors, making it well suited to examining complex implementation processes such as ANC delivery in low-resource settings. The framework informed both the development of interview guides and the analysis, organization, and presentation of findings.

Study team and reflexivity statement

This study was conducted through a partnership between researchers at Emory University (USA) and Kathmandu University School of Medical Sciences (Nepal) with expertise in maternal health, implementation science, and qualitative methods. Guided by a critical realist perspective, the study assumes that real-world mechanisms influence behavior, while participants' accounts provide partial, socially situated insights [38]. The research team included Nepali and non-Nepali researchers, with several team members bilingual in Nepali and English. Participants were informed of the researchers' institutional affiliations and roles in the study. Given LH's position as an external researcher, the team remained attentive to potential power differentials and the possibility that providers could perceive the research as evaluative. Reflexivity was supported through open-ended, neutral questioning and emphasis on understanding participants' experiences, as well as ongoing team discussions to examine positionality, power dynamics, and how researchers' assumptions and prior knowledge might shape emerging interpretations [39].

Interpretation of findings involved LH and Nepal-based team members (SU, AO, TR, PP), incorporating both non-Nepali and Nepali perspectives and contextual knowledge.

Study design, sampling, and recruitment

This study was reported in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) [40]. Purposive sampling was used to recruit pregnant women and healthcare providers from three tertiary hospitals in Nepal that were selected for high patient volume of pregnant women, advanced obstetric care, and variety in hospital type (e.g., community-based, government-run, and private).

Pregnant women ≥ 18 years of age and ≥ 20 weeks' gestation were recruited during ANC visits while awaiting their appointments. A screening form was used to assess eligibility, including age, gestational age, and pre-eclampsia risk status. Eligible women included both those with pre-eclampsia risk factors (as defined by WHO criteria [20]) and uncomplicated pregnancies to capture a range of experiences. Risk status was determined through self-report and ANC card review, with ANC cards prioritized in cases of discrepancy. Healthcare providers, including obstetricians, residents, nurses, and midwives involved in ANC and high-risk pregnancy management, were recruited via departmental heads

Data collection and analysis

Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection. Semistructured interview guides were developed for each participant group in English, translated into Nepali by a native Nepali-speaking team member, and back translated to ensure conceptual equivalence and preserve meaning across languages. Guides covered ANC experiences, pre-eclampsia risk screening, and prevention, including LDA, and were piloted with three participants and iteratively refined prior to formal data collection; pilot interviews were not included in the analysis.

In-depth interviews with pregnant women were conducted in private hospital spaces and lasted approximately 60 minutes. Key informant interviews with healthcare providers were also conducted in private hospital spaces and lasted approximately 30 minutes. Key informant interviews with MCH stakeholders were conducted either in person or virtually via Zoom, depending on participant preference and feasibility, and lasted approximately 45 minutes. Interviews were conducted in English or Nepali according to participant language preference. SU conducted all interviews with pregnant women in Nepali; LH conducted interviews with doctors and MCH stakeholders in English, while AO conducted interviews with nurses and midwives in Nepali. LH provided training in best practices for recruitment and qualitative data collection and analysis, including interviewing, probing, rapid qualitative analysis (RQA), and COM-B-guided data summarization and mapping, with ongoing supervision and support throughout the research process [39].

All interviews were audio recorded. Interviews were transcribed verbatim in the language in which they were conducted by Nepali team members and external contractors. Transcripts of interviews conducted in Nepali were subsequently translated into English for analysis. Weekly debrief sessions throughout data collection were used to practice reflexivity by providing opportunities for team members to reflect on interpretations and challenge assumptions regarding ANC experiences and preeclampsia prevention. These sessions also informed iterative refinement of the interview guides including the addition of probes to delve deeper into topics that arose in previous interviews, and identification of emergent themes [39, 41]. Concurrent analysis supported an iterative assessment of whether sufficient data had been collected to address the study objectives [41] [42] [43] [44] [45]. This assessment considered whether additional interviews were generating new substantive themes, indicating thematic saturation, as well as the breadth of perspectives represented across participant groups and hospital sites. Recruitment continued iteratively until no new substantive themes emerged and the research team judged that sufficient data had been collected to address the study objectives [41] [42] [43] [44] [45].

Data analysis followed a framework-guided RQA approach [46], to facilitate systematic and transparent data reduction and synthesis within a multidisciplinary team and to support iterative analysis during data collection. Analysis was led by LH, with input from ECR and AS, and conducted with Nepali team members (SU, AO, PP, and TR), who supported transcript summarization and mapping.

Weekly team discussions supported consistency and resolution of differences in interpretation, with LH reviewing and finalizing the summaries, mapping, and narrative synthesis. Structured Excel templates were used to summarize transcripts by interview question, with deductive coding guided by COM-B.

Analysts also applied inductive coding to capture emergent concepts not predefined by the framework, comparing these against COM-B components to assess fit. Illustrative quotations were extracted alongside summaries to preserve linkage to the raw data. The template was piloted with five transcripts and refined iteratively prior to use with the full dataset.

Next, barriers and facilitators were mapped by participant group and COM-B component from the completed summaries into an Excel matrix followed by narrative synthesis. All identified determinants could be meaningfully mapped to a COM-B component. Findings were organized along the pre-eclampsia prevention pathway, conceptualized as a sequential and interdependent process comprised of four behaviors: 1) women's early engagement with ANC, 2) healthcare provider screening and risk assessment, 3) healthcare provider adoption (i.e., prescribing) of LDA, to 4) women's uptake of LDA. Within each stage, determinants (i.e., barriers and facilitators) were analyzed and organized by COM-B components across patient-, provider-, and health-system levels.

Ethical considerations

Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection. Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Ethical Review Board of the Nepal Health Research Council (No. 676/2023) and the Emory University Institutional Review Board (No. 2025P013433). The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

At the patient-level within reflective motivation, many women reported low perceived risk and seriousness of pregnancy, which reduced the perceived need for ANC and information on conditions such as pre-eclampsia. This was often linked to absence of personally experiencing symptoms or prior experience, leading to a more reactive approach to health in which information was only sought if problems arose. Conversely, prior experiences with high blood pressure during pregnancy increased perceived risk and facilitated ANC engagement. Within automatic motivation, emotional responses to pregnancy risks, including fear, also shaped engagement with information and care. Concern about complications increased vigilance for some women. One woman described becoming very frightened after learning about pre-eclampsia online, stating, "these days it feels like I don't even want to hear such news, just hearing about it makes me very scared. It has kind of stayed in my mind." This distress prompted her to want her blood pressure checked. For other women, however, frightening information discouraged care-seeking, as concerns about potential complications or negative outcomes created stress and contributed to avoidance of ANC.

Participants proposed additional facilitators spanning psychological capability and social opportunity, including greater knowledge of pregnancy risks and the importance of early and continued ANC, and family, community, and peer support that normalized ANC and increased awareness of preeclampsia.

Delayed engagement was also noted to increase reliance on higher-cost screening and diagnostic care later in pregnancy, in addition to limiting the opportunity to initiate preventive strategies such as LDA within the effective gestational window.

Determinants of provider screening and risk identification during ANC encounters

Following ANC engagement, screening and risk identification during ANC encounters represented a critical but often incomplete step in the prevention pathway, as entry into care alone did not ensure that women eligible for LDA were identified within the relevant gestational window. Thus, screening and risk identification were inconsistent across and within facilities, reflecting the combined influence of patient-, provider-, and health-system level determinants across COM-B components.

Psychological capability determinants were evident at both providerand patient-levels. At the provider-level, knowledge of pre-eclampsia, including risk factors, consequences, screening approaches, and international guidelines was generally strong. However, most providers lacked awareness of Nepal's National Medical Standard for Maternal and Newborn Care (2022), which includes recommendations for blood pressure measurement and urine testing to identify pre-eclampsia risk. In addition, limited knowledge of correct blood pressure measurement techniques among frontline nurses, who are primarily responsible for routine measurement, reduced screening reliability, with inaccurate results often requiring re-check by doctors: "Sometimes the nursing student write whatever blood pressure.

So if we think that she [pregnant woman] is having some risk then we'll take blood pressure ourselves. And sometimes the nurse blood pressure is something and our blood pressure is different" [Doctor #6].

At the patient-level, understanding of pre-eclampsia and screening was constrained by communication barriers, including limited shared language, use of technical terminology, and inconsistent counselling. For example, several healthcare providers noted that the absence of a clear Nepali term for pre-eclampsia made it difficult to explain the condition to patients: "There is no proper word for pre-eclampsia. We have to explain in English because we haven't that proper term…so when I say that you might have pre-eclampsia, then they don't understand what is pre-eclampsia. Then we have to explain again and they are so much confused, and they just walk away" [Doctor #4].

Participants described information overload and exposure to conflicting externally sourced health information (e.g., from Google or ChatGPT) as determinants that could further reduce patients' ability to interpret and act on clinical guidance.

Physical opportunity was a key component across patient-and health-system levels. At the patient-level, asymptomatic presentation of pre-eclampsia, brief consultations, and limited opportunities for questions constrained opportunities for early detection. Participants identified facilitators including access to educational materials about pregnancy risks, adequate time for counselling and patient-family education, and opportunities for women to ask questions and communicate openly with providers.

At the health-system level, physical opportunity was shaped by workforce and service delivery constraints. Shortages and uneven distribution of skilled healthcare providers, staff transfers, and rural staffing gaps limited opportunities to screen, while high patient volumes, competing clinical responsibilities, and limited protected time reduced healthcare provider's opportunity to deliver consistent screening and counselling. As one healthcare provider described: "The doctors are burned out here.

Because for maybe 50, 60 patient there are only one doctor.

And so we are unable to take care of them, and we are unable to give them more time…maybe we need to just for one day there need be certain number of patient. And the hospital should like to say cut off [patients]. Yeah, after certain so that not only for doctors there it's benefit for the patient also, no? They'll find more time to talk with the doctor" [Doctor #6].

Participants identified facilitators including decentralized service delivery infrastructure that could enable community-based care and existing continuing medical education programs that could facilitate ongoing provider training. These health-system level workforce and service delivery determinants also shaped healthcare providers' ability to prescribe LDA for at-risk women.

Participants described how screening was further constrained by equipment and infrastructure limitations, including faulty blood pressure devices, limited availability of uterine artery Doppler machines, supply chain challenges, high diagnostic costs (for both health facility procurement and patients), and inadequate space for screening services. Fragmented service delivery, particularly in peripheral facilities, disrupted continuity between screening, risk identification, and follow-up. Although uterine artery Doppler machines were available at all three facilities, access was not always consistent due to factors such as limited appointment availability within radiology services, which often delayed screening scans to subsequent visits, which many women do not return for. One healthcare provider described this challenge: "Sometimes what happened is they are coming from far, so we advise them to do the scan, but the scan is already full.

So they cannot get an appointment in that particular period of time…that window period of 11 to 13 weeks is gone... So, if they are coming like eight weeks, nine weeks, then okay, they can take an appointment after two weeks and do the scan. But even in sometimes like 11 to 13 weeks they are coming, we do not have the appointment you know.

Already it's already full so they're unable to do on that particular time" [Doctor #5].

Conversely, participants identified existing ANC platforms, including routine blood pressure measurement, urine testing for albumin, history taking, and the use of ANC cards (paper records used to document maternal health information), as well as nationally implemented programs such as Skilled Birth Attendant training and government incentive programs (e.g., Aama Surakshya Programme), as opportunities that could be leveraged to support systematic screening.

Importantly, the absence of national prevention-specific clinical guidance for pre-eclampsia and standardized hospital screening protocols were key barriers contributing to variability in screening and risk identification across and within healthcare facilities.

Reflective motivation further shaped screening practices across all levels, with the strongest influence at the providerand health-system levels. At the provider-level, perceived blame and accountability for adverse outcomes following delayed care-seeking contributed to risk-averse practices, including earlier referral that could delay screening, while limited policy support and uncertainty regarding the diagnostic accuracy of uterine artery Doppler reduced confidence in screening approaches.

At the health-system level, low salaries, contract-based employment, and limited career opportunities, reduced healthcare provider motivation to engage in high-quality care and contributed to provider attrition from the public health system through migration abroad and transition to privatesector employment for better opportunities. One MCH stakeholder described how decentralized decision-making at the local level could result in government funding for nursing and midwifery positions being divided among multiple staff, reducing individual salaries and undermining motivation: "How they can be motivated?" MCH stakeholders and providers described improved remuneration and in-country career opportunities as facilitators to strengthen provider motivation, thereby improving retention and workforce stability.

In addition, participants highlighted that competing health priorities within the government could potentially limit attention and resources available for establishing systematic pre-eclampsia screening. However, pre-eclampsia was widely recognized by MCH stakeholders and healthcare providers as a major contributor to maternal morbidity and mortality and was viewed as a priority area for investment, including strengthening screening and LDA integration. Despite this recognition, concerns regarding the reliability of nurse-conducted obstetric scans limited confidence in task-shifting and expanding diagnostic roles within the health system.

Determinants of provider prescribing of LDA for at-risk pregnant women

Multiple determinants shaped prescribing behavior for LDA across providerand health-system levels of multiple COM-B components.

From a psychological capability perspective, knowledge gaps and uncertainty regarding preeclampsia prevention and LDA use contributed to inconsistent prescribing among both providerand health-system levels. At the provider-level, knowledge of LDA by doctors, including timing, dosing, safety, and eligibility criteria, functioned as both a facilitator and barrier: strong understanding supported appropriate prescribing, whereas uncertainty limited its routine and consistent use. For example, one doctor described that LDA prescription was "not practiced here" except by "few doctors"

and that prescribing decisions were "individual to them," noting that some providers initiated LDA among high-risk patients, including those with advanced maternal age or previous pre-eclampsia.

At the health-system level, similar gaps were reflected in limited familiarity with LDA as a preventive intervention among MCH stakeholders, as well as uncertainty regarding its clinical use, including timing, risks, and prescribing authority. For example, one MCH stakeholder acknowledged limited familiarity with the evidence supporting LDA effectiveness: "Hmm I think there are evidence that it helps…but I don't have much more than that."

Within physical opportunity at the health-system level, similar to screening, participants described limitations in diagnostic capacity and availability of specialized, costly tests (e.g., PLGF, β-hCG, coagulation profiles) that reduced confidence in risk assessment and safe clinical decision-making to prescribe LDA. Participants also described workforce shortages and uneven distribution of skilled providers with prescribing capacity as further limiting opportunities for LDA prescribing. Existing national guidelines support pre-eclampsia management (e.g., use of magnesium sulfate), but as noted previously, the absence of national prevention-specific guidance for pre-eclampsia also emerged as a major barrier to LDA prescribing, contributing to uncertainty around how and when LDA should be used after women are identified as being at risk. Although LDA is prescribed by some healthcare providers in tertiary care settings, it is not included in national essential medicine lists or routine government protocols, restricting standardized provision within the public health system. As one MCH stakeholder noted:

In major document there is no, any provision of any recommendations for aspirin use in pregnancy. But we have heard that some hospitals, they are practicing by providing the low dose of aspirin in pregnant women…but the distal document for the country is, there is no information" [MCH stakeholder #1]. MCH stakeholders emphasized that adoption of LDA into routine care will require alignment across existing national systems, with gaps in policy, financing, and implementation infrastructure acting as barriers to consistent prescribing. Participants identified several national systems relevant to this alignment, including ANC guidelines, the Essential Medicines List (which guides procurement and availability of medications), the Basic Health Service Package (a list of essential medicines provided free of cost), insurance schemes, and government training platforms (e.g., Skilled Birth Attendant programs and Emergency Obstetric and Newborn Care job aids). Stakeholders described such alignment as important for supporting procurement and financing, clarifying eligibility criteria and provider roles, and establishing referral pathways linking risk identification to prescribing. Inclusion within systems such as the Basic Health Service Package was viewed as particularly important because it would formally mandate government provision; however, MCH stakeholders noted that adoption would require sufficient financing not only for medication availability but also for training health workers. For example, one MCH stakeholder explained, "It's not only about making [LDA] available, it's also about giving the training to all the lower health staffs…so that would be a quite budgetary aspect." Stakeholders further emphasized the importance of sustainable financing mechanisms, recognizing that "the government can only afford so much to be free," and suggesting that integration with social health insurance schemes could help reduce financial barriers and support long-term implementation. In the absence of national protocols and standardized delivery mechanisms, Nepal's decentralized health system may further contribute to variability in LDA adoption, as local differences in resources, oversight, and service delivery capacity influence how preventive interventions are operationalized. Restrictions on prescribing authority among lower-level providers (e.g., nurses and auxiliary nurse midwives) also limit decentralized delivery. One MCH stakeholder highlighted how guideline endorsement could enable broader provider roles, noting: "So, right now, aspirin might be difficult because it's not a part of a formal guideline, but then if it becomes a part of the national guideline, then it's like there might be avenues which it might open those avenues for the nurses and midwives as well" [MCH stakeholder #2]. Reflective motivation shaped LDA prescribing behavior at both providerand health-system levels. At the provider-level, perceptions of LDA safety and effectiveness influenced prescribing confidence, with providers who viewed LDA as evidence-based and beneficial reporting greater willingness to prescribe. In private-sector settings, some providers perceived national guidance as less relevant to their clinical contexts, particularly when they viewed their care practices as exceeding those in the public sector, potentially contributing to variation in prescribing practices. At the health-system level, stakeholder willingness to integrate LDA into routine care was shaped by perceptions of evidence strength, safety, and feasibility within existing systems. A major concern noted by many participants was the capacity of peripheral facilities to manage potential complications associated with LDA use. As one MCH stakeholder noted: "Developing this low-dose aspirin guideline would be feasible, but also I'm not sure to what extent it increases the possibility or incidence of postpartum hemorrhage…especially given the ability of the peripheral level health facilities and the hospitals to deal with postpartum hemorrhage" [MCH stakeholder #2].

In addition, perceptions of feasibility were shaped by competing ANC priorities (e.g., achieving recommended ANC visit schedules) and concerns that adherence to an additional medication may be limited, given existing challenges in adherence with established free supplements such as iron-folic acid.

Determinants of LDA uptake by pregnant women

Uptake reflects the translation of prescribing into sustained medication use and is shaped primarily by patient-level determinants across COM-B components, with additional influences from providerand health-system level physical opportunity factors.

Within psychological capability at the patient-level, building on the limited knowledge of preeclampsia described above, women also had limited knowledge of LDA, including those at-risk for preeclampsia who were eligible for, and even actively taking, the medication. Many reported little or no understanding of its purpose, benefits, safety, or dosing, with some confusing LDA with other pregnancy supplements such as calcium or iron and questioning the need for additional medication. For example, one woman reported that "there isn't much" that can be done to prevent high blood pressure during pregnancy and was unaware of any preventive medications. Only after the interviewer mentioned aspirin as a preventive strategy did she realize she was already taking it, explaining, "The doctor didn't explain the reason. When I asked, they didn't explain. They just said to take it." Limited recall of clinical instructions, as well as pregnancy-related "brain fog" and forgetfulness, further constrained sustained uptake. In contrast, physical capability was not perceived as a barrier, as women taking LDA reported that the medication was easy to take, with no difficulty or discomfort swallowing the pill.

Physical opportunity influenced LDA uptake across patient-, providerand health-system levels.

At the patient-level, financial constraints (e.g., prioritizing food over medication), difficult travel conditions, and time constraints related to competing household responsibilities (e.g., childcare), limited women's ability to access and sustain LDA use. Many women described experiencing multiple barriers simultaneously: "it could be financial difficulties, bad road conditions, or longer distance from home to hospital…or a lack of someone at home to help when needed." At the provider-level, clear, simple, and structured counselling on LDA timing, dosage, purpose, benefits, and safety supported correct and sustained use, whereas limited counselling constrained uptake. One woman described receiving LDA from a provider but returning it because she did not understand why it had been prescribed or how it related to her pregnancy: "The doctor had given it to me... I even brought it home. Then I returned that medicine. I was wondering why it was prescribed." Another woman emphasized the importance of receiving this information: "What are its benefits? What happens to us when we take it? How does it work in our body?" Pharmacist counselling at the point of dispensing provided additional adherence support.

At the health-system level, access, affordability, and service organization shaped uptake. LDA was generally perceived as affordable and its reported availability supported access; however, financial constraints remained a barrier for some women, particularly those with limited resources. Requiring a provider prescription to obtain LDA through pharmacies or health posts limited ease of access by requiring formal health system contact, while also supporting clinical oversight of use. Once prescribed, consistent availability and co-located supply at points of care reduced additional barriers to obtaining the medication.

Social opportunity at the patient-level shaped acceptance and sustained uptake of LDA. Low community awareness of LDA limited normative understanding of its preventive role, while family and peer influences could either discourage or reinforce uptake through the spread of concerns (e.g., inaccurate information about safety) or supportive experiences (e.g., shared experiences with peers).

Family support, including reminders, assistance obtaining medication, and financial or logistical help, facilitated use. As one woman explained, "My family is supportive, which makes it easier for me…they do everything-from helping with household tasks to getting the medicine."

Trust in health information was also shaped by social hierarchies, with patients often preferring advice provided by doctors and placing greater weight on guidance from male doctors than female doctors (frequently misidentified as nurses), reflecting broader gendered perceptions of provider credibility. Despite limited awareness, LDA was generally viewed as socially acceptable, with less stigma than other pregnancy medications such as antihypertensives, facilitating uptake when women understood its purpose.

Reflective motivation at the patient-level shaped women's perceptions of LDA's necessity, safety, and value during pregnancy. Concerns about potential harm to the fetus, broader beliefs against medication use in pregnancy, preference for home remedies, and uncertainty about long-term effects reduced motivation to initiate or continue LDA. Additional concerns included the perceived need and burden of adding another medication alongside existing supplements, doubts about LDA's necessity compared with familiar pregnancy supplements such as iron and calcium, and perceptions that LDA was less essential because it was not universally provided free of cost. As one woman explained: "Ah, sometimes people get confused…Should I really take this medicine? I have been taking iron and calcium regularly, so now, is it necessary to take aspirin as well? That question often comes to mind" [Pregnant woman #7].

Conversely, trust in healthcare providers and clear clinician recommendations strongly facilitated uptake by increasing the perceived legitimacy and necessity of LDA. As many participants emphasized, "everyone trusts the doctor." One woman further explained: "After the doctor properly explains how aspirin works, we will be convinced, and that is why we take the medicine...because of the doctor's convincing power, we, the patients, continue taking the medicine" [Pregnant woman #7].

Belief in LDA's effectiveness, awareness of pre-eclampsia risks, responsibility to protect the fetus, and previous positive experiences during pregnancy with LDA further strengthened motivation to initiate and sustain use. As one woman explained, "If it keeps my blood pressure under control, I will take it. If I were concerned about my health and the baby's health, I would take it."

Automatic motivation at the patient-level was supported by existing medication-taking routines for pregnancy supplements such as iron and calcium, which provided established habits that could facilitate incorporation of LDA into daily practices.

Discussion

This study provides novel insight into the many determinants shaping adoption and uptake of LDA for pre-eclampsia prevention in Nepal. While extensive evidence has established the effectiveness of LDA and documented persistent gaps in its implementation globally, emerging work has conceptualized LDA implementation as a multistep pathway [47]. Building on this work, our findings extend the prevention pathway upstream to ANC engagement and provide empirical insight into how determinants across interdependent stages shape progression from timely care engagement and risk identification to LDA prescribing and uptake. Although LDA is available, low-cost, and acceptable to women once prescribed, its impact is constrained when gaps in these upstream stages prevent women from progressing through the prevention pathway.

Timely ANC engagement represented the first critical bottleneck in the prevention pathway.

Delayed ANC initiation and inconsistent attendance reduced opportunities for pre-eclampsia risk assessment and LDA initiation within the effective gestational window, consistent with evidence from LMIC settings demonstrating that late entry into care limits access to preventive and diagnostic interventions [23, 48, 49]. In Nepal, these delays reflected interacting patient-and health-system level determinants across COM-B components, including transportation barriers, costs of care, household responsibilities, gendered decision-making dynamics, and limited awareness of pregnancy risks and the importance of ANC engagement -determinants consistently reported as barriers to ANC engagement across LMIC settings [49, 50]. Our findings extend this evidence by illustrating how these barriers create upstream constraints that shape progression through the pre-eclampsia prevention pathway, limiting subsequent opportunities for risk identification, prescribing, and LDA uptake.

Among women who accessed ANC, screening to identify women at risk for pre-eclampsia was a major implementation bottleneck. While routine ANC platforms provided opportunities for screening through blood pressure measurement, urine testing, and clinical assessment [51], our findings suggest that these processes were inconsistently implemented due to interacting determinants across COM-B components, particularly limited provider capability and constrained physical opportunity within health services. Variability in basic screening practices, including concerns regarding blood pressure measurement quality, reflects broader evidence from LMICs showing that blood pressure measurement is not always performed, and when undertaken it is not consistently conducted using standardized techniques [52]. Beyond routine screening, the use of more advanced approaches such as uterine artery Doppler were limited by cost, equipment availability, and access to trained personnel, consistent with FIGO guidance highlighting that although Doppler-based approaches improve predictive accuracy for first-trimester pre-eclampsia screening, their resource requirements limit uptake in LMICs [53].

Following risk identification, LDA prescribing represented a transition from clinical eligibility to preventive action. At this stage, adoption was less constrained by provider motivation and more by the extent to which the health system provided the physical opportunity to support standardized prescribing. Although providers generally recognized the value of LDA and expressed willingness to prescribe, prescribing remained dependent on individual clinical judgement and local capacity in the absence of national guidance, standardized protocols, and clear delivery pathways. These system-level constraints may be compounded by broader workforce challenges, including burnout, which can affect providers' capacity and motivation to deliver preventive care [54]. However, our findings suggest that addressing provider motivation alone is insufficient; prevention must also be institutionalized within routine ANC systems. Evidence from implementation research in LMICs similarly demonstrates that clinical recommendations alone rarely translate into routine practice without supporting mechanisms, including training, supervision, procurement systems, and integration into routine service delivery [55].

LDA uptake among pregnant women appeared to be less constrained by medication availability or acceptability than by the conditions under which prescribing occurred. Women generally accepted LDA when its purpose and safety were clearly explained, while limited knowledge, concerns about medication use during pregnancy, and uncertainty regarding preventive benefits influenced uptake.

Consistent with broader implementation literature, LDA use during pregnancy is shaped by interacting patient, provider, and health-system determinants, including communication, trust, risk perception, and access to care, rather than individual motivation alone [56]. Provider counselling emerged as a key mechanism linking prescribing decisions to sustained medication use, highlighting that uptake depends not only on whether LDA is prescribed, but on how preventive care is communicated and supported within routine ANC encounters.

Strengths and Limitations

This study has several strengths. First, it incorporates perspectives from multiple stakeholders involved across the prevention pathway, including pregnant women, healthcare providers, and MCH stakeholders, allowing us to understand how determinants are experienced and understood by different actors. Second, the inclusion of three tertiary hospitals with differing institutional contexts enhanced contextual diversity. Third, use of the COM-B model provided a theory-informed approach to identifying and organizing determinants, while the pathway-based analysis moved beyond classification of barriers and facilitators by examining how determinants interact across sequential stages of prevention.

This study also benefited from purposive sampling across participant groups and hospital settings, alongside an iterative assessment of data sufficiency throughout data collection and analysis.

Thematic saturation was considered alongside the breadth of findings across participant groups and hospital sites when determining whether the data were sufficient to address the study objectives. This approach reflects broader debate regarding the use of saturation as a marker of qualitative sufficiency and rigor, particularly in complex, multi-site health systems research where variation across actors and settings is an important feature of the phenomenon under study [42] [43] [44] [45].

Analytic rigor was further supported by standardized templates and stepwise procedures that enabled consistency, transparency, and traceability throughout data reduction and interpretation, while enabling simultaneous engagement by a multidisciplinary team with varying qualitative expertise [42].

Involvement of bilingual Nepali team members in data collection and interpretation strengthened contextual understanding and helped identify linguistic, cultural, and contextual nuances, while researchers from different backgrounds brought additional perspectives to interpretation.

Several limitations should be considered. While the findings may be transferable to ANC in tertiary hospital settings, transferability to community settings and populations with less engagement with formal ANC services may be limited [42]. Further, perspectives of other actors who may influence LDA uptake, such as husbands and mothers-in-law, were not captured, potentially limiting our understanding of broader social influences on uptake. Additionally, all interviewed pregnant women reported literacy, potentially underrepresenting women experiencing greater informational barriers.

These barriers may be even more pronounced among women with less access to information and resources; thus, our findings may underestimate the extent of these barriers in more disadvantaged populations.

Healthcare provider interviews may also have been influenced by social desirability bias, particularly given the professional context of discussions about clinical practice. This was mitigated through private interviews, assurance of confidentiality, and the use of open-ended questions and probes to encourage detailed discussion of providers' experiences and practices. Although informal observations of clinic settings provided contextual understanding and informed interpretation of interview data, they were not systematically recorded or analyzed; future research incorporating direct observation could provide additional insight into routine care practices. Finally, implementation contexts evolve rapidly. Changes observed during data collection, for example, the introduction of new screening and prescribing protocols at one of our study hospitals, highlight the challenge of capturing implementation processes in real time and underscore the need for approaches capable of responding to rapidly evolving service delivery contexts while maintaining analytic rigor.

Implications for research, practice, and policy

The findings highlight the need to strengthen the systems through which LDA prevention is delivered, rather than focusing solely on changing individual patient or provider behaviors. At the policy level, this includes incorporating LDA into national ANC guidelines, aligning recommendations with essential medicine and procurement systems, and establishing training and supervision mechanisms to support delivery. At the health facility level, implementation strategies should support early ANC engagement, strengthen routine risk screening, establish standardized risk assessment-to-prescribing pathways, clarify provider roles, and integrate LDA counselling into routine ANC. Together, these approaches can strengthen the interdependent stages of the pre-eclampsia prevention pathway and reduce missed opportunities for prevention.

More broadly, the findings highlight an important consideration in how preventive interventions are targeted and delivered within antenatal care. Risk-based approaches to LDA depend on timely care and accurate risk identification, whereas universal provision could reduce losses associated with missed identification but would expose more women to treatment with potentially lower expected benefit and greater cumulative risk of adverse effects [57]. Similar trade-offs have been examined for other antenatal interventions, including iron supplementation, where decisions regarding targeted versus broader delivery must consider potential benefits, risks, adherence, and health-system capacity [58].

Improving adoption and uptake will require a set of multilevel strategies that address capability, opportunity, and motivation across patients, providers, and health-system levels. The determinants identified in this study provide an empirical foundation for co-designing implementation strategies to improve LDA adoption and uptake in Nepal and potentially other settings facing similar challenges in translating LDA and other evidence-based maternal health interventions into routine practice.

In the next phases of this work, we will co-design and evaluate implementation strategies to improve LDA adoption and uptake in Nepal. Future research should build on these efforts by operationalizing and quantifying the full pre-eclampsia prevention cascade to determine how many women progress through each stage and whether upstream gaps constrain downstream LDA prescribing and uptake. These measures could then be used to evaluate whether implementation strategies improve performance at each stage and translate into gains across the cascade. Research should also examine how implementation strategies perform across different health-systems and community contexts and how they can be adapted to settings with different implementation challenges and opportunities.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that LDA adoption and uptake for pre-eclampsia prevention are shaped by interdependent determinants across the prevention pathway, extending from early ANC engagement and risk identification to provider prescribing and pregnant women's uptake. While LDA is acceptable, available, and supported by strong evidence, its impact may be constrained when gaps at upstream stages prevent progression through the prevention pathway. Improving LDA adoption and uptake will require coordinated, multilevel implementation strategies that strengthen policies, service delivery systems, provider capacity, and patient communication. These findings provide context-specific evidence to inform efforts to integrate LDA into maternal health systems in Nepal and other settings seeking to translate evidence-based preventive interventions into routine care.
