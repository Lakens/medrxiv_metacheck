How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Tandem repeats (TRs), consisting of consecutive repeated units of 1+bp, are highly polymorphic features found throughout the genome that have been shown to have widespread impacts on gene expression and other traits. However, despite their prevalence, TRs are not included as features in polygenic prediction models, which typically focus on common single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs). Here, we develop a framework to incorporate TRs into gene expression models, which consider variants in a cis-window around each gene to predict expression levels. Simulation analyses demonstrate that including linear effects of TR copy number as features can result in substantially improved predictions, particularly when high impact causal TRs are poorly tagged by individual SNPs. We applied our framework to train and evaluate SNP+TR expression prediction models in lymphoblastoid cell lines (LCLs) using data from the Geuvadis cohort (n=359 European individuals) and compared these to SNP-only models. Overall, across 2,553 genes for which models trained successfully, we observed that SNP+TR models give significantly improved performance for 78 genes and a mean gain in adjusted R-squared 0.0037 across all genes, with most gains replicating in the independent Gene-Tissue Expression Project (GTEx) cohort. We additionally find that incorporating TRs results in simpler models and improves portability of models when testing on data for 89 Yoruban individuals from Geuvadis. Finally, we extended our models to consider nonlinear effects of TR copy number on expression. Under our nonlinear TR association testing, we identify 2,255 nonlinear significant genes not captured by linear association testing models, and identify exemplar genes, including PALD1, with highly polymorphic TRs for which incorporating their nonlinear effects results in substantially improved gene expression prediction. Overall, these results demonstrate the utility of including TRs in expression prediction models and show promise that they will have similar impact across other models such as polygenic risk scores for complex traits.

Introduction

Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have been instrumental in identifying genetic variants associated with complex traits. However, GWAS cannot directly discern how genetic variants in associated regions mechanistically impact a trait. Transcriptome-wide association studies (TWAS) [1] [2] [3] [4] attempt to address this shortcoming by identifying individual genes with genetically predicted expression levels that are associated with a trait such as diseases. Compared to GWAS, TWAS offers easier interpretability by focusing on genes rather than individual variants; has improved power due to reduced multiple testing burden; and has the potential to generate tissue-specific hypotheses 5 . TWAS typically first imputes expression values for each individual based on pre-trained linear models to predict tissue-specific gene expression from genetic variants 1, 2, 6 . Mostly, these gene expression prediction models focus on common single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and small indels in a cis-window around the gene. They have not typically considered the contribution of more complex variant types such as repeats and structural variants which have been shown in some cases to have large contributions to expression [7] [8] [9] [10] [11].

Tandem repeats (TRs), which consist of repeating units of 1 or more bp in tandem, comprise over 2 million distinct loci throughout the human genome 12, 13 . We 10, 14 and others 9, 15, 16 have identified thousands of TRs at which variation in copy number is predicted to causally impact expression and we previously estimated that TRs explain around 10-15% of the cis-heritability of gene expression in humans 14 . Further, recent studies have identified hundreds of TRs predicted to be the causal drivers underlying top GWAS loci for a range of complex traits including blood traits 17 , height 7 , cancer risk 18 , and intellectual disability 19 . However, due to their rapid mutation rates and high multi-allelicness, TRs are often imperfectly tagged by nearby SNPs 20 and thus their effects might not be well captured by SNP-based gene expression prediction models.

Here, we evaluate incorporating TRs alongside SNPs into gene expression prediction models. To this end we used genotyping and RNA sequencing data from the 1000 Genomes Project 21, 22 and Geuvadis 23 for 359 European individuals to generate gene expression prediction models for 12,605 genes in lymphoblastoid cell lines (LCLs). We evaluate the improvement of model predictions with TRs compared to SNP-only models, perform replication analysis in LCL data available from the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) Project, and compare cross-ancestry portability of SNP-only vs. SNP+TR models. Finally, we assess the contribution of nonlinear effects of TR copy number on expression and incorporate these effects into gene expression prediction models. Our results demonstrate that the models that incorporate TRs in some cases result in dramatic prediction performance improvement as well as simplified and more portable models. Overall, our study demonstrates the benefit of considering a range of variant types for gene expression prediction models and suggests incorporating TRs will also benefit related applications such as polygenic risk scores for complex traits and diseases. Results

A framework for integrating TRs into gene expression prediction models

We constructed a framework to generate gene expression prediction models for each gene based on cis-variants within 500kb of the gene boundaries (Fig. 1a). For this, we train LASSO regression models including either single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) alone, or SNPs in combination with TRs. Due to potential variability of training LASSO models with cross-fold validation, especially in settings with low sample size, we train 20 separate models in identical ways for each gene and report the average results across models (adjusted R-squared based on 3-fold cross validation; Methods) for the SNP and SNP+TR models.

We first performed simulations to evaluate the robustness of our framework and gain insight into how the potential gain from including TRs in prediction models might vary across different genetic architectures of gene expression. Our simulations are based on real SNP+TR genotypes for 359 individuals of European (EUR) ancestry from the Geuvadis cohort, which are used in the real data analysis presented below. We evaluated performance under two different settings (Fig. 1b-c). In the first, we simulated a polygenic architecture with 8 total causal variants consisting of randomly chosen variants (4 SNPs and 4 TRs) with heritability explained distributed evenly across causal variants (Methods). The total heritability due to cis-variants was set to 0.8 and we varied the portion of cis-heritability ( ) driven by SNPs vs. TRs from 0 to 0.8. In the second, we ℎ 2 𝑐𝑖𝑠 simulated a single causal effect for a highly polymorphic TR. The single causal effect matches patterns previously observed for the several strong eSTRs, including several known to influence medically relevant phenotypes (e.g. eSTRs for CBL 17 and NOP56 10, 24 ). Simulations for this second setting are based on the TIMM10 locus for which we previously identified a strong multi-allelic eSTR 25 . In this setting, we varied the total heritability explained by the TR from 0 to 0.8. For each setting, we repeated the simulation 100 times and reported the mean and standard error of the adjusted R-squared across simulations.

For the first setting (polygenic architecture with randomly chosen causal variants), we found that as expected SNP+TR models tend to outperform SNP-only models at genes for which a substantial portion of the cis-heritability of gene expression is driven by TRs (Fig. 1b). We observed similar results when varying the number of causal variants (Supplementary Fig. 1). In the second setting of a single large-effect highly polymorphic TR, we found that the SNP+TR models consistently generated models with adjusted R-squared close to the simulated cis-heritability (Fig. 1c), whereas the SNP-only models showed reduced performance (e.g. mean adjusted R-squared 0.788 for the SNP+TR model compared to 0.714 for the SNP-only model for =0.8). Across both simulation settings, we observed that gains for the SNP+TR models for a particular ℎ 2 𝑐𝑖𝑠 gene depend on how well causal TRs are tagged by nearby SNPs. In the first setting, simulations in which TRs were selected to have low LD with the best tag SNP show higher gains compared to simulations in which they 3 are selected to have high LD with tag SNPs (Supplementary Fig. 2 3). Similarly, when repeating the simulation for the second setting using other causal TRs, we found that the gain in performance for the SNP+TR models expectedly reduces as the LD of the causal TR with the best tag SNP increases (Supplementary Fig. 4). Overall, our simulation results demonstrate that the subset of genes whose expression is driven by causal TRs that are not well tagged by cis-SNPs are expected to show substantial gains when incorporating TRs into gene expression prediction models. 4

Constructing SNP+TR gene expression models in the Geuvadis LCL dataset

We applied the framework described above to construct both SNP-only and SNP+TR gene expression prediction models in the Geuvadis dataset, which includes RNA-sequencing from LCLs for 359 European (EUR; from 1000 Genomes https://paperpile.com/c/EXMOD0/9s3J populations CEU, FIN, GBR, TSI) and 89 Yoruban (YRI) individuals. SNP and TR genotypes have been previously generated for these samples based on available high-coverage whole genome sequencing 22, 25 . After restricting to protein coding genes and filtering genes in segmental duplications or with low overall expression (Methods), https://paperpile.com/c/EXMOD0/2TPk2,605 genes remained, of which 1,195 were estimated to have cis-heritability significantly different than zero, similar to previous estimates at similar sample sizes 1 . Gene expression values were adjusted for technical covariates and population structure prior to downstream analysis (Methods). After filtering on minor allele frequency (>0.01) and excluding variants failing Hardy Weinberg Equilibrium, 8,975,030 SNPs and 1,711,093 TRs remained for consideration in gene expression prediction models.

Of the 12,605 genes considered, LASSO models for either SNPs only or SNPs+TRs were successfully generated (non-negative adjusted R-squared) for 2,553 genes. Of these, we obtained SNP-only, SNP+TR models, or both, for 145, 203, and 2,205 genes, respectively. These genes are highly enriched for genes for which cis-heritability was estimated to be significantly non-zero (Fisher's exact test odds ratio=21.57 one-sided P=1.35e-43; Methods). We evaluated model improvement using two metrics: the difference in adjusted R-squared (set to 0 when negative) based on 3-fold cross-validation and the difference in proportion of total cis-heritability explained between the SNP+TR vs. SNP-only models. Overall, SNP+TR models resulted in an average gain in adjusted R-squared of 0.0037 across all 2,553 genes analyzed. This gain was stronger for cis-heritable genes (0.0066; n=1,195) compared to non-heritable genes (0.0012; n=1,358; Mann Whitney [MW] U one-sided P=5.88e-5; Methods; Fig. 2a). The gain in adjusted R-squared exhibited a long tail, with 83 genes showing a gain of 0.05 or more (top 10 shown in Table 1) and 78 genes showing statistically significant gains (FDR<10%) in R-squared for SNP+TR models. We observed a similar trend when considering the percentage of cis-heritability explained (Methods), with on average an additional 1.79% explained by the SNP+TR vs. SNP-only models for the 2,553 genes but with a long tail of genes showing large improvements when adding TRs (Supplementary Fig. 5). Overall, our results show that incorporating TRs improves gene expression prediction models, with a subset of genes showing large predictive performance gains.

We hypothesized that in cases where the addition of TRs improved the model, it could be because one or more TRs contained predictive information that was either not captured by SNPs or required multiple SNPs to explain the effect of a single TR. To test this, we examined whether genes for which incorporating TRs was beneficial for prediction resulted in simpler overall models. This analysis was restricted to genes for which the difference of adjusted R-squared values between the SNP+TR model and SNP models was greater than 0, leaving a total of 1,150 genes. We quantified the complexity of trained models as the number of variants with non-zero LASSO coefficients (defined as the number of coefficients > averaged across the 20 models for ϵ 5 each gene). Despite the increase in the number of features (variants) in the SNP+TR models (median 1,572 features) compared to the SNP-only models (median 1,362 features; Wilcoxon signed-rank test one-sided P=5.75e-190; Fig 2b), we saw that SNP+TR models had lower overall numbers of non-zero coefficients (median 8.125 with =10 -8 ) compared to SNP-only models (median 9.375; Wilcoxon signed-rank test one-sided ϵ P=2.01e-44; Fig. 2b). These trends were robust to thresholds used to determine which genes showed improved adjusted R-squared or the value of used to determine the number of non-zero coefficients per ϵ model (Supplementary Fig. 6). Further, the reduction in the number of non-zero coefficients was strongest for genes with the highest improvement in adjusted R-squared (change in medians of 1.25 and 6.95 for gain in adjusted R-squared threshold of 0 and 0.05 respectively; Supplementary Fig. 6). We evaluated the ability of our gene expression prediction models trained on EUR samples to predict expression in individuals of Yoruban (YRI; Yoruba in Nigeria) ancestry. Incorporating nonlinear effects of TRs on gene expression

The gene expression models described above consider only linear effects of TR copy number on expression.

Given the existence of multiple known examples of nonlinear TR-phenotype associations in both humans and other species [27] [28] [29] , as well as our recent observations of widespread nonlinear effects of TRs on expression using a massively parallel reporter assay 28 , we evaluated whether modeling nonlinear effects of TR copy number on expression would improve our gene expression prediction models. Although multiple classes of nonlinear models exist, we focused on a quadratic model (Methods) given its relatively low model complexity (one additional parameter compared to linear models), limitations of sample sizes of existing expression datasets to fit more complex models, and the similar ability of a quadratic model to capture many types of effects (e.g. threshold, bell-shape curve) observed in real data compared to more complex alternatives.

We first sought to identify examples of nonlinear effects and quantify their prevalence in the LCL datasets of interest. For this, we fit both linear and quadratic models (Methods) to identify associations between TR copy number and expression of nearby genes (within the gene body +/-500kb) in the 359 EUR samples from the Geuvadis cohort (Fig. 4a) and evaluated replication of effects in 109 EUR samples from GTEx. In total we performed 3,427,940 variant-gene association tests for each model across 12,605 unique genes. We identified 88,809 and 100,674 gene-TR pairs (3,266 and 3,339 unique genes) meeting a gene-level FDR<5% when applying the linear and quadratic models, respectively, with the linear association result in line with the total number of eGenes expected at this sample size based on previous studies 10, 30 . Association statistics (-log 10 P-values) for linear tests were strongly correlated between Geuvadis and GTEx (Pearson r=0.751; two-sided P<1e-200; n=16,961 TR-gene pairs with adjusted P<5% in Geuvadis that achieved nominal P<5% in GTEx;

P=2.0e-6).

Visual inspection of the association between TR length and expression shows a bell-shaped trend with highest expression for alleles of 8-9 repeat copies and reduced expression at either longer or shorter alleles (Fig. 4d; Supplementary Fig. 15). Another example is a C n repeat at hg38:chr10:70,473,982 associated with expression of PALD1 (KIAA1274) for which a quadratic model is strongly favored in both Geuvadis (ANOVA P=8.4e-26) and GTEx (ANOVA P=0.0014). In this case, the median allele (copy number 10) is associated with lower expression compared to longer and shorter alleles (Fig. 4e; Supplementary Fig. 16).

Finally, we evaluated whether incorporating nonlinear effects of TRs could improve gene expression prediction models. In addition to the SNP-only and SNP+TR linear models above, we tested a third set of models for each gene containing SNPs as well as both linear and quadratic TR features for each TR included. As in our association testing models (Methods), the quadratic term is composed of the sum of squared allele lengths for the two alleles in each diploid individual. Overall, incorporation of quadratic TR effects resulted in improved adjusted R-squared values for 737 genes compared to the SNP-only and SNP+TR models. In most cases, improvements are modest, but 100 genes showed improvements of more than 0.01 (Supplementary Table 1).

One of these is PALD1, for which a strong nonlinear effect of a C n repeat was detected as described above, which showed an improvement in adjusted R-squared of 0.012 over the SNP+TR linear model and 0.069 over the SNP-only model. The LASSO model fit, which assigns a large coefficient to the quadratic term for this TR (0.11), closely matches the observed data and model fit from quadratic association tests (Fig. 4e). Taken together, our results show that although robust examples of nonlinear effects of TRs on expression exist, incorporation into expression prediction models, at least using available sample sizes, has minimal overall improvement on prediction performance for most genes (Discussion).

14 Discussion

Here, we presented a framework for incorporating TRs into prediction models. Benchmarking our framework on simulated data demonstrated that including TRs can result in substantial improvement in predictive performance over SNP-only models, particularly at genes for which TRs with high effect sizes on expression are in weak to moderate LD with nearby SNPs. By applying this framework to gene expression for LCLs, we found that incorporating TRs results in simplified LASSO models with fewer non-zero coefficients, and provides especially large performance gains for a subset of 83 genes. For many genes, inclusion of TRs resulted in models that were more portable across ancestries, even when the SNP+TR model showed only a modest improvement in predictive performance over SNP-only models in the training European cohort. Finally, we identified tens of thousands of examples of nonlinear associations between TR copy number and expression, with 252 associations that could only be detected when applying nonlinear models. We highlight several strong examples of nonlinear effects, including a C n repeat associated with PALD1, and show that incorporating the nonlinear effect resulted in an improved gene expression prediction model.

We hypothesize that the gains in performance, portability, and simplification of prediction models is due to the direct inclusion of additional causal variants as predictors. Current polygenic scores including gene expression prediction models primarily rely on common SNPs. In many cases, the variants included are not causal, but rather serve as LD proxies for other underlying causal variants. As a result, they may achieve low performance in cases where causal variants are not well tagged, and performance degrades when predicting on individuals of other ancestries with different LD structures. Recent studies have demonstrated a widespread impact of TR variation on gene expression variability 10, 28 . In many cases, these TRs are highly polymorphic and in weak to modest LD with individual nearby variants, although can often be imputed to high accuracy using combinations of SNPs 20 . As a result, LASSO prediction models may need to include multiple SNPs to achieve the same performance that can be achieved by including a single TR. Further, if a TR included in the model is truly causal, its predictive power is likely to remain high even when applying models to ancestries with different LD structures, as many TR effects on expression have been observed to be shared across ancestries 25.

Our results have important implications for the design of polygenic risk scores (PRSs), which assess an individual's disease risk or other complex trait values based on their genetic variants. Similar to gene expression prediction models, standard frameworks [31] [32] [33] typically rely on common SNPs, and have shown low portability across ancestries with different LD structures and allele frequencies 34 . Beyond gene expression, recent studies have implicated highly polymorphic TRs as a major source of high impact causal variants across a range of medically relevant complex traits 7, [17] [18] [19] . Overall, this suggests incorporating TRs into PRS will have similar gains to those observed here for gene expression prediction models.

Our study faced several limitations. First, the Geuvadis dataset only had a sufficiently large sample size to train across individuals of European ancestry. While inclusion of TRs resulted in improved portability for some genes, TRs often show population-specific allele ranges and frequencies, whose effects will not be captured by our models here. Expanding to additional datasets such as the multi-ethnic study of atherosclerosis (MESA) dataset which contains 574 European samples and 224 African samples of monocyte tissue 35 may result in improved models for non-European samples in future studies. Third, we only considered a single cell type (LCLs). Still, we anticipate the overall trends observed here will generalize to other settings, and that expanding to more cell types and tissues will identify additional genes for which incorporating TRs provides a major improvement in performance of gene expression prediction models.

Finally, our models are based on a limited sample size (n=359 samples from Geuvadis). This limits our ability to accurately model variants with low effect sizes and contributes to high variance in expression predictions.

We also anticipate that low sample size reduces our ability to detect and model nonlinear effects. Despite evidence from our recent work on massively parallel reporter assays (MPRA) 28 that nonlinear effects of TRs on expression may be widespread, we found only modest improvement in model performance when including nonlinear TR features. One potential explanation is that our use of a LASSO model with quadratic TR length effects is insufficient to capture some types of nonlinear trends. Other model classes such as random forests, gradient boosting, or neural networks may better capture more complex relationships between TR length and expression. However, these models are more expensive to train and require larger sample sizes to avoid overfitting. Another challenge in modeling nonlinear effects at low sample sizes is that we may not observe a sufficient number of different TR lengths to reliably infer nonlinear trends. For example, although we observed nonlinear trends when considering more than a dozen different repeat lengths at particular locus in an MPRA context 28 , if only two or three alleles are observed they may still be best fit by a linear model.

Overall, our results demonstrate that incorporating TRs into polygenic prediction models can result in improved and simpler models with improved portability across ancestries. Computing gene expression models

We trained models to predict expression for each gene based on cis-variants using LASSO regression implemented in the scikit-learn package version 1.7.2 (ULRs) with non-default parameter max_iterations=50000 and tolerance 1e-4. For each gene, we considered SNPs and TRs from an interval encompassing the gene boundaries +/-500kb. Prior to training genotypes each variant was standardized to have mean zero and variance 1. We trained two separate models for each gene, including either SNPs only or SNPs+TRs. For each model, the alpha parameter for LASSO was tuned using the GridSearchCV class from scikit-learn to train models using 3-fold cross validation with n_repeats=2. Tuning was performed in two steps, the first performed a broad search using a range of 0.1-0.9 with step 0.04. The second performed a higher 18 resolution search using the value learned in the first round +/-0.09, with minimum allowable alpha 0.001, searching 51 possible values. Due to our relatively low sample size (n=359 European samples), we repeated the training process above 20 times to assess variability in performance across runs. Geuvadis dataset preprocessing

Gene expression for EBV-transformed lymphocytes was obtained from the Geuvadis project for European (super population code EUR, n = 359) and YRI (super population code AFR, n = 89) individuals 23 (URLs). After these filters, 12,605 protein-coding genes remained for analysis. Expression values for each gene were first quantile normalized across samples to ensure all samples follow similar distributions, then values for each gene were inverse rank normalized to follow a standard normal distribution. SNP genotypes for these individuals based on high coverage whole genome sequencing of 1000 Genomes Project samples 22 and sex information were obtained from the IGSR data portal (URLs). We filtered all SNPs using plink2 version v2.0.0-a.6.9LM with a minor allele frequency less than 0.05 and failing Hardy-Weinberg Equilibrium (P<0.001). Additionally, we removed duplicate SNP entries (those with the same chromosome and position) keeping the first SNP listed in the given position. Genotype principal components (PCs) were computed from 8,975,030 autosomal SNPs using scikit-learn 1.7.2 separately for European and Yoruban individuals present in Geuvadis. Prior to downstream analyses we regressed out the top 5 genotype principal components (PCs), sex, and PEER factors 37 . PEER factors were computed using normalized expression values. We included 36 PEER factors for Europeans and 9 for Yorubans based on our previous finding that sample_size/10 PEER factors optimized discovery of TR-gene expression associations 10 . TR genotypes for 1.7 million loci were obtained using EnsembleTR as described previously 25 (URLs). We filtered TR loci with two or fewer unique alleles and with a heterozygosity less than 0.10.
