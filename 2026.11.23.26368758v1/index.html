Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Abstract

Most signals from genome-wide association studies (GWAS) of complex traits implicate noncoding variants with putative gene regulatory effects. However, currently identified regulatory variants, particularly expression quantitative trait loci (eQTLs), explain only a small fraction of GWAS associations. Moreover, GWAS loci are enriched near evolutionarily constrained genes, whereas constrained genes are depleted of detectable eQTLs, suggesting that weak or context-specific regulatory mechanisms may contribute substantially to disease risk but remain poorly captured by existing approaches. Here, we introduce scHINT (single-cell gene expression Heritability estimation with genotype × context INTeraction), a computationally efficient framework for quantifying celllevel expression heritability and context-dependent genetic regulation from large-scale single-cell data. Applying scHINT to human brain single-nucleus RNA-seq data, we found that genotype-bycell-state heritability was widespread across brain cell types and, on average, exceeded main genetic heritability, indicating that context-dependent genetic effects explain a substantial fraction of heritable expression variation. G×Cell-State heritability was enriched among low-frequency variants and constrained genes, and showed stronger enrichment for functional gene sets and disease heritability than main genetic effects. Using a novel G×Cell-State TWAS framework, we identified 68% more disease-risk genes and recapitulated rare-variant burden-test gene TREM2, which was missed by state-agnostic TWAS. In blood, we observed the same direction of enrichment for G×Cell-State heritability at constrained genes, but weaker than in brain, suggesting that cell-state-dependent regulation at constrained genes may be more prominent in brain. Finally, extending scHINT to perturbation screens revealed substantial perturbation-by-cell-state effects, highlighting broad context dependence in transcriptional responses.

Introduction

Large-scale genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified many robust disease-associated loci, but most associated variants lie outside protein-coding regions 1 and are thought to act through gene-regulatory mechanisms [2] [3] [4] . This has motivated extensive efforts to connect GWAS loci to molecular function through expression quantitative trait loci (eQTLs), using approaches such as colocalization [5] [6] [7] , transcriptome-wide association studies [8] [9] [10] [11] [12] [13] , and Mendelian randomization [14] [15] [16].

However, despite the success of resources such as GTEx 17 in mapping genetic effects on gene expression across human tissues, many GWAS loci cannot be confidently linked to steady-state gene expression measured in bulk tissues 18, 19.

Disease-relevant regulatory effects that do not colocalize with steady state expression may be weak, context-dependent, or active only in specific cellular environments 20, 21 . Bulk-tissue eQTL studies aggregate expression across heterogeneous mixtures of cells and may therefore miss genetic effects that are restricted to rare cell populations or transient cellular states. This limitation is particularly important for complex diseases, where genetic risk is often enriched in regulatory elements active in disease-relevant cell types 22 , and where the same variant may exert different effects depending on cellular identity 23, 24 , stimulation [25] [26] [27] [28] [29] , development [30] [31] [32] , or pathological state 33 . GWAS and bulk eQTL studies also tend to implicate systematically different classes of genes 18 . GWAS loci are enriched near evolutionarily constrained genes 34, 18, 35 and key functional annotations, whereas bulk cis-eQTLs are depleted near constrained genes and tend to cluster near transcription start sites 18 . Taken together, these paYerns suggest that disease risk may often involve genes under strong functional constraint, where large steady-state regulatory perturbations are selected against and common diseaseassociated regulatory effects are weak or revealed only under specific cellular contexts 36.

Single-cell RNA sequencing 37, 38 provides an opportunity to address these limitations by measuring gene expression within individual cells rather than averaged tissues. Population-scale single-cell eQTL studies have demonstrated that genetic effects can be highly cell-type-specific [39] [40] [41] [42] [43] [44] 22, 45 , and methods have been developed to connect cell-type-specific regulation with GWAS through single-cell expression [46] [47] [48] [49] [50] [51] [52] [53] . In addition to cell-types, single-cell data can reveal the presence of cellular "states"transient and continuous gene activations such as cell cycle, hypoxia, or stress response 54 . However, most existing approaches rely on pseudo-bulk profiles, which aggregate cells from the same individual within a cell type 17, [55] [56] [57] . Although pseudo-bulk analysis improves robustness and reduces technical noise 36, 43 , it loses cell-level resolution and cannot directly capture genetic effects that vary across continuous cell states within cell types.

Although single-cell eQTL mapping has been highly informative for identifying regulatory variants, eQTL discovery remains strongly constrained by sample size, effect size and statistical power 58 . In contrast, heritability-based approaches do not require individual variant-gene associations to reach significance and can therefore quantify the aggregate contribution of genetic variation to molecular or complex traits. Heritability partitioning has been widely used in complex-trait genetics to characterize enrichment across functional annotations 2 , tissues 59 , and cell-type-specific regulatory elements 3 . Similarly, prior studies have quantified the heritability of gene expression and estimated the extent to which disease heritability is mediated through genetically regulated expression 55, 60 , but these analyses have largely relied on bulk or pseudo-bulk level expression profiles. More recently, Xu et al. introduced scGeneHE 61 , a scalable mixed-model framework that extended SAIGEQTL 62 for estimating gene expression heritability directly from single-cell count data without requiring pseudobulk aggregation. However, no studies have quantified the extent to which gene expression heritability may be explained by genotype-by-cell-state interactions at single-cell resolution.

In this work, we introduce scHINT (single-cell gene expression Heritability estimation with genotype-by-cell-context INTeractions), a framework for quantifying cell-level expression heritability. scHINT models genotype × cell-state interactions and partitions expression variance into main genetic, G×Cell-State, individual-level, and cell-level components, enabling estimation of both main genetic effects shared across cells and context-specific genetic effects that vary continuously with cell state. We applied scHINT to 3.8 million cells from six major brain cell types across 584 ROS/MAP individuals 40, 63 . Through simulations and empirical analyses, we evaluated the accuracy and robustness of scHINT and generated a high-resolution map of context-dependent genetic regulation in the aging human brain. These analyses showed that cell-state-dependent genetic effects capture a distinct component of expression heritability enriched among low-frequency variants, constrained genes, activation dependent genes, and disease-relevant pathways. We extended scHINT to downstream disease interpretation using a novel G×Cell-State TWAS framework and show that it can link context-dependent regulatory effects to disease-risk genes missed by traditional TWAS.

Finally, we demonstrate that scHINT can be generalized to additional seYings, including different tissues, interaction with cell-type, disease status and perturbational screens, providing a flexible framework for dissecting context-dependent genetic regulation from single-cell data.

Single-cell data reveals substantial G×Cell-State interaction heritability

We applied scHINT to the combination of two large brain snRNA-seq datasets from Mathys et al. 63 and Fujita et al. 40 , both generated from ROS/MAP samples 76, 77 . After processing the snRNA-seq data (Methods), we focused on six major cell types: excitatory neurons (Exc), inhibitory neurons (Inh), astrocytes (Ast), oligodendrocytes (Oli), oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPC), and microglia (Mic), which comprise 3.8 million nuclei from 584 individuals. As in the simulation analyses, we benchmarked scHINT against three alternative approaches, HEreg 64 which was applied to cell-typespecific pseudo-bulk expression, scGeneHE 61 and scHINT-base, which were applied directly to celllevel expression. Specifically, we used within-cell-type PCA 67 to infer the cell states and estimate the G×PC heritability from each of the top five PCs jointly (Supplementary Fig. 10).

We found that G×C heritability was pervasive across major brain cell types (Fig. 3a), indicating that cell-state-dependent genetic effects contribute broadly to single-cell expression variation in the human brain. Across most cell types, the aggregate heritability from the five G×PC components were comparable to, and in several cases exceeded, the main genetic heritability, supporting the importance of context-dependent regulation beyond average genetic effects. For example, excitatory neurons, astrocytes, microglia, and OPCs showed a substantially larger combined G×PC heritability than the main genetic component (192.8%, 88.9%, 69.2% and 52.8% higher respectively). The leading cell-state axis did not necessarily explain the largest interaction heritability. Although PC1 contributed appreciably in some seYings (0.0086 on average), other PCs often showed equal or stronger effects (0.0087 on average), such as PC3 in excitatory neurons (0.022) and astrocytes (0.018), PC2 in OPCs (0.022) and PC5 in oligodendrocytes (0.023). Taken together, these results suggest that genetic regulation is modulated by multiple, distinct cell-state axes.

The main genetic and G×Cell-State component heritability estimates were largely independent, with correlation coefficients ranging from -0.022 to 0.031 (Supplementary Fig. 11). Consistent with this interpretation, the scHINT-base model produced a slightly higher average estimate than the corresponding scHINT-G (0.0295 versus 0.0284). Moreover, scHINT G×Cell-State heritability was broadly distributed across all bins of scHINT-G heritability (Supplementary Fig. 12). Together, these results indicate that direct genetic regulation can be weak even when context-dependent genetic effects remain appreciable (and vice versa). Genes exhibiting this paYern may therefore represent particularly informative candidates for future G×Cell-State eQTL analyses.

We carried out multiple robustness and sensitivity analyses. First, we performed a down sampling analysis to evaluate sensitivity to cell sampling depth. Pseudo-bulk HE regression estimates decreased as fewer cells were used to construct pseudo-bulk expression profiles, consistent with our simulations. In contrast, scHINT estimates remained broadly stable across cell numbers, for both the main genetic (scHINT-G) and G×Cell-State (scHINT-G×C) components within each cell type (Fig. 3b).

Second, we compared to estimates of main effect heritability from scGeneHE. Because of the computational burden, we first performed a meta-cell analysis and compared the resulting scHINT and scGeneHE estimates. We found that scGeneHE estimates were highly sensitive to covariate adjustment: including 25 pseudo-bulk and five cell-level PCs roughly doubled the mean estimate (0.044 to 0.088), whereas unadjusted scGeneHE estimates were closer to scHINT-G (mean 0.035). This is consistent with our simulations, in which scGeneHE is inflated when covariates explain substantial variance, and is detailed in the Supplementary Note 5 (Supplementary Fig. 13). Thirdly, we demonstrated the robustness of scHINT to alternative cell-state derivation methods, the number of PCs included in the model, the sparsity of read counts, the inclusion of genes to generate cell states, and the interaction with diseases status in Supplementary Note 6 (Supplementary Fig. 14 15 16 17 18 19 20 21).

Finally, complementary to the G×Cell-State analysis, we applied the scHINT-G×Cell-Type model to the same datasets after pseudo-bulk aggregation. To evaluate cell-type-dependent genetic effects, we performed both pairwise analyses between cell types and a joint analysis modeling genetic heterogeneity across all six major cell types (Supplementary Note 7 and Supplementary Fig. 22 23 24 25 26).

In the joint analysis, G×Cell-Type heritability was on average 0.92 times the main genetic heritability, lower than the corresponding G×Cell-State ratio of 1.72. Thus, cell states modulate genetic effects at least as strongly as discrete cell types.

G×Cell-State heritability is enriched at constrained genes and low frequency variants

Having established that G×Cell-State heritability is pervasive across brain cell types, we next sought to characterize the genetic architecture and functional relevance of these context-dependent effects.

First, we asked whether genes with higher G×Cell-State heritability exhibit unique paYerns of evolutionary constraint (Fig. 4a, Supplementary Table 5). Across all six cell types, main genetic heritability showed negative correlations with multiple constraint annotations (with the lowest cor = -0.15, p-value = 1.50 ×10 -54 ), implying that genes with more/larger main genetic effects were less likely to be under selection. These results are consistent with prior findings from analyses of bulk and pseudo-bulk eQTLs [78] [79] [80] . In contrast, many G×Cell-State components showed positive correlations with the same constraint metrics (with the highest cor = 0.13, p-value= 1.12×10 -36 ). We further examined the relationship between evolutionary constraint and the proportion of total heritability aYributable to G and G×Cell-State. This produced consistent but stronger correlations (with the highest cor = 0.17, p-value= 1.49×10 -68 ) (Supplementary Fig. 27, Supplementary Table 6). A larger absolute G×Cell-State heritability at more constrained genes could be consistent with a larger mutational target size for interactions. These opposing correlation directions suggest that contextdependent genetic effects preferentially concentrate in genes under stronger selective constraint, whereas main genetic effects are relatively depleted among constrained genes. We also examined model with additional G×PC terms (10, 15 and 20 PCs). Overall, the model with 5 G×PC terms shows the highest correlation with constraint scores, although the optimal number depends on cell type and read depth. Interestingly, excitatory and inhibitory neurons still retained a positive correlation with more PCs or after down-sampling (Supplementary Table 7, Supplementary Note 6, Supplementary Fig. 18).

We also examined associations between heritability components and regulatory, network, and genestructure annotations. All three components, G, G×Cell-Type, and G×Cell-State were positively correlated with regulatory features such as promoter counts, suggesting greater heritability for genes with more complex regulatory architecture. For network annotations, G and G×Cell-Type were negatively correlated, whereas G×Cell-State was positively correlated, potentially reflecting stronger constraint on stable genetic effects but greater flexibility for state-dependent regulation. Genestructure features, including gene length, were positively correlated with G and G×Cell-Type, while their associations with G×Cell-State varied in direction, indicating a more context-specific relationship.

Motivated by the gene level correlation results, we next examined how heritability is partitioned by minor allele frequency (MAF), where components under stronger directional or stabilizing selection are expected to have a larger contribution from lower frequency variants. Strikingly, low-frequency variants (MAF between 1% and 5%) accounted for 60% of G×Cell-State heritability, while highfrequency variants (MAF >20%) explained only 19% on average. In contrast, main genetic heritability was primarily driven by common variants (Fig. 4b): low-frequency variants accounted for only 9% of main genetic heritability, while high-frequency variants explained 60% on average. A similar paYern was observed for G×Cell-Type heritability, where low-frequency variants explained 9% and highfrequency variants explained 61%. Similar frequency partitions were observed at highly and lowly constrained genes (boYom and top 20% of pLI), although highly constrained genes exhibited a higher proportion of G×Cell-State heritability overall (Supplementary Fig. 28). Together, these results reveal a striking shift in allele-frequency architecture, with genetic effects that vary across continuous cell states disproportionately driven by low-frequency variants, particularly among constrained genes, in contrast to the common-variant-dominated architecture of average genetic effects and cell-type-level interactions.

Finally, we quantified the distribution of heritability with respect to physical distance from each gene.

It is well known that eQTLs tend to be tightly clustered near transcription start sites (TSSs) 81, 82.

Interestingly, G×Cell-State heritability exhibited distinct enrichment paYerns with respect to gene distance. Promoter regions, defined as a 2-kb window around the TSS, and promoter-proxy regions, defined as a 20-kb window around the TSS, explained 41% and 68% of main genetic heritability, and 31% and 53% of G×Cell-Type heritability, respectively, but accounted for only 3% and 6% of G×Cell-State heritability (Fig. 4c). Because GWAS-associated variants tend to lie farther from the nearest TSS than conventional eQTLs, this pronounced depletion of promoter-proximal effects suggests that G×Cell-State regulation may follow a more distal, GWAS-like regulatory architecture.

scHINT generalizes to perturbation single-cell data

Finally, to experimentally validate our findings of broad cell state dependence, we extended scHINT to data from perturbational screens. In this seYing, we define perturbation "heritability" as the proportion of expression variance explained by perturbation effects in perturbed cells. This extension allows scHINT to quantify both the average transcriptional response to a perturbation and the extent to which this response changes across continuous cell states or discrete cell types.

We first applied this framework to a large genome-wide perturb-seq data set from K562 cells 108 , which contains 2.06 million cells and 7,189 gene perturbations. Across response genes, the summed perturbation-by-cell-state component explained about 4-fold expression variance than the main perturbation component, indicating that perturbation effects often vary across transcriptional states rather than acting uniformly across cells (Fig. 7a). To prevent the PCs from capturing the perturbation itself, we also derived them from non-targeted control cells and projected this PC space onto the targeted cells. Heritability estimates obtained with control-derived PCs were highly concordant with those obtained with PCs derived from targeted cells (Pearson r = 0.992 for the main perturbation effect and r = 0.964 for the summed perturbation × cell-state component; Supplementary Fig. 38), indicating that PCs derived from control and from targeted cells capture consistent cellular programs and that the choice between them has liYle influence on the results.

Next, we applied scHINT to a multi-ome perturb-seq dataset containing 137,604 cells, 19 transcription factor knockouts and 10 cell types 109 . In this multi-cell-type perturbation seYing, perturbation-by-celltype interaction effects explained 4.5-fold more expression variance than main perturbation effects alone (Fig. 7b). Together, these analyses show that scHINT can be applied beyond germline genetic regulation to quantify context-dependent transcriptional responses in experimental data. The large perturbation-by-cell-state and perturbation-by-cell-type components indicate that perturbation effects vary substantially across cellular contexts, mirroring our findings in population-scale single-cell QTL data.

Discussion

A central unresolved question in human genetics is why many GWAS loci often cannot be explained by regulatory effects measured in bulk tissues or by conventional eQTL studies. Our method, scHINT, suggests that a substantial fraction of this missing regulatory signal arises because genetic effects vary across continuous cellular states rather than acting uniformly across cells. Across major brain cell types, the G×Cell-State heritability was often greater than the main genetic heritability, and the two components were only weakly correlated. Thus, genes with liYle detectable average genetic regulation can still show substantial state-dependent genetic effects.

These context-dependent effects also showed a markedly different genetic architecture from conventional eQTL effects and even from cell-type specific eQTL effects. Main genetic and G×Cell-Type heritability was driven primarily by common variants located near transcription start sites. In contrast, G×Cell-State heritability was disproportionately explained by low-frequency variants and variants farther from promoters. This more closely resembles the architecture of GWAS signals and suggests that cell-state-dependent regulation may help bridge the long-standing gap between disease-associated variants and steady-state eQTLs.

A related question is why GWAS loci frequently implicate evolutionarily constrained genes, whereas standard eQTLs are depleted near such genes. We found that main genetic heritability was negatively associated with gene constraint, while aggregated G×Cell-State heritability was positively associated with constraint. State-dependent regulation may affect functionally important genes without producing large constitutive changes in expression that would be strongly selected against 110.

To beYer understand and interpret G×Cell-State heritability, we evaluated its biological and disease relevance using several complementary analyses. G×Cell-State effects showed extensive pathway and disease enrichments that were largely absent for main genetic heritability. An external activation-eQTL dataset further provided orthogonal validation that the inferred cell-state axes capture genuine biological contexts, demonstrating that states derived from observational single-cell data can recover regulatory programs activated by experimental neuronal stimulation. Incorporating G×C effects into the TWAS framework also improved cell-level expression prediction and identified additional disease-risk genes with stronger concordance with rare-variant burden-test results. Together, these complementary lines of evidence support the robustness and functional importance of statedependent genetic regulation.

Our findings complement recent work by Chen et al. 55 , who developed CIGMA to quantify cell-typeshared and cell-type-specific genetic regulation across discrete blood cell types. Methodologically, CIGMA can be viewed as conceptually similar to our scHINT-G×Cell-Type model, which is applied to sample-level expression, with cell type serving as the interaction context. In contrast, scHINT-

G×Cell-State model operates directly on individual cells and models genotype interactions with continuous cell-state variation within a cell type. This distinction is important because substantial regulatory heterogeneity exists among cells assigned to the same cell type and cannot be captured by discrete cell-type labels or sample-level aggregation. Resolving such cell-level context therefore requires methods such as scHINT that can directly model single-cell observations while remaining computationally scalable to millions of cells. Interestingly, both approaches implicate selectively constrained genes, but reveal different paYerns of context dependence. Chen et al. found that constrained genes have lower absolute shared and cell-type-specific genetic variance but greater relative eQTL cell-type specificity in blood. In contrast, we find that constrained genes exhibit consistently greater absolute G×Cell-State heritability in brain. In blood, the direction of individual G×PC heritability effects varies across cell-state axes, but the relative contribution of G×Cell-State heritability is consistently greater in constrained genes, in line with the increased relative context specificity reported by Chen et al 55 . These results suggest that the brain may harbor particularly strong state-dependent genetic regulation at constrained genes, while selected blood cell states show similar features. Despite these tissue-specific differences, both analyses support a common paYern in which a greater fraction of genetic regulation at constrained genes emerges in specific cellular contexts. Together, these findings underscore the importance of studying both cell-type-specific and cell-state-specific effects to more fully characterize how genetic regulation varies across biological contexts.

Several limitations and opportunities for future development remain. First, although scHINT can prioritize genes with strong cell-state-dependent effects and reveals that G×Cell-State heritability is disproportionately contributed by low-frequency variants, it does not identify the causal variants underlying these interactions. Resolving such variants will require dedicated G×Cell-State QTL mapping at single-cell resolution. Our results support the feasibility and potential value of this direction, particularly for genes with weak main genetic heritability but substantial G×Cell-State heritability, whose regulatory effects may be obscured in conventional bulk or pseudobulk eQTL analyses. Second, scHINT models normalized cell-level expression within a Gaussian framework and therefore does not explicitly account for the sparsity of single-cell count data. This sparsity reflects technical factors, including sequencing depth, batch effects and molecular sampling, as well as biological processes such as transcriptional bursting. Poisson or negative-binomial models may beYer capture count distributions, but their computational cost remains substantial for datasets containing millions of cells. As population-scale single-cell datasets continue to grow, developing scalable cell-level models that retain appropriate count-based assumptions will be increasingly important 111 . Third, the definitions of both cell type and cell state remain dependent on analytical choices. Our analyses focused on variation within predefined cell types and therefore rely on the accuracy and consistency of cell-type annotation. Marker-based classifications may obscure transitional or difficultto-classify populations, while annotation strategies often differ across cohorts and resolutions.

Continuous cell-type assignments may provide a more robust alternative, particularly for differentiating or ambiguous populations. Similarly, the cell states analyzed here were inferred from scRNA-seq embeddings. Incorporating directly measured single-or multimodal contexts, including chromatin accessibility, protein abundance, spatial features and epigenomic profiles, could improve the interpretation of interaction effects. Fourth, to reduce computational burden, G×C-TWAS models were trained using approximately 10,000 metacells and restricted to HapMap3 variants. This approximation likely reduces cellular heterogeneity and may miss signals driven by lower-frequency variants. More computationally efficient prediction models could improve both resolution and power. Extending mediation frameworks such as MESC to explicitly model G×C-regulated expression could further quantify the proportion of disease heritability mediated through cell-state-dependent genetic effects.

Finally, our application of scHINT to perturbation datasets identified substantial perturbation-bycell-type and perturbation-by-cell-state interaction effects. This context specificity presents an important challenge for the development of virtual cell models: accurate prediction of perturbation responses will require models that capture how genetic, environmental and experimental perturbations interact with both cell identity and cell state.

In summary, scHINT reveals that cell-state-dependent genetic effects represent a substantial and distinct component of gene-expression regulation that is largely missed by bulk and conventional eQTL analyses. By linking these effects to constrained genes, disease-relevant pathways, TWAS associations and perturbation responses, scHINT provides a scalable framework for characterizing how genetic effects vary across continuous cellular contexts.

Processing of OneK1K scRNAseq data

We analyzed the OneK1K scRNA-seq, which includes 1,248,980 cells from 981 donors and provides raw UMI counts for cells that passed the original quality control, together with Azimuth level-2 annotations. We grouped cells into 15 fine cell types: CD4 Naive, TCM, TEM, and CTL; CD8 Naive, TCM, and TEM; B naive, intermediate, and memory; NK, NK CD56bright, and NK proliferating; and

CD14 and CD16 monocytes. These cell types contained 1,660-272,553 cells from 661-928 donors. We retained genes detected in at least 1% of cells within each cell type, resulting in 9,161-10,065 genes.

We normalized UMI counts as log1p(TP10K) using each cell's total count across the retained genes.

Within each cell type, we regressed normalized expression for each gene on sex, age, percentage of mitochondrial reads, sequencing pool, genotype PCs 1-5, and log library size using ordinary least squares. We then computed cell state principal components from the residual expression of highly expressed genes within each cell type.

Cell-State estimation

To define continuous cell-state axes for scHINT, we evaluated multiple approaches, including withincell-type principal component analysis (PCA 67 ), across-cell-type PCA, residual PCA 69 , and consensus non-negative matrix factorization (cNMF 70 ). For PCA-based cell-state construction, we used the residualized meta-cell expression profiles as input.

For within-cell-type PCA, this procedure was performed separately within each major brain cell type, allowing cell-state axes to capture transcriptional heterogeneity specific to each cell type. For acrosscell-type PCA, PCA was performed using meta-cells from all major cell types together, and the resulting PCs were then used as shared cell-state axes across cell types. We also applied the residPCA framework, a recently proposed approach for identifying disease-associated or contextdependent cellular states from single-cell RNA-seq data. In parallel, we applied cNMF to identify non-negative gene expression programs and used the resulting program usage scores as alternative cell-state representations.

We compared these different cell-state definitions in downstream scHINT analyses and used them to evaluate the robustness of genotype-by-cell-state heritability estimates across alternative representations of cellular heterogeneity.

Partitioning heritability by minor allele frequency and distance to the TSS

To partition heritability by variant class, we extended scHINT to include multiple genetic relationship matrices (GRMs), each calculated from a subset of the cis-SNPs, and calculated a corresponding G×PC term for each GRM. For the minor allele frequency (MAF) partition, SNPs were grouped into low-frequency (1-5%), intermediate (5-20%) and high-frequency (>20%) classes. For the partition by distance to the transcription start site (TSS), SNPs were grouped into those inside and outside a window around the TSS, using a ±1-kb promoter window and a ±10-kb promoter-proxy window.

The proportion of G (or G×Cell-State) heritability aYributable to each class was calculated as the variance component of that class divided by the sum across classes. The same partition was applied to the G×Cell-Type model.

Stratified LDSC analysis of disease heritability enrichment

To evaluate whether genes with high expression heritability were enriched for disease-associated genetic risk, we performed stratified linkage disequilibrium score regression (S-LDSC) using GWAS summary statistics from seven brain-related diseases and traits. For each heritability model, genes were ranked by their estimated heritability, and the top 300 genes were selected to define a gene set.

We performed this procedure separately for each heritability component, including main genetic heritability and interaction heritability from the one-cell-type, two-cell-type, all-cell-type, and cellstate scHINT models.

For each selected gene set, we constructed a binary genomic annotation by including variants located within a 100-kb window around the transcription start site of each gene. These annotations were then tested using S-LDSC to estimate whether SNP heritability for each brain-related disease was enriched near genes with high expression heritability. Disease heritability enrichment was quantified as the proportion of SNP heritability explained by the annotation divided by the proportion of SNPs included in the annotation. Enrichment p values were obtained from the S-LDSC regression coefficient for each annotation.

Meta-cell analysis

To reduce sparsity and computational burden in estimating heritability using scGeneHE and building TWAS gene expression prediction model, we constructed donor-specific meta-cells. Meta-cell construction was performed separately for each dataset and each major brain cell type.

Within each donor and cell type, raw counts were first normalized to transcripts per 10,000 reads before microclustering. Microclustering was performed using the "applyMicroClustering" function from the VISION R package 96 , which groups transcriptionally similar cells prior to aggregation. The target number of cells per microcluster was determined adaptively based on the total number of cells, with the goal of retaining approximately 50,000 meta-cells per cell type for scGeneHE and 10,000 meta-cells for TWAS. Donors with too few cells were assigned to a single fallback meta-cell containing all available cells from that donor and cell type.

After microclustering, gene expression was aggregated on the raw-count scale. For meta-cell (k), the count for gene (g) was defined as the sum of raw UMI counts across all cells assigned to that microcluster, where (𝑀 5 ) denotes the set of cells in meta-cell (k).

Thus, the resulting meta-cell expression matrix represents summed UMI counts and remains suitable for downstream count-based normalization and modeling.

We also summarized covariates at the meta-cell level. Library size was computed by summing total UMI counts across cells within each microcluster. Mitochondrial percentage was summarized as the total cell-level mitochondrial fraction across all cells in the microcluster. Donor-level variables, including study, sex, age and genetic principal components, were assigned uniformly to all meta-cells derived from the same donor.

Individual genotypes define a genetic relatedness matrix (GRM), which is expanded to the cell level by mapping each cell to its donor; continuous cell-state features (for example, within-cell-type principal components) define a cell-state relationship matrix (CS-RM). Expression of each cell is modelled as the sum of a main genetic effect (G), gene × cell-state interaction effects (G×Cell-State; element-wise product of the expanded GRM and the CS-RM), marginal cell-state effects, effects of other sample-and cell-level covariates, an individual-level effect shared by all cells of a donor, and cell-level residual noise. Expression variance is thereby partitioned into genetic (σ X $ ), G×Cell-State (σ X×W $ ), cell-state (σ W $ ), other-covariate (σ Y $ ), individual-level (σ Z $ ) and residual (σ [ $ ) components. To avoid constructing the full cell-by-cell matrices, scHINT accumulates sufficient statistics separately from within-individual and between-individual cell pairs and estimates the variance components by solving the resulting Haseman-Elston normal equations.
