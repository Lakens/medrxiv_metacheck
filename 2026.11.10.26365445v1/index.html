To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 6 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

While the risk of cognitive impairment and dementia increases with advanced age, a fraction of individuals maintains high levels of cognitive function at extreme ages. Here, we compared whole-exome sequencing variant frequency data for 136 centenarian and offspring cognitive superagers, whose episodic memory performance matches normative expectations for persons ~30 years younger, 114 average-cognition controls, and the gnomAD population reference database. A rare variant allele frequency analysis identified 114 rare coding variants significantly enriched in cognitive superagers, of which the ten most significant variants were exceedingly rare missense variants residing in nine genes implicating neurovascular biology (LOX, CLEC4M, ZNG1F), immune regulation (HLA-B, HLA-DRB1, ITGAX, MUC4, MUC1), and neuronal structure (CEP131, two variants). Together, these findings support the study of an extreme phenotype as a tractable framework for prioritizing rare coding variation and nominating a set of candidate loci for functional follow-up to help elucidate protective genetic mechanisms in cognitive superagers.

Introduction

Advances in public health and medicine have markedly extended human life expectancy, illustrated by a 50% increase in the United States centenarian population from 2010 to 2020 1 with projections estimating 3.2 million centenarians worldwide by 2050 2 . However, older age is the strongest risk factor for cognitive impairment, late-onset Alzheimer's disease (AD) and vascular dementia. Alzheimer's disease and related dementias (ADRD) risk rises steeply with age: prevalence in the US increases from 3% at ages 65-69 to 35% at 90 and older 3 , reaches 53% in women and 45.5% in men beyond age 95 4 , and incidence climbs from 21.2% per year at ages 95-99 to 40.7% per year after 100 5.

AD is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by profound cognitive impairment. Although anti-amyloid immunotherapies have recently led to modest slowing of clinical decline in early disease, treatment options remain limited 6, 7 . Early-onset AD (<65 years) is largely attributable to rare, highly penetrant variants 8 . Late-onset AD is substantially heritable, with estimates of 58-79%, yet, beyond APOE, the strongest AD risk factor, common-variant genome-wide association studies (GWAS) identified roughly 150 AD-associated loci, which explain only about one-third of the inheritability of AD [9] [10] [11] [12] [13] [14] . Since standard GWAS pipelines typically filter out extremely rare variants, whole-exome sequencing has become a key approach for directly capturing rare protein-coding variation, implicating genes such as TREM2, SORL1, ABCA7, ATP8B4, and ABCA1 15, 16.

Notably, reaching extreme old age is also highly heritable with recent heritability estimates of human life span over 50% 17, 18 . There are likely many different genetic signatures associated with survival to 100 that vary according to ancestry and prevalent non-genetic influences of exceptional longevity. Survival to even more extreme ages involves a more distinct and rare genetic profile 19 , including enrichment of protective alleles and depletion of Alzheimer's disease (AD) risk alleles, such as APOE ε4 [19] [20] [21] [22].

Although the prevalence of cognitive decline and dementia is high in the older adult population, these conditions are not inevitable consequences of aging, as evidenced by the portion of the population that reaches age 100 and beyond, while maintaining relatively high levels of cognitive and physical function 23, 24 . These exceptional individuals raise fundamental questions about the biological mechanisms that preserve cognitive ability and delay dementia onset at very advanced ages, and whether genetics influence these protective features. Protective mechanisms may confer resistance to amyloid and tau accumulation in the brain or resilience to cognitive decline despite the presence of AD pathology 25, 26 . Because familial clustering of extreme longevity points to rare variants with larger effects than the modest common-variant associations, comparing extreme phenotypes has proven a powerful discovery strategy [27] [28] [29] [30] . For example, using cognitively healthy centenarians as controls nearly doubled the effect sizes of known AD variants 22, 31 , enabled a polygenic score for protection against AD 32 , and, in a comparison of the top 1% of survivors with population controls, identified multiple longevity loci 33.

In the New England Centenarian Study (NECS), 12-25% of centenarians exhibit no symptoms of clinical dementia, and roughly 90% of those who eventually develop cognitive impairment remained cognitively intact well into their 90s 24 . These individuals are not merely surviving longer; evidence from Amsterdam's 100+ study shows they maintain prolonged periods of stable, high cognitive function 34 and are substantially more likely than cognitively impaired centenarians to survive to ages 105 and beyond 35.

An even smaller subset of centenarians, approximately 10% of the NECS centenarians, not only avoid cognitive decline and clinical dementia at advanced ages but have exceptional episodic memory, performing similarly to people 2-3 decades younger. Such individuals are called "cognitive superagers" [36] [37] [38] . A substantial portion of their offspring likewise are "cognitive superagers" and exhibit approximately half the rate of age-related diseases and disability compared to their birth cohort 35, 39 , representing a related and materially informative opportunity for discovery 40 . Numerous studies support the hypothesis that the exceptional cognitive preservation observed in extremely elderly individuals reflects active protective mechanisms that confer resistance or resilience to cognitive impairment and dementia at least through the mid-nineties 17, 41, 42 , rather than reflecting the relative absence of neurodegenerative risk factors.

Here, we analyzed whole exome sequencing data from the Resilience/Resistance to Alzheimer's Disease in Centenarians and Offspring (RADCO) study to identify protective genetic signals that support late-age cognitive preservation. By comparing rare variant allele frequencies across cognitive superagers, selected from centenarians and their offspring, whose episodic memory performance matches normative expectations for persons ~30 years younger, with average-cognition RADCO participants as well as a population-level reference panel (gnomAD), we identified rare functional coding variants that are significantly enriched in cognitive superagers. These loci should help elucidate protective genetic mechanisms and associated biological pathways contributing to healthy brain aging.

Results

The RADCO study is designed to identify the biological basis of cognitive resilience and resistance to AD in a cohort of centenarians, their offspring, and offspring spouses. RADCO participants are identified from ongoing studies of centenarians and their offspring (UH3AG064704: The Integrative Longevity Omics Study and U19AG023122: the Centenarian Project of the Longevity Consortium) and are classified into cognitive superagers, individuals whose performance on memory tasks matches normative expectations for persons ~30 years younger, and individuals with average-cognition. A total of 256 RADCO participants (138 cognitive superagers and 118 subjects with average-cognition) met eligibility criteria to be included in the primary cognitive superager status endophenotype cohort, with 55 subjects being centenarians. After quality control and covariate exclusions (see Methods), 231 participants (129 cognitive superagers and 102 controls) were included for downstream single variant and gene-burden association analyses. The primary allele-frequency analysis included 250 RADCO participants (136 cognitive superagers and 114 controls) of European ancestry. In addition, we had access to a secondary endophenotype, cognitive status, available for 1292 WES subjects from the American Federation for Aging Research (AFAR) cohort. Discussion

Here we present the largest whole-exome sequencing analyses of clinically defined cognitive superagers conducted to date. Using complementary single-variant, gene-based burden, and extreme-phenotype allele-frequency frameworks, we identified 114 rare coding variants significantly enriched in cognitive superagers relative to population-scale reference controls. We highlight the top ten tier 1 candidate variants, which converge on three biologically coherent themes: immune regulation, neurovascular biology, and neuronal structure.

While standard single variant-level and gene-based burden analyses did not identify exome-wide significant associations with the cognitive superager phenotype, likely due to low sample size, we observed suggestive signals at several loci. These include DBH, SLC25A23, PATJ, HLA-DOB, and SPATA31A1 in the single-variant analysis, and PGAP2, IGSF10, PRDM1, PTGDS, HSPE1P22, DQX1, and AP002907.1 in the gene-based burden analysis. DBH was the leading single-variant locus, with four linked variants depleted in cognitive superagers. DBH encodes dopamine beta-hydroxylase, which converts dopamine to norepinephrine. Previous studies have reported small effects of DBH on AD risk, with stronger interaction signals by sex, inflammatory context, and gene-gene epistasis 44, 45 , or by AD biomarkers such as CSF p-tau181 46 . The tight junction scaffolding gene PATJ has been linked to frailty phenotypes 47 related to broader age-related physiology. PGAP2 was the leading burden locus. Biallelic hypomorphic PGAP2 variants disrupt GPI-anchor remodeling and cause neurodevelopmental syndromes, supporting neurological relevance but not an established role in AD or cognitive 48, 49 . Suggestive signals at the immune regulator PRDM1 and the prostaglandin D2 synthase PTGDS provide additional hypotheses for follow-up [50] [51] [52] [53].

The strongest findings arose from the two-way allele-frequency analysis, which required enrichment in cognitive superagers versus gnomAD NFE while controls remained concordant with gnomAD NFE. After functional, family-lineage, and Hardy-Weinberg filters, 114 significant candidates were retained (10 conservative tier 1 and 104 broader tier 2). This design reduces cohort-wide technical or ancestry signals. An exploratory over-representation analysis of the 114 genes did not resolve a single dominant pathway; rather, it converged nominally on immune and antigen-presentation processes, mucin-type O-glycosylation, and complement/phagosome function, supporting the biological themes that dominate the tier 1 variants discussed below.

Our ten tier 1 variants span three broad functional themes: neurovascular biology, immune regulation, and neuronal structure. The theme of neurovascular biology is represented by LOX (vascular matrix crosslinking, CAA), ZNG1F (intracranial aneurysm expression signature), and CLEC4M (endothelial clearance). This theme is consistent with a large body of evidence linking preserved cerebrovascular integrity to cognitive aging 54, 55.

LOX and its paralogs LOXL1-LOXL4 are extracellular amine oxidases that catalyze collagen and elastin cross-linkage, thereby setting vessel-wall tissue stiffness and governing the matrix remodeling that maintains vascular integrity 56, 57 . Increased LOX-family signaling has been linked with senescence-associated phenotypes, including fibrosis and vascular remodeling 58 . Directly relevant to the neurovascular theme, LOX has been reported to localize with amyloid-beta pathology and is associated with cerebral arteriolar stiffening and degeneration 59- 61 . Along these lines, pharmacological inhibition of LOX has been proposed as a strategy to improve intramural periarterial drainage and reduce the accumulation of Aβ in the parenchyma and within blood vessel walls 62 . In silico profiling with AlphaGenome predicts a large histonemodification effect for the cognitive superager-enriched LOX variant, S132F, in brain and neurons, consistent with the possibility that this allele affects chromatin state at a locus already implicated in vascular matrix remodeling. S132F resides in the propeptide and replaces a small polar residue with a bulky hydrophobic one suggesting a possible effect on precursor handling versus mature-enzyme function.

CLEC4M encodes CD209L, a sinusoidal endothelial C-type lectin involved in vascular ligand handling and clearance at the immune-endothelial interface 63 . Human genetic and functional studies have shown that CLEC4M regulates circulating coagulation factor levels [64] [65] [66] . We found the rare CLEC4M variant, Q118R, enriched in cognitive superagers and showed moderate predicted RNA-seq and histone-modification scores in AlphaGenome, supporting continued evaluation of this locus as a vascular-immune interface candidate. Q118R adds a positive charge to the extracellular domain possibly affecting receptor oligomerization. ZNG1F was also identified among genes with elevated intraluminal expression in human intracranial aneurysms, nominating it as a tentative marker of cerebrovascular vessel-wall pathology 67 . The enriched rare ZNG1F missense mutation, L220I, might possibly affect the metal-ion binding zinc-chaperone or GTPase activities of this protein.

With regard to the immune regulation theme, the HLA findings align with prior associations of this region with longevity and neurodegeneration. The nominal association of the classical allele HLA-DRB1*04:01 with the secondary phenotype (cognitive status) was directionally concordant with its reported protective association with AD 68 , while the FDRsignificant HLA-DPB1*02:01 dominant-model result may represent a novel cognitive-aging signal requiring replication. In addition, Joshi et al. 20 reported an association of HLA-DQA1/DRB1 with longevity. Both HLA gene rare variants exhibit strong enrichment in cognitive superagers relative to gnomAD NFE and are absent in the RADCO average-cognition subjects.

The enriched HLA-B missense mutation, D201Y, resides at the end of the extracellular α2 domain and may potentially alter local stability, peptide presentation, or receptor interactions. The S66P HLA-DRB1 missense mutation enriched in cognitive superagers is a nonconservative change in the peptide-binding β1 domain and may potentially affect antigen specificity.

ITGAX encodes the CD11c integrin alpha-X subunit, which pairs with CD18 to form complement receptor 4 (CR4), a defining marker of a microglial subpopulation that accumulates around amyloid plaques and has been characterized as the "disease-associated microglia" (DAM) signature in mouse and human AD brain 69, 70 . Although ITGAX has not been identified as an AD-risk locus in genome-wide association studies, the central role of CD11c-marked microglia in the response to amyloid pathology makes it a biologically plausible candidate for influencing late-life cognitive trajectory. The enriched ITGAX missense mutation, A242V, resides in the extracellular αI ligand-binding domain could potentially affect ligand affinity.

AlphaGenome predicted moderate promoter and chromatin effects for the tier 1 immune variants HLA-B, HLA-DRB1, and ITGAX. Among tier 2 candidates, the TNFSF18 frameshift showed near-maximal predicted splice disruption (0.99), suggesting that some non-tier 1 hits may act through transcript instability or loss of function rather than missense effects alone. Functionally, these immune findings are aligned with a model in which HLA-mediated antigen presentation, complement-receptor engagement, and microglial activation jointly modulate maladaptive neuroinflammation, offering a plausible mechanism for preserved cognition in advanced age. Altered antigen presentation could influence microglial activation states, complement deposition on synapses, and clearance of amyloid-β, all of which are implicated in AD pathogenesis 71, 72.

The mucin family is represented by two tier 1 variants in MUC1 and MUC4. Both genes encode large transmembrane mucins best characterized at epithelial barriers and in cancer, organ-specific fibrosis, and chronic inflammation [73] [74] [75] [76] [77] . MUC1 domains have also been shown to physically interact with immune response-related proteins, including receptors for advanced glycation end products (RAGE), major histocompatibility complex II (MHC-II), and extracellular matrix receptor integrin alpha 2 (ITGA2). The cognitive superager-enriched MUC1 missense mutation, P423T introduces a potential O-glycosylation acceptor. MUC4 is a large cell-surface mucin involved in epithelial protection, adhesion-related behavior, and ERBB2-associated signaling. The enriched MUC4 missense mutation, V3971L, might potentially affect Erb-B2 receptor tyrosine kinase 2 (ERBB2) signaling. Interestingly, ERBB2 (HER2) has been implicated in AD as a suppressor of autophagic clearance of APP C-terminal fragment C99, thereby enhancing Aβ production 78 . MUC1 and MUC4 may also affect the function of Erb-B2 receptor tyrosine kinase 4 (ERBB4), which has recently been shown to exacerbate tau-tangle propagation in response to amyloid pathology in excitatory neurons 79 . Thus, these two genes may be directly related to resilience to AD pathology.

The third functional theme, neuronal structure, is represented by CEP131 (centrosome function, axonogenesis), in which two missense variants were found to be enriched in cognitive superagers. Both of these CEP131 variants were consistently observed together in 7 subjects, suggesting strong linkage disequilibrium. CEP131 has previously been implicated in frontotemporal dementia (FTD) with suggestive SNPs pertaining to both protective and risk haplotypes 80 , implicating impaired neuronal development, maturation, and axonogenesis. CEP131 has also been implicated in Parkinson's disease pathogenesis via de novo mutations 81 . Functionally, the missense mutation, S642G, increases backbone flexibility and could therefore affect protein interactions, or possibly eliminate a phosphorylation site. The other mutation, H238R, may potentially alter electrostatic interactions and binding to centrosomal partners.

A key strength of this study is the use of an extremely rare, robustly characterized phenotype centered on cognitive superagers, a subset of which have already reached extreme ages of 100+ years, in order to detect enrichment of extremely rare variants [82] [83] [84] . This population is inherently enriched for protective genetic architecture, thus this design concentrates biological signals in a manner not achievable in standard case-control studies 22 . Our pairwise Fisher's tests, across three groups design, minimized possible sequencing artifacts from the RADCO dataset.

Our study is not without limitations. Although it represents one of the largest rare variant analyses of cognitive superagers conducted to date, statistical power is constrained by the modest cohort size. Accordingly, the direct comparison within RADCO of cognitive superagers with average-cognition controls identified no variants after multiple-testing correction (smallest unadjusted P = 4.29 × 10 -6 ), so evidence for enrichment rests on the comparison with the external gnomAD reference, which is subject to cohort-specific technical differences. It is important to note that Fisher's exact tests cannot be adjusted for covariates. However, the extreme-phenotype enrichment framework was chosen because the Fisher's-exact framework retains full statistical resolution under sparse 2 x 2 tables 85, 86 . It is also important to note that due to the high inter-sample size imbalance when comparing the gnomAD NFE reference population (n = ~500,000) to our cognitive superager subgroup (n = 136), odds ratios from Fisher's exact tests may be unstable. Finally, to minimize confounding from population stratification, we restricted the current analysis to unrelated individuals of predominantly European ancestry. Expanding future efforts to more ancestrally diverse populations will be critical to detect population-specific variation and ensure that insights into genetically based optimal cognitive aging are broadly generalizable across human populations.

Collectively, our findings support a model in which cognitive superaging reflects active genetic protection rather than a mere absence of risk. More broadly, our results demonstrate that extreme-phenotype sampling is a tractable and biologically informative strategy for accessing protective genetic architecture and establishing a focused roadmap for the mechanistic dissection of cognitive resilience.

Methods

Study Populations

The Resilience/Resistance to Alzheimer's Disease in Centenarians and Offspring (RADCO) eligible participants are identified from ongoing studies of centenarians and their offspring (UH3AG064704: The Integrative Longevity Omics Study and U19AG023122: the Centenarian Project of the Longevity Consortium). RADCO seeks to determine the protective factors and underlying biological mechanisms that confer this extreme phenotype of optimal cognitive aging. The RADCO sample is comprised of centenarian cognitive superagers, centenarians with average-cognition, centenarian offspring cognitive superagers, offspring with average-cognition, and spouses of the offspring as a referent cohort. Phenotypic measures include sociodemographic, medical history, and lifestyle questionnaires; a neuropsychological assessment; blood pressure and grip strength measures; accelerometry; biospecimen collection (i.e., blood and stool); and an informant interview.

Participants in RADCO are classified as "cognitive superager" or "average-cognition" based on neuropsychological test performance criteria. Cognitive superagers performed at or above the normative mean for someone 30 years younger on the Hopkins Verbal Learning Test-Revised (HVLT-R) delayed recall, and at least average performance relative to same-aged peers on non-memory tests (i.e., semantic fluency [animals] and letter fluency [FAS]. The HVLT-R delayed recall cutoffs were ≥ 10 words for offspring and spouses and ≥ 8 words for centenarians. Participants with average-cognition performed within the average range (i.e., 1 standard deviation for offspring and 1.5 standard deviations for centenarians) on HVLT-R delayed recall and the non-memory tests compared to same-aged peers. Because episodic memory normative data are not available for centenarians, average values for nonagenarians were used instead. This primary endophenotype consists of 256 subjects from whom whole exome sequence data were generated. 138 were cognitive superagers and 118 subjects classified as having average-cognition based on the above definitions. This sample includes 55 centenarians, of which 20 were cognitive superagers and 35 had average-cognition (Table 1A).

Downstream association analyses used WES data from the American Federation for Aging Research (AFAR) sample of centenarians and offspring and a secondary binary endophenotype of cognitive status (cognitively normal vs cognitively impaired) derived from standardized cognitive screener and/or informant interviews (i.e., a Dementia Questionnaire) 87 . The secondary cognitive-status analysis included 507 centenarians (39.2% of 1,292 participants), comprising 220 cognitively normal participants (MMSE ≥ 24) and 287 cognitively impaired (MMSE ≤ 23) (Table 1B).

Cognitive status was determined by one or more of the following: the Mini-Mental State Examination 88 , the Telephone Interview for Cognitive Status -Modified 89, 90 , and the Blessed Information-Memory-Concentration Test 91 . Established cut-points were used to dichotomize each instrument: MMSE ≤ 23 was coded as impaired and ≥ 24 as normal; TICS-M ≤ 25 as impaired and ≥ 26 as normal; BIMC ≤ 26 as impaired and ≥ 27 as normal 92 ; and/or endorsement of memory problems and functional impairments on the Dementia Questionnaire according to the algorithm by Kawas et al. 93 . Assessments marked as participant refusal or invalid due to hearing, environmental, or dispositional factors were excluded. Administrations coded as "unable due to dementia" were coded as impaired. The resulting binary cognitivestatus variable was used as the secondary endophenotype in downstream association analyses, yielding 900 cognitively normal and 392 cognitively impaired participants.

Study Data and Pre-Processing

Whole Exome Sequencing (WES) was performed via funding from the American Federation for Aging Research (AFAR) using samples from multiple centenarian and offspring studies. Briefly, exome capture was performed using a custom reagent kit from Kapa Biosystems using a fully automated approach at Regeneron. A unique 6-base pair barcode was added to each DNA fragment during library preparation to facilitate multiplexed exome capture and sequencing. Multiplexed samples were sequenced using 75 bp paired-end sequencing on an Illumina v4 HiSeq 2500 to a coverage depth sufficient to provide greater than 20x haploid read depth. Raw sequence data from each Illumina HiSeq 2500 run were uploaded to DNAnexus for sequence read alignment and variant identification. Raw sequence data were converted from BCL files to sample-specific FASTQ-files, which were aligned to human reference build GRCh37.p13 with BWA-MEM 94, 95 . Single nucleotide variants (SNV) and insertion/deletion (INDEL) sequence variants were identified with the Genome Analysis Toolkit 96 . The dataset was then lifted-over to hg38 for ease of use.

WES data for 1473 subjects was received in GDS format and converted to variant call format (VCF) using seqGDS2VCF 97 . Variants were functionally annotated with SnpEff version 5.2 (build 2023-09-29 06:17) 98 using the GRCh38.99 reference genome. Annotated VCFs were indexed with tabix and converted to PLINK 1.9's binary files (.bed/.bim/.fam) to enable genomewide association analysis. Multiallelic variants were normalized using bcftools v1.11, which split multiallelic sites into multiple biallelic records. All downstream analyses used this normalized dataset, applying additional variant-level filters appropriate to each analysis stage. As of June 2026, 256 subjects with WES data had been classified with a clinical cognitive superager status by RADCO. 1292 subjects from the AFAR dataset had a secondary phenotype available (cognitively normal vs. cognitively impaired).

To identify cryptic relatedness and potential familial clusters, pairwise kinship coefficients were estimated using PLINK 2.0's --make-king-table function, which implements the KINGrobust method 99 . First-degree relationships (parent-child or full siblings) were identified by kinship coefficients above 0.177 for which there were 46 relational pairs and second-degree relation was defined as a kinship coefficient between 0.125 to 0.177 for which there were zero in our cohort. Cutoffs were set as per the Plink2 documentation 99 . Five first-degree family groups were detected, defined as clusters where all individuals shared kinship coefficients above 0.177. Allele frequencies were subsequently summarized within each family group and considered during the analyses to assess whether shared rare alleles arose from familial inheritance rather than independent occurrence related to the target endophenotype; a full summary of this family analysis can be found in Supplementary Table B2. For the single-variant and burden analyses, principal components were computed from an LD-pruned variant set to control for population structure; the number of principal components retained as covariates was determined by the scree plot of computed eigenvalues.

To place RADCO whole-exome samples in a common ancestry framework, we projected study genotypes onto the principal-component (PC) space of the 1000 Genomes Project Phase 3 reference panel 100 . Reference and study variants were harmonized on hg38 and intersected by chromosome and position. The reference panel was restricted to the lifted 1000 Genomes common-variant set used for ancestry inference. Cryptically related reference individuals were removed using KING-robust kinship estimates 99 , and reference outliers were excluded after an initial autosomal singular value decomposition (SVD), using the local outlier statistic S computed with bigutilsr::prob_dist on the reference PC scores, retaining individuals with S < 0.5. Study samples were then projected into the reference PC space using optimal ancestry projection (OADP) 14 as implemented in bigsnpr (bed_projectPCA) 101 , which matches variants between reference and target data, recomputes the reference SVD on the matched loci, and projects study genotypes without refitting the reference basis on study samples. Projected PCs were visualized with reference samples colored by 1000 Genomes super-population (AFR, AMR, EAS, EUR, SAS) and RADCO participants colored by self-reported race from study metadata. This ancestral projection, Supplementary Figure 1, in 245 subjects retaining their European labels, 5 subjects being re-labeled as European instead of their self-reported races and 6 subjects retaining their labels as non-European. This projection was used to assess overall genetic ancestry and population structure relative to global reference populations; it was complementary to, and separate from, the LD-pruned principal components generated within RADCO and included as covariates in association analyses.

Single Variant Association Analysis

Single-variant association testing was conducted to identify variants associated with cognitive superager status using a case-control design. Sample-level QC removed one individual that exceeded our heterozygosity and missingness thresholds, leaving 249 samples for variant-level processing. To control for population structure and possible batch effects, we calculated 20 principal components in PLINK v2.0.0-a.6.9LM from 83,046 LD-pruned, autosomal, biallelic SNPs identified by independent-pair pruning with a 50-SNP window, 5-SNP step, and an LD threshold of 0.5 (input set: variants with genotype call rate ≥ 98%, HWEP ≥ 1 × 10 -6 , and minor allele frequency ≥ 1%). Variant filtering for the single-variant analysis applied the following criteria, in order: (i) Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium (HWE)P ≥ 1 × 10 -10 assessed in the 114 average-cognition control samples (2,061 variants removed), (ii) genotype call rate ≥ 98% (≤ 2% missingness; 116,094 variants removed), and (iii) minor allele frequency ≥ 0.01% to retain high-confidence rare variants and remove monogenic variants (corresponds to a minor allele count of 5, 855,144 variants removed). After variant-and sample-level QC, 418,278 variants remained in 249 samples.

Restricting to participants with complete covariate data left a final analysis set of 231 individuals (129 cognitive superagers, 102 average-cognition controls). Logistic regression was performed using REGENIE v3.5 43 with cognitive superager status as the binary outcome and gender, cognitive age (treated as the midpoint of deidentified age categories; e.g., 100-104 → 102), and the first three principal components (PC1-PC3, retained based on inspection of the scree plot of eigenvalues) as covariates. Analyses were run with a block size of 1,000 and approximate Firth correction enabled at a P-value threshold of 0.05 to mitigate small-sample bias.

Gene-based Burden Analysis

Rare genetic variants individually tend to lack the statistical power to reach exome-wide significance; to account for this, gene-based burden testing was performed. We employed REGENIE v3.5 43 to aggregate rare variant effects within genes for cognitive superager status. Variant-level QC for the burden analysis was deliberately less stringent than for the singlevariant analysis to preserve sparse rare-variant signal: (i) HWEP ≥ 1 × 10 -15 (mid-p adjustment) assessed in the 114 average-cognition control samples (1,149 variants removed); (ii) genotype call rate ≥ 90% (≤ 10% missingness; 48,160 variants removed); and (iii) minor allele frequency ≥ 0.01% to remove monogenic variants (882,563 variants removed). After QC, 459,705 variants remained, aggregated into 29,376 gene sets across the same 231-individual analysis cohort used in the single-variant analysis (129 cognitive superager cases, 102 average-cognition controls).

To further distill our analysis to variants most likely to have the largest effect, variant functional categories were assigned based on SnpEff annotation. Loss-of-function (LoF) variants were defined as those with SnpEff annotations including frameshift_variant, splice_acceptor_variant, splice_donor_variant, splice_region_variant, start_lost, stop_gained, or stop_lost. Missense variants included missense_variant and missense_variant&splice_region_variant annotations, and all other functional annotations were coded as "other." We restricted our rare-variant analysis to predicted LoF and missense variants, as these mutation classes are most consistently associated with human disease phenotypes and most likely to exert meaningful biological effects [102] [103] [104] . Synonymous variants were placed in the "other" category as they are functionally silent at the protein level and serve as neutral controls in variant-class enrichment analyses 105.

A gene-based set list was generated by grouping annotated variants by gene symbol using REGENIE's gene-set specification format. Four masks were manually defined in Supplementary Table B9 from the functional categories above: (i) loss-of-function only, (ii) missense only, (iii) combined loss-of-function + missense, and (iv) "other" (synonymous and non-coding). Variants were stratified by allele frequency into bins of MAF ≤ 0.5%, ≤ 1%, ≤ 5%, and singletons, with rare variants of MAC ≤ 10 collapsed within masks. Variant-level weights were assigned using a Beta(MAF; 1, 25) function to upweight rare variants. The final regression model used the binary cognitive superager status as the endophenotype, with gender, age, and PC1-PC3 as covariates and approximate Firth correction applied to account for small-sample bias; a block size of 1,000 was used and a minimum MAC threshold of 5 was applied as in the single-variant analysis.

REGENIE's RGC-GENE-P test derives a single gene-level omnibus P-value by combining results from multiple burden tests, including ACATV (collapsing burden) and SKATO-ACAT (additive and heterogeneous rare-variant effects), via the Adaptive Cauchy Combination Test (ACAT). By testing across multiple frequency-stratified masks and applying joint ACAT combination, this framework maximizes statistical power to identify genes in which aggregated variants, regardless of frequency or functional impact, are associated with the cognitive superager endophenotype, while controlling Type I error through Firth correction for small, expected cell counts 43 . REGENIE also reported that 28,786 catalogued sets included variants absent from the exome panel and/or not mappable to the annotation file, and that a subset of 2,209 sets contained only "unknown" variants under the mask rules and were ignored, issues that mainly affect which genes can accumulate detectable signal rather than the validity of Pvalues for successfully tested sets 43, 86 . Of the 29,376 gene sets tested, 6,765 (23%) yielded no gene-level p-value, predominantly because the constituent variants either failed to match the genotype/annotation data (93% of dropped sets) or, for genes with only 1-2 rare variants, failed to meet the minimum mask allele count (MAC ≥ 5) required for burden mask construction.

To assess whether established Alzheimer's disease risk and protective signals showed consistent effects in the RADCO cognitive superager cohort independent of exome-wide significance, we performed a targeted lookup of known AD-associated variants and genes against both the single-variant and gene-based burden summary statistics described above. The lookup set comprised the 109 variants reported by Bellenguez et al. 10 , together with the two APOE variants rs429358 and rs7412, which jointly define the ε2/ε3/ε4 alleles 106 . Each Bellenguez index variant was additionally mapped to a single gene for the burden-test comparison using the prioritized causal gene reported by Bellenguez et al. 10 ; where multiple genes were reported per locus, the highest-confidence assignment was used. For the single-variant comparison, each lookup variant was matched to our REGENIE additive single-variant statistics by chromosome and position. Effect alleles were harmonized so that the tested allele in our statistics matched the allele reported by Bellenguez et al. 10 , with β estimates and standard errors sign-flipped where necessary. Under a protective-cognitive-superager hypothesis, AD-risk alleles are expected to be depleted in cognitive superagers relative to controls, yielding negative β values in our case-versus-control comparison.

For the known AD gene-based comparison, each Bellenguez-prioritized gene was matched against our REGENIE GENE_P statistics. Because GENE_P is a two-sided aggregate statistic that does not return a directional effect estimate, direction-of-effect concordance was not assessed at the gene level; the gene-based comparison was restricted to identifying overlap between Bellenguez-prioritized genes and genes nominally associated in our burden analysis.

Rare Variant Allele Frequency Enrichment Testing

Allele frequency analyses were performed using the normalized RADCO WES data in which all multiallelic sites had been decomposed into biallelic variants using bcftools as previously described. Allele frequencies were then calculated across the entire cohort as well as within cognitive superagers and average-cognition subjects using PLINK 2.0's --freq command, yielding subgroup-specific allele frequency (AF) and allele observation numbers (AN). Variantlevel missingness was calculated for the cohort as a whole to identify sites with poor call rates. Variants with cohort-wide call rate < 90% would have been excluded prior to the enrichment test had any met that threshold. For each variant, subgroup-specific allele frequencies and alleles observed were compared across RADCO cognitive superagers, RADCO average-cognition subjects, and external reference data from gnomAD's non-Finnish Europeans (NFE), allele counts (AC) were approximated as AN x AF, rounded to the nearest integer to represent counts of alternative alleles. 6 subjects including 2 cognitive superagers whose ancestry did not fall under gnomAD's NFE definition were excluded from the allele frequency enrichment analysis.

The Genome Aggregation Database, or gnomAD, v4.1 is an updated reference population released in April of 2024, aggregating and harmonizing exome sequencing data from over 730,000 participants including 416,555 UK Biobank and 314,392 non-UK biobank exomes. The v4.1 exome VCF files and their accompanying index files were downloaded directly from the gnomAD Browser provided by the Broad Institute 107 . These files contain genotypes and allele frequency information across the cohort enabling comparison of variant frequencies within the RADCO group and this large reference population. A subset of the entire gnomAD reference that focuses on the Non-Finnish European (NFE) population was used as it aligns most closely with the demographics of the RADCO cohort. Finnish people are excluded from this mixture due to the prevalence of Finnish Disease Heritage diseases 108.

Fisher's exact tests were then applied to 2 × 2 contingency tables of allele counts to test the null hypothesis of there being no difference in allele frequency between groups, consistent with standard practice in rare-variant case-control analyses where expected cell counts are small and asymptotic approximations are unreliable 85, 86 . Three pairwise Fisher's exact comparisons were conducted in this manner: (i) RADCO cognitive superagers vs. gnomAD NFE, (ii) RADCO average-cognition vs. gnomAD NFE and (iii) RADCO cognitive superagers vs. RADCO average-cognition. The primary aim was to identify variants for which cognitive superagers showed a statistically significant allele frequency difference relative to gnomAD, while the average-cognition group did not differ significantly from gnomAD, consistent with enrichment specific to the cognitive superager endophenotype rather than general cohort batch effects. Resulting Fisher's exact P-values were then Benjamini-Hochberg corrected for multiple comparisons.

Analyses started from 1,224,073 exonic variants. Variants that were not screened across all three groups were removed because their rarity and frequency could not be properly benchmarked between groups. In addition, common variants were excluded by filtering out variants with gnomAD AF NFE ≥ 0.001 or gnomAD AF NFE ≤ 0.999, restricting the analysis to the rarest exonic variants in the reference population. Variants were retained only if they demonstrated a statistically significant difference in allele frequency via Fisher's exact test between the RADCO cognitive superager group and the gnomAD NFE reference population (Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted Fisher's exact P < 0.01) while simultaneously showing no significant difference between the RADCO average-cognition group and gnomAD NFE (Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted Fisher's exact P > 0.01). This criterion was designed to isolate variants whose frequency enrichment is specific to the cognitive superager phenotype rather than batch effects. Further, we filtered out variants with a minor allele count less than 4 within the cognitive superager cohort resulting in 976 candidate variants. To test whether the functionalconsequence composition of these candidate variants deviated from expectation, we compared them to a frequency-matched empirical background comprising all 870,018 rare exonic variants retained after the gnomAD NFE rarity filter (AF NFE < 0.001) but prior to superager allele-e e d s frequency pruning. Each variant was assigned a SnpEff consequence class, and the missenseto-synonymous ratio of the candidate coding subset was compared to that of the background using a two-sided Fisher's exact test. This matched design controls for the whole-exome capture footprint, the rarity threshold, and the annotation pipeline, isolating any functional-class enrichment attributable to the superager phenotype.

Candidate SNPs were further filtered to retain only variants predicted to alter protein structure or function. Using SnpEff annotations, we retained variants in the missense, proteintruncating (stop_gained, stop_lost, start_lost, frameshift_variant), and splice-disrupting (splice_acceptor_variant, splice_donor_variant, splice_region_variant in combination with a synonymous, intronic, or missense context) effect classes, mirroring the high-and moderateimpact tiers used in standard rare-variant case-control analyses 102, 104 . Variants failing HWE at a threshold of 1 x 10 -6 within the entire RADCO WES cohort (n = 1473) were removed as well as those variants for which all alternative allele carriers came from the same first-degree family group. This filtering resulted in 114 variants that were then split into confidence tiers 1 & 2 by the cognitive superager vs. gnomAD NFE comparison at a threshold of P adj = 1.0 x 10 -8.

Five candidate variants from the allele-frequency analysis, two tier 1 and three tier 2, mapped to the HLA region leading to further investigation. Reliable HLA imputation requires chromosome 6 whole-genome sequence data that was not widely available in the cognitive superager allele frequency cohort but was available for a broader AFAR subset. We performed an accessory HLA association analysis using the secondary endophenotype of cognitively normal (n = 524) versus cognitively impaired (n = 248) status (n = 772 with covariate-complete data), 280 of which were centenarians (112 cognitively impaired, 168 cognitively normal). Classical two-field HLA types at seven loci were imputed from chromosome 6 WGS genotypes using HIBAG v1.44.0 (European reference model) 109, 110 , with population structure adjusted using WES-derived PC1-PC6 from the full RADCO WES panel (n = 1,473). For each locus, the lead allele was tested in logistic regression (additive dosage; sex, age, PCs) and Benjamini-Hochberg FDR was applied across seven lead-allele tests.

To evaluate whether allele frequency candidate variants are predicted to alter gene regulation or splicing in neural contexts, we performed in silico regulatory profiling of all 114 candidates using AlphaGenome 111 in brain (UBERON:0000955) and neuron (CL:0000540) ontologies. For each variant, we computed the maximum absolute predicted difference between reference and alternate alleles across RNA-seq, CAGE, splice-site, DNase, and histonemodification tracks within a ± 262 kb window. Splice-site scores are probabilities (0-1); the remaining modalities are normalized predicted-coverage signals in assay-specific, relative units and are therefore not directly comparable across columns. This analysis was intended to support functional interpretation of AF candidates rather than to provide independent validation of cognitive superager enrichment, because all 114 variants had already been ascertained on allele-frequency grounds. For each modality, we retained the maximum absolute difference between reference and alternate allele predictions across the two cell ontologies; larger scores indicate that the model predicts a greater regulatory or splicing perturbation for the alternate allele relative to reference. Variants were annotated with Ensembl VEP 112 . AlphaGenome scores were merged with cognitive superager carrier frequencies from the European-ancestry subset with non-missing cognitive superager phenotype (n = 138 cognitive superagers, n = 118 average-cognition controls).

To explore whether the prioritized candidate genes converged on shared biological processes, we performed over-representation analysis (ORA) on the unique protein-coding genes carrying the 114 candidate variants using Enrichr 113, 114 via the gseapy Python interface 115 . Gene symbols were queried against four annotation libraries, Gene Ontology Biological Process 116 , KEGG 117 , Reactome 118 , and MSigDB Hallmark 119 , and enrichment was assessed by Fisher's exact test with Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction applied within each library, with terms at FDR < 0.05 considered significant.
