Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

Abstract

High resolution arterial spin labeling (ASL)-based perfusion imaging is desirable for many applications but suffers from low SNR and motion sensitivity due to the necessity for highly segmented readouts. Acquisition and reconstruction strategies were optimized for motionrobust high-resolution pseudo-continuous arterial spin labeling (pCASL) of the brain at 3T using segmented 3D Gradient and Spin Echo (3D-GRASE) at 2.0-mm isotropic resolution.

A high-resolution 3D-GRASE pCASL sequence was implemented at 3T and evaluated with a focus on the interaction between background suppression strength and motion-corrected reconstruction, together with the fat suppression and control-tag subtraction strategies that strong suppression requires. Motion correction was performed using a novel motioncompensated (alignedSENSE-based) reconstruction that simultaneously estimates the motion and image, accounting for contrast changes between tag and control data. Eight healthy volunteers were scanned under motion-free and cued motion conditions. Image quality was assessed against motion-free reference images using peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR) and a structural similarity index measure (SSIM).

The optimized acquisition-reconstruction pipeline enabled robust 2.0-mm isotropic pCASL at 3T and showed improved resistance to motion-related degradation. Strong BGS provided near-complete suppression of static tissue, and water excitation reduced lipid contamination and improved coil sensitivity estimation. The regularized alignedSENSE motion correction further improved perfusion-image quality, increasing SSIM by 18.7% under Strong BGS.

Robust high-resolution pCASL at 3T can be achieved through combined optimization of acquisition and reconstruction. The combination of Strong BGS, water excitation, referencesuperimposed processing, and regularized motion correction mitigates motion-related degradation and yields higher-quality perfusion images for high-resolution ASL.

INTRODUCTION

Cerebral perfusion, the delivery of blood to brain tissue, is a fundamental physiological parameter that is altered in a wide range of neurological and cerebrovascular disorders, including stroke 1 , dementia 2 , brain tumors 3 , and neurodegenerative disease 4 . Perfusion imaging has traditionally relied on exogenous contrast agents, such as dynamic susceptibility contrast MRI 5 or CT perfusion 6 , which carry risks related to contrast administration 7 and are less suitable for repeated or longitudinal studies. Higher spatial resolution is desirable in perfusion imaging as it may improve the detection of focal abnormalities, laminar perfusion differences, and small-vessel pathology that can be obscured at conventional resolutions 8.

Arterial spin labeling (ASL) MRI provides a fully non-invasive alternative by using magnetically labeled arterial water as an endogenous tracer 9, 10 . Because no exogenous contrast agent is required, ASL is well suited to repeated measurements, longitudinal studies 11 , and vulnerable patient groups such as pediatric 12 or renally impaired populations 13 . Pseudocontinuous ASL (pCASL) 14 has become the recommended labeling strategy due to its high labeling efficiency and favorable SNR 15 . At conventional spatial resolutions (typically 3-4 mm isotropic), ASL has been successfully applied in a broad range of clinical and neuroscientific settings, including assessment of cerebrovascular reactivity 16 , tumor characterization 17 , and resting-state brain function 18.

Extending ASL to higher spatial resolution remains challenging. The perfusion signal is intrinsically small, typically on the order of 1-2% of the tissue signal 15 , and this low SNR becomes more limiting as voxel size decreases. High-resolution imaging also generally requires longer readouts or more highly segmented acquisitions, which increase sensitivity to physiological fluctuation and head motion. In particular, segmented 3D readouts such as 3D Gradient and Spin Echo (3D-GRASE) [19] [20] [21] are attractive for high-resolution ASL because of their acquisition efficiency and compatibility with background suppression (BGS), but they require multiple excitations to fill k-space, making the final perfusion image vulnerable to inter-shot motion 22 . As a result, high-resolution ASL at 3T is constrained not by a single factor, but by the combined effects of low SNR, physiological noise, and motion sensitivity.

Previous work has advanced high-resolution ASL through segmented 3D readouts, optimized BGS, acceleration and regularized reconstruction. At 3T, segmented 3D acquisitions achieved 2 × 2 × 4 mm 3 resolution 23 , while controlled aliasing in parallel imaging (CAIPI) 24 accelerated 3D-GRASE with z-deblurring enabled 2-mm isotropic whole brain imaging in about 5 min 8 . Spatio-temporal total generalized variation denoising 25 further improved low-SNR ASL, and optimized 7T pCASL with dynamic compressed sensing has reached 0.8-mm isotropic resolution 26 . These studies primarily addressed SNR and acceleration; head motion was generally assumed to be small or handled by conventional retrospective registration. However, segmented 3D-GRASE is inherently sensitive to inter-shot motion. Huber et al. jointly estimates motion and distortion 27 , and we previously introduced self-navigated intershot correction for segmented 3D-GRASE ASL with CAIPI sampling 28 . alignedSENSE 29 offers a complementary approach by jointly estimating the image and rigid-body motion from multicoil k-space and has been extended to 3D multi-shot EPI 30, 31 . These formulations, however, assume common contrast across shots and do not account for the systematic intensity difference between tag and control data in ASL or how BGS strength affects motion estimation.

Background suppression is central to this problem. Strong background suppression is attractive for high-resolution ASL because it lowers the physiological noise arising from incompletely subtracted static tissue, and by reducing the residual CSF signal 32 , limits the amount of background signal that head motion can convert into subtraction artifacts. This suppression, however, removes most of the static-tissue contrast on which image-based registration depends, so the degree of suppression that is most favorable for the perfusion measurement is also the most challenging for conventional motion correction. More moderate suppression retains anatomical contrast that aids motion estimation but leaves more residual static signal that can be transformed into artifacts when motion occurs. This trade-off is not resolved by simply extending our previous self-navigation approach 28 , because at 2.0 mm isotropic resolution each shot is too heavily undersampled to provide the self-navigating information that method relies on. The interaction between background suppression strength and motion-correction therefore remains undetermined.

In this work, we address this question for high-resolution 3D-GRASE pCASL at 3T. We propose alignedSENSE-PR, which adds perfusion regularization (PR) to alignedSENSE and estimates inter-shot motion directly from the multicoil k-space data through the forward model. The PR term accounts for the systematic contrast difference between tag and control images, helping to maintain robust tag-control alignment even when strong background suppression removes most image-domain tissue contrast. This allows strong background suppression to be used to its full advantage for high-resolution ASL. Adopting strong suppression in turn places specific demands on the rest of the pipeline: residual lipid becomes proportionally more conspicuous, requiring effective fat suppression through water excitation, and the suppressed signal can approach or span zero, so that magnitude subtraction becomes biased and a sign-preserving subtraction strategy is needed. We therefore evaluate background suppression strength, fat suppression, control-tag subtraction method, and motion-corrected reconstruction together at 2.0 mm isotropic resolution and identify the combination that yields the most robust perfusion image quality under conditions of head motion.

METHODS

Sequence Design

An overview of the sequence design is shown in Figure 1a. A 3D-GRASE 21, 33 pCASL sequence previously developed in our group was further modified and implemented on a 3T scanner. The labeling parameters were labeling duration (LD) = 1800 ms, and post-label delay (PLD) = 1800 ms.

Background suppression used a four-pulse (4-inv BGS) 34 scheme applied after pre-saturation and before excitation. The inversion pulses interleaved with the pCASL pulse train were slabselective frequency-offset-corrected inversion (FOCI) 35 pulses to achieve spatially uniform inversion proximal to the labeling plane with sharp edges, while the remaining inversion pulses were non-selective hyperbolic-secant pulses. After each inversion pulse, the labeling state was inverted accordingly. The degree of background suppression was controlled by the post-null time (PNT), defined as the interval between the theoretical signal nulling point and the excitation pulse, which determines the level of residual longitudinal magnetization at the time of readout. Two 4-inv BGS timing schemes were evaluated. In the "Moderate BGS" condition, tissue signals were nulled 100 ms before excitation (PNT = 100 ms), allowing partial longitudinal recovery and resulting in residual background signal. In the "Strong BGS" condition, tissue signals were nulled at the time of excitation (PNT = 0 ms), maximizing background suppression. The inversion pulse timings were computed using the coefficients of the quadratic polynomial equations described by Maleki et al. 34.

A 1-2-1 binomial water excitation pulse 36 was used for fat suppression. Four outer volume suppression (OVS) pulses were applied prior to the readout to reduce aliasing from outside the imaging slab 21.

A segmented 3D-GRASE readout 21, 33 was employed with a time-dependent CAIPI 2×8(4) sampling pattern 28, 37 (Figure 1b) with in-plane acceleration 8, through-plane acceleration 2, and sampling steps ∆ 4. k-space was divided into 16 segments, with turbo factor = 8 and EPI factor = 57. The sampling indices begin at the k-space center and step outward, with the ky-kz trajectory shifted between segments so that each shot samples the full k-space volume rather than a localized region. This uniform coverage maximizes the information each shot provides for estimating and correcting its own motion, while also distributing the undersampling artifacts incoherently across k-space. The timedependent CAIPI sampling strategy has been successfully adopted in several recent studies for both single-shot and segmented 3D acquisitions 28, 37, 38 . In addition, the complete k-space coverage across shots allows all segments to be combined to form a fully sampled dataset for robust coil sensitivity calibration. Readout parameters were refocusing flip angle = 120°, TR = 4 s, TE = 46.5 ms, bandwidth = 1994 Hz/px, matrix size = 114 × 114 × 64, yielding 2.0 mm isotropic resolution.

Tag and control encoding was interleaved shot-by-shot before advancing the sampling mask, so that corresponding tag and control segments were acquired in close temporal proximity, minimizing motion-related misalignment 15.

The perfusion image ∆ estimated in this step plays no role in the final perfusion measurement and its only function is to stabilize the motion estimation. Any bias introduced by the L2 penalty therefore affects the estimated motion parameters alone, not the reported perfusion signal, which was formed in a separate step from the estimated motion using a reference-superimposed (RS) reconstruction 39.

Because alignedSENSE-PR corrects both inter-volume and inter-shot motion of the tag and control data, its output can be brought into alignment with the non-suppressed equilibriummagnetization reference ( ), allowing the k-space to be superimposed onto each volume. Adding the signal, which shares the phase of the underlying tissue, lifts the tag and control signals away from the null point before the magnitude is taken, so that magnitude subtraction no longer suffers the sign ambiguity and noise rectification that can arise under Strong BGS when the residual longitudinal magnetization approaches or spans zero. Concretely, the k-space data from the two repeats were averaged and then added to each aligned tag and control dataset; each dataset was reconstructed and converted to magnitude, and the perfusion-weighted image was formed by magnitude subtraction. All reconstructions were mapped to a common motion-free reference space for metric computation as described in the Quantitative Evaluation section, and the reference was acquired in this same space.

No denoising was applied to any result reported in the main text. However, an optional locally low-rank (LLR) constraint, which exploits the redundancy of the perfusion component across repeats, is described in Supporting Information Text S1.

Reconstruction was performed on a workstation with 4 AMD EPYC 9274F CPUs (24 cores) and 128 GB RAM, requiring approximately 20 min per reconstruction.

Data Acquisition

Eight healthy volunteers (1 female; age: 29.6 ± 3.2 years) were scanned on a 3T Prisma scanner (Siemens Healthineers) with a 32-channel head coil. All imaging was conducted under a technical development protocol agreed by local ethics and institutional committees.

Each subject underwent four ASL acquisitions after the scan in a fixed order: Strong BGS without instructed motion, Moderate BGS without instructed motion, Strong BGS with cued motion and Moderate BGS with cued motion. For the first two acquisitions, subjects were instructed to remain stationary throughout and these data served as nominal motion-free references. For the last two acquisitions, subjects performed cued moderate head motion 28, 40 , were used for motion correction evaluation. Each acquisition comprised three tag-control pairs and lasted 6 min 29 s.

An reference was acquired at the start of each session with labeling and background suppression disabled, using the same 3D-GRASE readout, CAIPI sampling, and spatial resolution as the ASL acquisitions (TR/TE = 5500/46.54 ms). Two repeats were acquired (total acquisition time = 2 min 56 s) and averaged to form the reference. This reference was shared across the Strong BGS, Moderate BGS, motion-free, and motioncorrupted acquisitions, and provided the reference for the reference-superimposed reconstruction described above.

In each session, a T1-weighted anatomical image was acquired for registration and tissue segmentation (resolution: 1.7 mm isotropic; FOV = 220 × 200 × 218 mm; TR/TE/TI = 1900/3.71/904 ms; flip angle = 8°; acquisition time = 3 min 40 s).

Quantitative Evaluation

To allow image-similarity metrics to be compared fairly across reconstruction methods, the output of every method was brought into a common reference space before evaluation. This reference space was defined by the temporal average of the tag and control volumes from the motion-free reference acquisition. For each method, each reconstructed tag/control volume was registered to this motion-free reference with FSL registration tool FLIRT 41 to obtain a residual rigid-body transformation. This residual transformation was then composed with the intra-scan motion parameters estimated by that method, and a final reconstruction was run with the combined motion, mapping every method's output into the identical reference space whilst avoiding image-space interpolation effects. Brain masks were extracted by using FSL brain-extraction tool BET 42 from T1-weighted images and then registered to the reference space. Image quality was assessed by computing two metrics within the brain mask against the motion-free reference: the Structural Similarity Index Measure (SSIM) and the Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR). Both metrics were computed on the perfusion-weighted image.

Statistical significance of differences between reconstruction methods was assessed using paired-sample t-tests, with significance levels set at p < 0.05 (*), p < 0.01 (**), and p < 0.001 (***).

Comparison Conditions

Having established Strong BGS as the target operating regime, we evaluated a set of baseline and ablation conditions to test whether each accompanying choice is required in that regime and to isolate the contribution of the proposed reconstruction.

For background suppression, 2-inv BGS was included as a reference for Moderate and Strong BGS, consistent with prior ASL implementations 21, 43.

Fat suppression compared the vendor-provided fat saturation with 1-2-1 water excitation. Additional binomial and no-fat-suppression conditions were evaluated at 3.6-mm isotropic resolution (Figure S2).

For reconstruction ablation, alignedSENSE-PR was compared with alignedSENSE-IS (intershot correction only) and alignedSENSE-MF (inter-shot correction was followed by mcFLIRT 41 image-based inter-volume registration). Because RS requires tag, control, and k-space in a common frame, the ablation used complex subtraction, which is applicable to all variants (Figure S3).

Complex subtraction was also evaluated as an alternative to RS with and without LLR denoising (Figures S4 to S6).

RESULTS

Background Suppression

Figure 2a shows the simulated residual longitudinal magnetization ( / ) as a function of tissue T1 for the three BGS configurations evaluated in vivo. At the matched PNT of 100 ms, 2-inv BGS provided only limited suppression, leaving 8.9% residual CSF signal, whereas Moderate BGS reduced the residual CSF signal to 2.6%. Strong BGS reduced it further to -0.2% and achieved near-complete suppression across tissue types.

The representative in vivo images in Figure 2b (normalized individually for visualization) are consistent with the simulation. Compared to 2-inv BGS, Moderate BGS substantially reduced CSF signal in the ventricles in both tag and control images, lowering physiological noise in the perfusion-weighted image (PWI). Strong BGS further reduced the static background and increased the intensity difference between tag and control images. As 2-inv BGS provided substantially inferior suppression, it was included only as a visual reference and was not carried forward into the quantitative motion correction analysis.

Strong BGS leads to changes in image contrast and motion sensitivity. First, once the tissue background is strongly suppressed, residual lipid signal becomes proportionally more conspicuous and appears as a ring-like structure at the periphery, motivating the fat suppression comparison in the following section. Second, the suppressed tissue also operates in a low-signal regime in which the residual longitudinal magnetization can approach zero or reverse sign, motivating the subtraction strategy comparison in the following sections. Third, the strong reduction of static-tissue contrast that makes the subtraction cleaner also removes much of the anatomical information on which conventional image-based motion correction relies, which is the central tension addressed by the proposed reconstruction.

Fat Suppression: Fat Saturation vs Water Excitation

Figure 3a compares reconstructed images obtained with conventional fat saturation and with water excitation. Under fat saturation, residual chemically-shifted lipid signal was incompletely suppressed and appeared as focal hyperintensities in the tag and control images (yellow arrows), predominantly in regions adjacent to subcutaneous fat. These signals propagated into the PWI as structured artifacts (yellow box) that were not removed by magnitude subtraction. In contrast, water excitation effectively eliminated lipid signal, resulting in cleaner tag and control images and improved spatial homogeneity in the PWI.

The residual lipid signal also affected coil sensitivity estimation. As shown in Figure 3b, ESPIRiT 44 -based sensitivity maps obtained under fat saturation exhibited spatial inconsistencies and structured distortions, whereas water excitation produced smooth and more physically plausible sensitivity profiles across all virtual coils. Because these sensitivity maps enter the reconstruction forward model, errors in their estimation propagate into every reconstructed image, making robust fat suppression a prerequisite for the strongly suppressed regime rather than a cosmetic improvement.

Additional binomial pulse comparisons supported the use of 1-2-1 water excitation as a balance between fat suppression and excitation duration (Figure S2).

Subtraction Strategy

Figure 4 compares PWI obtained with magnitude subtraction (MS), complex subtraction (CS) under Strong BGS and a reference-superimposed approach followed by magnitude subtraction (RS). Under Strong BGS the tag and control images operate in a low-signal regime in which the residual longitudinal magnetization can approach zero or become negative. MS discards the sign of this magnetization, so where tag and control have opposite polarity, the subtraction is biased and fine perfusion structure is lost or distorted.

Both RS and CS recovered this structure, but by different routes. CS retains the phase and therefore the sign directly. RS instead adds the signal to lift the tag and control images away from the null point before the magnitude is taken, so that the data phase is essentially aligned and the sign ambiguity does not arise. The two gave comparable perfusion image quality (Figure 4), but RS additionally suppressed background noise, most visibly outside the brain, because the superimposed signal moves the measurement away from the low-signal regime in which magnitude data are noise-rectified. RS was therefore adopted for all subsequent analyses, with CS retained as a comparison condition (Supporting Information Figure S5).

Motion Correction

Figure 5 shows representative in vivo images from a single subject under Moderate BGS. Inter-shot motion introduced blurring and severe artifacts in the control and tag images, with more pronounced degradation in the PWI. The proposed alignedSENSE-PR reconstruction recovered sharp structural boundaries in the control and tag images and restored most of the perfusion signal, improving PWI quality over the motion-corrupted data (PSNR 21.3 to 23.3 dB, SSIM 0.297 to 0.517). Compared with the motion-free reference, the corrected images remained noisier but showed markedly improved structural definition relative to the motioncorrupted data.

Figure 6 presents the same comparison under Strong BGS. Despite the strongly reduced anatomical signal, alignedSENSE-PR recovered clear grey matter delineation and improved PWI quality (PSNR 24.3 to 25.3 dB, SSIM 0.474 to 0.604), demonstrating that accurate motion correction remains achievable in the low-background regime that is most challenging for conventional registration. Consistent with the BGS analysis above, the motion-corrupted Strong BGS data showed fewer tissue-related artifacts than Moderate BGS, reflecting the more complete suppression of static signal. Under complex subtraction, alignedSENSE-PR outperformed the inter-shot-only (alignedSENSE-IS) and mcFLIRT-based (alignedSENSE-MF) variants under both BGS conditions and the ablation is reported in Supporting Information Figure S3.

The estimated motion parameters in Figure 7 clarify why the proposed method remains reliable under Strong BGS. Under Moderate BGS, all three variants produced broadly consistent rigid-body trajectories. Under Strong BGS, the large intensity difference between tag and control images destabilized the image-based registration step in alignedSENSE-MF, producing increased variability and local inconsistencies in the estimated parameters. alignedSENSE-PR instead produced smooth, consistent trajectories that agreed closely with those obtained under Moderate BGS, in line with the matched motion instructions used across the two acquisitions. Because alignedSENSE-PR estimates motion directly from the multicoil k-space data through the forward model rather than from image-domain tissue contrast, its motion estimates do not degrade when the background is suppressed.

Figure 8 summarizes the perfusion image metrics across all eight subjects. alignedSENSE-PR significantly improved PSNR and SSIM relative to the motion-corrupted data under both Moderate and Strong BGS (paired-sample t-tests). Higher mean PSNR and SSIM were observed under Strong BGS than Moderate BGS across the cohort. The same motioncorrection benefit was observed when CS with LLR denoising was used in place of RS (Supporting Information Figure S6), indicating that the improvement from alignedSENSE-PR is independent of the subtraction strategy.

Figure 9 contrasts the two BGS schemes in the same subject: in the motion-free condition the two schemes were comparable, whereas under motion Moderate BGS showed greater loss of perfusion contrast and structural definition. After correction, alignedSENSE-PR recovered perfusion image quality in both conditions, with stronger recovery under Strong BGS, particularly in cortical regions.

DISCUSSION

This work evaluated a set of sequence and reconstruction design choices for motion-robust high-resolution 3D-GRASE pCASL at 3T. We found that some of these choices interact with others, such as the use of strong background suppression, which yields cleaner perfusion measurement but is more challenging for conventional image-based motion correction. The reconstruction that estimates motion without relying on static-tissue contrast can use Strong BGS to its full advantage with water excitation and reference-superimposed processing for further reducing artifacts. The combination of Strong BGS, water excitation, referencesuperimposed processing, and alignedSENSE-PR reconstruction provided robust performance in the presence of head motion.

Motion-corrected reconstruction becomes increasingly important as spatial resolution increases, because higher-resolution segmented 3D-GRASE acquisitions require more shots and therefore longer acquisition times, increasing vulnerability to inter-shot motion. Each shot is too heavily undersampled to reconstruct the self-navigator for motion estimation 28 . alignedSENSE-PR addresses this by estimating inter-shot motion directly from the multi-coil k-space data through the reconstruction forward model, jointly with the image. Because the motion estimation is driven by consistency of the multi-coil data rather than by imagedomain tissue contrast, it does not degrade when the static background is suppressed. And alignedSENSE-PR also simultaneously achieved the inter-volume correction by encouraging the minimum difference between tag and control images during the reconstruction. This allows accurate motion correction to persist into the Strong BGS regime, where the reduced anatomical signal and the large tag-control intensity difference make image-based registration unreliable.

With motion correction no longer dependent on the residual tissue contrast, the primary disadvantage of Strong BGS is eliminated. A principal benefit of the 4-inv BGS scheme is the more effective suppression of signals from all tissue types, particularly CSF, which is an important source of physiological fluctuation arising from pulsatility 32 . Such CSF-related signal cannot be fully removed by control-tag subtraction and can contaminate the perfusion signal through partial-volume effects. By suppressing static tissue signal more completely at excitation, Strong BGS also reduces the amount of background signal that can be converted into subtraction artifacts when motion occurs. This likely explains the reduced corruption observed in the motion-contaminated perfusion images and the improved recovery after reconstruction. Therefore, Strong BGS should be considered not as a harder regime, but as one that is intrinsically cleaner for the perfusion measurement while shifting the challenge onto the reconstruction that our proposed alignedSENSE-PR is designed to overcome. Adopting strong suppression places specific demands on the rest of the pipeline. The first concerns fat. Once tissue is strongly suppressed, lipid becomes relatively more conspicuous and can emerge as a dominant structured contaminant in both the source images and the reconstructed perfusion images. This behavior is consistent with the simulations shown in Figure 2, where fat recovered substantially faster than the suppressed tissue compartments. Conventional fat saturation is vulnerable to failure because it relies on spectrally selective presaturation, which is sensitive to B0 and B1 inhomogeneity and frequency offsets arising from B0 drift across repetitions, all of which can produce spatially variable residual lipid signal. Water excitation is also sensitive to off-resonance, but in our implementation, it provided more stable fat suppression, likely because it is less sensitive to B1 variation and does not rely on the timing between fat saturation and excitation. This reduced structured chemically-shifted residual contamination in the strongly suppressed data. This advantage comes with known trade-offs, including sensitivity to off-resonance and a longer TE, particularly for longer binomial schemes. Nevertheless, within this trade-off space, the 1-2-1 water-excitation scheme provided the best overall balance between robustness and image quality in the present implementation.

The second demand concerns the subtraction. Under Strong BGS the control and tag image intensities approach zero and can reverse in sign. Magnitude subtraction discards this sign, leading to biased subtraction and structural distortion. Reference-superimposed processing resolves this by adding the signal to lift the tag and control images away from the null point before the magnitude is taken, additionally suppressing background noise. Complex subtraction resolves the same ambiguity by retaining the phase and resulted in comparable image quality without requiring an reference. Reference-superimposed processing is particularly well suited to this framework because alignedSENSE-PR brings the tag and control data into the frame as part of the reconstruction itself, rather than requiring a separate registration step. Otherwise, any residual misalignment between and the tag/control data would introduce extra error. Reference-superimposed processing therefore places a stricter alignment requirement on the pipeline than complex subtraction.

The optional locally low-rank constraint occupies a different role from the choices discussed above. Because the perfusion component is highly redundant across repeats, a low-rank reconstruction provides additional noise suppression under Strong BGS, where perfusion SNR is lowest, and the motion-correction benefit of alignedSENSE-PR is preserved under this back-end (Supporting Information Figure S6). It was deliberately excluded from the main analysis so that the reported image quality reflects the acquisition and reconstruction choices themselves rather than a subsequent denoising step, but it remains available where SNR, rather than fidelity to the underlying perfusion distribution, is the limiting factor. Across subjects, Moderate BGS tended to produce more severe motion-related artifacts and less complete recovery of the perfusion signal after correction than Strong BGS. This indicates that Strong BGS is intrinsically more robust to motion contamination, despite placing greater demands on motion estimation. In other words, Strong BGS leaves less residual background from static tissue and CSF to be transformed into subtraction artifacts, so once motion is handled the perfusion signal is recovered more fully than under a moderate regime. Together these observations support Strong BGS, combined with alignedSENSE-PR, as the preferred strategy rather than the conservative suppression levels typically chosen to protect retrospective registration. Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study included only eight healthy volunteers and employed instructed motion rather than naturally occurring motion. Although the cued-motion paradigm provided a controlled way to test robustness, motion amplitude was not strictly standardized across subjects, and the observed motion may not fully represent involuntary or pathological motion encountered in clinical practice. Second, the image quality was assessed against an assumed noise-and motion-free reference. Third, the current study focused on technical optimization and did not directly assess how the improved highresolution perfusion images translate into specific clinical applications.

Future work should therefore evaluate this framework in patient cohorts and determine whether the observed gains in image robustness translate into more accurate quantitative cerebral blood flow estimation. It will also be important to investigate whether the improved spatial fidelity of these high-resolution perfusion images enables clinically meaningful benefits, for example in resolving cortical or periventricular perfusion abnormalities that are otherwise obscured by partial-volume effects.

CONCLUSIONS

This work demonstrated that combining strong background suppression with the proposed alignedSENSE-PR reconstruction can achieve motion-robust high-resolution 3D-GRASE pCASL at 3T. Strong BGS leaves less residual static-tissue and CSF signal, reducing contamination of the perfusion image, while alignedSENSE-PR estimates and corrects motion reliably even when static background is strongly suppressed. Together with water excitation and reference-superimposed processing, this combination enabled robust 2.0 mm isotropic perfusion imaging within a clinically acceptable scan time.
