Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. How Reproducibility Check works

It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

Abstract

Wearable inertial measurement unit (IMU) sensors provide a promising method to assess real-world human motion kinematics. However, multi-day multi-IMU tracking is difficult due to the desynchronization driven by inaccurate IMU clocks. In this study, 15 volunteers wore IMUs continuously on their thighs and shanks for 5 weekdays (Mon-Fri), which included 3 repeated in-lab motion capture sessions. We synchronized thigh and shank IMUs using two methods. The first was physical calibration, where we maneuvered the IMUs in a rigid holder to produce sync signals pre-donning (Mon) and post-doffing (Fri), then linearly resampled data from one IMU to match the other via cross-correlation. The second was signal-based periodic updates, where we shifted IMU clocks hourly based on the timewise alignment of preprocessed body-worn acceleration signals. After syncing, we used sensor-tosegment alignment and accelerometer-gyroscope fusion to estimate knee flexion and compared results to camera-based motion capture. Physical calibration alone removed clock offset in most cases, where IMU-estimated knee flexion generally matched motion capture (correlation ≥0.944; root-mean-square difference RMSD ≤6.9°). But in some participants, residual mid-week desynchronization (~0.2s) resulted in misestimated knee flexion (Wed: RMSD up to ~19°). After applying signal-based updates or combining it with physical calibration, IMU matched motion capture on all 3 days (Mon: r ≥0.975, RMSD ≤6.1°; Wed: r ≥0.971, RMSD ≤6.2°, Fri: r ≥0.977; RMSD ≤5.9°). Our multi-modal synchronization enables week-long knee flexion tracking and lays a foundation for advanced syncing tools, for improving the feasibility of wearable multi-IMU systems to continuously monitor real-world knee kinematics in clinical studies.

I. INTRODUCTION

NEE mobility is critical for daily function and longterm joint health. Conventional assessments of knee kinematics rely on camera-based motion capture in the lab, which do not capture the frequency and variability of habitual movement in daily life. Wearable inertial measurement units (IMUs) offer a promising alternative by using accelerometer-gyroscope fusion [1], [2] to track segment orientation and joint angles outside the laboratory. Recent studies, including our own [3], have shown that IMUs track knee flexion with accuracy comparable to camera-based motion capture during common movements such as gait [1], [4], [5] and stair navigation [6]. However, nearly all such validation studies were performed in a single experimental session, leaving it unclear whether this tracking quality holds after multiple days of continuous recording -the use case that most multi-day IMU monitoring studies require.

Several studies have recorded multi-day IMU data to report cumulative real-world metrics, most commonly step count and activity level [7], [8], [9] and more recently cumulative kinematics like limb elevation duration or body rotation [10], [11], [12]. A critical technical barrier to extending this work to joint kinematics is inter-sensor synchronization. Unlike singlesensor metrics, multi-IMU measures like the knee flexion angle -an established biomarker in knee osteoarthritis [13], [14] -require that all sensors share a common, accurate clock, which is difficult to guarantee over multiple days [15]. Research-grade high-end IMUs can synchronize in real time via wired triggers or radio-frequency signal checks [16], but these constrain sensor placement, shorten battery life, and increase user burden, making them impractical for large multiday studies. Lower-cost, unconnected IMUs such as the Axivity AX6 (Axivity Ltd., Newcastle upon Tyne, UK) can log a full week of accelerometer and gyroscope data without recharging, which improves wearer adherence [8], [11] but often sacrifice sensor synchronization features for extended logging capabilities. Such low-cost sensors rely on internal quartz clocks that are inaccurate by tens of parts per million [17] -enough for two AX6 units to desynchronize by several seconds per day [15], far exceeding the precision needed to align thigh and shank motion for knee flexion estimation.

Past studies have addressed this by prescribing wearers to physically maneuver multiple IMUs together to generate a shared calibration signal [11], [12], but participants do not always adhere to these instructions [11]. A simpler strategy is for research staff to perform this calibration maneuver only at the start and end of the wearing period and resample the data assuming a linear inter-IMU clock drift [17]. However, this procedure relies on the linearity of sensor clock drift, which is sensitive to acceleration and temperature [17]. Importantly, calibration-based strategies are easy to implement in theory but challenging in practice. Requiring multiple lab visits or relying on participants to perform the sensor synchronization themselves will result in data attrition. Early sensor stoppage due to battery loss, insufficient memory, and either forgetting or incorrectly performing the maneuver could all disable calibration-based synchronization. Moreover, nearly all prior multi-day kinematic monitoring has targeted the upper limb or torso [10], [11], [12]. To our knowledge, no study has confirmed whether a multi-IMU system can track knee flexion with camera-comparable accuracy across multiple days of continuous wear.

Our goal was therefore to develop simple, reliable postprocessing synchronization methods for low-cost IMUs (specifically, the AX6) to continuously quantify the knee flexion angle over multiple days, with accuracy comparable to camera-based motion capture. We expected that, after proper synchronization, IMU-estimated knee flexion would show small magnitude differences and strong waveform agreement with camera-based motion capture across clinically relevant activities throughout the entire tracking duration.

II. METHODS

II. METHODS

A. IMU sensor device

We used Axivity AX6 (Axivity Ltd., Newcastle upon Tyne, UK), which is a low-cost (~$250 USD), water-resistant research-grade IMU housed in a compact plastic casing with a micro-USB port for charging and data offload (Fig. 1a). At a 100 Hz logging rate with both accelerometer (16g range) and gyroscope (2000 dps range) active, a fully charged AX6 records approximately 7 days of continuous data without wearer interaction, including recharging. We used these settings (16g, 2000 dps, 100 Hz) for both thigh and shank sensors in all experiments. For each participant, we programmed the thigh and shank IMUs to begin recording simultaneously at a specified time on the day of their first lab visit (always a Monday), shortly before sensor calibration and attachment.

B. Study participant characteristics

We recruited 15 healthy young adult volunteers from our local community (8 male, 7 female, 27.1 ± 5.8 years old, body mass index = 24.7 ± 5.8 kg/m²) between September 2025 and March 2026. Participants had no current or self-reported lower-limb or back injury in the prior 6 months that could interfere with knee movement in daily living and were required to be able and willing to visit our lab 3 times in one week (Monday, Wednesday, Friday) for motion capture and to wear IMUs on their thighs and shanks continuously across this 5-day span. All participants provided written informed consent. This study was approved by the University of Pennsylvania Institutional Review Board (Protocol #850424) and performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

C. Experimental procedure: IMU 5-day monitoring and 3 repeated in-lab motion capture sessions

Sensor attachment. Each participant's initial visit occurred on a Monday, a mid-week visit that Wednesday, and a final visit that Friday, at approximately the same time each day. We attached IMUs to both legs on Monday and removed them on Friday. We secured a total of 4 AX6 IMUs to each participant -one pair per leg, on the lateral mid-thigh and lateral midshank -using a 3-layer medical-grade adhesive system (protective base layer, double-sided bonding tape, and a waterproof cover layer; full product details and application steps in Supplemental Methods S1). Sensor orientation (charging port down, text facing the skin) was matched across all 4 IMUs, which was necessary for extracting calibration syncing signals (Section E) and sensor-to-segment alignment (Section F). Participants replaced the outer waterproof cover as needed but did not replace the base or bonding layers; they were instead instructed to abort the experiment if those layers detached, which occurred for only 1 of 15 participants (later re-enrolled for a successful 5-day monitoring period). For the remaining 14, all sensors stayed at their original locations for the full week.

In-lab motion capture. After sensor attachment, each participant performed a static pose trial (for sensor-to-segment alignment; Section F) followed by 6 movement trials common to daily living -overground straight walking, improvised arbitrary walking, double-legged squat, stair step-up, stair step-down, and 30-second sit-to-stand -at a self-selected pace, recorded with markerless motion capture [18] (specific movement start/end definitions are provided in Supplemental Table S1). We recorded at least 3 gait cycles of straight walking and 3-5 repetitions of each other trial. This protocol was repeated on Wednesday and Friday. After the Friday session, we removed all sensors, performed the end-ofmonitoring physical calibration (Section E), and offloaded the 5-day IMU data. Participants then completed an exit survey on wearing experience and a diary of notable high-intensity activity during the week (Results are summarized in Appendix A). Briefly, all 15 participants found the 5-day bilateral wear feasible, and 11/15 (73%) would wear sensors for a full week in future studies.

D. Preparation of IMU data for post-processing

Our IMU processing pipeline converted thigh and shank accelerometer-gyroscope data into knee flexion angle in 3 steps: inter-sensor synchronization, sensor-to-segment realignment, and accelerometer-gyroscope fusion (Fig. 2). We performed all data processing using Python 3.11.5. Although IMUs were worn bilaterally (for a larger ongoing study and for feasibility assessment; Appendix A), we report findings from one leg per participant: the dominant leg for 13 of 15 participants, and the non-dominant leg for the other 2 participants because of sensor recording errors on the dominant leg.

Original time stamps logged by the AX6 were non-uniform. Therefore, immediately after data import (using the Open Movement Project's Python module [20]), we resampled each accelerometer and gyroscope channel to an exact 100 Hz using cubic spline interpolation (SciPy). We also removed rare highmagnitude transient error signals (observed once, >100× physical range) that would otherwise interfere with downstream processing that references global signal maxima.

E. Inter-IMU synchronization: two methods

We developed two independent inter-sensor synchronization methods: (1) calibration-based linear resampling, using syncing signals from a physical calibration tool and maneuver performed before and after the wearing period; and (2) signal-based periodic updates, using engineered features from the body-worn acceleration signals themselves. Both methods began with a shared initial clock alignment, established from the physical calibration signal recorded on Monday. This initial time synchronization does not impose a meaningful burden on experimental setups because it can be performed at the time of IMU software initialization by the researchers rather than study participants immediately before sensor donning.

Physical calibration tool and initial alignment. Because AX6 does not synchronize in real time, we designed and 3Dprinted a rigid holder to perform initial time synchronization between the 4 IMUs following established recommendations for physical inter-sensor calibration [15], [16]. We inserted all 4 IMUs into the holder facing upward (Fig. 1a) -opposite their on-body orientation -so that a distinctive, automatically detectable signal is produced (Fig. 3). Immediately before the scheduled recording start on Monday, we placed all 4 IMUs into this static "calibration standby" position and left it for at least the first 30 seconds of all recordings, then performed a 3step rest-swing-rest maneuver (lifting and swinging the holder for ~10 s, then resting it again) to produce an isolated syncing signal. We repeated this procedure on Friday, immediately after doffing sensors from the participant. We detected each "calibration standby" interval automatically from characteristic accelerometer/gyroscope signatures (full detection criteria in Supplemental Methods S2) and used cross-correlation (SciPy) of the resulting waveforms (Fig. 3) to establish the initial Monday clock alignment, which was used by both synchronization methods below.

Linear resampling via physical calibration.

We computed each participant's average linear inter-IMU clock drift rate from total offset between the Monday and Friday calibration signals (Fig. 3ab) and used it to linearly "dilate" the slower IMU's time stamps, then resampled all data channels via cubic spline interpolation to a common, uniform 100 Hz clock (Fig. 3c). We identified valid physical calibrations at the experimental beginning and end for all 15 participants. This approach is only available when both the initial and end-ofmonitoring calibrations are captured, and its accuracyparticularly mid-week where data is farthest from calibration "anchors" -depends on the assumption that inter-IMU clock drift is linear across the full recording. This was an assumption previously reported to generally hold for AX6 [17], although we did not find it to be universal (Results B & D, Discussion). Periodic updates via body-worn signals. This approach instead assumes that, over sufficiently long windows, thigh and shank acceleration are generally in sync during real-world movement: the shank is moving whenever the thigh is moving, and vice versa. The periodic updates require no physical calibration signal, only concurrent IMU data. We derived a scaled resultant acceleration signal for each sensor as the main syncing signal. We first used a jerk-based algorithm developed in reference to prior work [21] to identify the active daytime periods (as opposed to sleep/rest) (Fig. 4), since acceleration signals during sleep were too quiet to reliably synchronize. Within each daytime hour (Fig. 5), we selected the first sufficiently active 5-minute window, then low-pass filtered (4 Hz cutoff) and demeaned the thigh and shank signals within that window to emphasize their shared, general motion envelope over sensor-specific waveform features (Fig. 6). We then cross-correlated them to estimate that hour's clock offset, discarding implausibly large offsets (full parameterization in Supplemental Methods S2). We shifted the faster IMU's data to match the slower one and iterated this check hourly across the full 5-day recording (Fig. 5). Example from one participant on hourly clock offsets between the thigh and shank IMUs, iteratively identified by the hourly clock updates ("+" crosshairs). The updates, which were larger at the first checkpoint of each day as we expected, kept desynchronization <0.05 s for most of the time. Although misidentifications occasionally occurred, they were usually selfcorrected at the next hourly checkpoint (blue arrows). For context, the black dots illustrate offsets of the same IMU pair identified with no day-night parsing (2), criteria-based bout selection (4), filtering (5), or correction. A notable non-linear drift occurred (red arrow), which led to residual desynchronization on much of Wednesday when only using linear resampling for syncing (green dashed line = assumed linear drift correction). There were also substantially more instances of misidentified sync offsets, especially during nighttime (unshaded areas).

Combining both methods and post-hoc verification.

We additionally tested applying periodic updates to data already synchronized via linear resampling and expected this to further reduce any residual non-linear drift. We evaluated the syncing outcomes for all three workflows (linear resampling alone, periodic updates alone, and combined) by rerunning the periodic-update algorithm on each synchronized dataset to quantify the residual inter-IMU clock offset -ideally near 0on IMU data over the timespan of each in-lab session (Results B). Residual offset was defined as any remaining clock offset between IMUs after data had passed through synchronization.

F. Knee flexion angle from synchronized thigh and shank IMUs

We performed sensor-to-segment alignment and accelerometer-gyroscope fusion independently on the synchronized thigh and shank data, following our previously validated single-session procedure [3]. Briefly, we virtually rotated each IMU's local-frame data into an anatomically meaningful frame aligned with the knee flexion axis and gravity. We approximated the sagittal knee flexion axis via principal component analysis of gyroscope data during Monday's squatting trial (in place of literature-recommended walking trial [22] due to our short in-lab walkway) and the proximal-distal axis from the static standing pose. To correctly extract IMU data during the time periods of the pose and squat trials, we corrected for IMU-to-motion-capture clock offset (independent of the inter-IMU synchronization) by crosscorrelating segment sagittal velocity during the squat trial (full axis derivation details in Supplemental Methods S3).

We then estimated each IMU's orientation using an opensource, accelerometer-gyroscope fusion algorithm (Madgwick filter, no-magnetometer version, β = 0.5) [2], [3]. We applied data fusion across the full 5-day continuous recording rather than only the brief windows concurrent with reference data, as in most prior work [3], [22]. We converted the resulting orientation quaternions to Euler angles and subtracted the sagittal shank and thigh angles to obtain knee flexion angle, sign-corrected per our established convention [3], [22]. Results yielded continuous ~90-100 hours of knee flexion time series per participant (30-40 million samples).

G. Comparison between IMU and in-lab motion capture

We matched IMU-estimated knee flexion to corresponding motion capture trial during each lab session using ground-truth camera time stamps. We first removed the residual absolute IMU-to-lab clock offset (independent of the inter-IMU synchronization) via cross-correlation on each day's 30-second improvised walk trial. We then applied that offset to all trials recorded that day (same-day trials were recorded <15 min apart, so additional drift was assumed negligible).

We then computed, for each trial repetition, the Pearson correlation coefficient (Rxy) and root-mean-square difference (RMSD) between IMU-estimated and camera-based knee flexion waveforms, averaged across repetitions and then across participants for each lab day. Following Schober et al. [23], we defined Rxy ≥ 0.7 as strong and ≥ 0.9 as very strong agreement; consistent with prior literature [24], [25] and our own work [3], [19], we defined RMSD ≤ 5° as a minimal magnitude difference.

H. Sensitivity analysis: effect of induced desynchronization on estimated knee flexion magnitude

We performed an ancillary sensitivity analysis to isolate the effect of desynchronization on knee flexion angle estimation independent of other errors known to impact joint kinematic estimates like skin artifact, sensor attachment, and algorithm noise [15], [16]. Using each participant's post-synchronization, post-alignment, pre-fusion data from Monday's straight walking trial, we induced artificial clock shifts to the shank data (0.01-0.05 s, in 0.01 s increments) while holding the thigh data fixed, re-ran data fusion, and computed the RMSD between the resulting and the original (synchronized) knee flexion estimates. We used this sensitivity analysis to establish what level of synchronization precision is needed to achieve acceptable knee flexion estimates, under the assumption that all errors are explained by temporal offsets. We considered an error of less than 5° an acceptability threshold [3], [25], but this error tolerance should be reconsidered by investigators based on their own research questions and study designs.

III. RESULTS

III. RESULTS

A. Inter-IMU clock drift behaviors

Inter-IMU clock drift was both device-and experimentspecific; the same IMU pair exhibited different drift patterns in multiple experiments. Using our Monday and Friday calibration anchors as the start and end timepoints, total monitoring durations averaged 95.6 ± 2.1 h (range 92.0-99.2), consistent with our intended 4 full days (96 h). Total inter-IMU drift varied widely across participants (0.47-6.95 s; mean 2.76 ± 2.34 s), corresponding to an assumed-linear drift rate of 0.005-0.074 s/h (mean 0.029 ± 0.025 s/h). The largest rate observed (0.074 s/h, ≈20.5 ppm) fell within previously reported ranges for the AX6 [15], [17]. There was no consistent trend in which IMU's clock ran faster (shank faster in 9/15 participants, thigh faster in 6/15).

C. Sensitivity of estimated knee flexion angle to induced clock desynchronization

IMU-estimated knee flexion diverged progressively from reference result (synchronized) as induced desynchronization increased (Fig. 7). Averaged across participants, RMSD rose from 1.7° at ±0.01 s to 3.4° (±0.02 s), 5.0° (±0.03 s), 6.6° (±0.04 s), and 8.2° (±0.05 s). At ±0.03 s, RMSD ranged 4.0°-5.6° across participants, and at ±0.05 s, 6.5°-9.2°. Given our 5° acceptability threshold [25], this places the tolerable inter-IMU sync offset at approximately ±0.03 s.

D. IMU-estimated knee flexion angle vs. camera-based motion capture on all 3 days with in-lab session

Across all 3 in-lab sessions and all three synchronization workflows, IMU-estimated knee flexion showed very strong group-level waveform agreement with camera-based motion capture, with magnitude differences that met or narrowly exceeded our a-priori 5° threshold (full results by syncing workflow, movement type, and day are reported in Table I).

Linear resampling alone produced very strong agreement on each lab day (Rxy ≥ 0.944, RMSD ≤ 6.9°), but this group-level result masked two participants with markedly worse Wednesday tracking (Rxy: 0.609-0.919, RMSD: 7.0°-18.9°; Fig. 8a) -the only instance where Rxy fell below our "strong" criterion. These two outlier sessions were the same two sessions with the largest residual clock offsets identified in our post-hoc verification (Results B) and were the primary source of the elevated group-level variability seen on Wednesday for this method (Table I).

Periodic updates alone produced comparable group-level agreement (Rxy ≥ 0.971, RMSD ≤ 6.2° across days) but with substantially reduced Wednesday variability, because both linear-resampling outlier sessions tracked well under this method (Fig. 8b); post-hoc verification confirmed their residual offsets fell from 0.19 s and 0.18 s to 0.01 s and 0 s, respectively. Combining both methods produced the most consistent results overall (Rxy ≥ 0.975, RMSD ≤ 6.1° across days), with the best Wednesday agreement of the three workflows and no outlier sessions (Table I, Fig. 9).

IV. DISCUSSION

Our goal was to develop simple, reliable post-processing synchronization methods that support continuous monitoring of knee flexion over multiple days using low-cost, unconnected IMUs. We developed and compared three workflows -physical calibration-based linear resampling, signal-based periodic updates, and a combination of these strategies -on thigh-shank AX6 pairs worn by 15 healthy adults for 5 days. At the group level, all three workflows produced knee flexion estimates of acceptable accuracy through our previously established kinematic processing pipeline [3]. At the individual level, however, they differed: linear resampling alone produced multiple mid-week outliers, which we traced to non-linear inter-IMU drift that this method cannot correct. Periodic updates fixed this non-linear drift, and combining the two methods further reduced misidentified or unresolved offsets. Knee flexion angle results showed strong agreements with concurrent camera-based motion capture regardless of whether periodic updates were applied on linear resampled or the original IMU data. Our findings therefore generally favor periodic updates over linear resampling, though each method carries its own assumptions and constraints. More broadly, this 5-day validation extends the tracking quality previously demonstrated only in minute-long sessions to ~100 hours of continuous data, supporting the technical and practical feasibility of multi-day, multi-IMU knee kinematic monitoring in future clinical studies on chronic conditions like knee osteoarthritis.

Consistent with a prior report for the AX6 [17], the inter-IMU drift rates we observed fell within expected ranges. However, unlike that report, drift was not always linear in our data (13/15 participants, 87%) and varied by experiment even for the same IMU pair, suggesting AX6 clock behavior is not fully device intrinsic. Although device age and ambient temperature are both known to affect quartz-oscillator timing accuracy [26], they were similar across our experiments and sensors (relatively new, <4-year-old). Rather, the two nonlinear drift events we observed each coincided with a period of unusually vigorous activity reported in the participant's activity diaries (a basketball game and an outdoor run). This indicates high-acceleration events, not time or temperature, may be the more relevant driving factor of non-linearity. This observation raises an important caveat for the linearresampling approach: because it assumes constant drift, it may be less reliable for physically active wearers, even as it performed well for other non-outlier participants and on Monday/Friday even for the two outliers (because calibration "anchors" bound the Monday and Friday sessions directly). Linear resampling may still be preferable in some contexts because it requires no shared motion between adjacent body segments, thus it remains available for bilateral or otherwise unconnected segments. It may also suit studies with shorter monitoring windows or where sensors can be attached, detached, and recalibrated mid-experiment, since the rigidholder signal is inherently more reliable than cross-correlating each segment's naturally distinct motion. Linear resampling may be better suited to less physically active populations, such as many patients with knee osteoarthritis, though this requires direct validation. Periodic updates, by contrast, kept mid-week data synchronized even in the presence of non-linear drift and produced no poor-tracking outliers at the individual level (Fig. 8). When combined with linear resampling, 98% of sessions stayed within 0.05 s of synchronization. Because our periodic update method uses unlabeled body-worn signals rather than prescribed physical action, it is robust and usable even when an end-of-monitoring calibration is missing or invalid. Also, since it updates forward through the recording, it can still synchronize data up to the point where a sensor stops recording prematurely, a failure mode that would otherwise disable linear resampling entirely. To our knowledge, this is the first method to synchronize thigh and shank IMUs via their shared general motion-intensity envelope. It suggests that valid multi-day knee flexion tracking could in principle extend as long as the paired recordings last, supporting the broader feasibility of week-long multi-IMU kinematic monitoring with low-cost, unconnected sensors.

Our walking RMSD (5°-6°) was modestly larger than the <4° typically reported between IMU and marker-based motion capture [1], [4], [5], which may be partly attributed to our use of markerless reference data (instead of marker-based), though we previously found the two reference systems to closely agree on knee flexion during gait (RMSD <4°) [19]. Camerabased motion capture is itself imperfect relative to groundtruth trackers like biplane fluoroscopy [24]. In one recent study that directly compared IMU-based knee kinematics to fluoroscopy, IMU and marker-based motion capture both showed similar accuracy (RMSD ≈4°) [27]. Combined with our sensitivity analysis that places the 5° error threshold [25] at an approximately 0.03 s sync offset, our group-level RMSD of 5°-6° on all 3 days suggests our synchronization kept the IMU pair within roughly 0.03 s of true alignment throughout the week. This means our synchronization methods were able to extend prior single-session validation -typically limited to minutes of data -to continuous tracking across 5 days and to a range of clinically relevant tasks (gait, stair navigation, squat, sit-to-stand). These findings support our synchronization approach as a practical route to reliable, multi-day IMU kinematic monitoring in patients with chronic knee pathology.

Because our approach minimizes wearer interaction, it may be well suited to clinical populations with lower technology literacy or adherence, but several challenges remain before deployment in clinical monitoring studies. First, while sensor donning can occur at a clinical visit with the help of researchers, sensor doffing -and the end-of-monitoring physical calibration required for linear resampling -will often need to happen at home before sensors are returned by mail. This may be difficult for patients with reduced hand dexterity and suggests a need for a more user-friendly calibration hardware. Second, our hourly periodic updates occasionally misidentified an offset before self-correcting it at the next checkpoint (Fig. 5). This imperfection could transiently bias knee flexion estimates for up to an hour and may be caused by either imperfect active bout selection or incomplete separation of the shared versus segment-specific motion features. Tuning the frequency of periodic update intervals may help address this weakness. It also remains to be confirmed whether those shared motion features between thigh and shank remain valid for patients with pathological knee motion patterns. Third, our results represent the best case in which no sensor was repositioned during the week, which will not always hold in larger studies. Marking the sensor footprint on the base tape to guide self-reattachment is one practical mitigation strategy, though automatic detection and post-hoc data correction in response to repositioning would be a valuable future addition.

More broadly on the limitations of this study, our synchronization approach was validated only on the AX6. While we expect similar quartz clock behavior in other unconnected low-cost IMUs, we cannot confirm this directly. We did not assess frontal-or transverse-plane knee motion, for which we lacked reliable reference data [24]. The knee flexion estimate is resilient to gyroscope signal drift, but 3-axis Euler angle decompositions would introduce drift-related errors that are not corrected in absence of a magnetometer. Recent work by Phan et al. developed novel strategies to account for these drift errors but still found that knee frontal and transverse plane kinematics produced unacceptably high errors compared against ground-truth biplane fluoroscopy [27]. Finally, our cohort of healthy young volunteers likely adhered to our athome IMU wearing protocol better than a patient or older population would. Larger studies in such populations should anticipate more non-adherence and practical issues like skin irritation. We nonetheless believe continuous, real-world IMU kinematic data is critical for gaining objective and patientspecific insights into how musculoskeletal diseases and treatments affect movement outcomes in patients during realworld scenarios. Therefore, we encourage continued development of wearable protocols and analytic methods that support multi-day kinematic tracking.

V. CONCLUSION

Our study developed a simple and reliable syncing paradigm for low-cost logging IMUs to continuously quantify the knee flexion angle during real-world activities. We showed that our bimodal algorithm reliably syncs two low-cost IMUs to quantify knee flexion for 5 consecutive days, ~100 uninterrupted hours with comparable accuracy to lab camerabased motion capture. Our 5-day IMU monitoring strategy is a viable solution for clinical studies because it requires minimal wearer interaction and addresses common challenges of patient monitoring with unsupervised software solutions. Our novel syncing algorithms support low-cost wearable IMUs like AX6 to be implemented in at-home experiments on a larger scale. We hope our technical contributions will help expedite the translation of wearable technologies into larger clinical studies, like continuously monitoring multi-day knee kinematics to evaluate how musculoskeletal diseases and treatments affect real-world knee mobility in patients with chronic pathologies like degenerative knee osteoarthritis.
