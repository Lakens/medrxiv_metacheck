Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Abstract

Mitochondria are the energy producers of our cells. The POLG gene helps keep them working. Changes in the POLG gene cause rare inherited diseases that affect muscles, nerves and the brain. Some people with these diseases also develop symptoms like those of Parkinson's disease (PD). Earlier studies disagreed on whether POLG changes raise the risk of PD in people without these diseases.

We used genetic data from the Global Parkinson's Genetics Program (GP2), which collects samples from people with and without PD worldwide. We used two types of data: a genetic chip test of about 98,600 people, and DNA sequencing (reading the DNA directly) of about 36,000 people. We looked for rare harmful POLG changes, common POLG changes, and differences in the length of a repeated stretch of DNA in the gene. About 7 in 1,000 people with PD and 5 in 1,000 people without PD carried one harmful POLG change. A few other comparisons looked different at first, but none held up once we accounted for the many comparisons we made. Five people with PD carried two harmful POLG changes. For three of them, we could not confirm that the changes sat on different copies of the gene. We could not tell if these changes caused their PD.

We found no strong evidence that POLG changes raise the risk of PD in general. They may contribute to PD in a small number of cases. We had little clinical information about each person, so larger studies with detailed clinical information are needed.

Introduction

The polymerase gamma (POLG) gene encodes the catalytic subunit of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) polymerase gamma, which is essential for mtDNA replication and maintenance. 1 Genetic variants in POLG have been associated with mtDNA depletion or deletion, contributing to a broad spectrum of mitochondrial disorders, including progressive external ophthalmoplegia (PEO), Alpers syndrome, SANDO (sensory ataxia, neuropathy, deafness, and ophthalmoplegia), and other mitochondrial ataxia syndromes. 1, 2 To date, more than 300 pathogenic POLG variants have been reported, most of which predominantly associated with autosomal recessive disorders, whereas fewer exhibit autosomal dominant inheritance.

Beyond its established role in mitochondrial disorders, emerging evidence suggests a potential link between POLG and Parkinson's disease (PD), possibly through impaired mtDNA maintenance leading to impaired mitochondrial function, increased oxidative stress, and neuronal loss in the substantia nigra. 3 Postmortem brain analyses have revealed high levels of mtDNA deletions in dopaminergic neurons of the substantia nigra in both PD patients and aged individuals. [4] [5] [6] In mouse models, intracerebral injection of damaged mtDNA induced PD-like symptoms that propagated in a prion-like manner. 6 Moreover, patients with POLG-related mitochondrial disease exhibit marked neuronal cell loss in the substantia nigra, with α-synuclein pathology detected in some cases, further supporting a potential link between impaired mtDNA maintenance and neurodegeneration. 7 Monoallelic and biallelic POLG variants have been reported in individuals with early-and late-onset PD and parkinsonism, sometimes accompanied by additional neurological manifestations, including external ophthalmoplegia and sensory neuropathy. [8] [9] [10] [11] [12] [13] [14] In families affected by progressive external ophthalmoplegia, some POLG variants have also been found to co-segregate with parkinsonian symptoms. 3 However, whether POLG variants contribute to idiopathic PD susceptibility remains unclear. Previous case-control studies investigating common POLG variants have generally found no significant enrichment in PD patients compared with controls. 15, 16 Associations between POLG exon 2 CAG repeat variation and PD have been reported in several populations, including Finnish, Norwegian, North American, Swedish, and Chinese. 15,17- 21 However, these findings have been inconsistent across studies, 22 leaving the contribution of POLG genetic variation to PD risk unresolved.

In this study, we examined the frequency and genetic spectrum of rare disease-associated and common POLG variants, as well as exon 2 CAG repeat genotypes, in individuals with PD and other neurological phenotypes, and in controls across diverse ancestries. We addressed this by leveraging large-scale, multi-ancestry datasets from the Global Parkinson's Genetics Program (GP2), including NeuroBooster Array (NBA) genotyping, short-read whole-genome sequencing (WGS), and clinical exome sequencing (CES).

Methods

Demographics

The demographics of the study cohort are detailed in Supplementary Table 1. We analyzed imputed NBA genotyping data from GP2 Data Release 11 (https://gp2.org/; DOI 10.5281/zenodo.17753486), comprising a total of 98,589 samples: 52,257 PD patients, 5,580 individuals with other phenotypes (including atypical parkinsonism and other neurodegenerative conditions), 39,470 healthy controls, and 1,282 unaffected family members of PD patients.

We further utilized the WGS dataset from GP2 Data Release 11, including 28,190 samples: 15,483 PD patients, 3,974 individuals with other phenotypes, 7,585 healthy controls, and 1,148 unaffected family members of PD patients. In addition, we incorporated CES data from 7,832 PD cases included in GP2 Data Release 11 generated by the Parkinson's Foundation (PD GENEration) initiative, a large multi-center initiative based in North America. 23 Individual cohort recruitment, sample collection, and data collection within GP2 have been described previously. [24] [25] [26] All samples underwent genetic ancestry predictions using the GenoTools pipeline (https://github.com/GP2code/GenoTools), as previously described. 27 Individuals were classified into eleven genetic ancestry groups: African Admixed (AAC), African (AFR), Ashkenazi Jews (AJ), Latinos and Indigenous People of the Americas (AMR), Complex Admixture History (CAH), Central Asian (CAS), East Asian (EAS), European (EUR), Finnish (FIN), Middle Eastern (MDE), and South Asian (SAS).

Quality Control

For the NBA genotyping data, PLINK binary files were processed using the GenoTools pipeline, following standardized quality control (QC) procedures described previously. 27 Briefly, samples were excluded if they failed any of the following criteria: call rate < 0.95, inconsistency between genetically inferred and reported sex, or excess heterozygosity (|F| > 0.15). After preliminary sample-level QC, variants with Hardy-Weinberg Equilibrium (HWE) ≤ 1 x 10 -5 in control samples were removed. Variants showing non-random missingness by case-control status (P ≤ 1 x 10 -4 ) or by haplotype (P ≤ 1 x 10 -4 ) were also excluded.

For sequencing data, PLINK binary files were processed using the same GenoTools pipeline, following standardized QC procedures as described above. High-quality variants were retained using the following thresholds: call rate > 0.95, genotype quality >20, read depth >5, and heterozygous allele balance between 0.25 and 0.75. POLG rare pathogenic variant screening POLG genomic coordinates were obtained from the Ensembl database (hg38: chr15: 89,305,198-89,334,861https://www.ensembl.org/).

For WGS and CES, POLG variants were extracted from variant call format (VCF) files using Bcftools (http://samtools.github.io/bcftools/howtos/publications.html) and annotated with ANNOVAR 28 and Slivar (https://github.com/brentp/slivar). Slivar-specific parameters were used to filter variants by population allele frequencies, and rare variants with a gnomAD PopMax allele frequency <0.005 were retained.

Candidate variants were prioritized based on predicted deleteriousness and clinical pathogenicity classifications. Variants were retained if they met the following criteria: (i) located in coding or splice-affecting regions of POLG, including missense, predicted loss-of-function (stop-gain, stop-loss, frameshift, start-loss), or short insertion/deletion variants; (ii) Combined Annotation Dependent Depletion (CADD) score ≥12.37; and (iii) classification as pathogenic or likely pathogenic by at least two of three annotations resources: ClinVar 29 , Franklin 30 , and Varsome 31 , the latter two based on the consensus guideline of the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG). 32 The previously described co-occurring POLG variants 33 p.Thr251Ile and p.Pro587Leu were additionally assessed using raw genotyping data, with carriers identified through PLINK v2.0. For individuals with both genotyping and WGS data available, carrier status was crossvalidated across platforms to ensure concordance.

POLG CAG repeat analysis

The POLG exon 2 CAG repeat, which encodes a polymorphic polyglutamine (polyQ) tract, was analyzed using ExpansionHunter (EH) on GP2 R11 WGS CRAM files (GRCh38, chr15:89,333,590-89,333,629). 34 The POLG polyQ tract consists of an uninterrupted CAG repeat followed by a CAA interruption and two additional CAG codons, represented as (CAG)n-CAA-CAG-CAG. To align with the previously used nomenclature for the uninterrupted CAG tract, three units were subtracted from the reported repeat length, accounting for the CAA interruption and two downstream CAG codons. 21 Associations between POLG CAG repeat genotypes and PD risk were evaluated using three genotype comparisons: non-10/10 versus 10/10, non-11/11 versus 11/11, and non-10/11 versus 10/11. Logistic regression models were adjusted for age, sex, and the first five genetic principal components (PCs) except in the CAH, CAS, EUR, and MDE ancestry groups, which were adjusted only for sex and the first five PCs due to substantial missingness in age.

Gene-based rare variant analysis

To evaluate the cumulative contribution of rare POLG variants to PD risk, we performed gene-based rare variant analyses leveraging the WGS data using the Combined Multivariate and Collapsing -Wald Test (CMC-Wald) and sequence kernel association test optimal unified SKAT (SKAT-O) methods as implemented in RVTESTS. 35 Rare variants with MAF <1% were grouped into four masks: (i) potentially functional variants, including all except intronic variants; (ii) coding variants; (iii) loss-of-function variants (i.e., splicing, stop-gain, stop-loss, and frameshift), and (iv) variants with CADD >20. Gene-based association analyses were not performed in the AAC, CAH, CAS, FIN, and SAS ancestry groups due to the limited sample sizes (<1000) among PD patients and controls.

Common variant association analysis

We further investigated the association between common POLG variants and PD risk by analyzing NBA genotyping imputed data. We used PLINK v2.0 for variant extraction and ANNOVAR 34 for variant annotation; only variants with an imputation quality score (R²) ≥0.8 were kept for further analyses. Additional pruning was applied to remove rare variants with MAF < 1% in each ancestry group and variants with a minor allele count (MAC) <2. SNP-phenotype association analyses were conducted using a generalized linear model implemented in PLINK v2.0. 35 Logistic regression models included sex, age, and 5 PCs as covariates to account for population stratification. Bonferroni correction was applied to adjust p-values for all POLG variants within each ancestry group.

Results

Potentially biallelic pathogenic or likely pathogenic POLG variant carriers

Five PD patients carried either homozygous POLG variants or combinations of heterozygous POLG variants classified as pathogenic or likely pathogenic for POLG-related mitochondrial disorders (Table 1). Two had homozygous genotypes; the other three had potentially compound heterozygous genotypes for which phase could not be determined.

One EUR PD patient (GP2-ID-1) was homozygous for p.Gly737Arg. Another EUR PD patient (GP2-ID-2) carried the co-occurring POLG p.Thr251Ile-p.Pro587Leu variant pair, along with the heterozygous p.Leu304Arg. The third EUR PD case (GP2-ID-3) carried heterozygous p.Gly737Arg and p.Ala143Val variants, whereas the fourth EUR PD case (GP2-ID-4) harboured heterozygous p.Pro648Arg and p.Ala467Thr variants. Lastly, a PD patient of AMR ancestry (GP2-ID-5) was homozygous for both p.Thr251Ile and p.Pro587Leu and also carried a homozygous pathogenic PRKN c.1286-3C>G variant. No relationship among these five cases were reported, and no additional pathogenic variants in other genes linked to genetic PD (except GP2-ID-5) were identified.

Clinically, all five were recorded as having typical PD, without documented features suggesting a mitochondrial syndrome, although the available phenotyping was limited and absence of documentation should not be interpreted as absence of symptoms. There was a wide variability in the ages at onset (AAO) of these PD patients, ranging from 20 to 60 years. The youngest case, GP2-ID-5 (onset at 20 years), also had a homozygous pathogenic PRKN variant (chr6:161350214:G:C [hg38]; c.1286-3C>G), a variant known to cause early-onset PD, which likely explains the early disease onset in this individual. Among all five carriers, two reported a negative family history of PD, two reported positive history, and one was unknown. Based on the available clinical data, all five patients had a diagnosis of typical PD without mention of relevant atypical features indicating a different diagnosis. Data on medication was only available for two individuals, both of whom showed a good response to dopaminergic treatment. In the context of non-motor features, one individual was found to have mild cognitive impairment (MCI; MoCA score 23/30). Furthermore, two PD cases were found to have comorbidities, including kidney disease and hypertension, respectively. A summary of clinical characteristics is provided in Table 1; overall, the availability of extended clinical data was limited.

Single heterozygous pathogenic or likely pathogenic POLG variant carriers A total of 34 distinct pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants were reported in heterozygous state across ancestries, most of which were located within the polymerase domain of the POLG protein (Figure 2). A summary of all the variants and their respective frequencies is provided in Table 2; several variants were exclusively identified in PD cases and absent from controls. All variants have been reported before as disease-related for different neurological phenotypes, and the majority of them (n=23/34, 67.6%) have previously been linked to diseases with either dominant or recessive inheritance, according to the National Institute of Environmental Health Sciences (NIEHS) database (https://portal.niehs.nih.gov/polg/).

Of the 34 variants, two were found in members of two European families. The p.Gly737Arg variant was identified in two siblings, both affected with PD (AAO 64 and 69 years, respectively) supporting potential familial cosegregation. In the second family, p.Ala467Thr was found in a PD proband with a very early disease onset (AAO 18 years) and his unaffected mother (aged 67 years). Notably, the early-onset PD patient also carried a PRKN deletion involving exons 3-4 and the p.Cys253Tyr missense variant, whereas his unaffected mother carried only the PRKN missense variant. Overall, heterozygous pathogenic or likely pathogenic POLG variants were present in <1% of PD patients (n=161/23,315; 0.69%), patients with other neurological phenotypes (n=28/3,974; 0.70%), and controls (n=39/7,585; 0.51%) across all ancestries. No significant enrichment was observed in PD patients as compared to controls (OR=1.35, 95% CI=0.94-1.96, p=0.0996). At the individual variant level, p.Ala467Thr showed nominal enrichment in PD patients of European ancestry (p=0.0443; Table 2).

Carrier frequency of two co-occurring POLG variants

The POLG variants p.Thr251Ile and p.Pro587Leu variants have been reported together on a cis haplotype and are classified as pathogenic in combination according to ClinVar (Supplementary Figure 1). We examined their co-occurrence among PD patients, individuals with other phenotypes, and controls in the NBA and WGS datasets. Carrier frequencies were below 1% in the reported ancestry groups, with no statistically significant case-control differences (Table 3).

POLG CAG repeat length distribution

The 10/10 CAG repeat genotype was the most frequently observed genotype across all ancestry groups, although its frequency varied substantially between populations (Table 4). Among PD patients, the frequency of the 10/10 genotype ranged from 42.1% in African admixed (AAC) individuals and 44.4% in Africans (AFR) to 94.6% in East Asians, with similar patterns observed among controls. The 10/x and x/x genotypes were less frequent across most ancestry groups, although the x/x genotypes were more prevalent among AFR (10.9%) and AAC (7.9%) PD patients, as well as among controls (11.6% and 4.6%, respectively). Overall, genotype distributions were broadly comparable between PD patients and controls within ancestry groups.

Findings from the genotype association analyses between POLG CAG repeat genotypes and PD risk across ancestries are summarized in Table 4. In the non-10/10 versus 10/10 comparison, a nominally significant association was observed in the AAC cohort (OR=1.84, 95% CI=1.06-3.18, p=0.0291), but failed to survive the Bonferroni correction. For the non-11/11 versus 11/11 and non-10/11 versus 10/11 comparisons, no nominal significance was observed. A detailed breakdown of the genotypes identified in each ancestry was summarized in Supplementary Table 2 and forest plots illustrating the distribution of repeat genotypes were shown in Figure 3.

The results of the burden analysis across the investigated variant masks and ancestries are summarized in Supplementary Table 3. However, none of the masks showed significant association with PD risk.

Discussion

In this study, we characterized the frequency and spectrum of POLG variants in individuals with PD, other neurological phenotypes, and unaffected controls across diverse genetic ancestries. To our knowledge, this represents the largest multi-ancestry analysis to date examining the relationship between POLG genetic variation and PD. By integrating rare pathogenic and likely pathogenic variants, common genetic variation, and POLG CAG repeat genotypes, we found no robust evidence supporting an association between POLG variation and PD risk after correction for multiple testing. Nevertheless, we identified a small number of individuals with PD carrying homozygous or potentially compound heterozygous POLG variants, highlighting the possibility that POLG may contribute to PD in a small number of individuals.

Five PD patients carried homozygous or multiple heterozygous POLG variants previously associated with mitochondrial disorders. Among these, GP2-ID-1 was homozygous for p.Gly737Arg, whereas GP2-ID-3 carried p.Gly737Arg and p.Ala143Val, although the phase of these variants could not be established. The p.Gly737Arg variant has previously been implicated in mitochondrial and peripheral nerve disorders, with clinical manifestations including ataxia, seizures, neuropathy, and parkinsonism. 3 It has been reported in compound heterozygosity with other POLG variants (e.g., p.Arg309His, p.Ser64Leu), and associated with axonal Charcot-Marie-Tooth-like neuropathy, occasional dystonia, and parkinsonism features. [36] [37] [38] Notably, two sisters with early-onset PD and sensory neuropathy were previously reported to carry the p.Gly737Arg and p.Arg853Trp variants on different haplotypes (trans). 8 Although these observations support the biological plausibility of POLG-related mitochondrial dysfunction contributing to parkinsonism, the absence of detailed clinical phenotyping and segregation data in our cohort prevents conclusions regarding the pathogenic contribution of these variants to the observed PD phenotypes.

The co-occurring POLG p.Thr251Ile and p.Pro587Leu variants were identified together with p.Leu304Arg in GP2-ID-2 and in the homozygous state in GP2-ID-5. These variants have been extensively described as a pathogenic cis haplotype in mitochondrial disorders, [39] [40] [41] [42] occurring either in homozygosity, 42 or in combination with additional POLG variants. 43, 44 Functional studies have demonstrated that, although each variant individually produces a relatively modest reduction in polymerase γ activity, their co-occurrence on the same allele acts synergistically, reducing DNA-binding affinity and polymerase activity to approximately 5-10% of wild-type levels. 33 Clinically, this haplotype has been associated with PEO and multisystemic mitochondrial manifestations, including ataxia, myopathy, epilepsy, neuropathy, and hepatic dysfunction. 33, 41 Clinically, all five individuals carrying homozygous or potentially compound heterozygous POLG variants had been diagnosed with typical PD, without obvious atypical features and with a good response to dopaminergic treatment in two individuals with available medication data. The ages at onset varied substantially, ranging from 20 to 60 years. Notably, one individual also carried a homozygous pathogenic PRKN variant, which is more likely to represent the disease-causing alteration in this case and is consistent with the very early onset of 20 years observed in this individual. While additional clinical data was very limited, particularly on non-motor features, one individual showed mild cognitive impairment. Interestingly, cognitive dysfunction has been previously reported in POLG-related disorders, including POLG-associated parkinsonism, 41, 44 although its frequency and severity appear to vary depending on the disease phenotype, age at onset, and underlying variant combination. Unfortunately, additional clinical information on variant carriers, including potential mitochondrial and systemic features, were unavailable, precluding us from investigating potential genotype-phenotype correlations in more detail. Based on the limited clinical data, the small number of affected individuals, and the uncertainty regarding true biallelic status due to the constraints of the available sequencing data, we cannot conclusively determine whether the identified POLG variants are causal, contributory, or merely coincidental in relation to the PD phenotype.

Among individuals carrying single heterozygous pathogenic or likely pathogenic POLG variants, we observed an enrichment of p.Ala467Thr in PD patients of European ancestry (p=0.043). This variant has been reported to occur in ~0.6% of the Belgian population, 45 and is one of the most frequently detected POLG variants associated with Alpers syndrome, autosomal recessive PEO and SANDO. 46 The variant is located within the linker region and has been found to reduce the catalytic efficiency of POLG via in vitro study. 47 Nevertheless the observed association with PD was nominal and should be interpreted cautiously. In addition, we identified two families with multiple POLG variant carriers. The Gly737Arg was present in two siblings diagnosed with PD at ages 64 and 69 years, respectively, without additional identified pathogenic variants in established PD genes. Although this observation raises the possibility of a contribution to disease susceptibility, the absence of extended segregation data, unaffected family members, and functional validation prevents conclusions regarding its pathogenic role in the heterozygous state. In another family, p.Ala467Thr was identified in an individual with very early-onset PD (18 years) and his unaffected mother, aged 67 years. Importantly, the affected individual also carried a PRKN deletion involving exons 3-4 and the p.Cys253Tyr variant, potentially driving an alternative genetic explanation for his phenotype. However, confirmation of the phase is necessary before assigning causality. Taken together, these familial observations illustrate the difficulties of interpreting heterozygous POLG variants in PD, particularly in the presence of additional genetic findings and without comprehensive segregation information.

Overall, the role of heterozygous POLG variants in PD remains uncertain. While POLG is classically more commonly linked to recessive mitochondrial diseases, single pathogenic variants may act as reduced-penetrance risk alleles or require an additional genetic, environmental, or agerelated "second hit". This has important implications for clinical interpretation and particularly genetic counseling, as the presence of a single pathogenic POLG variant alone does not necessarily establish causality for PD. Future studies should therefore incorporate the systematic assessment of mitochondrial features to better estimate pathogenicity and distinguish incidental carrier status from POLG-related disease presenting with parkinsonism. Detailed phenotyping will be particularly important for counseling carriers, where current evidence often does not allow a clear distinction between causal, contributory, and incidental findings.

We also investigated the co-inherited p.Thr251Ile-p.Pro587Leu haplotype across genetic ancestries. Carrier frequencies were below 1% among both PD cases and controls, with no statistically significant differences between groups. The frequency observed in controls was broadly consistent with previous observations in an Italian population, 48, 49 where this haplotype was reported in approximately 1% of individuals. To our knowledge, our study is the first to examine the frequency of these two co-inherited variants across ancestries and at a large scale. Despite their predicted pathogenicity, the absence of significant enrichment among PD cases suggests that this haplotype is unlikely to represent a major genetic factor relevant to PD.

Variation in the POLG exon 2 CAG repeat tract has previously been investigated as a potential genetic risk factor for PD, with inconsistent findings across populations. In our multiancestry analysis, the 10/10 genotype was the most frequent across all ancestry groups, although its prevalence varied substantially. Non-10 repeat genotypes were more common among individuals of African and African admixed ancestries 50 than among several other ancestral groups, 15, 22, 51, 52 consistent with previously reported population differences in POLG CAG repeat variation. Although nominal association was observed for specific genotype comparison in the African admixed ancestry group, it did not survive multiple correction, and the overall directions of association across ancestries were inconsistent. These results do not support a robust association between the investigated POLG CAG repeat genotypes and PD susceptibility. They also highlight the importance of accounting for ancestry-specific repeat distributions when evaluating associations in genetically diverse populations.

Consistent with these findings, gene-based rare variant analyses using WGS data did not identify significant associations between POLG variation and PD risk after correction for multiple testing. Although a nominal association was observed in the East Asian ancestry group, this finding should be interpreted cautiously given the number of comparisons performed and the limited statistical power to detect rare variant effects in some ancestry groups. Similarly, our single-variant analyses of common POLG variants identified only a nominal association for the synonymous variant p.Leu752= in individuals of European ancestry, which did not survive multiple testing corrections. These findings are broadly consistent with previous studies reporting no robust association between common POLG variation and idiopathic PD. 15, 16, 19 A major strength of this study is the large, genetically diverse sample, which allowed us to investigate multiple classes of POLG variation using complementary approaches. The inclusion of individuals from ancestrally historically underrepresented in genetic studies of PD also enabled us to characterize population-specific differences in variant frequencies and CAG repeat distributions. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, despite the large overall sample size, the number of carriers of individual pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants was small, and some ancestry groups had limited numbers of cases and controls, reducing statistical power, particularly for rare variant analysis. Second, the phase of multiple heterozygous variants could not be established, limiting the interpretation of potentially compound heterozygous genotypes. Third, the available clinical information was insufficient to systematically assess mitochondrial manifestations or investigate genotype-phenotype relationships. Fourth, pathogenicity classifications are primarily derived from associations with established mitochondrial disorders rather than PD, and therefore may not directly reflect the relevance of individual variants to PD. Finally, individuals presenting with classical POLG-related mitochondrial syndromes (e.g., myopathy, PEO, or SANDO) accompanied by parkinsonism may have been underrepresented because GP2 recruitment primarily focuses on individuals diagnosed with PD. Consequently, our findings may not fully capture the contribution of POLG variation to the broader spectrum of mitochondrial parkinsonian disorders.

In conclusion, our large-scale multi-ancestry analysis provides no robust evidence supporting an association between POLG genetic variation and PD susceptibility at the population level. Although pathogenic or likely pathogenic POLG variants were identified in a small proportion of individuals with PD, their frequencies were broadly comparable between cases and controls, and neither rare variant burden nor common variant and CAG repeat analyses revealed significant associations after correction for multiple testing. The identification of patients carrying homozygous or potentially compound heterozygous POLG variants highlights the possibility that POLG may contribute to parkinsonism in a small number of individuals. However, limited clinical phenotyping, unresolved variant phase, and the presence of alternative genetic explanations in some cases prevent the establishment of a causal relationship. Future studies combining detailed clinical phenotyping, family-based segregation analyses, improved variant characterization, and functional assessment of mitochondrial dysfunction will be essential to clarify the role of POLG in parkinsonian phenotypes.
