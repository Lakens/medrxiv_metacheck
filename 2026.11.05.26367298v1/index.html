Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Background: Although direct-acting antivirals have transformed hepatitis C virus (HCV) treatment, 25-30% of active infections in Spain remain undiagnosed. We aimed to develop and validate an interpretable machine-learning model to identify undiagnosed HCV infection using only data already recorded within the health system.

We conducted a retrospective, population-based cohort study using the Andalusian Population Health Database (BPS). Individuals diagnosed with HCV between 2017 and 2024 (n=25,028) were matched, up to 10:1, to controls by birth year, sex, and health district. An Explainable Boosting Machine was trained on 2017-2022 data, with a threshold set in a 2023 validation set to guarantee ≥60% sensitivity, then evaluated unmodified in an independent, non-matched, population-representative 2024 sample approximating real-world screening. Predictors comprised chronic-disease diagnoses and laboratory summary statistics recorded ≥30 days before the index date; age and sex were excluded as matching variables. Predictors reflecting socioeconomic proxies or matching artefacts (pharmaceutical co-payment category, a zone-level deprivation index) were removed after explainability review.

Results: In the independent test set, the model achieved an AUC of 0.837 (0.826 in validation), sensitivity 63.5%, specificity 91.8%, positive predictive value 7.0%, and negative predictive value 99.6%. Leading predictors were minimum alanine.

Introduction

Hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection remains one of the principal preventable causes of chronic liver disease, cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma worldwide (Gamkrelidze et al., 2021). The introduction of direct-acting antivirals (DAA) after 2015 transformed the therapeutic landscape, achieving cure rates above 95% (Zhang, 2016) and prompting the World Health Organization (WHO) to establish the goal of eliminating viral hepatitis as a public health threat by 2030, including diagnosis of 90% of infected individuals and a 90% reduction in incidence (WHO, 2022). As therapeutic effectiveness is no longer the principal limiting factor, case finding has become one of the central bottlenecks in the final phase of HCV elimination.

Spain is internationally recognized as one of the countries closest to these targets with the introduction of universal access to direct-acting antivirals and the implementation of the Strategic Plan for Tackling Hepatitis C, in 2015. Since then, more than 172,000 patients have been treated with DAA, reducing the estimated national prevalence of active infection to approximately 0.14% (del Amo Valero et al., 2025; Romero Otero et al., 2026). Despite this progress, an estimated 25-30% of active infections remain undiagnosed, a proportion that has changed little in absolute terms since the baseline national seroprevalence survey of 2018, which already reported an undiagnosed fraction of 29.4% (Estirado Gómez et al., 2021; Thomadakis et al., 2026). These remaining infections are increasingly concentrated among people who are not reached efficiently by conventional pathways targeting individuals under classical risk factors, including those with remote exposures, people who inject drugs, migrants from higher-prevalence regions, people with a history of incarceration, patients with HIV or other blood-borne infections, and socially vulnerable populations. Some of these individuals nevertheless interact repeatedly with primary care, emergency departments, hospitals, laboratories or pharmacy services, leaving potentially informative clinical traces before HCV is suspected. This hidden reservoir is now regarded as the principal obstacle to elimination, since residual transmission and disease burden concentrate among individuals outside the conventional healthcare circuit or who have never been tested (Romero Otero et al., 2026).

Beyond conventional screening, or opportunistic screening in selected healthcare settings, simple clinical risk scores can improve targeting. For example, Structured questionnaires and simple risk scores developed for primary care (Martínez-Sanz et al., 2022; McGinn et al., 2008; Nguyen et al., 2005) achieve reasonable discrimination (areas under the curve typically between 0.77 and 0.90), but depend on the availability, completeness, and truthfulness of self-reported risk factors, which are inconsistently captured in daily practice. The increasing availability of longitudinal electronic health records (EHRs), administrative claims, laboratory data, prescriptions and healthcare-utilisation histories provides an alternative strategy: identifying high-risk individuals using only information already generated during routine care. Early large-scale studies using US insurance claims showed that ensemble models could achieve substantially higher precision than universal or risk-based screening at equivalent recall (Doyle et al., 2020), a finding later replicated with gradient-boosting models trained on ambulatory electronic medical records (Rigg et al., 2023). Further studies extended this approach by complementing structured EHR variables with risk factors extracted from clinical narratives through natural language processing (Fong et al., 2023; Sánchez-Antolín et al., 2026). Large databases produced models with excellent discrimination for high-risk HCV infection among tested individuals (Jang et al., 2025), and some RWD-based models were prospectively deployed in the health system (Dagan et al., 2024). Primary-care data from almost two million patients in North-East London also showed that HCV was the most accurately predicted of three blood-borne viruses using routinely recorded demographic, clinical and behavioural indicators (Manley et al., 2026).

Despite these advances, a recent systematic review identified only 13 HCV prediction models and found substantial heterogeneity, high risk of bias in most studies, scarce primary-care validation and inconsistent reporting (Leber et al., 2025). Many models are trained only among tested patients or use a future diagnosis as a proxy for true infection, creating verification and label bias. Other concerns include temporal leakage, class imbalance, low positive predictive value at population prevalence, limited calibration, and uncertain transportability across health systems and epidemiological contexts.

The present study aimed to develop and validate an interpretable predictor of previously undiagnosed or hidden HCV infection using exclusively routinely collected longitudinal data available within the health system. By identifying individuals with a high probability of infection without requiring questionnaires, new examinations or additional data collection, this approach seeks to complement existing screening strategies and provide a scalable instrument for the final phase of HCV elimination.

Material and methods

Study design, setting and data source

We conducted a retrospective, population-based cohort study to develop and temporally validate a machine-learning model for the identification of undiagnosed (hidden) HCV infection using data routinely collected within the public health system of Andalusia (Southern Spain). Clinical information was requested to the "Base Poblacional de Salud de Andalucía" (BPS) (Muñoyerro-Muñiz et al., 2020), a regional repository that integrates electronic health records, laboratory results, and diagnostic coding for more than 16 million users of the Andalusian Health system, under the existing regional framework for secure secondary use of health data (Muñoyerro-Muñiz et al., 2025).

Study population

All individuals diagnosed with HCV infection in the BPS between 2017 and 2024 were identified as cases. A control cohort up to 10 times larger than the case cohort was selected . from the same source population, matched by sex, age, and health district. Matched controls were assigned the same index date as their corresponding case (the date of HCV diagnosis).

In addition, an independent sample of randomly selected controls was drawn from the BPS, selected independently of the matched case-control dataset and not matched to any individual case. Eligibility for this independent control group required that individuals be alive throughout 2017-2023. Each was assigned a random index date within 2024.

Clinical Data

Clinical data corresponding to the patients meeting the inclusion criteria was transferred from BPS to the Platform of Generation of Medical Evidence (PAGEM), a Trusted Research Environment for secondary use of clinical data (Muñoyerro-Muñiz et al., 2025).

All clinical data available in the electronic health record from 30 days prior to the index date to the end of the patient's record, without any additional questionnaire or dedicated data collection, will be used to build the predictor model. Two domains of variables were considered: (i) chronic diseases and diagnostic history, including hypertension, diabetes, obesity, tobacco dependence, dependence on other substances, chronic liver disease other than cirrhosis, and HIV infection; and (ii) clinical laboratory analytes recorded at any point before the index date (including haemoglobin, alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, gamma-glutamyl transferase, platelet count, and total protein, among others), summarised for each patient using the mean, minimum, maximum, and standard deviation of all available determinations.

Data partitioning and threshold selection

The case-control sample was partitioned into training, validation, and test sets using a temporal, non-overlapping criterion based on the year of HCV diagnosis, so that the model was trained exclusively on historical cases and evaluated on progressively more recent, unseen periods. The training set comprised diagnoses from 2017-2022, the validation set comprised diagnoses from 2023, and the test set comprised diagnoses from 2024.

Two complementary modelling strategies were evaluated. The first included all available features, whereas the second excluded the income-based pharmaceutical copayment bracket and the area-level socioeconomic index. The income-based pharmaceutical copayment bracket and the area-level index were removed to reduce the risk of the model primarily capturing socioeconomic disadvantage rather than clinically relevant risk.

For each individual, a single prediction was generated 30 days before the corresponding index date, using only the clinical information available up to that time point. The index date was defined as the diagnosis date for cases, the matched date for matched controls, and a randomly assigned date for controls in the 2024 test set. This approach ensured that predictions reflected only information available before the relevant reference date and prevented any post-diagnosis information from contributing to the risk score.

The classification threshold was selected exclusively in the validation set, by requiring a minimum sensitivity (true positive rate) of 60%, prioritising case detection as appropriate for a screening application. This threshold, together with the aggregation function from which it was derived, was then applied without modification to the independent test set to obtain an unbiased estimate of performance.

Prediction horizon and observation window

To reduce the risk of information leakage and minimise bias related to diagnostic workup immediately preceding hepatitis C diagnosis, a prediction horizon was established 30 days before the index date. For each individual, only clinical data recorded before this time point was used to generate predictor variables.

The observation window therefore included all available clinical information prior to 30 days before the index date. This approach was intended to ensure that the model relied on pre-existing clinical information rather than on tests, diagnoses, or healthcare encounters directly associated with the eventual diagnosis of hepatitis C. Alternative prediction horizons were explored in sensitivity analyses to evaluate their impact on model performance.

Feature selection and feature engineering

Candidate predictors were derived from two main sources of clinical information: chronic disease diagnoses and laboratory test results. Chronic conditions were selected from the information available in the BPS, while laboratory variables were chosen based on their clinical relevance after review by expert hepatologists.

Chronic diseases were encoded as binary variables, indicating whether each condition was present or absent before the prediction horizon. Laboratory results were processed to capture both the central tendency and longitudinal variability of each biomarker over time. For each selected laboratory parameter, the following summary features were generated using all available values recorded before the prediction horizon: mean, standard deviation, minimum value, and maximum value. Sex and age were not included as predictors in the model because they had already been used as matching variables in the matched case-control design. This decision was made to reduce the possibility that the model would learn patterns driven by the matching procedure rather than clinically meaningful predictors of hepatitis C infection.

Model development

Predictive models were developed using an Explainable Boosting Machine (EBM), a generalized-additive-model-based gradient boosting algorithm that combines predictive performance comparable to standard black-box ensembles with feature-level interpretability, allowing the relative contribution of each predictor to the estimated risk to be quantified (Caruana et al., 2015; Nori et al., 2019).

For patients diagnosed with HCV, only information recorded before their individual diagnosis date was used for feature construction. For controls, all information available up to their matching date was used.

Threshold selection

The classification threshold was selected in the validation set by requiring a minimum sensitivity (true positive rate) of 60%, prioritising case detection as appropriate for a screening application, and the same threshold was subsequently applied, without modification, to the independent test set.

Outcome metrics

Model performance was assessed using sensitivity (true positive rate), specificity (true negative rate), false positive rate, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, F1score, Matthews correlation coefficient, and the false discovery and false negative rates, together with receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves for the training, validation, and test sets.

Because the classification threshold approach required a single decision criterion for the confusion matrix in Table 2, formal probability calibration (isotonic regression and Platt/sigmoid scaling, both fitted on the validation set) was explored as an alternative but not adopted for the primary analysis. Discussion

In this population-based study of the entire Andalusian health system, a machine-learning model built exclusively from structured electronic health record data (including chronic disease diagnoses and longitudinal laboratory summary statistics, without any selfreported risk factor) identified 63.5% of patients who would go on to receive an HCV diagnosis in 2024, at a specificity of 91.8%, with an AUC of 0.837 in this fully independent, population-representative test set (compared with 0.826 in validation and 0.841 in training), indicating that discrimination was well preserved despite the marked change in case-mix between the matched development cohorts and the unmatched test population. Precision at this operating point was low (7.0%), a direct and expected consequence of evaluating the model against a control population with a true HCV prevalence of approximately 1%, rather than the artificially enriched prevalence of a matched case-control sample. The negative predictive value, conversely, was high (99.6%). Predictor importance was dominated by longitudinal liver-related and haematological laboratory markers (alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, platelet count) together with HIV infection, substance dependence, obesity, and a small number of chronic comorbidities, a clinically coherent set of variables, once age, sex, and two variables identified as artefacts of the matched design (two socioeconomic proxies) were removed.

These results presented here extend a rapidly growing body of work applying machine learning to routinely collected health-system data for HCV case-finding. The most directly comparable efforts used US insurance claims (Doyle et al., 2020) and ambulatory electronic medical records (Rigg et al., 2023), respectively, and reported their models' advantage as a fold-improvement in precision over risk-based or universal screening rather than as a conventional AUC, limiting direct numerical comparison. Studies reporting a standard discrimination statistic offer a closer benchmark. The use of structured EHR data in a US academic health system achieved a C-statistic of 0.777 (Fong et al., 2023), while a value of 0.916 was reported using a larger all-payer claims database (Jang et al., 2025). In a prospective, population-wide deployment within an integrated Israeli health system, an AUROC of approximately 0.95 was reported together with a number needed to screen (NNS) of 10, versus 1,029 for conventional risk-based screening (a roughly 100-fold efficiency gain) (Dagan et al., 2024). Recently, a review of published HIV, hepatitis B, and HCV prediction models, reported a pooled AUC of 0.80 across 13 comparable HCV prediction models (Leber et al., 2025).

Our results sit at or above this pooled estimate but were obtained under a design explicitly built to avoid several of the specific weaknesses (matched or convenience test sets, absence of calibration assessment, unclear temporal separation between development and evaluation data) observed in most of the published predictors, as highlighted in the review (Leber et al., 2025).

Within Spain, the most direct comparator remains the LiverTAI model (Sánchez-Antolín et al., 2026), which combined electronic health records with natural language processing across six hospitals to detect unconfirmed HCV infection, reporting an AUC of 0.794 on independent geographic validation. Our model achieved higher discrimination (AUC 0.837 vs. 0.794) than LiverTAI, using structured data alone, without a natural-languageprocessing component, although the two studies target related but distinct outcomes (undiagnosed active infection versus unconfirmed serology) and are not strictly interchangeable. Our findings also complement two other Spanish initiatives built on automated, laboratory-triggered case detection rather than statistical risk modelling, Intelligen-C (Castro Urda et al., 2025) and CriVHado C (Griñó et al., 2025), illustrating two complementary approaches (rule-based alerting versus continuous risk scoring) to the same underlying public-health problem of residual undiagnosed infection, estimated at 25-30% of active cases nationally despite treatment coverage exceeding 80% of diagnosed patients (Romero Otero et al., 2026), against the WHO's 2030 elimination targets (Gamkrelidze et al., 2021).

Compared with earlier, questionnaire-based risk-assessment tools like self-administered instrument (Nguyen et al., 2005), primary-care screening tool (AUC 0.77) (McGinn et al., 2008), and the five-item score (C-statistic 0.896) (Martínez-Sanz et al., 2022) our model's discrimination is comparable to or below the latter. The distinction, however, is one of implementation rather than accuracy alone: those instruments require prospectively administered questionnaires capturing self-reported risk behaviour at the point of care, whereas our model operates entirely on data already present in the health record, requiring no patient contact, no clinician time, and no additional data-collection infrastructure, and can in principle be applied retrospectively across an entire historical population rather than only to patients presenting for a specific encounter.

Regarding the findings that our explainable approach has, the prominence of transaminases, platelet count, and other liver-related and haematological markers as relevant variables for the classification is consistent with the biochemical abnormalities long used as opportunistic screening triggers in Spain and elsewhere (Romero Otero et al., 2026), while the appearance of HIV infection and dependence on substances other than tobacco among the leading predictors mirrors the well-documented concentration of incident and recently acquired HCV infection among people living with HIV who report chemsex-associated substance use, particularly among men who have sex with men (Martín-Carbonero et al., 2024). The presence of obesity, hypertension, and markers of hepatic and haematological dysfunction (unspecified cirrhosis, prothrombin time, other unspecified liver disease) further aligns with a population accumulating undiagnosed liver injury before eventual diagnosis, of the kind identified through opportunistic testing in Spanish emergency departments (Camelo-Castillo et al., 2024). The final predictor set, obtained after removing pharmaceutical co-payment category, and a zone-level deprivation index, is free of variables that had shown implausible or bias-prone associations with the outcome, and is, correspondingly, more directly interpretable in clinical and biological terms. This pattern of laboratory-and comorbidity-driven prediction, rather than one dominated by demographic or socioeconomic proxies, is also broadly consistent with the determinants identified for HIV, HCV, and HBV risk in a comparable primary-care machine-learning model (Manley et al., 2026).

This study presents several characteristics that, taken together, distinguish it from previously published HCV case-finding models. First, as before, the analysis draws on a genuinely population-scale, multi-year cohort (more than 430,000 individuals across an entire regional health system followed from 2017 to 2024) evaluated under a strict, nonoverlapping temporal split. Second, and most distinctively, the final test evaluation was deliberately conducted against an independent, non-matched, population-representative control sample rather than the matched controls used for model development. This is a design choice that directly addresses the verification bias and limited real-world validity (Leber et al., 2025) a drawback of most published models in this field, and which, to our knowledge, few comparable HCV prediction studies have explicitly implemented. Third, the exclusion of pharmaceutical co-payment category, and a zone-level deprivation index was not an incidental modelling choice but the outcome of a deliberate bias-auditing step, in which model explainability was used prospectively to detect and remove predictors likely to encode socioeconomic proxies or artefacts of the matched design rather than genuine clinical signal. This is an equity-oriented methodological safeguard that is largely absent from the prior literature reviewed here. Fourth, the systematic evaluation of nine prediction horizons spanning 30 to 720 days before diagnosis provides, to our knowledge, the most granular published assessment of how model performance decays with increasing lead time, information directly relevant to designing a realistic screening workflow rather than a single, retrospectively optimal cut-point. Fifth, formal calibration was assessed and reported transparently, including its limitations, rather than assumed from discrimination alone, a step recommended but rarely implemented in this literature. Finally, retaining the EBM architecture (Caruana et al., 2015; Nori et al., 2019) preserved full interpretability of the final predictor set throughout this bias-auditing process, which would not have been straightforward with a conventional black-box ensemble, supporting internal consistency across Andalusian health districts, though this remains a withinregion check rather than external validation.

Obviously, our model is not exempt from limitations. The low precision (7.0%) and correspondingly high false discovery rate (93.0%) observed at a 60%-sensitivity operating point are an inherent consequence of screening for a low-prevalence condition (~1% in the test population) and would need to be communicated explicitly in any operational deployment, alongside the NNS framing offered above. Calibration, while modestly improved by isotonic or sigmoid post-hoc adjustment, remained imperfect at higher predicted probabilities in both calibrated and uncalibrated versions, and was not adopted for the primary classification analysis. Results for the full-variable model and for prediction horizons other than 30 days are reported only in the Supplementary Table 2, limiting the extent to which this model can characterise the trade-off between the excluded variables' predictive contribution and the fairness rationale for their removal. Finally, despite this study was conducted entirely within a single regional health system, external validity remains to be established. Transportability may be limited by differences in EHR coding conventions and laboratory panels across health systems, and by regional differences in the epidemiological profile of undiagnosed HCV (e.g., prevalence of HIV coinfection, injection drug use). The consistent performance observed across Andalusian health districts (see Results) is reassuring against overfitting to a single local population but remains a within-region check rather than external validation. Validation in an independent health system, ideally another Spanish region, and eventually beyond Spain, is an essential next step before broader deployment.
