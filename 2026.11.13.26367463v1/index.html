Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Introduction

The incidence of esophageal adenocarcinoma (EAC) and its precursor, Barrett's esophagus (BE), increased from 1975 through the early 2000s 1, 2 before plateauing in the last decade 3, 4 . Although only a small proportion of BE patients develop high-grade dysplasia (HGD) or EAC (~0.2-0.4% annually 5, 6 ), identifying those at highest risk remains a major clinical challenge. Established risk factors of EAC progression include high body mass index (BMI), male sex, tobacco smoking, older age, ulceration within the BE segment, long BE segment length, and presence of dysplasia 7- 9 . However, histopathologic detection of dysplasia, which minimally stratifies patients for a smaller endoscopic follow-up window, [10] [11] [12] is the only indicator of progression that is widely used, despite its limited positive predictive value 13 and substantial inter-observer variability 14 . Moreover, approximately 10% of BE patients develop HGD or EAC without any previous endoscopic indication such as low-grade dysplasia (LGD) 15 , highlighting the need for molecular biomarkers to improve risk stratification and enable more personalized surveillance rather than current 'one-size-fits-all' strategies.

Due to the likelihood that nearly all EAC arises in BE [16] [17] [18] , an important strategy for reducing EAC mortality is to identify BE patients before neoplastic progression and detecting HGD or early EAC during surveillance when treatment is most effective [19] [20] [21] [22] . Current guidelines recommend screening in selected high-risk individuals with multiple risk factors, including age ≥50 years, male sex, white race, history of smoking, chronic gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD), obesity, or family history of BE or EAC 10, 12, 23, 24 . However, recent studies suggest that BE diagnoses rates are decreasing in older populations while increasing in younger populations, particularly in those aged 30-39 years 25, 26 . Understanding when BE first develops and how long it persists before clinical detection may therefore help inform future screening strategies.

Although older chronological age is associated with increased EAC progression risk (60-69-yearold vs <50-year-old HR=2.43; 95% CI 1.43-4.13), substantial inter-patient variability 5, 27 may reflect differences in the age at which BE first develops 28 . Indeed, BE duration, or dwell time (patient age minus BE onset age), may be more biologically relevant to progression risk than chronological age alone, reflecting the progressive accumulation of somatic mutations, including TP53 alterations 29 , copy number changes 30 and epigenetic alterations over time 31, 32 . DNA methylation-based measures of biological aging have been associated with increased risk of neurodegenerative disease 33 , cardiovascular disease 34 , cancer 35 , and mortality 36, 37 . However, BE onset age has historically been difficult to estimate because metaplastic tissue may persist asymptomatically for decades before diagnosis.

We previously developed a Bayesian molecular clock that uses age-associated DNA methylation drift at selected CpG sites to estimate patient-specific BE onset age and tissue dwell time 28 . Briefly, certain CpG dinucleotides are susceptible to methylation changes over time (clock-CpGs) 38, 39 and the incidence and accumulation of de novo methylation in some specific CpG sites, known as epigenetic drift, can be used to determine accelerated aging rates in BE tissue versus normal esophagus. Here, we applied this molecular clock to three UK study cohorts to characterize BE onset ages and dwell times, including validation against normal gastroesophageal tissues and independent epidemiologic modeling. We further compared BE tissue ages between age-matched patients with non-dysplastic BE and those with prevalent HGD/EAC and evaluated whether tissue age at initial BE diagnosis was associated with subsequent neoplastic progression in a matched longitudinal case-control cohort. Together, these analyses aimed to clarify the natural history of BE and assess the potential of molecular tissue age as a biomarker for personalized surveillance.

Materials and Methods

Study populations and DNA methylation profiling

We analyzed BE and non-BE gastroesophageal tissue samples from three identified UK study populations: the Northern Ireland Barrett's oesophagus Register (NIBR) cross-sectional cohort (n=50 patients), the Cambridge Oesophageal Cancer Clinical and Molecular Stratification (OCCAMS)/Biomarker/BEST2 cohort 40, 41 (n=140 patients), and a nested NIBR case-control longitudinal cohort (NIBR-Progressor) of 42 HGD/EAC progressors and 42 matched nonprogressors (n=84 patients). Tissue samples (formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded [FFPE] from NIBR cohorts and fresh-frozen from Cambridge cohort) underwent expert pathological review. Cohort characteristics and selection criteria are detailed in the Supplementary Information.

After excluding samples that failed quality control (QC) and/or had missing demographic information on age at sampling (Supplementary Figure S1), the NIBR cohort included 35 patients with non-dysplastic BE (NDBE) and 6 with dysplastic BE. Patients with NDBE contributed biopsies from 1-4 time points, collected at incident endoscopy and during follow-up (≥6 months post-diagnosis), whereas patients with prevalent HGD contributed biopsies from a single time point at BE diagnosis. The Cambridge cohort included 140 patients, each contributing data from a single time sampling point at or after BE diagnosis: 22 with NDBE, 53 with adjacent EAC, 13 with adjacent HGD or intramucosal cancer (IMC), 19 with adjacent indefinite for dysplasia (IND) or low-grade dysplasia (LGD), 1 with adjacent crypt dysplasia, and 32 with unknown adjacent tissue dysplasia grade. In the NIBR-Progressor cohort, NDBE biopsies were collected at the time of BE diagnosis. In total, 291 samples were used in the downstream methylation analyses across the three cohorts: 61 from NIBR, 164 from Cambridge, and 66 from NIBR-Progressor.

Patient demographics and clinical characteristics are provided in Table 1 (see Supplementary Figure S2 for detailed study design). Genome-wide DNA methylation for each tissue sample was profiled using Illumina MethylationEPIC arrays (NIBR cross-sectional and Cambridge cohorts) or the Twist Human Methylome Panel (NIBR-Progressor cohort). Platform-specific preprocessing, normalization, and quality control procedures are described in the Supplementary Methods.

BE onset age and tissue age estimation

We applied a previously developed Bayesian epigenetic clock model based on methylation drift at BE-specific CpG sites to estimate patient-specific BE onset ages using Markov chain Monte Carlo sampling 28 . BE tissue age (dwell time) was calculated as patient age at biopsy minus estimated onset age. Sensitivity analyses assessed robustness of onset age estimates to variation in CpG data availability across samples, and estimates were compared with those from two independently developed epigenetic clock models 42, 43 (see Supplementary Methods). Mann-Whitney U tests and logistic regression evaluated group differences and associations with neoplastic progression.

Epidemiologic modeling

Independently, we inferred the population distribution of BE onset ages by fitting a scaled gamma cumulative distribution function to published age-specific BE prevalence data from three U.S. study populations (n=77,832 total BE patients) [44] [45] [46] . Simulated onset ages conditional on observed endoscopy ages were used to estimate cohort-specific distributions, with 95% simulation intervals obtained from 10,000 Monte Carlo replicates (see Supplementary Methods for details).

Results

We analyzed BE biopsies obtained from patients at a median age of 56 years (IQR: 45-66) in the NIBR cohort and 59 years (IQR: 53.3-65) in the NIBR-Progressor cohort, corresponding to their respective ages at BE diagnosis (Table 1). Epigenetic clock validation with normal tissue samples

The BE molecular clock uses epigenetic divergence from the normal epigenome (esophageal squamous or gastric cardia) to predict BE onset age. For two patients of the same chronological age at sampling, the patient whose BE developed earlier would have a younger estimated BE onset age and, consequently, a longer BE dwell time. In contrast, normal gastroesophageal epithelium is expected to have an onset age that is similar to the age of sampling (i.e., the clock model 'ticks' with age of the patient rather than BE age) and a dwell time of ~ 0 based on how the BE clock was originally derived 28 . We found statistically significant different estimated dwell times between non-BE and BE tissues (gastric cardia [GC] vs. BE: p = 1.0e-12; squamous esophagus [SQE] vs. BE: p = 9.0e-8; gastroesophageal junction [GEJ] vs. BE: p = 0.018), with negligible median dwell times in normal tissues (GC: median = 1.8 years [IQR = 1.3-3.8]; SQE: median = 0.7 years [IQR = 0.6-0.8]; Supplementary Figure S3A, B). 16 of the 49 normal samples (14 SQE and 2 GC, thus not shown in Supplementary Figure 3) had onset estimates indiscernible from patient age (dwell time = 0) given our Markov Chain Monte Carlo prior constraints. In contrast, the median dwell time in BE tissue across all cohorts (NIBR, Cambridge, and NIBR-Progressor) was 30.6 years (IQR = 16.0-40.1). The clock therefore behaves as expected in cohorts independent of those used for model development 28 , supporting its validity and applicability.

The remaining 12.5% of patients had a BE onset age between 50 and 57.6 years old. The median BE dwell time for these patients was 18.1 years (IQR = 11.2-23.8).

Validation of BE dwell times with independently developed epigenetic clocks

To further validate our model and compare its BE onset age estimates with independent measures of epigenetic aging in these two cohorts, we applied two orthogonal epigenetic aging clocks to our BE data. We first applied epiTOC1 which reports the average beta value across 385 CpGs associated with Polycomb target genes that are known to be unmethylated at birth and gradually drift toward hypermethylation throughout life 42 . Then we applied epiTOC2 which estimates the number of mitotic divisions that have occurred throughout life in that tissue 43 . Consistent with the substantial heterogeneity in BE dwell times estimated using our molecular clock (Figure 2C, Figure 3C), we observed substantial variability in epiTOC1 scores and epiTOC2 mitotic division rates (total number of mitotic divisions divided by chronological age at time of sampling) across BE tissue samples (Figure 4 A,C). For both epiTOC clock measures, we found the scores to be significantly higher in BE than the scores derived from the gastric cardia (GC), squamous esophagus (SQE), and gastroesophageal junction (GEJ) tissues (e.g., p = 5.9e-13 for epiTOC and p=2.6e-13 for epiTOC2 in the BE vs. GC comparison). Additionally, we found our dwell time estimates to be highly correlated with the epiTOC1 (R² = 0.9, p < 2.2e-16) and epiTOC2 (R² = 0.61, p < 2.2e-16) scores when applied to the same tissue samples (Figure 4 B,D).

Patients with adjacent dysplasia have biologically older BE than patients with indolent BE

To further investigate BE dwell times in relation to pre-EAC neoplastic risk, 13 BE patients with prevalent dysplasia (one of which had a duplicate biopsy) or IMC, were matched by age at sampling (within 2 years) and sex with 13 BE patients with NDBE in the NIBR and Cambridge cohorts. The 13 patients with NDBE were independent from those used in the comparison to EAC patients in Figure 3 D, E. BE onset ages and dwell times for all patients were already estimated above from NDBE tissue (Figures 2, 3). The median age at BE diagnosis for the dysplastic patients was 62 years old (IQR=54.0-66.0); the median onset age of BE adjacent to dysplasia was 27.8 years old (IQR=15.5.2-37.6); and the median dwell time was 34.2 years (IQR=28.4-40.3). The median age at diagnosis, onset age, and dwell time for the matched indolent BE cases were 62 years old (IQR=54.0-66.0), 45.6 years old (IQR=24.8-51.1) and 19.9 years (IQR=12.9-25.4), respectively (Figure 5). Consistent BE onset ages were found for one dysplastic patient (samples 2B and 2C in Figure 5A) when analyzing methylation data separately from two BE biopsies taken from different esophageal quadrants. The dysplastic patients had significantly earlier BE onset ages (median = 27.8 years old, IQR = 15.5-37.6) than matched NDBE patients (median = 45.6 years old, IQR = 24.8-51.1) for a median difference in BE onset age of 17.8 years (p=0.044; Figure 5B). Further, the median BE dwell times were significantly longer in the dysplastic patients (median = 34.2 years, IQR = 28.4-40.3) compared with the age and sex-matched non-dysplastic BE patients (median = 19.9 years, IQR = 12.9-25.4) for a median difference of 14.3 years (p=0.0042; Figure 5C).

Estimated BE dwell time is associated with subsequent progression to HGD/EAC

To independently evaluate the association between estimated BE tissue age and subsequent neoplastic progression, we applied our BE onset age model to an independent NIBR-Progressor case-control cohort with longitudinal follow-up. This cohort included non-dysplastic BE biopsies obtained before progression to HGD or EAC in patients who subsequently developed neoplasia ("progressors"), alongside matched patients who did not progress to HGD/EAC ("nonprogressors"). The initial cohort comprised 84 patients (42 progressors and 42 non-progressors). Following quality control, we estimated BE onset ages and dwell times for 66 patients with methylation data available for at least 30 BE clock CpGs, using all available clock CpGs for each patient (Figure 6; see Supplementary Methods). Across these 66 patients (n=31 progressors and n=35 non-progressors), the median estimated BE onset age was 37.6 years (IQR = 28.8-48.5), and the median estimated BE dwell time was 19.8 years (IQR = 7.3-34.7).

For these 66 patients, 24 matched pairs from the original study design were retained, and an additional six age-and sex-matched pairs were identified (age at sampling matched within 2 years), yielding 30 matched progressor/non-progressor pairs (Supplementary Figure S8). Among these pairs, progressors had significantly earlier estimated BE onset ages (median = 31.0 years, IQR=22.3-37.5) than non-progressors (median = 42.4 years, IQR=37.5-54.9; p=0.0001). Correspondingly, estimated BE dwell times were significantly longer in progressors (median = 32.2 years; IQR = 20.9-36.2) than in non-progressors (median = 10.4 years, IQR = 5.0-20.4; p=0.0004).

We next classified the 66 patients into younger and older BE tissue age groups using the cohortwide median estimated dwell time of 19.8 years as the threshold (Figure 6C). In logistic regression analyses adjusting for chronological age at sampling and sex, patients with older BE tissue ages (>19.8 years) had significantly greater odds of subsequent neoplastic progression than those with younger tissue ages (OR = 12.1, 95% CI 3.7-47.5; p=9.96 × 10⁻⁵; Figure 6F). Although the wide confidence interval reflects modest cohort size, this finding suggests that BE tissue age exceeding 20 years may represent an important threshold for surveillance risk stratification. Consistent with this finding, when BE dwell time was modeled as a continuous variable, each 14.2-year increase (equivalent to one standard deviation of BE dwell time among the 66 patients) was associated with nearly 4-fold higher odds of progression (adjusted OR = 3.94, 95% CI: 2.05-8.65).

Taken together, these findings suggest that patients undergoing BE surveillance who are similar in chronological age may nevertheless have substantially different BE tissue ages, as measured using epigenetic data derived from their bulk BE biopsy. Estimated BE onset ages supported by epidemiologic data on BE prevalence

As an independent validation of our epigenetic clock estimates, we examined published epidemiologic studies reporting age-specific BE prevalence among patients undergoing their first esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) [44] [45] [46] . To compare with our primary analyses of BE onset age estimates across both sexes (Figure 2), we used overall BE prevalence estimates combining males and females. These studies showed remarkably consistent, approximately linear increases in BE prevalence with age (Figure 7A). In the largest study (n=76,691 BE patients among 2,337,786 total patients undergoing EGD), the fitted prevalence curve increased approximately linearly between ages 15 and 65 years. Under a simplified model of persistent BE, this pattern is consistent with an approximately uniform distribution of onset ages over this interval, corresponding to a mean onset age of 40 years. Consistent with age of onset distributions for other common diseases, a fitted gamma distribution also recapitulates this data when constraining the mean onset age to 40 years (Figure 7B, see Supplementary Methods). The fitted gamma distribution had a shape parameter of 6.14 and an asymptotic prevalence of 4.36%, corresponding to a median onset age of 37.9 years and a mode of 33.5 years (Figure 7C, blue density).

Although this distribution is older than onset ages derived from our epigenetic data above, the distribution of onset ages among clinically ascertained prevalent cases depends on the ages at which patients are sampled 47 . Conditioning on BE being present at the time of diagnosis can therefore shift the observed onset age distribution toward younger ages. To account for this sampling effect, we simulated a cohort representative of the NIBR cohort by drawing a random sampling age (i.e., age at diagnostic EGD) with replacement from the 24 observed BE diagnosis ages (Figure 7C, orange density). For each simulated patient, we then sampled a BE onset age from the fitted gamma distribution, truncated at their age of sampling (Figure 7D). Among 10,000 simulated patients, the median estimated BE onset age was 31.5 years old (95% simulation-based interval for the median in cohorts of 24 patients, 26.5-36.7 years, see Supplementary Methods). The simulated onset age distribution was not significantly different from the distribution independently estimated from epigenetic data in the NIBR cohort (Mann-Whitney U-test p=0.65, Figure 7D). These findings demonstrate that the younger BE onset ages inferred from our epigenetic clock are consistent with the underlying onset age distributions implied by epidemiologic data after accounting for age at clinical ascertainment.

Discussion

Barrett's esophagus (BE) is an asymptomatic precursor to esophageal adenocarcinoma (EAC) that is often undetected before cancer diagnosis. Although the annual risk of progression from nondysplastic BE to EAC is low (0.1-0.3%), the estimated lifetime risk of ~10% 48 is sufficient to explain the overall burden of EAC in the US 16 . Reducing EAC mortality depends on first identifying individuals with BE and subsequently detecting and treating high-grade dysplasia (HGD) or early EAC during surveillance. Current US and European screening guidelines consider age ≥50 years as an established risk factor 10, 12, 23, 24 , yet rising BE incidence rates rising in younger populations 25 who have substantial prevalent neoplasia risk 46 highlights the need to better understand when BE develops and how long it persists before clinical detection.

We applied an established BE molecular clock to DNA methylation data from 294 total samples across three UK study cohorts to estimate BE onset age and biological tissue age (dwell time). Model validation using normal gastric cardia, squamous esophagus, and gastroesophageal junction tissues demonstrated minimal epigenetic divergence from the reference normal state, compared with significantly greater dwell times in the BE tissue. Estimates were further validated using two independent epigenetic clock models.

In the population-based Northern Ireland Barrett's Oesophagus Register (NIBR), we found the median estimated BE onset age was 29.5 years, with 87.5% of patients developing BE before age 50. Similar results were observed in the Cambridge cohort (median 29.3 years; 87.7% before age 50). In the independent NIBR-Progressor cohort, median onset age was somewhat higher (37.6 years), although 78.8% of patients still had estimated onset before age 50. To independently evaluate these estimates, we modeled BE onset using published US age-specific prevalence data. The fitted population onset-age distribution had a median of 37.9 years, compared with 31.5 years when conditioned on the endoscopy ages of the NIBR cohort, closely matching our methylationbased estimates. Simulations further demonstrated that median onset age can vary by approximately 10 years across modestly sized cohorts due to age-dependent ascertainment. These findings suggest that the population median BE onset age may be near 40 years, whereas clinically diagnosed cohorts may have younger median onset ages near 30 years. This ascertainment effect may also explain the tendency toward later estimated onset in women, who are generally diagnosed at older ages, as sex differences were not significant after matching on age at sampling.

Beyond informing BE natural history, molecular tissue age may provide a biomarker of neoplastic progression risk. Epidemiologic studies suggest that progression risk accumulates with time since BE diagnosis 27, 49, 50 , potentially reflecting ongoing mutation accumulation and clonal evolution.

However, biological BE duration may be more informative than time since clinical diagnosis. Consistent with this hypothesis, BE dwell times were significantly longer in patients with adjacent dysplasia (median difference 14.3 years; p=0.0042) or concurrent EAC (median difference 12.5 years; p=0.00019) compared with matched patients with non-dysplastic BE. Interestingly, we previously found that patients with a family history of BE/EAC, a known risk factor for EAC progression, also had older BE tissue ages 28 , but were unable to analyze this in the current study populations. Moreover, in an age-matched nested case-control cohort, BE tissue age exceeding 19.8 years at initial diagnosis was associated with 12-fold increased odds of subsequent HGD/EAC progression. Together, these findings suggest that molecular estimates of BE duration may capture clinically relevant variation in progression risk beyond chronological patient age.

Our study has several limitations, including modest sample sizes, although findings were consistent across independent cohorts, tissue preservation methods (archival FFPE and freshfrozen biopsies), and methylation platforms (Illumina EPIC and Twist Human Methylome Panel). Because BE onset cannot be directly observed histologically, our clock may capture an earlier molecular event preceding metaplasia rather than its precise initiation. Recent whole-genome sequencing evidence suggests that copy number alterations may arise decades before clinical BE detection 51 , supporting the possibility of early molecular evolution. Nevertheless, the agreement between methylation-based and independent epidemiologic estimates supports the biological plausibility of our inferred onset ages. Epidemiologic estimates may be affected by misclassification from diagnostic and procedure codes in the largest dataset evaluated, although similar age-specific prevalence trends were observed in two studies requiring histopathologic confirmation of BE.

Residual confounding by BE segment length, BMI, smoking, GERD, or treatment history cannot be excluded because these variables were not consistently available. Additionally, the absence of spatial sampling information in NIBR and the use of BE tissue adjacent to EAC tumors in Cambridge may introduce heterogeneity given the polyclonal nature of BE. However, our objective was to estimate the biological age of BE tissue as a potential global marker of accumulated risk, without requiring identification of the specific clone giving rise to cancer. Our findings suggest that bulk BE biopsies could provide clinically useful information, although larger longitudinal studies with spatial sampling are needed.

An additional consideration is that greater epigenetic aging in BE tissue from progressors may reflect longer dwell times, accelerated methylation drift, or both. Elevated drift rates could also indicate a broader epigenetic field effect associated with neoplasia. Although our hierarchical Bayesian model jointly estimates onset age and CpG-specific drift rates, these mechanisms cannot be fully disentangled. Nevertheless, the independent epidemiologic validation supports the plausibility of inferred onset ages, while the observed differences between progressors and nonprogressors suggest that clock-derived epigenetic aging captures biologically meaningful variation in progression risk.

Overall, our findings demonstrate the potential of methylation-based molecular clocks to reconstruct BE natural history and identify patients at increased risk of neoplastic progression. More broadly, leveraging passive DNA methylation changes to estimate biological tissue age from relatively low-cost bulk methylation data may provide a broadly applicable biomarker of cancer evolution, with potential to inform risk stratification across diverse precancerous conditions and cancer types 52 . Estimating when BE develops, often decades before diagnosis, may improve computational models of BE natural history and inform future screening strategies and clinical trial design. Importantly, these findings do not support broadly lowering screening ages. Rather, the association between BE tissue age exceeding approximately 20 years and subsequent progression suggests a prolonged window for early detection and intervention. This raises the possibility that screening beginning at age 50 could identify much of the high-risk BE population before neoplastic progression, warranting further population-based modeling. The clinical priority is therefore not simply earlier BE detection but identifying those patients most likely to progress to EAC. Incorporating molecular estimates of BE duration into future risk prediction models could enable more personalized surveillance and timely intervention to prevent EAC.
