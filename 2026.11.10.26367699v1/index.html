Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

p<0.05 When the total effect (c-path) was statistically significant (p<0.05), the proportion mediated was calculated by dividing the indirect effect by the total effect.

The APA manual states: Report exact p values (e.g., p = .031) to two or three decimal places. However, report p values less than .001 as p < .001. However, 2 decimals is too imprecise for many use-cases (e.g., a p value meta-analysis), so report p values with three digits.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 1 non-significant p value that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Text Sentence
p = 0.085 At follow-up, education remained strongly associated with BMI (p < 0.001), whereas gender no longer reached statistical significance (p = 0.085).

For metascientific articles demonstrating the rate of misinterpretations of non-significant results is high, see:

Aczel B, Palfi B, Szollosi A, Kovacs M, Szaszi B, Szecsi P, Zrubka M, Gronau Q, van den Bergh D, Wagenmakers E (2018). “Quantifying Support for the Null Hypothesis in Psychology: An Empirical Investigation.” Advances in Methods and Practices in Psychological Science, 1(3), 357–366. doi:10.1177/2515245918773742.

Murphy S, Merz R, Reimann L, Fernández A (2025). “Nonsignificance misinterpreted as an effect’s absence in psychology: Prevalence and temporal analyses.” Royal Society Open Science, 12(3), 242167. doi:10.1098/rsos.242167.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Introduction: Socioeconomic differences in body mass index (BMI) are well established, but less is known about gender differences and their behavioral pathways. We examined gender differences in the association between socioeconomic position (SEP) and BMI, and whether dietary factors and physical activity mediated the associations.

Methods: We used survey data from the employees of the City of Helsinki aged 19-39 collected in 2017 (n = 4,400, 80% women). SEP was measured by highest educational attainment, and the examined mediators were consumption of fruit and vegetables, red and processed meat, sweets and confectionery and alcohol as well as leisure-time vigorous physical activity (VPA) and sedentary time. We conducted gender-specific multiple mediation analyses using structural equation modeling and additional analyses using multiple imputation by chained equations.

Results: With increasing educational attainment, mean BMI decreased from 26.4 kg/m² to 24.0 kg/m² among women, and from 26.6 kg/m² to 25.2 kg/m² among men. Lower SEP was associated with lower consumption of fruit and vegetables, higher consumption of red and processed meat, and lower VPA in both genders. Women reported healthier diets but less VPA than men. The strongest SEP-BMI mediator among women was VPA (indirect effect: 0.35, 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.26, 0.44), followed by fruit and vegetable consumption (0.15, 95% CI 0.08, 0.23) and red and processed meat consumption (0.06, 95% CI 0.02, 0.11). Among men, significant mediators were red and processed meat consumption (0.12, 95% CI 0.03, 0.23), sedentary time (0.11, 95% CI 0.01, 0.24) and VPA (0.10, 95% CI 0.02, 0.21).

Conclusions: Socioeconomic inequalities in BMI appear stronger among women and are shaped through partly different mechanisms between genders. Improving VPA may be relevant among low-SEP women, whereas reducing sedentary time may be more relevant among low-SEP men. Reducing red and processed meat is beneficial for both genders.

Introduction

Overweight and obesity are rising globally and in Finland, and in high-income countries, higher body weights are increasingly concentrated in lower socioeconomic groups [1]. According to the World Health Organization, in 2022 the global prevalence of overweight and obesity among adults aged 18 years and older was 43% and 16%, respectively [2]. In Finland, the prevalence of obesity has increased substantially between 2000 and 2023, rising from 21% to 30% among men and from 22% to 30% among women, with the most pronounced increases seen among adults aged 30-44 years [3]. However, recent trends suggest that in high income countries, the rise in obesity is plateauing for instance due to better affordability of healthier foods compared to low-and middle-income countries [4].

Socioeconomic position (SEP) refers to social and economic differences within society that arise from educational, occupational, economic, and cultural resources [5]. Education is a widely used indicator of SEP because it is established relatively early in adulthood and captures resources that may shape health knowledge, working conditions, material circumstances, and health-related behaviors [6]. In high-income countries, body mass index (BMI) generally shows an inverse socioeconomic gradient, although the strength of this association differs by gender [7]. Previous studies have often reported stronger and more consistent inverse associations between SEP and obesity among women than among men [8, 9]. This pattern was already identified in the Finnish population in the 20 th century [10] and has since been replicated in later national and international studies [8, 11].

Dietary behaviors are among the most plausible mediators linking SEP to later BMI. Lower SEP is linked to consuming fewer fruits and vegetables [12, 13]. Fruit and vegetable consumption can support weight regulation through lower energy density, higher fiber content and greater satiety, and previous research suggests a higher consumption is associated with reduced obesity [14]. By contrast, red and processed meats are often energy dense and embedded in dietary patterns associated with greater adiposity but have also been independently associated with weight gain [15]. The consumption is higher in those with lower SEP, which makes it a potential mediator of the SEP-BMI association [12]. Sweets and confectionery are also plausible mediators, although the strongest evidence linking sugar intake to obesity concerns sugar-sweetened beverages [16]. Alcohol should also be considered, since it can contribute to excess energy intake directly or indirectly by weakening dietary restraint. The epidemiological evidence on alcohol and adiposity is mixed, but heavier intake and some beverage patterns seem less favorable [17]. However, no clear socioeconomic differences have been observed in sweets and confectionery or alcohol consumption, although the gender pattern suggests that women consume more sweets, whereas men consume more alcohol [12].

In addition to diet including alcohol consumption, moderate-to-vigorous physical activity as well as sedentary time are potential mediators since they are socioeconomically patterned, potentially modifiable and consistently linked to weight management [18] [19] [20]. Physical activity and less sedentary time may influence body weight not only through energy expenditure but also through improved appetite regulation and satiety signaling [21]. However, physical activity is socially patterned, and these patterns vary by domain and intensity: higher socioeconomic groups tend to be more active during leisure time and to accumulate more high-intensity physical activity, whereas lower socioeconomic groups tend to accumulate more occupational and light-intensity physical activity [19, 20, 22]. Sedentary time is positively associated with both general and abdominal obesity, and although sedentary time contributes to energy balance, the evidence is unclear whether increased sedentary time leads to increased BMI or whether the associations are due to reverse causation [23] [24] [25]. Socioeconomic differences also vary by sedentary domain, with higher SEP groups tending to report more occupational sitting, while lower SEP groups tend to report more leisure-time sitting [26].

Despite extensive research on socioeconomic differences in obesity, SEP-gender interactions in the associations are rarely explored. More specifically, the mechanisms through which SEP contributes to the development of obesity, and whether these mechanisms differ by gender, remain insufficiently understood. We hypothesize that lower SEP is linked to a higher BMI partly through dietary factors and physical activity and that the absolute effects of the mediators are larger among women because the SEP-obesity gradient is steeper in women. First, we examined gender differences in the associations between SEP and BMI. Second, we examined whether dietary factors and physical activity similarly mediate associations among both genders.

Methods:

Data and study population

This study is part of the Helsinki Health Study, which examines the health and well-being of employees of the City of Helsinki, the largest employer in Finland with approximately 38,000 employees across diverse occupational sectors [27, 28]. The target population consisted of 11,459 employees born in 1978 or later (aged 19-39 years in 2017) who had been employed for at least four months with a contract of at least 50% regular working hours. Data collection occurred in autumn 2017 (Phase 1) via online and mailed questionnaires, supplemented by shorter telephone interviews for non-responders.

The initial response rate was 51.5% (n = 5,898). While non-response analysis indicated slightly higher participation among those with higher SEP, the sample was found to reasonably represent the target population [27]. We excluded telephone interviewees (n = 510) because the interviews did not include all variables of interest needed for this study. We also excluded those with missing data on education, BMI as well as one or more of the mediators, as shown in Figure 1. Gender had no missing values. Outliers in physical activity and sedentary time were identified as values more than three standard deviations from the mean, following previous studies [20]. The final analytical sample included 4,400 participants of whom 80% were women, reflecting the gender distribution of both the City of Helsinki and the broader Finnish municipal sector. Two-thirds of the participants were aged 30-39 years, and one-third was aged 19-29 years.

Socioeconomic position and weight measures

Gender (male/female) was obtained from the surveys. SEP was measured using the highest educational attainment, categorized into three groups: upper secondary or vocational education or lower (low), bachelor's degree (middle), and master's degree or higher (high). BMI was computed from height and weight (kg/m²).

Dietary factors and physical activity

We included six behaviors, four related to diet: fruit and vegetable consumption, red and processed meat consumption, sweets and confectionery consumption, alcohol consumption, and two related to physical activity during leisure time: VPA and sedentary time. These mediators were chosen because they are directly related to body weight through energy intake or energy expenditure. Food consumption was assessed using a 14-item food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) covering the previous 4 weeks. Consumption of fruit and vegetables was calculated by summing four items: fresh vegetables, fresh fruits, cooked vegetables, and berries. Red and processed meat consumption was based on one questionnaire item, and sweets and confectionery consumption was calculated by summing two items, one on candy and chocolate and the other on coffee pastries and baked goods (biscuits/cookies, doughnuts, and other pastries). The variables were divided by four to obtain weekly servings. Alcohol consumption was assessed from participants' reported frequency of consuming three beverage types: beer, cider, long drink and similar beverages; wine and other lowalcohol beverages; and spirits or other strong alcoholic beverages. Weekly alcohol consumption was calculated by summing the reported consumption frequencies across beverage types. One unit of alcohol refers to 12 grams of pure ethanol. Participants reported their leisure-time and commuting physical activity during the past 12 months by classifying activities according to perceived intensity and estimating the average weekly duration at each intensity level. Intensity was converted to metabolic equivalents (METs) and multiplied by the duration of each intensity, following previous studies [29]. Only VPA (> 10 METs) was investigated as a mediator in the main analysis and total physical activity (sum of all the domains) in sensitivity analyses. Sedentary time was assessed as self-reported daily sitting time across four domains: at home while watching television or using a computer, at home while reading, during transport, and elsewhere. These were summed up and multiplied by seven to obtain time sitting per week. If a participant reported physical activity, sedentary time or alcohol consumption for some domain but left one domain blank, the missing value for that domain was set to zero, following prior studies [19, 20].

Statistical methods

Background characteristics of the participants were described by gender and SEP. Continuous variables were presented as means and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Age was categorized as 19-29 years and 30-39 years for descriptive analyses and treated as a continuous variable in mediation models, following previous studies [20]. Differences in dietary factors and physical activity across gender and SEP groups were examined descriptively and visualized using bar plots. The association between SEP and BMI was first examined visually and then tested using linear regression models. Interaction terms between SEP and gender were used to assess whether educational differences in BMI differed between women and men. Model assumptions, including the normality of residuals and heteroscedasticity, were assessed.

The mediating role of health dietary factors and physical activity in the association between SEP and BMI was examined using multiple parallel mediation models within a structural equation modeling framework in accordance with the conceptual model shown in Figure 2. All analyses were stratified by gender and adjusted for age. A three-category SEP variable (upper secondary or vocational education or lower/bachelor's degree/master's degree or higher) was created for men and women separately, with the highest education being the reference group. In each mediation model, SEP was specified as the exposure, BMI as the outcome, and dietary factors and physical activity as simultaneous mediators. All mediators were treated as continuous variables.

The mediation analyses were conducted using structural equation modeling and followed the standard path-analytic approach [30]. First, the total association (c-path) between SEP and BMI was estimated. Second, the associations between SEP and each mediator were estimated as a-paths. Third, the associations between each mediator and BMI, adjusted for SEP and all other mediators, were estimated as b-paths. The direct effect (c'-path) represented the association between SEP and BMI after adjustment for all mediators. Mediator-specific indirect effects were calculated as the product of the corresponding a-and b-paths. The total indirect effect was calculated as the sum of all mediator-specific indirect effects. When the total effect (c-path) was statistically significant (p<0.05), the proportion mediated was calculated by dividing the indirect effect by the total effect. Indirect effects and confidence intervals were estimated using bootstrapped standard errors with 5,000 replications. Model parameters are presented as non-standardized regression coefficients. Nonstandardized estimates were used to allow more direct comparison between women and men, as the focus was on gender differences rather than comparisons between mediators within genders. Standardized estimates depend on variable variances, which may differ by gender. Results are reported separately for women and men as total effects, direct effects, total indirect effects and mediator-specific indirect effects. Models were fitted using the lavaan package in R (version 4.5.3, 2026-03-11). ChatGPT (OpenAI; accessed May 2025) was used to assist in generating R code for statistical analyses. All AI-generated code was reviewed, tested, and modified as necessary by the authors, who verified the accuracy and reproducibility of the results. A STROBE checklist is provided as a supplementary file (File S1).

Additional follow-up analyses

To assess whether the observed mediation reflected socioeconomic differences in BMI already present at Phase 1, a secondary longitudinal analysis among those who had responded to the Phase 2 survey five years later in 2022 (n = 2581, 80% women) was conducted. The cross-sectional analysis was chosen as the main analysis to maximize statistical power, as 40% of participants were lost to follow-up between Phases 1 and 2. In the longitudinal model, Phase 2 BMI was used as the outcome variable, and the models were additionally adjusted for Phase 1 BMI. Education was taken from Phase 2 to consider new completed education between Phase 1 and 2.

By 2022, mean BMI had increased in all education groups, but the inverse gradient remained. The gender differences had narrowed, meaning that weight gain was more pronounced in women. At follow-up, education remained strongly associated with BMI (p < 0.001), whereas gender no longer reached statistical significance (p = 0.085). However, the education × gender interaction remained significant (p = 0.040), meaning that the gender difference in the association between SEP and BMI remained.

After accounting for baseline BMI in the follow-up analysis, the total effects of SEP on Phase 2 BMI were small and not statistically significant in any group, as is shown in Table S2. The a-paths showed a broadly similar pattern with lower educational level being still associated with lower consumption of fruit and vegetables and higher red and processed meat consumption. Lower sweets and confectionery consumption and more sedentary time were associated with lower education in men, whereas lower alcohol consumption and less VPA were associated with lower education in women. Discussion

Main findings

Our main finding was that the association between SEP and BMI was stronger among women than among men, and that this difference was partly explained by dietary factors and physical activity. Consumption of red and processed meat was an important mediator in both genders, although it explained a larger part of the SEP-BMI association among men. VPA was the most prominent mediator among women, followed by consumption of fruit and vegetables. Sedentary time was a significant mediator only among men, while alcohol and sweets and confectionery consumption did not mediate the SEP-BMI association in either gender. Additional analyses with 5-year follow-up BMI adjusted for baseline BMI suggested that educational differences in follow-up BMI were largely explained by baseline BMI instead of the mediators.

Comparison to previous literature

The steeper SEP-BMI gradient in women than in men has been well established in previous literature, both in the Finnish population and internationally [8, 11]. SEP tends to be associated with obesity regardless of the indicator used, but in studies examining multiple SEP indicators, the associations with body weight have generally been more consistent among women and more variable among men [31] [32] [33] [34]. Because we used education as the SEP indicator in this study, it remains to be seen whether the observed gender differences in SEP-BMI association would be equally consistent if other SEP indicators were used.

Lower SEP was associated with a less favorable behavioral profile in both genders, including lower consumption of fruit and vegetables, higher consumption of red and processed meat, and lower VPA. The educational differences in diet quality were in line with the Finnish national dietary surveys [12] as well as international evidence, where the clearest support is seen for fruit and vegetable consumption [13, 35]. Regarding physical activity and sedentary time, we observed clear socioeconomic gradients in VPA, which is consistent with previous literature [22, 36]. Higher levels of VPA among the highly educated may reflect greater participation in organized sports. The pattern for sedentary time was less clear, although the socioeconomic gradient appeared somewhat stronger among men. These inconsistencies may be explained by differences between domains [20]. Because our study measured only leisure-time vigorous activity, the observed socioeconomic patterning may reflect the possibility that lower educated individuals accumulate more occupational physical activity and less occupational sedentary time, whereas higher educated individuals engage more in leisuretime physical activity but may also have more sedentary work. Additionally, gender differences related to dietary factors and physical activity were consistent with prior national and international evidence [12, 37, 38].

There are some studies examining gender differences in the mediators of the SEP-BMI association using similar methodology to ours. In a Finnish study consumption of fruit and vegetables, self-rated dietary quality and sedentary time mediated the association in both genders, while leisure-time physical activity and alcohol were mediators only in women and smoking only in men [39]. Another study found significant indirect effects through consumption of fruit and vegetables only among women [40]. Lower levels of physical activity and a higher consumption of unhealthy savory foods were important mediators in an Australian cross-sectional study, with alcohol consumption and diet soft drink intake being additional mediators among women [41]. In the only longitudinal study investigating the mediators of the SEP-BMI association, conducted in the previously mentioned cohort, the association was mediated by physical activity, intake of nutrient-rich foods, and diet soft drink consumption, with the mediation effects being clearly stronger among women [42]. In our study, consumption of fruit and vegetables and VPA were statistically significant mediators among women, in line with the earlier findings [40, 42]. However, alcohol consumption was not a statistically significant mediator in either gender, and sedentary time was found to mediate the associations only among men. Despite the partly different behaviors included across studies, the overall magnitudes of the individual mediators explaining the SEP-BMI association were broadly similar to our results [39] [40] [41] [42].

We also tested subjective sleep quality and total physical activity as additional mediators. These variables did not show clear or statistically significant indirect effects and were therefore not included in the main mediation models. Because not all mediators were normally distributed, we also examined median values in the descriptive analysis. For all mediators except meat, socioeconomic gradients and gender differences were consistent with the mean-based results. For red and processed meat consumption, the differences disappeared, with a median intake of 3 servings per week in all groups. This suggests that the distribution between groups differs, which was confirmed visually using histograms, although the differences were quite small (Figure S2). Highly educated women and highly educated men had relatively more observations in the lower range of consumption, whereas women and men with low education had relatively more observations in the higher range.

In our follow-up analysis, BMI increased in both genders and in all educational groups during the 5year follow-up, which is in line with other Finnish studies [3]. However, we observed a faster increase in BMI among women than among men. The gender differences and educational gradients remained still visible, and previous research suggests that socioeconomic differences in body weight are relatively stable over time [43]. In additional mediation analyses with a 5-year follow-up BMI adjusted for baseline BMI, educational differences in BMI were largely explained by baseline BMI rather than by the mediators. In one longitudinal study, BMI gain was partly explained by health behaviors [42]. However, that study had a 13-year follow-up, during which educational differences in BMI gain were detected. It is therefore possible that our 5-year follow-up was too short to detect clear differences in BMI change. BMI increased rather similarly across educational groups, so adjusting for baseline BMI largely attenuated the educational differences.

Methodological considerations

Regarding the study population, this study used a large occupational cohort of young adults aged 19-39 years, including both manual and non-manual employees. However, the sample was mostly women (80%), which likely reduced statistical power in the male subgroup and may partly explain why some associations among men were not statistically significant. Although the data may be generalized with caution to the working-age Finnish population, they cannot be directly generalized to the entire adult population because the cohort consisted only of employees of the City of Helsinki. Thus, a healthy worker effect is likely, and the lowest SEP group in this study does not represent the most socioeconomically disadvantaged groups in society, such as the long-term unemployed [44].

We also excluded telephone interviewees, and our prior studies have shown that those who responded via email or mail are more often women and more highly educated [27]. Due to missing values, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) [45]. The results were highly consistent with the complete-case analyses: the effect sizes were similar, while the confidence intervals were narrower, reflecting the larger analytic sample (Table S3).

Regarding measurements, all variables were based on self-report. Self-reported height and weight may introduce bias, although they are generally considered sufficiently accurate for epidemiological research [46, 47]. Additionally, both predictors and outcomes were measured by questionnaire, so common method bias is possible [48]. The dietary measure was based on a short FFQ that was not formally validated and did not include portion sizes, meaning that absolute consumption could not be estimated. However, short FFQs have been shown to be suitable for assessing group-level differences in frequently consumed foods [49, 50] and portion-size questions may contribute relatively little to variance in intake [51]. The similarity between our results and the national dietary survey, which uses a more precise data collection method, supports our FFQ's validity [12]. Similarly, the physical activity and sedentary time questionnaires were not validated in this cohort, although comparable measures have shown acceptable validity in epidemiological studies [52, 53].

Regarding statistical analysis, our cross-sectional design does not allow for causal conclusions. We conducted a follow-up analysis, but it did not completely overcome this limitation because dietary factors and physical activity were measured at the same time as SEP. Additionally, the follow-up sample was smaller and more selective. We also adjusted the mediation models only for age, so some residual confounding may remain. However, the statistical framework of structural equation is built on the previous findings suggesting a causal relationship between SEP and BMI through health behaviors.

Implications and significance

Our results suggest that socioeconomic differences in BMI are not explained by a single behavioral pathway, but rather by a myriad of factors, with some mechanisms being gender specific. For example, improving VPA may be relevant among low-SEP women, whereas reducing sedentary time may be more relevant among low-SEP men. At the same time, consumption of red and processed meat emerged as a common pathway in both genders. The stronger SEP-BMI gradient observed among women suggests that women in lower SEP may be more vulnerable to weight gain, so health policies should focus on this group.

Because these six mediators explained only about one quarter of the SEP-BMI association, the findings indicate the importance of other mechanisms, including psychosocial stress, neighborhood environment, working conditions and access to healthy food and exercise opportunities being potential mediators of the SEP-BMI association. Future research is needed to identify additional mediators of the SEP-BMI association to better understand why the association is more pronounced in women. Future studies would also benefit from repeated measurements of both mediators and BMI, so that changes over time and causal pathways could be examined more clearly.

Conclusions

Taken together, our findings suggest that different behavioral mechanisms may underlie socioeconomic differences in BMI in men and women. VPA and fruit and vegetable consumption were significant mediators of the SEP-BMI association in women and sedentary time in men, while red and processed meat intake acted as a mediator in both genders. The association between SEP and BMI was stronger among women than among men, and the 5-year increase in BMI was more pronounced in women, especially among those with the lowest education. These results suggest that prevention of weight gain and the reduction of SEP-related health inequalities should focus on gender-specific approaches. At the same time, because only about one quarter of the SEP-BMI association was explained by the included dietary factors and physical activity patterns, broader structural, social, and environmental factors are likely to play an important role in maintaining weight-related inequalities.
