How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Dyslexia is a common neurodevelopmental condition characterized by persistent difficulties in reading and spelling. Although social and emotional challenges have been reported in individuals with dyslexia, the genetic relationships between dyslexia, social functioning, and well-being remain poorly understood.

We combined phenotypic and genetic data from the Lifelines cohort with publicly available genome-wide association study (GWAS) summary statistics. Associations between dyslexia and social traits were examined using a multi-layered approach encompassing complementary methods, including phenotypic correlation analyses, polygenic score (PGS) analyses, linkage disequilibrium score regression (LDSC), and bidirectional two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR). Correlation analyses revealed significant phenotypic and genetic correlations between dyslexia and several social traits, including lower levels of life satisfaction, higher levels of loneliness, and less collegial support on the work floor. PGS analyses demonstrated that genetic liability to dyslexia predicted lower life satisfaction and reduced support from colleagues in the workplace, whereas none of the social-based PGSs predicted dyslexia. Bidirectional MR analyses provided evidence that genetic liability to dyslexia contributes to increased loneliness and social isolation, while no evidence was found for causal effects of social-interaction or well-being traits on dyslexia. Together, our results consistently supported a directional relationship from dyslexia toward social and well-being outcomes. In conclusion, our findings demonstrate that dyslexia shares genetic influences with social interaction and well-being traits and suggest that genetic liability for dyslexia contributes to adverse (psycho)social outcomes. These results extend the impact of dyslexia beyond literacy-related difficulties and highlight the importance of providing social and structural support to individuals with dyslexia throughout the lifespan.

Introduction

In modern human society, written communication is used on a daily basis to facilitate social interaction.

It serves multiple functions, including the transmission of information, expression of ideas, management of social relationships, articulation of needs, and transmission of knowledge and culture from one generation to the next. In essence, writing refers to the ability to encode linguistic structures into written symbols with orthographic rules, while reading refers to the ability to decode these symbols to reconstruct meaning. The level of skill for these complex processes differs among individuals, with more notable difficulties observed in individuals diagnosed with dyslexia (1). Dyslexia is a neurodevelopmental condition characterized by persistent difficulties in accurate and/or fluent word reading and spelling, despite adequate intelligence and educational opportunities (2). As reading and writing are fundamental skills for academic achievement and everyday functioning, understanding the nature and consequences of dyslexia is important.

Although written communication does not directly affect an individual's verbal ability in social interaction, evidence suggests an indirect relationship between literacy skills and social well-being. For example, parents reported lower psychosocial functioning of their dyslexic children compared to typical readers already at 4 and 6 years of age, prior to their dyslexia diagnosis at age 8(3). Problems with psychosocial functioning and social interactions continue after entering school (3, 4), as poor readers in the 3 rd grade are more likely to experience loneliness in the 5 th grade (5). In adulthood, reading ability among typically developing readers correlates positively with social-emotional skills (r=0.23; 95%CI=0.19, 0.28) (6). Taken together, these findings suggest that individuals who experience difficulties with written communication may be at increased risk of challenges in social interaction across the lifespan.

The level of difficulties and prevalence of dyslexia diagnoses is higher within families, which is explained in part by a hereditary component as well as the influence of shared environment. Reading outcomes are influenced by genetic components (7). However, an upbringing in which a child is frequently encouraged to engage in reading activities also improves overall reading ability (8). Genetic contributions to reading outcomes were first demonstrated in twin studies, where the heritability of a trait is deduced by using the difference in genetic similarity between mono-and dizygotic twins, while taking the shared environment to be constant. The largest twin-based meta-analysis on reading ability to date estimated heritability for general reading (h 2 twin=66%), letter-word knowledge (h 2 twin=62%), phonological decoding (h 2 twin=68%), reading comprehension (h 2 twin=68%), phonological awareness (h 2 twin=52%), and spelling (h 2 twin=80%) (9).

Large-scale genotyping efforts enable researchers to quantify relatedness among unrelated individuals from their genetic information, giving opportunities to estimate heritability of complex traits using singlenucleotide polymorphism (SNP) data. The regression of SNP data with phenotypic scores forms the basis of genome-wide association studies (GWAS) (10) that are used to identify DNA variants involved in heritable traits, and this approach has been applied to various reading-related traits. Doust et al. (2022) performed a GWAS on self-reported dyslexia, which was conducted in data of the direct-to-consumer genotyping company 23andMe (n=1,138,870 with ncases=51,800), and identified 42 significantly associated loci, with an estimated SNP-based heritability (h 2 SNP) of 0.18 (SE=0.008) (11). Eising et al. (2022) targeted five reading-and language-related skills in up to 34,000 people within the GenLang consortium and identified heritability for word reading (h 2 SNP=0.19; SE=0.021), nonword reading (h 2 SNP=0.26; SE=0.028), spelling (h 2 SNP=0.23; SE=0.031), phoneme awareness (h 2 SNP=0.25; SE=0.040), and nonword repetition(h 2 SNP=0.13; SE=0.039) (12). The efforts of these large-scale GWAS studies have provided valuable data for further investigation of genetic associations with related outcomes, such as social interaction and well-being.

Few studies have investigated the shared genetic architecture between reading ability and social interaction experiences. Indications of genetic overlap have been provided by Doust et al. (2022), as analyses of their GWAS summary statistics yielded a significant genetic correlation between dyslexia and loneliness (rg=0.24)(11), while Eising et al. (2022) found a positive correlation between the combined reading-traits that they studied and friendship satisfaction (rg=0.15; SE=0.058), as well as a negative relationship with loneliness (rg=-0.24; SE=0.048) (12). However, genetic correlations between reading ability and a broader set of social interactive traits have not yet been investigated, nor has the power of genetic influences on reading ability as predictors of social outcomes.

Here, we investigated the shared genetic architecture and predictive power of dyslexia with respect to a range of social interactive traits. We used published GWAS summary statistics of dyslexia (11) and several social-interaction traits(13-16) combined with quantitative trait and self-report data of similar traits and SNP data in the Lifelines(17) cohort; a multi-disciplinary prospective population-based cohort study of individuals living in the North of the Netherlands. With these data, we i) assessed the relations between social-interactive traits in the Lifelines cohort using psychological network analysis, ii) investigated the shared genetic architecture between dyslexia and publicly available GWAS summary statistics representative of social interaction and well-being, iii) used polygenic score analysis to investigate the predictive power of genetic disposition to dyslexia for social-interactive outcomes and vice versa, and iv) 83 tested both directions of causality for SNPs robustly associated with dyslexia and social interactive 84 outcomes using two-sample mendelian randomization analysis. Together, these findings provide a 85 comprehensive overview of the relationship between genetic architectures underlying dyslexia and social 86 well-being. 87 88 89 90

Psychological network-based phenotype selection

Data for this study were derived from the Lifelines(17) cohort; a multi-disciplinary prospective populationbased cohort study of individuals living in the North of the Netherlands. Data on three reading-based items, obtained in 2022, were available for 31,131 to 33,859 individuals (Table 1). Thirteen (sub) scales representative of social interactive experiences were collected in two waves between 2012 and 2019 with a total of 54,264 to 123,833 participants (Table 2). Descriptives for all traits are reported in Supplementary Table 1.

We calculated phenotypic correlations between reading ability and social interaction in Lifelines(17) (Suppl. Table 2). Correlations between the reading-based items were moderate and positive (r=0.40-0.45; p<2.2E-16), whereas the associations between reading-and social-based traits were low, yet significant, with correlations ranging from 0.016 between dyslexia and SMAS_multifunctionality (p<9.12E-3), to 0.128 between learning to read and SMAS_investment (p<2.2E-16). After Bonferroni correction (pBonferroni-=0.05/120 tests = 4.17E-4), only 3 out of 91 tests between the reading-and social-based traits did not reach significance. Thus, the associations between reported reading skills and social-traits in Lifelines data were robust.

The number of scales for the social-based surveys differed. For example, the loneliness survey produced a single final score, and the SMAS survey yielded six scores for the six sub-scales. We reasoned that surveys with multiple scales were more prone to type I error, potentially leading to more biased results for surveys with multiple scales, compared to those that only consist of a single score. Therefore, for our genetic analyses, we selected one scale per social survey, and one item for the reading-based traits. First, we wanted to ensure that the selected traits could (partially) be explained by genome-wide SNP variance, estimating h 2 SNP for all traits using GCTA-GREML(18) (Suppl. Figure 1). Estimates for the reading-related traits were low (h 2 SNP=0.13-0.16; p<1.02E-06) and social-trait estimates were even lower, yet still significant (h 2 SNP=0.04-0.09; p<5.00E-03). Second, we selected the sub-scales that most strongly represented their respective survey through psychological network analyses, which models the statistical relationships between two variables while conditioning on all other variable-relationships in the dataset, resulting in partial correlation coefficients (19, 20). The network represents a complex structure of all traits (nodes) and their partial statistical relationships (edges). A mixed graphical model (21, 22) approach was conducted in order to allow modelling for various data distributions. The social-based network (Figure 1A) consisted of thirteen traits, distributed over 5 different surveys. All 128 scales clustered together within their respective survey, meaning that after accounting for all correlations 129 across the network, a large part of the shared variance across survey-specific sub-scales remained. Even 130 though there are correlations between different survey scales, the smaller edges between sub-scales from 131 different surveys in network show that the surveys are fairly distinct from one another, measuring 132 different social-interactive aspects. For the surveys with multiple sub-scales, we selected the sub-scale 133 with the highest strength index Si, which captures the sum of partial correlations it is connected with, for 134 follow-up analyses. Within the social-based network, we thus selected SMAS self-efficacy (S = 1.23), SPFIL 135 confirmation (S = 1.05), and COPSOQ colleagues (S = 0.77). In addition, we included LS (S = 1.27) and SWLS 136 (S = 0.79) as these were made up of a single scale. The dyslexia item was the strongest driver in the 137 reading-based network (S = 1.84) (Figure 1B), and was thus selected to represent reading ability in follow-138 up analyses. 139

Genetic disposition for dyslexia predicts the amount of help received from colleagues on the work floor and overall life satisfaction

Two social-based traits showed modest but significant genetic correlations with dyslexia after Bonferroni correction (pBonferroni=0.05/5 tests=0.01), with rg=0.21 (p=5.71E-14) for social isolation, and rg=0.23 (p=1.43E-18) for loneliness.

Next, we used polygenic scores (PGS) to further investigate genetic overlaps between dyslexia and socialinteraction outcomes. Because the heritability estimates for the social-based traits and their genetic correlations with dyslexia were low to modest (Suppl. Figure 1 and Suppl. PGS based on the GWAS summary statistics for loneliness, sociability, and social isolation each had 80% power to predict variance in dyslexia representative of rg>0.03, and subjective well-being had 80% power to identify an association representing a genetic correlation of rg>0.06 (Suppl. Figure 3A). Only the PGS for social trust had insufficient power to explain enough variation in dyslexia and was therefore excluded from the PGS analyses.

We first ensured that the calculated PGSs could predict trait outcomes within their own category. PGSdyslexia significantly predicted 5.6% of dyslexia variance in Lifelines (OR = 1.9; 95% CI = 1.8,2.2; pOR=4.1E-36) (Suppl. Figure 4A). PGSs based on all four social-based summary statistics significantly predicted all five social Lifelines traits, with explained variance ranging between 0.06-0.51% (2.9E-22<pβ<0.014) (Suppl.

Figure 4B). Next, we tested PGSdyslexia for prediction of the social traits in Lifelines (Figure 2A, Suppl. Table 6). The dyslexia scores robustly explained variance for help from colleagues (IncR2 = 0.07%; β = -0.48 [95% CI = -0.13, -0.83], pβ=7.36E-3) and life satisfaction (IncR2 = 0.11%; β = -0.22 [95% CI = -0.09, -0.36], pβ=1.22E-3), with higher PGSdyslexia being associated with lower levels of social-interactive outcomes. The remaining traits, social self-efficacy, social confirmation and loneliness, did not show significant relationships with PGSdyslexia. Lastly, we investigated whether genetic disposition for social-based traits could predict dyslexia (Figure 2B). However, none of the PGSs for social isolation(15), sociability(14), loneliness(13), and subjective well-being(16) explained any variance for dyslexia in the Lifelines sample.

We observed a skewed distribution of the incidence of dyslexia self-report across age in Lifelines (Suppl.

Figure 5A). This might be explained by a change in the national protocol for diagnosing dyslexia in 2006 in the Netherlands, leading to policy changes and a rise in dyslexia diagnoses due to increased attention on this trait in schools (27). As none of the social PGSs could account for dyslexia variance in the sample, we were concerned that true genetic effects might have been masked by phenotypic noise as a result of less restrictive dyslexia diagnoses in the younger subsample due to societal pressures. We therefore further investigated predictive behaviour of the PGSs for dyslexia and of social isolation, the latter having the largest power to identify an association with dyslexia, for the two other reading-based traits available in Lifelines: spelling difficulty and learning to read (Suppl. Figure 5B). PGSdyslexia explained less variance in spelling difficulty and learning to read than in the dyslexia outcome, even though results were still 6). Therefore, changes in the national diagnostic protocol do not seem to have caused the lack of associations between the social PGSs and dyslexia.

Causal implications for dyslexia towards social isolation and loneliness

To further investigate potential causal effects between dyslexia and social interaction, we performed mendelian randomization analyses using the dyslexia and social-based GWAS summary statistics (28, 29).

Analyses were performed with SNPs that reached genome-wide significance in the exposure GWAS (p<5E-8) as instrumental variables, after which we investigated their effect in the outcome GWAS summary statistics using five methods, of which the Inverse Variance Weighted (IVW) method was used as the main method, and the MR Egger, Simple mode, Weighted median, and Weighted mode were used as sensitivity analyses.

First, we used the dyslexia GWAS as an instrumental variable to investigate the causal relationship between dyslexia and the social-based GWASs (Suppl. Figure 6A, Suppl. Table 7). There was strong evidence that dyslexia predicted social isolation (βIWV = 0.05; p = 8.3E-6) and loneliness (βIWV = 0.04; p = 1.2E-5), even after Bonferroni correction (pBonferroni = 0.05/4 tests = 0.0125). The Weighted median provided further support for a causal effect of dyslexia on loneliness (βWeighted median = 0.03; p = 8.8E-3).

While the Weighted Mode and Simple Mode did confirm the direction of effect size, they did not reach statistical significance to confirm the IVW (see Methods for more details on the sensitivity analyses). The Cochran Q test showed no evidence of heterogeneity among the instrumental SNPs for dyslexia on loneliness, subjective well-being, and social isolation (pCochran Q > 0.08) (Suppl. Table 7). This was further supported by the leave-one-out analysis, which showed that excluding individual instrumental SNPs did not lead to a change in the IVW effect estimate (Suppl. Figure 7). Next, we investigated the causal effect of social-traits on dyslexia outcomes (Suppl. Figure 6A, Suppl. Table 7), but found no evidence for causal effects. Secondary analyses, with instrumental SNP variables that were selected based on a suggestive GWAS p-value threshold (p<1E-6), yielded highly similar results to the primary analyses (Suppl. Figure 6B, Suppl. Table 7). Social isolation and loneliness were predicted by the dyslexia SNPs, yet none of the socialinstrumental variables predicted dyslexia, even with higher numbers of instrumental SNPs, suggesting that our results are robust. These findings show that i) there are indications that dyslexia-associated SNPs may be causal for loneliness and social isolation, and ii) the SNPs associated with sociability, social isolation, loneliness, subjective well-being, and social trust are not causal for dyslexia.

Discussion

We investigated the phenotypic and genetic relations between dyslexia and social interaction/well-being traits using published GWAS summary statistics and direct phenotype and genotype data from the Lifelines cohort. When considered collectively, the phenotypic correlations, PGS, genetic correlations, and MR results provided a nuanced picture of the relationship between dyslexia and social functioning. First, we identified low yet significant phenotypic correlations between three reading traits and 13 measures of social interaction difficulties in adults. With 87 out of 91 phenotypic correlations surviving Bonferroni correction (pBonferroni=0.05/91 tests=5.50E-4), we considered our phenotypic results robust. Previous studies have reported social interaction difficulties in children with dyslexia prior to entering school(3), as well as during the schooling period(5,6), yet our results show that also adults with dyslexia experience more challenges in their daily social interaction. Next, we examined genetic correlations between dyslexia and social traits, observing significant positive correlations with loneliness and social isolation. Previous studies reported similar findings, with genetic correlations between dyslexia and loneliness (rg=0.24; SE=0.034) (11) and between reading ability and friendship satisfaction (rg=0.15; SE=0.058) and loneliness (rg=-0.24; SE=0.048)(12). Higher PGS for dyslexia predicted overall lower life satisfaction and less support received from colleagues, whereas none of the social-trait PGSs predicted dyslexia. Notably, the strongest PGS association of dyslexia was observed for workplace support from colleagues. These work environment-specific experiences for individuals with dyslexia have been reported before, as they experience more challenges in work confidence (30) and social relationships with co-workers (31). Lastly, MR analyses showed the same directional pattern, with evidence for dyslexia-related variants affecting loneliness and social isolation but no evidence that the social-based SNP-variants have causal effects on dyslexia.

Across PGS and MR analyses, our results suggested a directional relationship in which genetic disposition to dyslexia influences social outcomes rather than the reverse. Evidence for this directionality was strongest for loneliness and social isolation. Other studies also found correlations between dyslexia and heightened levels of self-perceived anxiety, depressive symptoms, and lower self-esteem (32) (33) (34). The International Dyslexia Association(35) describe how reading difficulties in day-to-day life, in spite of continuous efforts to prevent writing and reading mistakes, can give a heightened sense of anxiety and frustration. Furthermore, comparisons with typical readers in school, who can easily resolve reading and writing mistakes with practice, can lead to less confidence and lower levels of emotional well-being in individuals with dyslexia, with effects throughout the lifespan during schooling, studies, and working life. This description fits with our findings, and would explain why the underlying genetics for dyslexia may be causal towards social-interactive and well-being outcomes, and not the other way around.

To our knowledge, this is the most comprehensive investigation to date into the shared genetics underlying dyslexia and social well-being. A study with a comparable PGS framework and focus examined developmental language disorder (DLD) in relation to common psychiatric disorders, including major depressive disorder, anxiety disorder, and ADHD (36). That study found that individuals with DLD showed a stronger association between the genetic disposition for psychiatric disorders and conduct problems than individuals without DLD. Given the high comorbidity between DLD and dyslexia (37), and the substantial genetic contribution to DLD (38), some overlap in social and behavioural outcomes may be expected. Although direct comparisons of results for DLD and dyslexia across these studies cannot be made, these findings provide broader support that genetic liability for neurodevelopmental languagerelevant conditions may contribute to psychosocial outcomes later in life.

The strongest PGS association for dyslexia was observed for the amount of help received from colleagues on the work floor, where a higher genetic disposition for dyslexia predicted lower levels of perceived support (β=-0.48; p=0.007). Importantly, this measure specifically reflects experiences within the workplace rather than broader social interactions, highlighting the work environment as a potentially important context in which dyslexia-related social challenges arise. Previous studies examining dyslexia in the workplace relied primarily on phenotypic data and consistently reported difficulties and challenges.

For example, dyslexia severity has been associated with lower work confidence (r=-0.30, p<0.01) and stronger negative emotions unique to dyslexia (r = 0.37, p<0.01) (30). In addition, a large meta-analysis that focussed on workplace experiences of individuals with dyslexia identified the largest effects for factors related to obtaining and maintaining employment, support from colleagues, and personal coping strategies (31). Cognitive challenges specific to the work floor for individuals with dyslexia have been investigated and confirmed as well; self-reported dyslexia traits were a significant positive predictor of workplace cognitive failures (β=0.247; p<0.001), even after controlling for ADHD symptoms, personality, well-being, and workplace characteristics 47 . A survey among employees with dyslexia suggested that employees with dyslexia particularly value workplace cultures characterized by awareness, inclusivity, and organizational support, highlighting the potential importance of supportive workplace environments for dyslexia-related challenges 48 . Our results might suggest that these wishes are currently not fully met.

Given the central role of the workplace in adult life, these experiences may contribute to broader differences in well-being and life satisfaction, although this relationship was not evaluated in the present study.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, the available GWAS summary statistics did not fully align with the available phenotypes in the Lifelines data. For example, no comparable GWAS was available for workplace social support or social self-efficacy, disabling the evaluation of the associations between dyslexia and these social traits with LDSC and MR, and also disabling investigation of relationships of PGS for these social traits with dyslexia outcomes. Second, although a range of sensitivity methods were applied, we cannot rule out violations of the MR assumptions or limited statistical power, particularly for the subjective well-being GWAS, for which only 8 SNPs met the 1E-6 significance threshold. However, decent instrument strength was supported by the F-statistics exceeding the conventional threshold (F > 10), and also the secondary MR analyses using a larger number of suggestive genetic instruments (p<1E-6) did not provide evidence for causality. Third, the PGSs used in this study only captured a proportion of the underlying genetic liability in the Lifelines cohort. For example, the PGSdyslexia only explained 5.6% of the variance for dyslexia in the Lifelines cohort, while the captured h 2 SNP in both the Lifelines (h 2 snp = 13%) and the 23andMe sample (h 2 SNP = 16%) was higher. Consequently, the PGS analyses with dyslexia and social traits captures only the component of genetic liability represented by the available genetic instruments and may therefore not fully reflect the genetic architecture underlying and relationships between these traits. Fourth, both dyslexia diagnoses and social-interactive measures were based on self-report, which might be less reliable compared to a clinical derived diagnoses or scores derived by an assessment. The prevalence for self-reported dyslexia diagnoses in the 23andMe sample (5%) (11) was slightly lower compared to the prevalence in the typical United States population (5.3-11.8%) (41). Prevalence of dyslexia in the Netherlands was estimated in 2005 at 3.6% for children at the end of primary school, and at 8.8% when children with severe reading-and spelling difficulties were included (42). These estimates align with the 8% dyslexia prevalence in the selfreported Lifelines sample. Furthermore, the 23andMe GWAS summary statistics on self-reported dyslexia showed high genetic correlations with quantitative measures of reading and spelling performance in the independent GWAS meta-analysis by GenLang (12), demonstrating that the signal captured by the 23andMe dyslexia self-report question is reliable. The social-based measures in the Lifelines data were self-reported as well, though all scores were derived from validated instruments, and were therefore considered as reliable and robust. Lastly, this study lacks ancestral diversity, as all analysis were conducted in individuals from European ancestry, which limits the generalizability of our findings to other populations (43). Follow-up studies should investigate whether these findings replicate in samples of non-European ancestry.

Conclusion

This study provides empirical evidence that genetic disposition to dyslexia is associated with social interaction and well-being outcomes, while also offering insight into the potential directional relationship between dyslexia and overall well-being and life satisfaction. Together, these findings contribute to a broader understanding of the impact of dyslexia beyond literacy-related difficulties, highlighting its relevance for social and psychosocial functioning across the lifespan. We hope that this work encourages greater recognition of the challenges experienced by individuals with dyslexia and supports the development of targeted interventions and structural support systems that promote well-being and social participation throughout all stages of life.

Methods

Cohort data

For this study, phenotype and genotype data were obtained from Lifelines(17), a multi-disciplinary prospective population-based cohort study examining in a unique three-generation design the health and health-related behaviours of 167,729 persons living in the North of the Netherlands. It employs a broad range of investigative procedures in assessing the biomedical, socio-demographic, behavioural, physical and psychological factors which contribute to the health and disease of the general population, with a special focus on multi-morbidity and complex genetics. The data of the selected surveys on social wellbeing were collected between 2012-2019, and the "Speech, Language and Musicality" survey containing speech-and reading-based questions was administered in September and October of 2022.

Questionnaires were distributed and completed in Dutch; English translations are used in the manuscript.

The general Lifelines protocol has been approved by the UMCG Medical ethical committee under number 2007/152, and the "Speech, Language and Musicality" study was reviewed by the medical ethical committee of the Erasmus Medical Center (MEC-2022-0313). All participants provided informed consent for their participation in Lifelines (https://wiki.lifelines.nl/doku.php?id=informed_consent).

Measures

Social interaction scales

The Lifelines study had several measures on social interactive experiences that were obtained via selfreport surveys. For this study, we selected thirteen measures that are i) survey based, and ii) representative of various aspects of social well-being and/or social interaction (Table 2).

Results of the Social Production Function

Literacy-based measures

Reading-and writing ability was measured with three self-report items: whether the individual has dyslexia, whether the individual struggled with learning to read compared to their peers, and whether the individual's writing has often been corrected by others. The survey-questions and answer-options per item are shown in Table 1.

Phenotypic data cleaning and score coding Exclusion criteria

Individuals who had suffered from a stroke (n=985) or who have been diagnosed with dementia (n=17) were excluded. Additionally, to ensure that non-native language would not influence an individual's social interactive experience(49), individuals who were not born in the Netherlands were excluded as well (n=3,767).

Scoring of items and instruments

Traits representing reading-skills were coded as either binary or continuous (Table 1). Cases for individuals with dyslexia (question: "Do you have dyslexia?") were defined as those who answered "Yes, I have been diagnosed" or "I presume I have it, but I have never been tested" (Ncase=2,639). Individuals who did not know ("I suppose not") were excluded (Nexc=3,175), and those who answered "No" were used as controls (Ncontrol=28,492). The remaining items (learning to read and spelling difficulty) were coded as continuous.

To ensure consistency in scoring direction, scores were coded such that higher scores corresponded to more difficulty in reading and writing.

Coding of final scores for social-based surveys was done according to the established protocol for each respective questionnaire (SPF-IL(44); SWLS(45); COPSOQ (46); SMAS(47); LS(48)). For COPSOQ, we wanted to ensure that all individuals in the analyses were working with colleagues at the time of the survey. Therefore, we excluded participants from analyses that answered "no" to the question "Are you part of a working group at work?" (Nexc=15,396). There was no item in the questionnaire reporting on whether the individual had a supervisor/manager, thus we assumed that those who answered the supervisor-based items had a supervisor at the time of the survey. An overview of descriptives for the final scores can be found in Supplementary Table 1. Answer-options, scoring ranges, and sample sizes per trait are reported in Tables 1 and 2.

Phenotypic correlation analyses

Correlation analyses were performed across all traits (reading and social) (Suppl. Table 2). Analyses were performed in R (version 4.2.2) using the Pearson-method. P-values were Bonferroni corrected to account for multiple testing.

Psychological network analyses

In network analyses, psychological behaviour is conceptualized as a complex interplay of psychological and other components (50). The network consists of nodes representing observed variables, which are connected by edges representing statistical relationships. Estimation of the model is done using the Markov random field approach, where edges represent a full conditional association between two nodes after conditioning on all other nodes in the network. The resulting edges are defined as partial correlation coefficients. Mixed graphical models (21, 22) are an extension of the Gaussian-Ising model and the class of exponential family Markov Random Fields, which allows for modelling of traits following binomial, continuous and categorical distributions within one network. Furthermore, it allows for correlations within and between different groups of variables and the model does not enforce connections across all nodes if a statistical relationship is absent.

Mixed graphical models were estimated using the mgm function (21) of the bootnet package(20) (version 1.6) in R (version 4.2.1) on the phenotypic correlation matrix. Following model estimation, bootstrap tests were performed in order to i) assess the edge-weight accuracy by construction of alternative 95% confidence-intervals (Ntests = 1,000), and ii) assess the node stability using a drop-case approach (Ntests = 1,000). Only models with a correlation stability coefficient (CS) of >0.7 were interpreted (20, 51).

Genotype data cleaning

Imputed genotyping data for this study were provided by UMCG Genetics Lifelines Initiative (UGLI) using data from two genotyping batches. The first batch (UGLI1, release 2) was genotyped using the Infinium Global Screening Array MultiEthnic Disease Version 1.0. Cleaned genotype data (filtering steps included INFO > 0.8, MAF>0.01, HWE p≤10 -6 , and call-rate>0.99) from 36,339 individuals and 548,029 markers were imputed with the Sanger imputation service using the Haplotype Reference Consortium reference panel (version r1.1), resulting in a total of 7,424,407 imputed SNPs. The second sample (UGLI2, release 2) was genotyped on the FinnGen Thermo Fisher Axiom custom array. The cleaned genotype data (the same filtering criteria used for UGLI1 were applied) of a total of 28,149 participants and 460,136 markers were imputed following the same protocol as used for UGLI1, resulting in a total of 7,474,700 imputed SNPs.

For follow-up analyses, we used the principal components (PCs) provided by Lifelines, with PC scores generated separately for the genotyped individuals in UGLI1 and UGLI2. Further details about the genetic data provided by Lifelines can be found on their online wiki-page (https://wiki-lifelines.web.rug.nl/doku.php?id=ugli). Follow-up preparation of the genetic files included additional filter steps (MAF>0.01, HWE p≤10 -4 , and imputation quality >0.8) and merging of the UGLI1 and 2 imputed genotype data, resulting in a total of 6,965,423 variants for 64,488 individuals. Filtering and merging were done with PLINK (v1.90b6.10).

GCTA-GREML analyses

SNP-based heritability (h 2 SNP) was estimated using genome-wide complex trait analysis-genomic restricted maximum likelihood (GCTA-GREML) software(18) (version 1.93.2). The h 2 SNP represents the proportion of phenotypic variance explained by the variation in genetic relatedness, captured by a genetic relationship matrix (GRM) estimated from genotyped SNPs. The genetic correlation represents the proportion of genetic variance that is shared between two traits, based on the GRM estimates.

Analyses were performed using the threshold-GRM approach(52) which allows related and unrelated individuals to be analysed jointly. A second GRM was constructed by setting relationship coefficient values (π) of <0.02 to 0, which enables simultaneous estimation of the pedigree heritability (h 2 pedigree) and heritability explained by genotyped SNPs (h 2 SNP). Modelling both GRMs together with GREML helps to distinguish SNP-associated genetic effects from additional familial resemblance, thereby reducing upward bias in SNP-heritability estimates due to shared environmental and pedigree-associated effects. Gender, genotype batch, age at time of assessment and the first 10 PCs were included as covariates.

External GWAS summary statistics

Publicly available GWAS summary statistics representative of reading and social-interaction were selected for this study. We used the dyslexia GWAS summary statistics as the reading-based measure, which was based on self-report diagnoses of dyslexia ("Have you been diagnosed with dyslexia?" ; "yes/no") in the 23andMe cohort (n=1,138,870 with ncases=51,800) (11). We also selected five sets of social-interaction and well-being based GWAS summary statistics. The subjective well-being GWAS summary statistics came from a meta-analysis study of items evaluating levels of happiness (16). The original meta-analysis consisted of 59 participating cohorts with a total sample size of n=298,420. However, the publicly available data from that well-being study do not contain the 23andMe cohort. Furthermore, as the GWAS metaanalysis also included the Lifelines cohort, we received GWAS results excluding Lifelines.

The final total sample size included 192,994 individuals. All three loneliness-based measures (sociability, loneliness, and social isolation) were constructed from composite-scores in UK Biobank: the sociability GWAS summary statistic was based on (1) frequency of family/friends visits, (2) number and type of visited social venues, (3) worrying about social embarrassment, and (4) loneliness experience, with 342,461 participating individuals(14). The loneliness GWAS summary statistics were constructed from (1) frequency of feeling lonely, (2) number of individuals in the household, (3) frequency of family/friends visits, and (4) the ability to confide in someone, with a total of 452,302 individuals(13). GWAS summary statistics for social isolation were derived from (1) frequency of family/friends visits, (2) ability to confide in someone, and (3) number of social activities per week, with a sample size of 449,609(15) individuals.

Lastly, the social trust GWAS summary statistics were based on a composite score of (1) trustworthiness of other people, (2) if others take advantage of you, and (3) if others display altruistic behaviour (25). This latter study was conducted in a Danish cohort with a sample size of 33,882 individuals. A complete overview of the GWAS summary statistics can be found in Suppl. Table 5.

LDSC analyses

SNP heritability of and genetic correlations across GWAS summary statistics were estimated with Linkage Disequilibrium score regression (LDSC) (23, 24). LDSC software (v1.0.1) was used to munge summary statistics, followed by estimation of the heritability and genetic correlations. LD scores were based on the phase 3 1000 Genomes Project European reference genome panel, and analyses were restricted to a predefined set of HapMap 3 SNPs.

Power prediction for cross-trait PGS analyses

Power calculations were performed in order to estimate the expected predictive accuracy of PGS derived from selected GWAS summary statistics in Lifelines. First, the maximum possible prediction accuracy R² for the summary statistic was estimated by fitting the variant effect sizes using a best linear unbiased prediction(26), where h 2 corresponds to the summary statistic-based h 2 SNP as estimated with LDSC, N represents the GWAS sample size, and M represents the number of independent common variants that inform the PGS (for these analyses, M was based on the number of independent common SNPs that are present on a standard genotyping array for European ancestry (53), which is M = 60,000).

The maximum possible R² value for a within-trait predictor-outcome trait combination (e.g. dyslexiadyslexia) was used to further inform R² for cross-trait predictor-outcome trait combinations (e.g. dyslexialife satisfaction). We multiplied the maximum R² of the predictor GWAS with the genetic correlation (rg) between dyslexia and social traits, as estimated with LDSC, to account for the level of shared genetic variance in the cross-trait PGS analysis.

Then, we used a range of R² values to estimate the power for the cross-trait PGS analyses using simulations. First, a multivariate normal distribution was generated with mean 0 and a correlation parameter r defined as the square root of the adjusted R² , with the sample size equal to that of the outcome trait. Subsequently, a regression was conducted across the constructed multivariate data.

Simulations were repeated 10,000 times, where the power was estimated based on the frequency (percentage) of successful predictions (where p<0.0125). Regression analyses were conducted in R (v4.2.2) using the glm() function for binary outcomes, and the lm() function for continuous outcomes. First, the null model was constructed with the age at the time of assessment, the first five PC's, and gender as predictors, and the z-standardized phenotype score for dyslexia or social-based trait as outcome. The PGS-model included the z-standardized PGS as predictor as well. For the regressions with a binary outcome, McFadden R² values were calculated based on the fitted model's log likelihood values, and odds ratios were derived from the regression results. For the regressions with a continuous outcome, incremental R² values were estimated by deducting the nullmodel r 2 from the PGS-model r 2 , and β-estimates were derived from the PGS-model.

PGS analyses

Mendelian randomization analyses

The causal effect of exposure-associated SNPs on the outcome was estimated using the Mendelian Randomization (MR) framework, which uses genetic variants as instrumental variables to study the causal effect of an exposure (the instrumental SNPs associated with the exposure trait) on an outcome. Within the MR framework, three core assumptions must be met for the results to provide reliable evidence of causality (55, 56). First, the instrumental variants must be robustly associated with the exposure. Therefore, only SNPs reaching genome-wide significance (p <5E-8) in the exposure GWAS were selected as instruments. Second, the instrumental variants must be independent of potential confounders of the exposure-outcome relationship. Third, the instrumental variants must not directly affect the outcome, MR analyses were conducted using the TwoSampleMR(28,29) (v0.6.11) package in R (v4.2.1). Instrument strength towards the exposure was assessed with the F-statistic(58), retaining only those with F>10 to reduce bias from weak instruments. Next, causal effects were inferred from the SNP-exposure and SNPoutcome associations combined. The inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method was used as the primary method, as recommended by the MR guidelines (55), which combines SNP-specific Wald ratios and provides an unbiased estimate when all instrumental variable assumptions are met or when pleiotropy is balanced. Additional sensitivity analyses were performed to assess robustness of the results and potential MR violations. These included the MR Egger regression, which allows for directional pleiotropy by estimating an intercept term, the Weighted median estimator, which provides a consistent estimate if at least 50% of the weight comes from valid instruments, and the Weighted mode and Simple mode, which identify the most common causal effect estimate across SNPs under different clustering assumptions.

Follow-up sensitivity analyses for heterogeneity and pleiotropy were further evaluated using regression diagnostics and graphical inspection of SNP-specific effects, including funnel and scatter plots. Influence analyses were performed using leave-one-out analyses to assess whether results were driven by individual SNPs, and SNP-specific Wald ratio estimates were examined in relation to their standard errors to identify potential outliers.
