1;2 Background. Interstitial lung disease (ILD) is a potentially fatal toxicity of immune checkpoint inhibitors and antibody-drug conjugates. Drug-safety studies in healthcare databases identify ILD with claims-based algorithms that trade positive predictive value (PPV) against sensitivity. We assessed whether large language models (LLMs) improve on these algorithms, with and without electronic health record (EHR) notes, and whether these gains translate into measurable epidemiological benefits. Methods. In 309 oncology patients with ILD diagnosis codes at Mass General Brigham, adjudicated by two pulmonologists, we compared 12 open-access LLMs with 20 published claims-based algorithms. LLMs received claims, EHR notes, or both, without task-specific training. Measures were PPV and yield (proportion classified positive). We projected the attenuation of a drug-induced ILD odds ratio under non-differential outcome misclassification. Findings. LLMs extended the PPV-yield frontier of the published algorithms with all three inputs. Across the 36 model-input evaluations, LLM PPV exceeded that of the best algorithm without specialist-coding requirements (Algorithm 4) by 14.3 percentage points on average. With claims alone, Qwen3-8B achieved 89.8% PPV (95% CI 78.2-95.6) at 15.9% yield, versus 86.7% at 4.9% for the highest-PPV algorithm. With notes alone, Qwen3-32B achieved 78.9% PPV at 49.2% yield, versus 64.6% at 51.1% for Algorithm 4. With combined claims and notes, Qwen3-14B achieved 100.0% PPV at 24.3% yield. Under a simulated drugsafety scenario with a true odds ratio of 3.0, the projected observed odds ratio was 2.67 with combined inputs versus 1.07 with Algorithm 4, corresponding to a 46-fold difference in the estimated sample size required for 80% power (591 versus 27,200 patients). Interpretation. Zero-shot LLMs offer a higher-PPV alternative to rule-based claims phenotyping for ILD, on all three input types. Because the claims-only configuration uses the same claims data as existing algorithms, it could be deployed in administrative databases without linked notes, and may recover drug-safety signal otherwise lost to outcome misclassification. Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Abstract

Background. Interstitial lung disease (ILD) is a potentially fatal toxicity of immune checkpoint inhibitors and antibody-drug conjugates. Drug-safety studies in healthcare databases identify ILD with claims-based algorithms that trade positive predictive value (PPV) against sensitivity. We assessed whether large language models (LLMs) improve on these algorithms, with and without electronic health record (EHR) notes, and whether these gains translate into measurable epidemiological benefits. Methods. In 309 oncology patients with ILD diagnosis codes at Mass General Brigham, adjudicated by two pulmonologists, we compared 12 open-access LLMs with 20 published claims-based algorithms. LLMs received claims, EHR notes, or both, without task-specific training. Measures were PPV and yield (proportion classified positive). We projected the attenuation of a drug-induced ILD odds ratio under non-differential outcome misclassification. Findings. LLMs extended the PPV-yield frontier of the published algorithms with all three inputs. Across the 36 model-input evaluations, LLM PPV exceeded that of the best algorithm without specialist-coding requirements (Algorithm 4) by 14.3 percentage points on average. With claims alone, Qwen3-8B achieved 89.8% PPV (95% CI 78.2-95.6) at 15.9% yield, versus 86.7% at 4.9% for the highest-PPV algorithm. With notes alone, Qwen3-32B achieved 78.9% PPV at 49.2% yield, versus 64.6% at 51.1% for Algorithm 4. With combined claims and notes, Qwen3-14B achieved 100.0% PPV at 24.3% yield. Under a simulated drugsafety scenario with a true odds ratio of 3.0, the projected observed odds ratio was 2.67 with combined inputs versus 1.07 with Algorithm 4, corresponding to a 46-fold difference in the estimated sample size required for 80% power (591 versus 27,200 patients). Interpretation. Zero-shot LLMs offer a higher-PPV alternative to rule-based claims phenotyping for ILD, on all three input types. Because the claims-only configuration uses the same claims data as existing algorithms, it could be deployed in administrative databases without linked notes, and may recover drug-safety signal otherwise lost to outcome misclassification. 1 Introduction

Drug-induced interstitial lung disease (ILD) is a recognized class effect of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) and antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs), and one of the most consequential toxicities of modern oncologic therapy. 1, 2 In a pooled analysis of nine trastuzumab deruxtecan trials, 15.4% of patients developed adjudicated drug-related ILD or pneumonitis, including 2.2% grade 5 events, 3 and pneumonitis is the most common cause of death attributed to anti-PD-1/PD-L1 therapy. 4, 5 As these agents are used more widely, drug-safety studies increasingly rely on large claims databases, in which confirming cases by manual chart review does not scale. 6 Automated identification relies instead on rules built from diagnosis codes, and for ILD these codes are nonspecific. [7] [8] [9] J18.9 (pneumonia, unspecified organism), for example, is recorded for both ILD and infectious pneumonia. 6, 7 Rule-based algorithms therefore trade positive predictive value (PPV) against the number of cases identified. 8, 9 Across 20 claims-based algorithms evaluated by Shen and colleagues, whose cohort is the benchmark for the present study, broadly inclusive rules reached a PPV near 59%, whereas rules restricted to specialist-coded events reached PPVs in the high 80s but identified only a small fraction of patients. 10 Both failures matter for drug-safety studies: false-positive outcomes attenuate exposure-outcome associations, and overly restrictive definitions leave too few cases for adequately powered analyses. [11] [12] [13] The evidence on which an ILD diagnosis rests, such as imaging, pulmonary function testing and specialist assessment, is recorded in clinical notes rather than in claims, but it is unstructured and voluminous. 14 Large language models have shown the ability to interpret such unstructured information at scale, in both clinical notes and claims. [15] [16] [17] Unlike a fixed rule, an LLM can weigh whether an ILD code is corroborated or contradicted by other evidence in the record. Zeroshot LLMs have already outperformed rule-based methods for phenotyping chronic conditions from structured codes, 18, 19 and have supported case adjudication in observational research. 20 For ILD specifically, supervised machine-learning classifiers have outperformed rule-based identification on EHR data. 6 No study, however, has compared LLMs with validated claims-based algorithms for a drug-safety outcome on the same patients, separated the contribution of the input data from that of the model, or asked whether better phenotyping changes the resulting effect estimates. 19, 20 We sought to evaluate whether LLMs could improve ILD ascertainment for pharmacoepidemiologic drug-safety studies beyond investigator-defined algorithms, and whether any improvement would change the resulting drug-outcome effect estimates.

2 Methods

Study design and reference standard

We conducted a benchmarking study using an expert-adjudicated cohort of 360 oncology patients from Mass General Brigham (MGB), the largest healthcare system in Massachusetts, USA, between January 1, 2017, and December 31, 2024. 10 Patients were required to have at least two International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-10-CM) ILD diagnosis codes (J84.113, J84.114, J84.89, J84.9, J70.2, J70.3, J70.4, J70.8, J70.9, or J18.9) recorded 7-180 days apart and a diagnosis of breast, lung, colorectal, or gynecologic cancer. Patients were aged ≥ 18 years, or ≥ 66 years for Medicare beneficiaries, and had at least 365 days of continuous enrollment before the cohort entry date (CED). The CED was defined as the date of the second qualifying ILD code. Two pulmonologists independently used a standardized abstraction tool to review each patient's full EHR and assigned Probable ILD, Possible ILD, Not ILD, or Insufficient Evidence (inter-rater κ = 0.87). For binary evaluation, Probable and Possible ILD were combined into the positive class, following the source study. 10 We excluded 43 patients classified as Insufficient Evidence and 8 without retrievable EHR data, leaving a cohort of 309 patients with an adjudicated label and at least one clinical note within the observation window (Supplementary Fig. S1). Figure 1 summarises the study cohort, reference algorithms, and LLM evaluation workflow. We evaluated three primary input configurations: Claims, comprising structured claims alone; EHR notes, comprising clinical notes from EHR alone; and Fused, comprising claims and clinical notes together.

For each patient, we extracted structured claims and free-text notes within a window of -365 to +180 days from CED, identical to the chart-review window used to generate the reference standard. We framed the task as retrospective phenotyping: given the records available to an expert reviewer, determine whether the patient had ILD at the index date.

Data sources

We used structured claims from Medicare (January 1, 2016 to December 31, 2022), Medicaid (January 1, 2016 to December 31, 2021), and the MGB Health Plan (January 1, 2020 to December 31, 2024), comprising ICD-10-CM diagnosis codes, CPT and HCPCS procedure codes, and dispensed medications. Procedures included 42 Part-B-administered antineoplastic J-codes treated as ILD-relevant (Supplementary Table S2). Free-text data comprised typed clinical notes within the observation window. Note metadata were used to assign each note one of ten EHR note types: chest CT reports, pathology and biopsy reports, pulmonary function tests (PFTs), discharge summaries, chest X-rays (CXR), pulmonology encounters, progress notes, history-and-physical documents, ambulatory notes, and other documents.

Claim codes were serialized as text descriptions, each dated relative to the cohort entry date. Medications associated with drug-induced ILD were tagged inline with their risk level and drug class (for example, "[HIGH ILD risk: ICI]"), so that the model saw the risk alongside the drug rather than having to infer it (Supplementary Methods S1.2). Clinical notes were ordered by EHR note type, most informative for ILD first (chest CT, then pathology, then pulmonary function tests), so that these documents entered the first model input when a patient's notes exceeded one context window (Supplementary Table S3). No disease-specific keyword filtering was applied. Patient demographics were included in every model input (Table 1). The study was approved by the MGB Institutional Review Board (Protocol #2017P002659), which waived the requirement for informed consent. The study protocol was not registered or published. Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, or reporting of this study.

Patient-level aggregation through chunking

Because models tokenize the same text differently, each note was tokenized with the tokenizer of every evaluated model and assigned the largest of those token counts. Notes exceeding a single context window were then packed, in the fixed EHR note type priority order, into chunks of up to 123,964 tokens measured against that worst-case count, leaving the remainder of the 128,000-token window for the system prompt and the generated output. For fusion, the structured-claims block was included as a fixed prefix in every chunk. The notes-only and fused configurations contained the same clinical notes for each patient, although chunk boundaries could differ because the claims prefix occupied part of the available context.

The model generated one prediction per chunk. For binary evaluation, a patient was classified as positive if any chunk was labelled Probable or Possible ILD, following the human annotation rule. The three-class patient label was the highest-ranked chunk label, ordered as Probable ILD > Possible ILD > Not ILD. We compared this rule with three alternatives applied to the same chunk-level predictions (Supplementary Table S7): first chunk only, which uses the label of the first chunk, containing the highest-priority EHR note types; majority vote, which uses the most frequent label across a patient's chunks; and maximum confidence, which uses the label of the chunk with the highest model-reported confidence. Per-patient token counts and chunk distributions are shown in Supplementary Fig. S2. We also examined how applying thresholds to model-reported ILD probabilities affected PPV and yield (Supplementary Fig. S3).

Models and inference

We evaluated 12 diversed open-access LLMs spanning medical and general-purpose training, parameter scales, reasoning augmentation, and model families. These included MedGemma models with 4 and 27 billion parameters 21 and the general-purpose base model Gemma-3-27B. 22 We also evaluated Qwen3 models with 1.7, 4, 8, 14, and 32 billion parameters; 23 the reasoning-augmented HuatuoGPT-o1 medical models with 7 and 72 billion parameters; 24 and the general-domain non-thinkg models: Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct 25 and Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct models. 26 Details are presented in Supplementary Table S1.

Within each input configuration, all models received the same prompts and patient-data chunks. Prompts were developed on this cohort by the study team. Inference was zero-shot, without fine-tuning, retrieval, or in-context exemplars. We used greedy decoding (T = 0, with top-p and top-k sampling disabled), a fixed seed (42), no repetition penalty, a 128,000-token context, and tensor parallelism over four H100, served with vLLM (version 0.19.0). 27 For each chunk, the model returned a structured JSON response containing a categorical label, an estimated probability that the patient had ILD, a self-rated confidence in that label, and a short rationale with supporting evidence. The configuration-specific system prompts are reproduced in Supplementary Table S8. All computation was performed on servers within the Mass General Brigham network.

Evaluation

The primary measures were positive predictive value (PPV) and yield. PPV was the number of patients classified as ILD-positive according to the reference standard (i.e., manual chart review), divided by the number of patients classified as ILD-positive by the algorithm of interest.

Yield was the number of patients classified as ILD-positive, divided by the number of patients evaluated (309). Like sensitivity, yield describes the breadth of case identification. We used yield because patients were pre-selected by ILD diagnosis codes, preventing assessment of overall detection sensitivity, including cases missed by this initial screening. We compared PPV-yield frontiers, defined by methods for which no alternative achieved equal or higher PPV and yield with a strict improvement in at least one measure.

To assess whether individual EHR note types supported improved ILD phenotyping, we evaluated chest CT reports, pulmonary function tests, discharge summaries, chest radiographs, and progress notes, each alone or combined with claims. MedGemma-27B was used throughout to hold the model constant while varying the EHR note type. For each EHR note type, the LLM and reference algorithms were evaluated on the same patients with that EHR note type, and LLM-to-rule ratios (LLM_metric/rule_metric) were calculated for PPV and yield. Detailed results are reported in Supplementary Tables S4-S6.

Each model also reported a probability that the patient had ILD. Because such probabilities could be used to rank patients for review or to set positivity thresholds, we assessed whether they were reliable. Discrimination was measured with the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), the probability that a randomly chosen patient with ILD receives a higher score than one without; 0.5 corresponds to chance, and values below 0.5 indicate that patients without ILD tend to receive higher scores. Calibration was measured with the expected calibration error (ECE), a measure commonly used for machine-learning classifiers: 28, 29 the average absolute difference between the reported probability and the observed proportion of patients with ILD at that probability, weighted by the number of patients; 0 indicates perfect calibration. We also examined PPV and yield across probability thresholds (Supplementary Fig. S3).

To assess implications for drug-safety studies, we projected how each classifier's outcome misclassification would attenuate a hypothetical drug-ILD association under non-differential misclassification. 12, 30 Each classifier's sensitivity and specificity, computed within the study cohort, were assumed to apply to the hypothetical study population and to be identical in exposed and unexposed patients. For a true odds ratio of 3.0 and ILD prevalence of 0.10 among unexposed patients, we reconstructed the expected misclassified two-by-two table and calculated the observed odds ratio, the sample size required to detect it with 80% power, and power across sample sizes for a cohort with equal numbers of exposed and unexposed patients, at α = 0.05. We also examined projected odds ratios across baseline prevalences of 0.05-0.20.

Statistical analysis

Proportions are reported with Wilson 95% confidence intervals. Aggregation-strategy comparisons used 1,000 bootstrap resamples with a fixed seed (42). Paired differences in classification accuracy relative to Algorithm 4 (best non-specialist coded algorithm) were assessed using twosided exact McNemar tests. For epidemiological projections, uncertainty in the within-cohort sensitivity and specificity was propagated using 2,000 Monte Carlo draws from Jeffreys-prior Beta posteriors to obtain 95% intervals.

Model inference used vLLM (version 0.19.0), 27 with tokenizers and chat templates loaded through Hugging Face Transformers. 31 Analyses were performed in Python 3.11 using pandas, NumPy, and SciPy. Reporting followed TRIPOD+AI and TRIPOD-LLM. 32, 33

3 Results

Study cohort

The study cohort comprised 309 oncology patients with at least two claims-coded ILD diagnoses and retrievable clinical notes (Fig. 1; Table 1). After excluding 8 patients with missing modality, expert adjudication classified 86 patients as Probable ILD, 97 as Possible ILD, and 126 as Not ILD. Combining Probable and Possible ILD yielded 183 ILD-positive patients (59.2%).

Zero-shot LLMs extend the PPV-yield frontier across input modalities

The LLMs extended the rule-based PPV-yield frontier under all three input configurations: claims alone, clinical notes alone, and their combination (fusion; Fig. 2A). Under claims alone, 10 of the 12 models were non-dominated, meaning that no published rule achieved both a higher PPV and a higher yield; the same held for 11 of 12 models under notes and 11 of 12 under fusion. The exceptions were models like Gemma-3-27B which classified a large fraction of the cohort as positive and therefore approached the cohort prevalence. This advantage was evident even without clinical notes. With claims alone, Qwen3-8B achieved 89.8% PPV at 15.9% yield, compared with 86.7% PPV at 4.9% yield for Algorithm 4a, the highest-PPV specialist-coded rule (Fig. 2B). The model therefore achieved a higher PPV while classifying more than three times the proportion of patients as positive, demonstrating that improved phenotyping was possible using claims alone.

Clinical notes also supported high-PPV phenotyping at yields above those of the specialist-coded rules. Qwen3-14B achieved 92.9% PPV at 31.7% yield with notes alone (Fig. 2C). Adding claims increased its PPV to 100.0% (95% CI 95.1-100.0), while reducing yield to 24.3% (Fig. 2D). Other models offered higher-yield operating points. Qwen3-32B with fusion achieved 80.5% PPV and 48.2% yield, compared with 64.6% PPV and 51.1% yield for Algorithm 4, the best-performing rule. This represented a 15.9-percentage-point increase in PPV with a similar yield. Thus, the LLM advantage extended from highly selective case identification to phenotyping at yields close to the broader rule-based comparator.

Document-restricted LLM phenotyping improves PPV over matched rulebased comparators

We next tested whether the advantage over rule-based phenotyping persisted when LLMs received a single EHR note type instead of the whole breadth of available notes. For that experiment, we fixed the LLM to be MedGemma-27B. Each comparison used the same patients with the relevant EHR note type. PPV point estimates exceeded those of Algorithm 4 in nine of ten evaluations across five EHR note types (Fig. 3A,B). Among patients with chest CT reports (n = 283), fusion achieved 80.5% PPV and 52.7% yield, compared with 64.8% PPV and 51.2% yield for Algorithm 4. Discharge summaries alone (n = 244) also achieved higher PPV than Algorithm 4, at 89.6% versus 63.7% (Fig. 3C,D). Because eligible populations differed across EHR note types, these findings do not establish which EHR note type performs best. The matched comparisons nevertheless show that the PPV advantage over rule-based phenotyping can persist when the model receives a single EHR note type.

Clinical notes increase yield, while model choice shapes PPV

We next examined how input data and model choice shaped the PPV-yield trade-off. Replacing claims with clinical notes increased yield for 11 of 12 models (Fig. 4C; Supplementary Table S5). The exception, Gemma-3-27B, already classified every patient as positive with claims alone. Model choice also affected PPV. Under fusion, MedGemma-27B achieved 77.5% PPV, compared with 60.0% for its general-purpose base model, Gemma-3-27B (Fig. 4B). This higher PPV was accompanied by greater selectivity, with yields of 46.0% and 98.7%, respectively. Several generalpurpose models also achieved higher PPV than MedGemma-27B, indicating that high PPV was not restricted to medically specialised models (Fig. 4A). Adding claims to notes further changed the operating point in different ways: some models gained PPV at lower yield, whereas others gained yield at lower PPV (Fig. 4D). Thus, clinical notes generally increased yield, while the resulting PPV-yield trade-off depended on the model and input combination. Performance stratified by cancer type is presented in Supplementary Fig. S4.

Strong categorical performance does not ensure reliable probability estimates

We next examined whether the probabilities accompanying categorical predictions reliably reflected ILD status. Within each model-input evaluation, the emitted probabilities occupied only 3 to 13 distinct values, depending on the model and input, limiting the resolution of probabilitybased thresholds. Overall, high categorical PPV did not translate into reliable probability-based discrimination: Qwen3-14B achieved AUROCs of 0.300 with notes and 0.322 with fusion, below the 0.5 expected by chance, meaning that patients without ILD tended to receive higher probabilities than patients with ILD. Calibration was also poor. For MedGemma-27B under fusion, the expected calibration error was 0.238: reported probabilities differed from the observed proportion of patients with ILD by 24 percentage points on average (Supplementary Fig. S5). Thus, accurate identification of a high-PPV case set did not establish the reliability of the accompanying probabilities, which require separate validation before use as estimates of uncertainty.

LLM phenotyping is projected to reduce effect attenuation and sample-size requirements

We finally assessed whether improved phenotyping could preserve drug-ILD associations and reduce the sample size required to detect them. We modeled a cohort with equal numbers of exposed and unexposed patients, with a hypothesised true odds ratio (OR) of 3.0 and baseline ILD prevalence of 0.10. Each classifier's measured sensitivity and specificity were used to project the observed OR under non-differential outcome misclassification (Methods). The best rule-based algorithm (Algorithm 4) reduced the observed OR to 1.07, nearly erasing the true association, whereas Qwen3-14B preserved it to increasing degrees: 1.31 with claims, 1.80 with notes, and 2.67 with fusion (Fig. 5C). LLM with fusion input therefore preserved the largest proportion of the hypothesised association. The lower projected attenuation relative to Algorithm 4 persisted across the examined baseline prevalences of 0.05-0.20. These differences translated into substantially lower projected sample-size requirements. At 80% power (α = 0.05), Algorithm 4 required approximately 27,200 patients. The corresponding requirements for Qwen3-14B were 7,978 with claims, 840 with notes, and only 591 recruted patients with fusion (Fig. 5A,B). The claims-only configuration therefore reduced the required sample size even without linked clinical notes, while fusion reduced it from tens of thousands of patients to hundreds. Under the specified assumptions, these phenotypes preserved more of the exposure-outcome association and enabled its detection in substantially smaller studies.

4 Discussion

In this matched evaluation of 309 oncology patients, selected LLMs identified ILD cases with higher PPV than the reference algorithms with similar or greater yields. The advantage was observed with the input of claims alone, clinical notes alone, and the fusion. Previous studies have shown that zero-shot LLMs can outperform rule-based methods for phenotyping chronic conditions, 19 and that supervised machine-learning classifiers can outperform code-based rules for ILD identification. 6 Our study extends this work by comparing models and rules on the same expert-adjudicated patients and examining the implications for association studies. The principal advance is an improvement in the PPV-yield trade-off compared with 20 rule-based claim algorithms, 10 with potential consequences of reducing number of cases identified or a reduced sensitivity.

The projected epidemiological benefit gives this classification improvement a practical research consequence. Outcome misclassification can attenuate associations and increase the sample size needed to detect them. 12, 13 Under the modelled scenario, the best LLM configurations required far fewer patients than the best rule-based algorithm to detect the same association. Improved automated detection of a phenotype could therefore make associations detectable in cohorts of a size that rule-based ascertainment would leave underpowered. The result depends on the classifier's sensitivity and specificity and on the assumed prevalence; PPV alone does not determine the projected benefit.

The model and input comparisons clarify how such gains might be obtained. Clinical notes generally increased yield, while model choice altered the balance between PPV and the proportion classified positive. MedGemma's higher PPV than its general-purpose base model was accompanied by greater selectivity, suggesting that medical pretraining shifted the operating point rather than uniformly improving classification. General-purpose models also achieved high PPV, and larger models generally performed better. These findings support evaluating candidate models empirically for the intended phenotype. Fusion also changed which cases were identified, gaining some detections and losing others relative to the separate inputs. Several individual EHR note types supported higher PPV than rules evaluated in the same eligible patients. Useful evidence could therefore be recovered from a single EHR note type.

The claims-only result is particularly relevant to administrative databases without linked clinical notes. Using open-weight models, we performed all inference within institutional infrastructure, providing a practical basis for analyzing protected records. Zero-shot inference avoided taskspecific model training, although pipeline development and validation still required clinical expertise. The operating point could also be adjusted after inference by changing how a patient's chunk-level predictions were combined, which could support different requirements for case confirmation or cohort assembly. The categorical label, rather than the emitted probability, should be the basis for case definition: using LLM outputs to rank patients for human review would require uncertainty estimates that these models did not provide.

This study has several limitations. First, this was a single-institution study of oncology patients already flagged by ILD codes. The findings therefore concern retrospective ILD ascertainment and do not independently validate prospective prediction or attribution to a particular drug. Performance in other health systems, and in larger populations not pre-selected by ILD codes, remains unknown. Second, high PPV often accompanied selective classification. We do not claim the PPV-yield phenotyping tradeoff solved, but merely found a way to improve its limitations across several patient input data. Third, we compared LLMs only with already deployed rule-based algorithms. Supervised machine-learning classifiers trained on claims or notes were not evaluated and could match or exceed zero-shot LLMs where labelled training data are available. Finally, the projections assume that sensitivity and specificity estimated in this enriched cohort apply to populations with lower baseline prevalence, and that misclassification is nondifferential by drug exposure. Because model inputs included drug exposures annotated with their ILD risk, classification errors could differ by exposure. These assumptions were not tested in an actual association study. External validation and empirical drug-outcome analyses are needed to determine whether the projected gains are realised.

In summary, our findings suggest that LLMs achieved more favorable trade-offs between case identification and diagnostic precision than published claims-based algorithms, across different data input settings: claims only, EHR note only, or claims-EHR-note combined. Importantly, these improvements were achievable even with claims alone, without requiring linked clinical notes. Under specified outcome-misclassification assumptions, improved ascertainment was projected to reduce effect attenuation and substantially lower sample-size requirements, potentially enabling drug-safety studies that would otherwise be underpowered. Further advances through few-shot learning, 34 knowledge-enhanced inference, 35, 36 and retrieval-augmented generation may improve phenotyping performance beyond zero-shot approaches and warrant future investigation. 37 More broadly, our findings highlight the potential of LLM-based phenotyping to address outcome misclassification as a fundamental limitation of real-world evidence generation, enabling more reliable and efficient drug-safety and comparative effectiveness research.
