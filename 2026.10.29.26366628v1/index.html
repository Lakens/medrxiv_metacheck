Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background: Accurate blood pressure (BP) measurement, essential for hypertension care, requires proper technique. Posture errors can raise systolic BP by more than 20 mm Hg, yet technique is rarely verified objectively. We aimed to develop and evaluate Coach-BP, a computer vision system that detects technique errors from video to support real-time coaching.

Methods: We enrolled adults attending an academic cardiovascular prevention center between February and April 2026 and filmed BP measurements with front and side cameras. We analyzed 408 encounters from 225 participants. Coach-BP uses machine learning models trained on clinician labels to assess five American Heart Association criteria using pose, visual context, and audio-visual features. We evaluated its accuracy against expert clinician ratings by leave-one-participant-out cross-validation. We then examined robustness to simulated camera artifacts and interpreted the features important for each criterion. Findings: At baseline, 138 (62%) of 223 participants had at least one technique error and 54 (24%) two or more. Coach-BP achieved a mean area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) of 0•93 (95% CI 0•90-0•94), with 0•97 (0•94-0•99) for legs uncrossed, 0•97 (0•93-1•00) for no talking, 0•92 (0•85-0•98) for back supported, 0•91 (0•86-0•95) for feet flat, and 0•86 (0•81-0•91) for arm supported at heart level. Mean balanced accuracy was 0•88 (0•85-0•90). Performance was robust to simulated camera shift, zoom, and rotation. Its models relied on features clinicians would assess, such as cuffed-hand height and foot position. One model classified back support similarly from either camera.

Introduction

Hypertension is a leading modifiable contributor to cardiovascular morbidity and mortality, affecting more than 1 billion adults worldwide. [1] [2] [3] Yet only about one in five people with hypertension have their blood pressure controlled. 3 Despite the importance of accurate blood pressure (BP) measurement for clinical decision-making, measurements are frequently obtained under conditions that introduce substantial error.

The American Heart Association (AHA) and other professional societies recommend that patients be seated with their back supported, feet flat on the floor, legs uncrossed, without talking, and the measurement arm supported at heart level. [4] [5] [6] Deviations from these recommendations are common 4 and raise measured systolic BP, 7 in some studies by more than 20 mm Hg. 8 Errors of this size can lead to hypertension being misdiagnosed and treatment being increased unnecessarily. 7, 9 Despite guideline recommendations, BP measurement technique is rarely verified in routine practice. Office measurement is often poorly standardized, 9 the errors behind a recorded reading can rarely be identified afterwards, 8 and checking technique would require an observer at every reading. Automated office devices, which take several unattended readings, reduce observer-related error but do not verify posture. 10 Home monitoring, which enables longitudinal assessment and timely titration, 9,11 moves measurements used for treatment decisions to unsupervised settings where technique is unverified.

Computer vision may make automated verification of technique feasible. 12 Here we present Coach-BP, a computer vision system intended to check, at the time of a seated measurement, whether each of five technique criteria is met, so that technique can be corrected before a reading is used for clinical decisions (Figure 1). Coach-BP uses pose-estimation, visual-representation, and audio-visual models to assess body positioning and speech from standard video recordings. [13] [14] [15] [16] In this study, we evaluated its accuracy for the five recommended criteria from front and side video of adults at a cardiovascular prevention clinic.

Methods

Study design and participants

In this prospective, single-center study, we enrolled adults aged 18 years or older attending scheduled appointments at, or employed by, the University of California San Francisco Cardiovascular Prevention Center, San Francisco, CA, USA, between Feb 3 and April 22, 2026. Patients were approached for participation regardless of hypertension status (Appendix) and had to be able to sit upright in a chair. At each encounter, study staff performed a research BP measurement with an Omron 10 Series monitor (BP7465, Omron Healthcare, Kyoto, Japan), a validated upper-arm oscillometric device, before the participant's appointment. The research reading was not used in care.

Participants completed a four-item intake questionnaire on history of hypertension, antihypertensive medication, home BP monitoring, and previous instruction in measurement technique. Age, sex, gender, race, and ethnicity were not collected, because the protocol was approved as a minimal-risk study that A1). (D) Example Coach-BP verdicts on frames of a volunteer. No study participant is shown. A tick marks a criterion met, a cross a criterion violated, and n/a a criterion not shown for that view. In this preprint, the video frames in panels A and D are shown as line drawings without facial detail. The original images are available from the corresponding authors on request. BP=blood pressure.

used no protected health information after recruitment. Reporting follows STARD-AI, 17 with STROBE 18 for the prevalence analysis and TRIPOD+AI 19 for model development. The University of California San Francisco institutional review board approved the study (IRB 25-45067), and all participants gave written informed consent.

Coaching procedure

Each visit began with a baseline reading without instruction on technique. When staff observed incorrect positioning, they gave corrective feedback following the AHA's home BP measurement guidance 20 and repeated the measurement in the same sitting. Participants whose technique appeared correct were generally not re-measured. Feedback was delivered by four study-team members (RB, JS, JN, and AJA), two researchers trained in AHA measurement technique by a study cardiologist (JAL-M) and two nurse practitioners. Coach-BP was not used for real-time analysis or feedback during the study. Participants had been seated for at least 5 min before the baseline reading while the study was explained and consent obtained. A repeat reading was taken about 1-2 min later.

Video acquisition

Each encounter was recorded for about 30-60 s per view with two tablet cameras (Figure 1B), one in front of and one beside the seated patient, both on the patient's right. Placement was not fixed (Appendix). The front view was used for the arm, legs, feet, and talking criteria and the side view for back supported, which was also tested from the front.

Landmark extraction

MediaPipe (BlazePose) 13, 14 located 33 body landmarks, such as the shoulders, knees, and ankles, in two and three dimensions in each frame. Up to 30 evenly spaced frames per view were summarized by each landmark's mean position, its SD, and its mean visibility score. Each landmark's position in the image was then measured from the center of the patient's shoulders, in units of the patient's torso size. The arm, legs, and feet models are therefore not affected by where the patient sits in the frame or how far they are from the camera (Appendix Table A2).

Criterion definitions and models

Each criterion was scored as met or violated. For each criterion except no talking, we trained an L1regularized (lasso) logistic regression model with balanced class weights (scikit-learn) 21, 22 on the landmark variables of the relevant view. The feet model also used the output of the legs model. The back model, trained on both views so that it could be tested from the front, also used whole-image features from DINOv2. 15 Model settings were tuned using the training participants only. Legs and feet models were also trained on left-right mirrored copies of each recording. Scores were converted to verdicts at a threshold of 0•5 fixed before evaluation (Appendix). For no talking, we used TalkNet, 16 a pretrained audio-visual model that detects whether a visible face is speaking, without fine-tuning. An encounter was classed as talking when TalkNet's highest score for the patient's face exceeded zero, its default threshold.

The AHA arm recommendation comprises two conditions, the arm resting on a support and the cuff at heart level. Both panels scored them separately, and the criterion was met only when both were satisfied. Because the cuffed arm varied between encounters, an open-vocabulary object detector (OWLv2) 23 located the cuff in each front-view video without using clinician ratings. The landmarks were mirrored when the cuff was on the right, so that the cuffed arm was always on the same side.

Rule-based baselines

To measure what Coach-BP adds, each criterion was also assessed with a rule-based baseline, a decision rule using one or two measurements from the same recording. Back supported was judged by a preset cutoff on the angle between the trunk and the chair backrest, and legs uncrossed by whether the ankles or knees had swapped sides relative to the hips. Our earlier arm and feet rules had been tuned on this cohort, so their baseline was instead the single measurement and cut-off that best separated met from violated in the other participants. For no talking, the baseline was a cut-off on the proportion of the clip with both speech and lip movement (Appendix). Unlike Coach-BP, which gives a score for each encounter, the baselines return only met or violated, so they were compared on balanced accuracy, not AUROC.

Reference standard

Each encounter was scored from the video by one reviewer from each of two panels of three clinicians. The first panel, a cardiologist and two nurse practitioners from the study team, provided the ratings used in development. The second, two nurse practitioners and a cardiology fellow with no involvement in the study, rated independently. Each reviewer in a panel scored different encounters. Reviewers did not see Coach-BP outputs, second-panel reviewers did not see the first panel's ratings, and reviewers were not masked to whether an encounter followed coaching.

Evaluation and statistical analysis

All models were evaluated by leave-one-participant-out cross-validation, 24 testing each participant with models trained on the others. Performance was summarized by the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) for the scores and by balanced accuracy, the mean of sensitivity and specificity, for the verdicts at the prespecified threshold. Balanced accuracy, which weights violations and met criteria equally, was preferred to accuracy, which rewards never flagging rare violations. Both were also averaged across the five criteria. When no face or person was detected, the encounter was left out only for the criterion affected.

We estimated 95% CIs by bootstrap, resampling participants 2000 times. 25 Agreement between the two expert panels served as a benchmark for agreement between clinicians. Because Coach-BP was developed on the first panel's ratings, Coach-BP and the first panel were each compared with the second panel. Agreement was measured as balanced agreement, which is balanced accuracy with one rater as the reference. It was preferred to Cohen's κ, which depends strongly on how often each rater flags a violation when violations are rare. Cohen's κ is given in the Appendix. Proportions are reported with Wilson intervals. Tests were two-sided and not adjusted for multiple testing.

In a secondary analysis of participants with more than one reading, the proportion with any error at the first and last readings was compared with the exact McNemar test. Associations between the four selfreported characteristics and any error at the baseline reading were estimated by multivariable logistic regression. For each error that the first expert panel rated as corrected, we report whether Coach-BP classified the corrected reading as meeting the criterion.

Model interpretability

To interpret each landmark model, closely related variables (Spearman |ρ| of at least 0•7) were grouped into features. In 2000 refits on random halves of participants, 26 features selected in at least 70% of refits and with an AUROC of at least 0•55 when used alone (single-feature AUROC) are shown. For back supported, whose image features do not correspond to body positions, we instead measured how accuracy changed when parts of the image were replaced with uniform gray (Appendix).

Robustness to recording conditions

Robustness was tested by altering each participant's recording and reassessing it with the same crossvalidated model, without retraining. We simulated changes in framing (sideways shifts, changes in apparent body size, and rotation), changes of camera viewpoint, low light, reduced resolution, video compression, motion blur, and shortened recordings, with failure prespecified as a fall in AUROC of more than 0•03 or in balanced accuracy of more than 0•05, with the paired 95% interval excluding zero (Appendix).

Results

Cohort and prevalence of technique errors

Ten of the 418 recorded encounters were excluded because ratings were missing or the video was unusable (Appendix). The analysis included 408 encounters from 225 of 227 participants. Of the 222 who answered the intake question, 112 (50%) reported a history of hypertension (Figure 1C).

Of the 223 participants whose first reading was analyzed, 138 (62%) had at least one technique error and 54 (24%) two or more (Figure 2). The most common errors were the arm not supported at heart level (76 [34%]) and feet not flat (65 [29%]), followed by lack of back support (42 [19%]), crossed legs (24 [11%]), and talking (11 [5%]). Of 148 participants who routinely measured BP at home, 87 (59%) had at least one error. No self-reported characteristic was associated with having an error after mutual adjustment (Appendix).

Detection accuracy

Against the expert ratings used in development (Figure 3A-C ). Results were similar for baseline readings alone and in encounters on which the two panels agreed (Appendix). Coach-BP gave no talking verdict in seven encounters, where no face was tracked, and no back verdict in two of these, where the participant was not detected. Counting these encounters as errors would lower balanced accuracy by at most 0•011 and AUROC by at most 0•022 (Appendix).

Where both gave a verdict, Coach-BP matched or improved on the rule-based baseline for all five criteria and raised mean balanced accuracy from 0•74 to 0•87 (Figure 3A), a difference of 0•13 (95% CI 0•09-0•18). Gains were clear for feet flat, the arm criterion (0•08 [0•01-0•14]), back supported, and no talking. Model interpretability and feature importance

Most features corresponded to what a clinician would assess (Figure 4). For the arm criterion, the feature selected in every refit was the height of the cuffed hand above the hip midpoint (f1, single-feature AUROC 0•81 [95% CI 0•74-0•87]), with the hand and elbow positioned forward and out to the side (f3-f5), the pattern of an arm resting on a surface. The second most selected, heel detection confidence (f2), reflects how clearly the lower body was seen. Features for legs uncrossed were the sideways positions of the feet and knees (f6, f7), foot height (f8), and stillness of the near heel (f9, 0•93 [0•89-0•96]), and for feet flat the sideways and forward positions and height of the feet (f10, f11, f13, f14) and the legs-model output (f12).

The arm criterion's two conditions diverged in 63 (15%) of 408 encounters, and cuff height was harder to classify than arm support (Appendix).

For back supported, graying out everything except the patient and backrest lowered balanced accuracy from 0•94 to 0•90 (95% CI 0•84-0•94), whereas graying out the patient reduced it to 0•63 (0•56-0•69), and the front view showed the same pattern (Figure 4E, F). No talking rests on one number, the maximum TalkNet score, which classified 13 of 14 talking and 359 of 387 silent encounters correctly (Figure 4D).

Agreement with independent raters

Mean balanced agreement of Coach-BP with the second expert panel was 0•85 (95% CI 0•82-0•88), compared with 0•88 (0•85-0•90) with the first panel and 0•89 (0•85-0•92) between the two panels (Figure 3D). Coach-BP's agreement with the second panel was slightly lower than that between the panels (difference -0•041 [95% CI -0•074 to -0•004]), and lower for the arm criterion (-0•10 [-0•18 to -0•03]) and feet flat (-0•08 [-0•14 to -0•02]).

Robustness to recording conditions

Sideways shifts and changes in apparent size left the landmark models unchanged, and no change in framing altered any model's AUROC by more than 0•02 (Figure 3E-G). Under simulated changes of camera viewpoint, AUROC for legs uncrossed and feet flat stayed within 0•82-0•99, whereas for the arm criterion it fell from 0•84-0•88 between head-on and 60° to the patient's right to 0•73-0•81 with the camera on the patient's left (Appendix Figure S3). The same back model was similarly accurate from the front camera, placed about 30° to the patient's right, as from the side camera (AUROC 0•92 and 0•92, balanced accuracy 0•88 and 0•91, Figure 3H, Appendix Table A6), but less robust to frame rotation (Appendix). A back model trained on one view alone was much less accurate from the other camera (Appendix Table A6). No model failed under reduced resolution or motion blur. Severe low light degraded the trained models, severe compression degraded the legs and back models, and quarter-length recordings degraded the legs model (Appendix).

Technique before and after coaching

The 171 participants with a repeat reading more often had a baseline error than the 52 without one (75% vs 19%). Among them, the number with any error fell from 128 (75%) to 12 (7%), a difference of -67•8 percentage points (95% CI -74•8 to -60•8, p<0•0001). All 43 participants correct at baseline remained correct. By the second panel's ratings, the number fell from 114 (67%) to 16 (9%) of the 170 rated at both readings. When an error was corrected, Coach-BP classified that reading as meeting the criterion in 172 (91%) of 189 cases (Appendix). At the repeat reading, Coach-BP flagged at least one error in 40 (24%) of the 170 with all five verdicts, compared with 12 (7%) by the first panel.

Discussion

Coach-BP detected errors in all five AHA criteria from ordinary clinic video, with a mean AUROC of 0•93, ranging from 0•86 for the arm criterion to 0•97 for legs uncrossed and no talking. Such errors were common, affecting 62% of participants at baseline, including 59% of those who routinely measured BP at home. These estimates describe patients positioning themselves without instruction while waiting for their appointment, which is closest to unsupervised home measurement, rather than routine clinic measurement, in which staff are expected to position the patient. Back support, for which we had placed a second camera, was assessed about as well from the front camera, so a single front camera, placed as in the study, could assess all five criteria, although it was more sensitive to frame rotation.

Our prevalence estimates are consistent with studies that assessed technique by observation or video. Among 100 patients with hypertension filmed while measuring their own BP in primary care, only 3% made no error, judged against a broader checklist than ours. 27 Such assessment depends on trained observers. The only previous automated check of seated posture we found, a sensor-equipped chair, 28-30 requires dedicated equipment and does not assess the arm or feet. We are not aware of a previous system that assesses all five criteria from ordinary video and has been evaluated against clinician review. Because it returns a verdict for each criterion, Coach-BP is designed to show what needs correcting.

The arm criterion was the least accurately classified, and Coach-BP's agreement with the second panel was lowest for it (balanced agreement 0•75, compared with 0•85 between the two panels). With the cuff on the right arm, the camera usually stood on that side, and there the arm criterion was less accurate (AUROC 0•76 vs 0•90) and clinicians also agreed less (0•77 vs 0•88) (Appendix). Adding an estimate of heart level, depth information, or the side camera did not improve the arm criterion, and for cuff height alone the best model agreed with the second panel as well as the two panels agreed with each other (balanced agreement 0•71 for both, Appendix). This suggests that cuff height is hard to judge from video, for clinicians and models alike. A more precise definition of heart level, scored separately from arm support, would help both.

Coach-BP assesses the conditions of a reading, not the BP value. A missed error lets through a reading that is usually biased upwards, whereas a false alert costs an unnecessary repeat reading but, if frequent, could erode trust. Here, 123 (31%) of the 401 encounters with all five verdicts had at least one false alert, and 28 of 41 talking alerts were false, so thresholds for coaching remain to be set. Withholding verdicts with a score between 0•3 and 0•7 raised mean balanced accuracy from 0•88 to 0•93, but 125 (31%) of 401 encounters then received all five verdicts (Appendix Table A8). Deployment should show each verdict with the image it is based on, keep a human check in clinic use, and audit verdicts against human review. Video also captures faces, speech, and, at home, other household members. Because the body-tracking model was designed for mobile devices, 14 a deployed system could analyze video on the device and store only the verdicts.

The substantial reduction in technique errors following brief staff-delivered coaching suggests that most errors are readily correctable. Future studies should evaluate whether Coach-BP can reproduce these improvements through real-time automated feedback in both clinic and home settings, including determining the type, intensity, and frequency of coaching needed to achieve and sustain proper measurement technique. More broadly, establishing automated technique verification as a standard component of home BP monitoring will require validation across diverse home environments, camera types, lighting conditions, furniture, clothing, and patient populations. Such studies should evaluate detection accuracy and the feasibility and acceptability of integrating automated coaching into routine home BP monitoring.

Limitations

Coach-BP was developed and evaluated in one cohort by cross-validation, at a single center, with the same tablet model for every recording. Simulated changes in framing, camera viewpoint, and recording quality partly address this, but validation across sites, devices, and homes is needed. No demographic or anthropometric data were collected, so performance by age, sex, race and ethnicity, skin tone, or body size remains to be examined. Pose and face detection models can perform differently across these groups, and future studies should record these characteristics. Talking and crossed legs were uncommon (14 and 26 encounters), so their confidence intervals are wider, and larger samples would narrow them. The coaching comparison was observational, in participants whom staff re-measured because they saw an error. The second panel, which had no role in coaching, found a similar fall in errors. Thus, automated coaching is expected to achieve the same, which remains to be established.

2. Video acquisition and camera geometry

Each BP encounter was recorded with two tablet cameras (iPad Air 11-inch [M3], Apple, Cupertino, CA, USA, running iPadOS 26.3, 1080 × 1920 pixels, about 30 frames per s), one in front of and one beside the seated patient, both on the patient's right, for about 30-60 s per view. Placement was not fixed. Measured retrospectively from the recordings, the front camera viewed the patient from a median of 31° to their right (IQR 21-37°) and pointed a median of 20° downward (IQR 17-22°). The front-view video was used to assess arm supported at heart level, legs uncrossed, feet flat on the floor and no talking. Back supported was assessed from the side-view video and, separately, from the front-view video (Section 11). Coach-BP was applied offline to the clips described below, and its outputs were not shown to participants or staff. Real-time use was not evaluated, nor was analysis on the recording device. For analysis, most recordings were cut to their central 20 s, and the rest were used whole or cut by hand. Of the 816 clips analyzed, two views of each of the 408 encounters, 694 (85%) lasted about 20 s (19-21 s), 29 (4%) were shorter, and 93 (11%) were longer. The reviewers rated these clips, and Coach-BP was run on the same clips. In four encounters the central 20 s did not show the participant, and these encounters were excluded (Section 1). Coach-BP is intended for patients measuring their own blood pressure and for clinic staff taking a measurement, and it requires no clinical or technical expertise beyond placing a camera.

Camera tilt was estimated from the vanishing point of vertical edges in the room (door frames, wall corners, furniture), in three frames per recording, with the focal length estimated from the same images. The estimate changed little with an error of ±20% in focal length or when only the patient's body was masked. The front camera's angle to the patient was estimated as the angle of the three-dimensional shoulder line to the camera, and so reflects both where the camera stood and how the patient sat. Both cameras were on the patient's right whichever arm was cuffed (front camera in 97% of left-arm and 91% of right-arm sessions, side camera in 95% and 94%), but the angle to the front camera was larger with the cuff on the left arm (median 33°, IQR 28-38°) than on the right (16°, 7-30°), consistent with patients turning slightly toward the cuffed arm. These values describe how placement varied rather than a protocol.

3. Landmark variables

We extracted body landmarks with MediaPipe (BlazePose), 5, 6 an open-source on-device pose-estimation framework that returns 33 anatomical landmarks in each video frame, each with a visibility score. Landmark positions were represented both in two-dimensional image coordinates and in three-dimensional coordinates, 7 in approximate meters, centered on the hip midpoint, extracted from the same frames. Up to 30 frames per view were sampled at evenly spaced intervals across each recording. For each encounter, landmark coordinates were summarized across frames as the mean and SD of each coordinate and the mean visibility score, giving 165 two-dimensional landmark variables (33 landmarks × five summary statistics) and 198 three-dimensional landmark variables (33 landmarks × six). Twodimensional coordinates of both views were then expressed relative to the patient's body rather than the video frame. Each coordinate was measured from the midpoint of the two shoulders and divided by the distance from one shoulder to the opposite hip, and within-recording SDs were divided by the same distance. This makes the arm, legs and feet models exactly invariant to where the patient sits in the frame and to the patient's distance from the camera, but not to rotation of the frame or a change of viewpoint, which were tested by simulation. The inputs of the back model also include scene embeddings of the whole image, which are not referenced to the body, so its robustness to framing was tested on real image crops (Section 9).

The shoulder-to-opposite-hip distance was chosen as the scale because it varies less between recordings than shoulder width (coefficient of variation 0•140 vs 0•214), which rotation of the body foreshortens.

4. Models

Each of the five AHA criteria was scored as a binary outcome (criterion met vs violated). We evaluated rule-based baselines, supervised models and, for back supported and no talking, feature-specific models.

Reviewers also recorded whether the back was straight, whether the patient remained still and whether the cuff was on bare skin. These items were outside the five criteria that the study set out to assess and are not analyzed, except back straight in Section 11.

Rule-based baselines. Back supported was classified by the angle between the trunk and the chair backrest identified in the side-view video, and legs uncrossed by the relative ordering of the left and right knees and ankles, with thresholds fixed a priori from ergonomic considerations. For the arm and feet criteria, whose geometric rules had been tuned on the study cohort, the baseline reported is instead the single front-view variable that best separated the classes within each training fold, with its cut-off searched over every candidate value in that fold, evaluated on the held-out participant. For no talking, the baseline was an audio-visual heuristic, the proportion of the clip in which detected speech coincided with movement of the patient's lips. Its threshold had been selected on the study data, and it was run on the front clip that the reviewers saw with that threshold unchanged.

Supervised machine-learning models.

For each criterion we trained an L1-regularized logisticregression classifier (LogisticRegressionCV with the liblinear solver and balanced class weights) 8, 9 on the encounter-level landmark variables. The legs model used front-view two-dimensional variables, and the feet model used front-view two-dimensional and three-dimensional variables and the output of the legs model. The arm model used front-view variables aligned to the cuffed side (described below). The inputs of the back model were the two-dimensional and three-dimensional variables of a recording and its scene embedding, and it was trained on the side and front recordings of every training encounter together, so that one model could be tested from either camera (Section 11). The regularization strength was selected within each training fold, using the training participants only, by five-fold inner cross-validation, stratified by outcome and grouped by participant, over a logarithmic grid of eight values between 10⁻² and 10¹, with AUROC as the selection metric. The inner cross-validation was repeated ten times with different random fold assignments, and out-of-fold predictions were averaged across the repetitions. Random seeds were fixed for every fold assignment and solver, so all results reproduce exactly. Models for legs uncrossed and feet flat were trained with left-right mirrored copies of each training recording, so that they tolerate a camera placed on the patient's other side. Further augmentation was prespecified only for a criterion that failed a robustness test, and none was adopted (Table A2). Each model produced a score between 0 and 1, converted to a verdict at a threshold of 0•5 fixed before evaluation. With balanced class weights, this threshold treats sensitivity and specificity as equally important. Because of this class weighting, the scores rank encounters but are not calibrated probabilities (calibration is reported below). Choices between feature representations were made by comparing cross-validated performance, so the estimates may be modestly optimistic. The main choices were repeated within the training folds (below). For deployment, each model was refitted on all participants.

Feature-specific models. For back supported, pose features were augmented with 768-dimensional embeddings from the pretrained DINOv2 ViT-B/14 model, averaged across uniformly sampled frames of the recording. 10 For no talking, we used TalkNet, a pretrained audio-visual active-speaker detection model, 11 without fine-tuning. The patient's face was detected and tracked with the TalkNet reference pipeline, the largest face track was taken as the patient's, and frame-level active-speaker scores were summarized as their maximum over the encounter. An encounter was classified as talking when this exceeded zero, TalkNet's native decision boundary. The maximum was chosen from three summaries after an initial run on 34 encounters that included all 14 then labeled as talking. Any talking during the reading violates the criterion, so the maximum over the reading is the natural summary. In the 401 analyzed encounters with a tracked face, 14 of them with talking, the maximum had an AUROC of 0•97 and, at the cutoff of zero, flagged 13 of the 14 with 28 false alerts among the 387 silent encounters. The 90th percentile had an AUROC of 0•92 and flagged eight of the 14 with four false alerts, and the mean had an AUROC of 0•79 and flagged no encounter.

The arm criterion.

The AHA recommendation that the measurement arm be supported at heart level comprises two conditions, that the arm rests on a supporting surface and that the cuff sits at heart level. Both rating panels scored these as separate items, and we defined the arm criterion as met only when both were satisfied, consistent with the guideline. The relevant arm is the one wearing the cuff, which varied between encounters. We therefore located the cuff automatically in each front-view video with an openvocabulary object detector (OWLv2), 12 applied to a crop of each arm from shoulder to wrist in three frames, and took the arm with the higher cuff score as the cuffed arm. The clinician ratings were not used.

In four encounters the detector did not find the cuff, and the left arm, the more common side, was assumed. This matched the staff record in all four. The detected side agreed with the staff record in 354 (90%) of the 392 encounters that had both a cuff detection and a staff record (Table A1). Pose landmarks of encounters with the cuff on the right arm were mirrored, so that the cuffed arm always occupied the same variables, and a single L1-regularized logistic-regression model was trained on the composite label using the mirrored two-dimensional and three-dimensional front-view variables.

5. Reference standard

Each encounter was independently scored for the five criteria by one reviewer from each of two threeclinician panels, working from the recorded video rather than by observation at the time of measurement. Review of the recording was chosen so that every reviewer and Coach-BP judged the same clip, and so that a second panel could rate it independently. The first expert panel comprised three clinician reviewers who were members of the study team, and the second expert panel comprised three clinician reviewers who had no involvement in the study. First-panel reviewers were a cardiologist specializing in prevention (JAL-M) and two cardiology nurse practitioners (JN and AJA). Second-panel reviewers were two nurse practitioners in adult cardiology and a clinical fellow in cardiology at the same institution, none of whom had any other part in the study. Within each panel, reviewers scored disjoint batches of encounters, so each encounter received one first-panel rating and one second-panel rating, except one analyzed encounter that the second expert panel did not rate because its front video did not load for the reviewer.

Comparisons with the second expert panel therefore include 407 encounters. Each reviewer viewed the front and side recordings with sound and recorded each criterion on a standard form. Because recordings were identified by reading number, reviewers were not masked to whether an encounter followed coaching. Second-panel reviewers did not see first-panel ratings, and no reviewer saw Coach-BP outputs.

The second expert panel's ratings were not used to train any model.

Two first-panel reviewers recorded only met criteria explicitly and left violated criteria blank. The third first-panel reviewer and all second-panel reviewers completed every cell explicitly. Blank cells were therefore interpreted as violations, and cells explicitly marked not available were excluded. The second expert panel confirmed 79-100% of the feet, back and legs violations recorded as blanks, but 8 of 16 and 28 of 49 of the arm violations recorded as blanks by the two first-panel reviewers who used that convention, against 16 of 17 recorded explicitly by the third. Agreement with the second expert panel does not depend on this interpretation. Coach-BP was evaluated separately against the two sets of expert ratings, without combining them, to assess whether performance was sensitive to the reference labels used for model development.

6. Statistical analysis and software

We estimated 95% CIs by percentile bootstrap with 2000 resamples of participants rather than encounters, so that encounters from the same participant were resampled together. 13 Means across the five criteria took back supported from the side camera and were bootstrapped jointly, resampling participants once for all five criteria, and differences between two quantities measured on the same encounters were bootstrapped as paired differences. Agreement between Coach-BP and each clinician label set, and between the two sets of expert ratings, was quantified as balanced agreement, that is, balanced accuracy with the second expert ratings as the reference for every comparison with them, and as Cohen's κ. The comparison between panels was used as a benchmark for agreement between two independently generated clinician reference sets, rather than as a formal estimate of within-panel inter-rater reliability. Proportions are reported with Wilson score intervals, and sensitivity and specificity with exact Clopper-Pearson intervals. Tests were two-sided. Per-criterion and secondary comparisons were not adjusted for multiple testing and are interpreted descriptively. Missing landmark summaries were set to zero. Analyses used Python 3.11 with scikit-learn 1.8, NumPy 2.4, SciPy 1.17 and pandas 3.0, MediaPipe BlazePose 0.10.5, the DINOv2 ViT-B/14 checkpoint, the OWLv2 base patch-16 ensemble checkpoint and TalkNet weights pretrained on TalkSet with faces detected by S3FD, run with PyTorch 2.5 and Transformers 5.9. No performance target was prespecified, and accuracy is reported descriptively with 95% CIs. The analyses by reading, by agreement between the panels, by cuff side, and by camera tilt were exploratory. The out-of-fold score and verdict of every encounter for every criterion are stored with both expert panels' ratings and are part of the data available on request.

8. Model interpretation

The models' inputs are landmark variables summarizing each recording, for example the mean sideways image position of a heel over all frames, its SD or its mean detection confidence. There were 363 for the arm criterion, 165 for legs uncrossed and 364 for feet flat. Several landmark variables often measure the same thing, so for interpretation they were combined into features. For each criterion, the Spearman correlation between every pair of landmark variables was computed across all encounters, and the variables were grouped by agglomerative hierarchical clustering with complete linkage on the distance 1 -|ρ|, cutting the tree at 0•3 so that every pair of variables within a feature had |ρ| of at least 0•7 (Figure S2). The outcome was not used. Cutting the tree at |ρ| of 0•65 or 0•75 instead gave the same 14 displayed features, with selection frequencies within 5 percentage points. Stability was assessed in 2000 refits of the L1 model, each on a random half of the participants. 14 A feature was counted as selected in a refit if any of its landmark variables was retained, and features selected in at least 70% of refits that also had a standalone association with the criterion (single-feature AUROC of at least 0•55) are shown. Each feature's combined measurement is the mean of its standardized landmark variables, with signs aligned. Its single-feature AUROC, the AUROC of that measurement used alone, is reported with a 95% CI from 2000 participant-bootstrap resamples. For back supported, whose scene features have no anatomical meaning, we instead edited the image and recomputed only the scene features, holding the pose features at their observed values. We grayed out either everything outside a box enclosing the patient and adjoining backrest or the box itself, setting the pixels to the mean color of the images DINOv2 was trained on, and compared each edit with graying out the same area at a random position. This was done for the side-view images and, separately, for the front-view images, with the model fitted in each fold on the unedited images of both views. The grouping into features was used only for interpretation. The models were trained and evaluated on the individual landmark variables, and none of these analyses affected model training or prediction.

Most features of the landmark models corresponded to what a clinician would assess (Figure 4A-C). For the arm criterion, the feature selected in every refit was the height of the cuffed hand above the hip midpoint (f1, single-feature AUROC 0•81 [95% CI 0•74-0•87]). It was followed by how far the cuffed hand sat in front of the hip midpoint (f3, selected in 89% of refits, AUROC 0•56 [0•49-0•64]) and how far the elbow and hand of the cuffed arm sat out to the side (f4, 87%, 0•74, and f5, 72%, 0•75), the pattern of an arm resting on a surface rather than hanging or lying in the lap. One arm feature, the detection confidence for the heel opposite the cuff (f2, 91%, 0•61), describes how clearly the lower body was seen rather than the arm itself. For legs uncrossed, the features were the sideways positions of the near and far foot and knee relative to the shoulder midpoint (f6, 97%, 0•63, and f7, 92%, 0•82), the height of the feet (f8, 89%, 0•73), and stillness of the near heel (f9, 79%, 0•93 [0•89-0•96]). For feet flat, they were the sideways position of the far foot (f10, 99%, 0•84 [0•79-0•89]), how far the near foot and knee sat in front of the hip midpoint (f11, 98%, 0•76), the output of the legs model (f12, 91%, 0•83), the height of the near foot (f13, 88%, 0•80), and the height of both feet (f14, 72%, 0•73). Near and far refer to the front camera. Because the legs and feet models were trained with mirrored copies, the same cues apply whichever side the camera is on. The scene evidence therefore lies in the patient and chair rather than elsewhere in the room. These analyses used the 288 encounters, 31 with a violation, in which the pose model located the patient in every sampled side-view frame, as was needed to place the boxes. From the front camera the pattern was the same, in the 390 encounters, 45 with a violation, in which the patient was located in every sampled front-view frame. The values are in Figure 4E, F. 9. Robustness to recording conditions

Robustness was tested without retraining. In each fold the model was fitted on unperturbed training participants, held fixed and applied to the held-out participant's perturbed recording. We simulated a sideways shift of the frame of up to 10% of its width (about 15 cm at the patient), a zoom that changed apparent body size from 85% to 115%, and rotation of the frame by up to 10°. For the arm, legs and feet models the held-out participant's landmark coordinates were transformed. For back supported and no talking, whose inputs are computed from the image itself, new inputs were generated by cropping, scaling and rotating real pixels of the original frames, with a central 80% crop as reference (75% for rotation) so that no content was invented at the image border. For back supported the fold model was fitted on the central 80% crops of the training participants' recordings from both cameras, and it scored the held-out participant's reframed recordings from each camera. For no talking, the full TalkNet pipeline, including face detection and tracking, was rerun on every encounter at every framing. Changes in camera viewpoint were simulated by rotating each held-out recording's three-dimensional pose about the hips, round the patient (pan) or up and down (tilt), by up to 30° from the recording's own camera angle, and projecting the change into the image coordinates. Within-recording movement variables were not rotated and pose was not re-estimated. Real and simulated viewpoints were grouped by absolute camera angle, measured as in Section 2. Robustness to recording quality was tested in the same way after the video was degraded (low light with sensor noise, reduced resolution, heavy compression or motion blur, each at three severities) or shortened to a random 75%, 50% or 25% of the recording, the mild, moderate and severe levels of recording length. A model was prespecified to fail a condition if AUROC fell by more than 0•03 or balanced accuracy by more than 0•05 with the paired bootstrap interval excluding zero. Augmentation was to be considered only for failures at mild or moderate severity, and failures only at the severe level were to be handled by recording-quality checks.

11. One back supported model for both cameras

The study placed a second camera beside the patient because back support was expected to need a side view. To test this, one back supported model was trained on the side and front recordings of every training encounter together, with the same inputs from either view and nothing telling it which camera a recording came from. Each held-out participant's side and front recordings were scored separately at the threshold of 0•5. The side test covers 406 encounters, 47 rated not supported, and is the main result. The front test covers 408, 48 rated not supported, and shows the same model from another camera position (Figure 3H).

From the side camera the model flagged 43 of the 47 encounters rated not supported, with 33 false alerts among the 359 rated supported. From the front camera it flagged 41 of 48, with 36 false alerts among 360 (Table A6). AUROC and balanced accuracy are in Table A6 and The expert ratings used in development rated back straight separately from back supported. From either camera the verdicts followed the rating of support much more closely than the rating of straightness (AUROC from the front camera 0•92 for not supported against 0•66 for not straight, difference 0•26 [95% CI 0•19-0•32]).

For comparison, two separate models were fitted with the same inputs on one view each (Table A6). On its own view each did about as well as the one model, and every paired difference included zero. The front-only model had the highest AUROC, 0•94 (0•91-0•97), but its scores were concentrated near 1, and at 0•5 it flagged 35 of the 48 encounters rated not supported. Neither carried over to the other view, with balanced accuracy 0•71 and 0•51.

Because the scores of the front-only model lay far from 0•5, a threshold set within each training fold was examined for every trained model, with a rule fixed before it was applied. In each fold the threshold maximized balanced accuracy on the training participants' inner cross-validation scores and was applied to the held-out participant. For every model of the paper it stayed close to 0•5 (medians 0•47-0•55) and changed balanced accuracy by -0•019 to 0•009, with every interval including zero (Table A7). Only for the front-only model did it move far from 0•5, to a median of 0•77.

With the two-dimensional positions of the side recordings left as fractions of the frame, the AUROC, balanced accuracy, and agreement with the second expert ratings of the one model differed from those of the model as specified by at most 0•006 from either camera.

The robustness analyses of Section 9 were run for the one model from each camera. From the side camera no framing failed the prespecified rule. These results show similar accuracy from either camera with one model, not that the cameras are equivalent. With 47 or 48 encounters rated not supported, the 95% CI for the difference in balanced accuracy reached 0•10 in favor of the side camera. The front camera was more sensitive to cropping and to rotation, so the result may depend more on how the camera is placed. The model was developed and tested at one site with one placement of each camera, and the choice to train it on both views followed a rule written before its results were seen, applied to cross-validated performance in this cohort (Section 4). The front camera was tested only at the position used in the study, and other positions could not be simulated for a model whose inputs include the image itself. It needs confirmation in recordings made elsewhere before a single camera is relied on for all five criteria.
