To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 2 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. 1 Introduction

The development of accurate and generalizable medical AI requires large, heterogeneous training datasets to capture variation in disease phenotypes, yet such data are often scarce. In medical imaging, data availability is constrained by cost, privacy regulations, patient confidentiality and radiation exposure. Although AIgenerated synthetic data has been proposed as a solution, it can introduce artifacts that degrade model performance, including hallucinations observed in both text-and imagebased generative models [18, 38]. When exposed to data outside their training distribution, generative models may collapse or produce low-quality samples, particularly in underrepresented diseases or rare disease manifestations [20]. In addition, generative models can amplify biases present in the training data. Common evaluation metrics such as Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) and Kernel Inception Distance (KID) quantify distributional similarity between real and synthetic data [13, 26], but are insufficient in medical settings where anatomical and physiological plausibility are critical. More fundamentally, a generative model can only reproduce acquisitions represented in its training data. Acquisitions that clinical practice rarely or never performs such as dynamic expiratory CT (4D CT), largely confined to radiotherapy planning, or tomosynthesis across a range of compressions, which no clinical system acquires, therefore remain beyond reach.

As an alternative, we propose knowledge-based synthetic data generation, leveraging mechanistic models of human physiology to generate such acquisitions from a single routine scan, without any training data. Mechanistic knowledge can take many forms, including biological principles, mathematical models of tissue mechanics, organ motion, or treatment response [28, 29, 35]. Ordinary and partial differential equation (ODE/PDE) frameworks are widely available and used to model such processes and provide a principled means of constraining synthetic variation. Because distributional similarity metrics alone incompletely capture the relevance of synthetic data in biomedical applications, we evaluate realism through blinded clinician reader studies and assess downstream utility via segmentation performance.

We study three detection and segmentation tasks across distinct imaging modalities: breast cancer in 3D mammography, lung cancer in chest computed tomography (CT), and calcified carotid plaque in ultrasound. For each modality, we construct patientspecific physics-based models that simulate the underlying tissue deformation, generating acquisitions the original study did not capture. Finally, we validate realism with an expert readers study and in the instance of 4D CT, by comparing generated respiratory motion to real 4D CT.

2 Background and Methods

2.1 Scenario 1: 3D mammography and variation in breast compression

Breast cancer is the most common non-skin cancer among women [31]. In the US, screening and diagnosis are primarily performed using digital breast tomosynthesis (DBT), where the breast is compressed between a paddle and detector (below referred to as plates) while low-dose exposures are acquired over an x-ray tube arc and reconstructed into ∼1 mm slices (Figure 1A). Compression varies across patients and exams and contributes to study-to-study image variation, but it also reduces tissue overlap and improves image clarity and resolution, directly affecting lesion visualization. Here, we simulate clinically realistic variation in breast compression and the resulting change in image appearance to improve AI-based breast cancer detection and segmentation. An animation (breast.gif) of the simulated breast compressions is shown in the supplemental material.

2.1.1 Dataset and Gold Standard Segmentation

We used the BCS-DBT dataset [6, 7], a publicly available digital breast tomosynthesis collection hosted on TCIA. From the full dataset (22,032 volumes from 5,060 patients), we selected biopsy-proven malignant cases, yielding 222 images from 158 patients (Table 4). Bounding box annotations provided with the dataset were used to guide manual lesion segmentation. We generated the first gold-standard tomosynthesis segmentation dataset by producing physician-reviewed manual lesion segmentations together with tissue segmentations of glandular tissue, fat, and pectoralis muscle (Figure 1B). Tissue labels were created by manually annotating a subset of images, training a 2.5D DeepLabV3 model to segment the remaining cases and subsequent manual corrections using ITK-SNAP [42].

When the deformed surface penetrates a plate, a penalty traction is applied along the plate normal: 𝒕 pen = 𝑘 pen 𝜒(𝑥) 𝛿(𝑥) 𝒏 M, where 𝑥 = 𝑋 + 𝑢 is the deformed position, 𝜒(𝑥) is an indicator active only when penetration occurs, 𝛿(𝑥) is the signed penetration depth, and 𝒏 M is the plate normal.

Following our implementation, the penalty stiffness was scaled with mesh resolution via 𝑘 pen = 𝐶 pen 𝐸 eff /ℎ, where ℎ is a characteristic element size and 𝐸 eff is an effective Young's modulus derived from (𝜆, 𝜇).

2.1.3 Simulation and image generation

The simulation was implemented in FEniCSx [4] using a nonlinear finite element formulation of 3D hyperelasticity. We used continuous first-order (P1) Lagrange elements to discretize 𝑢 on the 3D mesh (Figure 1C). Compression was applied by incrementally increasing the symmetric plate displacement over multiple load steps; at each step, the nonlinear variational problem was solved with Newton's method using PETSc linear solvers, and contact forces were incorporated via the penalty traction described above.

The resulting displacement field 𝑢(𝑥) was exported and used to warp the original tomosynthesis volume using scikit-image [36]. For each voxel position 𝑥, the warped location was computed as 𝑥 % = 𝑥 + 𝑢(𝑥), and the intensity at 𝑥 % was interpolated from the original image using linear interpolation. Areas outside the mesh were removed to prevent artifacts. This procedure yielded physically plausible synthetic views under increased compression (see supplemental breast.gif), which were used as synthetic data in the reader study and AI segmentation tasks.

2.2 Scenario 2: Lung CT with variable respiration

Lung cancer is the second most common cancer and the leading cause of cancer death in the United States [32]. Current clinical practice relies heavily on computed tomography (CT) of the chest, a 3D imaging modality capturing the lung and surrounding tissue (Figure 1D) for detection, diagnosis and monitoring of lung lesions. During imaging, patients are asked to hold their breath resulting in capture of lung volumes at inspiration. Currently, lesions are almost exclusively identified and measured manually, which is time-consuming and labor-intensive. Accordingly, there are numerous efforts for automated segmentation methods to aid medical professionals in identifying and measuring lesions [40]. However, limited labeled data and high appearance variability remain key challenges. Here, we simulate clinically realistic variation in lung inspiration and the resulting change in image appearance to improve AI-based lung cancer detection and segmentation.

2.2.1 Dataset and Segmentations

We used the LOTUS chest CT dataset, derived from NSCLC-Radiomics with additional annotations from the MAASTRO clinic [1]. The dataset contains one CT volume per patient (300 publicly available) with expert-delineated gross tumor volume and body contours (Figure 1E). Lung and bone segmentations were obtained using TotalSegmentator [39], selecting labels corresponding to lung parenchyma and bony structures. Provided tumor and body contours were converted into voxel-wise segmentations using standard rasterization tools [5, 21]. To ensure a watertight simulation domain required by the physics model, internal cavities were filled and all classes were merged into a single composite segmentation.

Respiratory motion was simulated using a patient-specific 3D finite-element model with subdomains for body, ribs, lung, and tumor. The resulting displacement field was exported (with time indexing) and used to warp the original CT volume and all corresponding segmentation masks, yielding label-consistent augmented data.

2.3 Scenario 3: Ultrasound and arterial wall distension of the carotid

Carotid ultrasound is a widely used, noninvasive modality for assessing atherosclerotic plaque burden and estimating stroke risk by measuring luminal (vessel) narrowing in the common and internal carotid arteries. During the cardiac cycle, pulsatile blood flow induces cyclic arterial wall motion that alters vessel geometry, lumen diameter, and plaque appearance; a still B-mode image captures the vessel at a single, arbitrary point in that cycle. Here, we simulate clinically realistic variation in arterial wall distension and the resulting change in image appearance to improve AI-based plaque detection and segmentation.

2.3.1 Dataset and Gold standard Segmentations

The Carotid Ultrasound Boundary Study (CUBS) dataset [23], accessible via kaggle, is a collection of 500 B-mode ultrasound images of the common carotid artery (Figure 1G). We excluded 100 synthetically generated images, due to unrealistic appearances. A radiologist manually segmented each image [37] to denote the lumen, vessel wall, and plaque if present as shown in Figure 1H.

2.4 Training AI segmentation models

To evaluate the impact of physics-based synthetic data, we trained segmentation models with and without it across all modalities. For lung CT, two synthetic acquisitions were generated by increasing peak exhalation pressure; for breast tomosynthesis, four compression levels were simulated by decreasing plate distance (2.5, 5, 7.5, and 10 mm); and for carotid ultrasound, three cardiac-phase samples were selected at peak systole, diastole, and the dicrotic notch, together with an additional constant-flow profile of 85 cm/s. Generated 3D volumes were post-processed with modality-specific windowing, and 2D slices containing the largest lesion extent were extracted together with the corresponding segmentation masks.

Data were split into five cross-validation folds with an additional held-out test set per dataset, ensuring that all synthetic images remained within the same fold as their source image to prevent leakage. Synthetic images were excluded from test sets and from baseline training runs. Images were resized to fixed input resolutions while preserving aspect ratios (breast: 650 × 800, lung: 500 × 400, carotid: 800 × 600).

All models were trained for 200 epochs using a batch size of 32 and the Adam optimizer with a combined cross-entropy and Dice loss. A base learning rate of 10 +-with 5-epoch linear warm-up and cosine decay to 10 +. was used; class imbalance was addressed via dataset-derived log-scaled class weights. For each fold, the best checkpoint was selected based on validation performance and evaluated on the test set, yielding five scores per experiment.

For lung CT, we additionally evaluated generalization across architectures, including DeepLabV3 with EfficientNet-B4, ResNet-50, and ConvNeXt backbones, as well as SegFormer. The effect of standard image-space transformations was assessed both independently and in combination with physics-based augmentation. All experiments were performed on NVIDIA RTX 6000 Pro GPUs.

3 Results

3.1 Generation of acquisitions absent from the original study

From a single routine scan, the framework produces acquisitions absent in the originating study. For lung CT, one breath-hold volume yields a complete respiratory cycle sampled at 24 phases per patient, finer than the ten phases of the gated 4D CT cohort used for validation and obtained without the additional exposure a gated acquisition requires. For breast tomosynthesis, one compressed volume yields the breast across a range of plate separations (2.5-10 mm); because compression is advanced incrementally over load steps, any intermediate state can be recovered, of which four were sampled for training. For carotid ultrasound, a single frame yields the vessel across the cardiac cycle. In each case the acquisition is derived from data already collected in routine care, requiring no repeat imaging and no additional radiation.

The two radiographic acquisitions differ in whether a reference standard exists.

Respiratory motion can be compared directly against gated 4D CT, which is acquired clinically, if rarely, and we report that comparison in Section 3.4. Tomosynthesis at multiple compressions has no such counterpart: no clinical system images a patient at more than one compression, because each state would require its own exposure. For that acquisition no reference data exist against which to validate, and perceptual judgment by expert readers is the available standard. Generation is inexpensive in either case, requiring ∼8 min per 3D volume on CPU and no specialized hardware (Section 6.2.5).

3.2 Anatomical plausibility of generated images

We assess the realism of the proposed physics-based synthetic data through qualitative visualization using animations (breast.gif and lung.gif in supplemental material) and deformed images.

Figure 2 presents examples of physics-based synthetic data for breast tomosynthesis, lung CT, and carotid ultrasound. Across all modalities, the induced deformations are spatially coherent and constrained by the underlying physiological process modeled. In the breast scenario, simulated increases in compression produce heterogeneous tissue expansion and lesion displacement while preserving structural constraints, including limited motion of the stiffer pectoralis muscle in contrast to the more compliant fatty and glandular tissue. In the lung scenario, respiratory motion induces lesion displacement within the lung volume together with physiologically plausible downward motion of the diaphragm, while surrounding bones and body wall remain comparatively stationary. In the carotid artery scenario, the cardiac cycle produces outward movement of the arterial wall, together with localized plaque displacement at peak systole.

Across all cases, the generated deformations selectively modify target anatomical structures while preserving global anatomical consistency, providing a qualitative demonstration that the physics-based models generate realistic, label-consistent deformations suitable for data augmentation. This contrasts with other augmentation strategies, which may introduce arbitrary warping (see Figure 4). Because physicsbased deformations arise from models that closely mimic the underlying biology, they preserve anatomical relationships while introducing physiologically meaningful variation.

3.3 Reader Study

To assess the perceptual realism of the proposed physics-based synthetic data, we conducted a blinded reader study in which expert physicians were asked to classify images as real or synthetic. Readers routinely interpret the corresponding imaging modalities as part of their clinical workflow. Performance was summarized using sensitivity, specificity, and estimated AUC, noting that these metrics reflect qualitative perceptual judgments rather than objective diagnostic ground truth. For breast DBT, synthetic images with visible edge artifacts at the chest boundary were excluded; for carotid ultrasound, a warped on-image scale bar was cropped out before review.

Across all modalities, reader performance was near chance. Synthetic images were correctly identified in 60% of mammograms, 52% of chest CT images, and 42% of carotid ultrasound images (Table 1). Corresponding sensitivity and specificity values were balanced and low across modalities, and inter-reader agreement was poor to slight, with Fleiss' 𝜅 values close to zero (Table 6). Together, these results indicate that expert readers were generally unable to reliably distinguish physics-based synthetic images from real clinical images.

Performance for lung and carotid was essentially at chance. Performance above chance for mammography (AUC = 0.61) was driven primarily by a single reader (Reviewer 3), who achieved an estimated AUC of 0.83 as seen in Table 7. Upon inquiry, this reader identified a localized artifact at the image border (20 × 20 pixels) of full-resolution mammograms (1889 × 2457 pixels) that did not affect the realism of the overall breast tissue or areas used for diagnostic purposes. When excluded, mammography performance returned to near chance (estimated AUC = 0.53). Overall, the combination of near-chance classification performance and low inter-reader agreement supports the conclusion that physics-based synthetic images are perceptually indistinguishable from real images for expert readers.

3.4 Validation of Simulated Respiratory Motion

We next evaluate the ability of the proposed simulation to capture physiologically realistic motion, using lung respiration as a case example due to the availability of a separate 4D chest CT cohort.

Simulated lung motion was compared against ground-truth masks from gated 4D chest CT scans of twenty patients from TCIA, spanning ten increments of an individual breathing cycle (see lung.gif animation in supplemental material). Starting from the CT acquired at the initial respiratory phase, the simulation predicted the remaining nine increments, and the resulting lung masks were evaluated against ground truth. For each patient, intrathoracic pressure was optimized to match the observed lung-volume trajectory varying between 2.4-4.5 kPa. This flexibility indicates that the simulation can be readily adapted to generate both inter-and intra-patient variability, enabling multiple physiologically plausible augmentations rather than a single deterministic deformation, which can be leveraged for more robust AI training. Across all patients, simulated lung-volume trajectories closely matched the ground truth, with Dice scores of at least 0.90 (Table 8). Spatial discrepancies were further quantified using the average symmetric surface distance (ASSD) and Hausdorff distance (HD). Across all respiratory increments and patients, ASSD remained below 3 mm, with an average HD of 22.8 mm. The largest deviations occurred at peak expiration and are illustrated for Patient 1 in Figure 3. In these cases, false positives were primarily located along the outer lung boundary, while false negatives clustered in between the lung lobes -the mediastinum. These patterns are consistent with the model's simplifying assumption of fully rigid bone structures, which reduces lung contraction especially at peak expiration. Despite this, mean surface error remained below 3 mm even in the least optimal case.

Although no direct benchmarks exist for this public dataset, our approach achieved competitive performance relative to prior work evaluated on other datasets, including the lowest mean Hausdorff distance (22.80 ± 8.44 mm) among the compared methods (Table 10). While deformable registration approaches [12, 27] achieve higher Dice scores and lower ASSD, they require access to full 4D CT sequences and therefore cannot generate synthetic motion from a scan acquired at a single time point. In contrast, our method relies on a single, clinically common CT acquisition, enabling scalable augmentation for downstream training. Taking into consideration differences in datasets and evaluation protocols, our approach achieves higher Dice scores than the physics-based lung biomechanical simulation of [16] (0.94 ± 0.02 vs. 0.85 ± 0.06). Performance even remained high in an ablation study, simulating each patient without volume optimization (pressure fixed to 2.4 kPa) and no global alignment, see Table 9 (mean Dice: 0.94, ASSD: 1.58, HD: 22.32)

Overall, these results demonstrate that physics-based simulations can generate accurate and physiologically plausible respiratory motion from a single CT acquired at a single time point, supporting their use as a practical and scalable augmentation strategy.

3.5 AI Segmentation Improvements

We first evaluate physics-based augmentation against standard image-space transformations (color jitter, rotation, flipping) and advanced alternatives (elastic deformation, CutMix [41]) using a fixed EfficientNet-B4 DeepLabV3 architecture across breast, lung, and carotid datasets. Each augmentation is tested alone and in combination with physics-based augmentation (Table 2).

Across all three datasets, physics-based augmentation consistently improves segmentation performance relative to no augmentation. Performance gains are most pronounced for breast tomosynthesis and lung CT, with Dice score improvements of 6.6% (p < 0.05) and up to 10.1% (p < 0.05), respectively. For carotid ultrasound, physics-based augmentation yields a consistent but non-significant improvement of 4.5% (p > 0.05), likely reflecting the limited number of plaque-positive samples (38 cases) available for synthetic data generation. Despite this, the overall trend mirrors that observed for breast and lung imaging.

Physics-based augmentation consistently outperforms standard image-space transformations when applied in isolation, while also complementing them when used in combination. For lung CT, physics-based augmentation alone (Dice 0.616) outperforms jitter (0.540), rotation (0.595), and flipping (0.555), a pattern that similarly holds for breast tomosynthesis, where physics-based augmentation alone exceeds all standard transformations. Notably, for both lung CT and breast tomosynthesis, the highest performance is achieved when combining physics-based augmentation with rotation (Dice 0.671 and 0.707, respectively), indicating that physics-based and standard augmentations provide complementary sources of variation rather than redundant transformations. For carotid ultrasound, although differences are not statistically significant, the combination of physics-based augmentation and rotation likewise yields the highest observed performance among the evaluated transformations, underscoring that physics-based augmentation can be effectively integrated with existing augmentation strategies to further improve model performance.

The pattern holds for more advanced image-space transformations. Elastic deformation applies a random smooth displacement field to the image, while CutMix replaces a random rectangular region with a patch from another training image [41]. For both breast and lung, neither elastic deformation nor CutMix outperforms physics-based augmentation alone. In all cases, combining these transformations with physics-based augmentation further improves performance.

4 Discussion

Across three modalities, mechanistic simulation produces imaging acquisitions rare or absent in standard clinical practice. Since generation follows from a constrained physical model rather than learned image synthesis, the method requires no training data, no paired examples, and no generative model, bringing patient-specific acquisitions within reach. The simulated respiratory cycle reproduced gated 4D CT closely, and the compression states were indistinguishable from real images to expert readers.

Quantitatively, physics-based augmentation consistently improved Dice scores relative to no augmentation and increased performance when combined with other transformations. The strongest performance was achieved when physics-based augmentation was paired with simple transformations such as flipping, outperforming other more complex deformation techniques such as CutMix or Elastic Deformation. Furthermore, these gains generalized across multiple backbones and architectures, indicating that improvements are not tied to a specific network family.

Physics-based synthetic data generation provides a practical mechanism to enrich datasets with controlled, mechanistically grounded variation that is difficult to acquire at scale in clinical practice. Computationally, the pipeline runs on standard CPU hardware, with a single simulation supplying an entire cycle at ∼20 s per generated image (Section 6.2.5). Varying simulation parameters within physiological bounds enables systematic exploration of rare anatomical configurations and acquisition conditions, selected deliberately rather than encountered by chance.

This approach may also be valuable for rare diseases, where few available cases limit the development of imaging AI. By simulating physiologic and acquisition variability around each observed case, mechanistic augmentation could expand the range of conditions under which a model encounters an uncommon pathology, extending usage of a scarce annotated data. Although these images do not replace independent patients or introduce new disease phenotypes, they may improve robustness to changes in positioning, compression, or motion that are poorly represented in small datasets. Evaluating this potential in rare disease cohorts is an important direction for future work.

Several limitations warrant consideration. Physics-based synthetic data generation deforms existing anatomy rather than generating entirely novel structures, which may limit out-of-distribution generalization. The biomechanical models also rely on simplifying assumptions, such as rigid ribs, that introduce potential inaccuracies. Material properties were taken from published values rather than measured per patient. Further, each modality was only evaluated on a single public dataset.

Overall, mechanistic simulation offers a route to imaging data that cannot otherwise be obtained, producing physiologically accurate imaging acquisitions extrapolated from standard imaging, with its effectiveness holding across modalities, architectures, and transformations.

5 Conclusion

We demonstrate that mechanistic simulation generates imaging acquisitions rare in standard clinical practice-a full respiratory cycle from a single breath-hold CT, a range of breast compressions from a single tomosynthesis volume, arterial wall distension across the cardiac cycle from a single carotid ultrasound frame -without training data, paired examples, or additional patient exposure. Where a reference standard exists, the generated motion matches real gated 4D CT; where none exists, expert readers cannot distinguish the result from real images. Training on these acquisitions improves lesion detection and segmentation across modalities, indicating that physiologically constrained synthesis supplies variation that neither standard transformations nor generative models provide.

6.2.4 p-value multiple hypothesis correction

In order to view the effect of physics-based augmentation, we compared physics-based augmentation training against their matched non-physics counterparts within the same augmentation setting. For each matched comparison, we used a two-sided Welch's ttest which were applied to their respective dice metrics. Since we conduct multiple hypothesis tests for the dice metric, we controlled for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure. FDR correction was applied only on a per dataset and per metric basis, so the adjustment for tumor dice in the breast dataset depends only on the set of tumor-dice comparisons within that dataset and vice versa for the other datasets.

6.2.5 Run time analysis

For the lung dataset, the full generation pipeline was executed on CPU (AMD Threadripper 7965WX, 24 cores). Per case, segmentation took ∼1 min using TotalSegmentator, mesh generation ∼2 min, simulation ∼2 min, and applying the deformation field ∼3 min, totaling ∼8 min per 3D volume. For 292 cases, this corresponds to approximately 1.5 days of wall-clock time without specialized hardware such as GPUs. Critically, a single simulation produces multiple augmented samples (e.g., 24 respiratory time steps per breathing cycle), amortizing the per-sample cost and enabling diverse augmentations without repeated simulation.
