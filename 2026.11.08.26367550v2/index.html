Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

p>0.05 A non-significant test (p>0.05) was interpreted as consistent with the assumption; otherwise, the corresponding ATT was not interpretable as a causal effect.
p<0.05 In the primary IPCW analysis, the pre-test detected departures from parallel trends for current tobacco use and LDL cholesterol in the adjusted model and for systolic blood pressure in both models (p<0.05; Supplementary Table S7), whose ATTs were therefore not interpreted causally, but not for the remaining outcomes, including nightly sleep duration.
p<0.05 † Parallel-trends assumption not supported (pre-test p<0.05; Supplementary Table S7); the ATT is not interpretable as a causal effect.

The APA manual states: Report exact p values (e.g., p = .031) to two or three decimal places. However, report p values less than .001 as p < .001. However, 2 decimals is too imprecise for many use-cases (e.g., a p value meta-analysis), so report p values with three digits.

American Psychological Association (2020). Publication manual of the American Psychological Association, 7 edition. American Psychological Association.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 1 non-significant p value that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Text Sentence
p>0.05 A non-significant test (p>0.05) was interpreted as consistent with the assumption; otherwise, the corresponding ATT was not interpretable as a causal effect.

For metascientific articles demonstrating the rate of misinterpretations of non-significant results is high, see:

Aczel B, Palfi B, Szollosi A, Kovacs M, Szaszi B, Szecsi P, Zrubka M, Gronau Q, van den Bergh D, Wagenmakers E (2018). “Quantifying Support for the Null Hypothesis in Psychology: An Empirical Investigation.” Advances in Methods and Practices in Psychological Science, 1(3), 357–366. doi:10.1177/2515245918773742.

Murphy S, Merz R, Reimann L, Fernández A (2025). “Nonsignificance misinterpreted as an effect’s absence in psychology: Prevalence and temporal analyses.” Royal Society Open Science, 12(3), 242167. doi:10.1098/rsos.242167.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. Abstract

Gentrification is a global, accelerating urban process involving complex socio-spatial transformations.

Although increasingly recognised as a determinant of urban health, quantitative evidence on its population health impacts remains scarce and inconsistent, limiting evidence-informed urban policymaking, especially in rapidly gentrifying contexts. Thus, we assessed the health impact of neighbourhood-level gentrification among adults in Porto (Northern Portugal), a hotspot of transnational gentrification. A quasi-experimental study was conducted using data from the EPIPorto population-based cohort (1999-2022), with 2,485 participants at baseline. A validated, context-sensitive 16-variable index of transnational gentrification, derived from 2011 and 2021 census and tourism-accommodation data through Principal Component Analysis, enabled neighbourhood classification as gentrifying or non-gentrifying. Difference-in-differences models estimated the effect of living in gentrifying neighbourhoods on ten cardiometabolic and behavioural outcomes. Inverse probability of censoring weighting was used to account for loss to follow-up and missing data. At baseline, 26.2% of participants resided in gentrifying neighbourhoods. Residence in a gentrifying neighbourhood was associated with a statistically significant reduction in nightly sleep duration (-0.346 h; 95% CI -0.650, -0.042), with statistically significant reductions in the lower-education and ≥65-year subgroups. No significant effects were observed for body mass index, overweight/obesity, total/HDL/LDL cholesterol, blood pressure, and tobacco or alcohol use. Transnational gentrification in Porto showed limited effects on most cardiometabolic outcomes but was associated with reduced sleep duration, suggesting that the health impacts of gentrification may initially emerge through psychosocial pathways before translating into measurable cardiometabolic changes.

1. Introduction

With nearly two-thirds of the world's population projected to live in urban areas by 2050 (United Nations Department of Economic and Social Affairs, 2018) and up to 80% by 2100 (Jones et al., 2020), cities have become central to contemporary public health (Kadakia & Galea, 2023). Growing attention has therefore been directed towards the urban environment as a determinant of population health and to the neighbourhood-level processes that reshape it (Kadakia & Galea, 2023; Ribeiro, 2018), among which gentrification has become prominent in urban health research (Cole, 2020).

Gentrification is a socio-spatial process in which reinvestment in the built environment to suit the preferences of higher-socioeconomic-status residents and users (Cole, 2020) is typically accompanied by the in-migration of more affluent newcomers, rising housing costs and, frequently, the displacement of long-standing residents (Sigler & Wachsmuth, 2020). By reshaping both the physical and social dimensions of the urban environment (Bryson, 2013) -including the food environment (Breyer & Voss-Andreae, 2013), green spaces (Jelks et al., 2021), social cohesion and safety (Schnake-Mahl, Jahn, et al., 2020) -gentrification can plausibly affect a broad range of health outcomes, acting as a double-edged process that may simultaneously create health-promoting opportunities and undermine health (Schnake-Mahl, Jahn, et al., 2020).

The local food environment and access to health-promoting resources illustrate these opposing influences. Gentrification may improve access to healthier food options and to renovated spaces for physical activity, such as public parks. However, as prices rise and the commercial offer changes, such amenities may become unaffordable to, or poorly aligned with the needs of, long-standing residents, who may also lose established local resources and services that supported their daily routines and healthrelated behaviours (Breyer & Voss-Andreae, 2013; Schroeder et al., 2019). Displaced residents may not benefit from these improvements at all. Conceptual frameworks on gentrification and urban health describe several pathways through which neighbourhood change may influence physical and mental health, and emphasise that these effects may be unevenly distributed across residents (Cole et al., 2021; Cole et al., 2024). Through psychosocial and neighbourhood-level mechanisms, gentrification may shape health-related behaviours such as alcohol and tobacco use (Pennay et al., 2014), impair sleep (Bozick et al., 2021) and affect downstream cardiometabolic outcomes such as body weight and blood pressure (Chen et al., 2023; Cole et al., 2021). Reflecting this complexity, previous studies have reported beneficial, null and harmful health effects of gentrification, and reviews have found no consensus as to whether its net influence on population health is positive or negative (Bhavsar et al., 2020; Delong, 2023; Schnake-Mahl, Jahn, et al., 2020; Tulier et al., 2019). In Los Angeles County, for example, gentrification was associated with modestly better hypertension control, more markedly among non-Hispanic white and older residents, but not with diabetes control (Chen et al., 2023).

In a large prospective cohort of Hispanic/Latino adults, neighbourhood socioeconomic change was not associated with the six-year incidence of metabolic syndrome (Pichardo et al., 2023), whereas adverse associations, including poorer self-rated health and greater psychological distress, have been consistently documented among historically marginalised residents (Anguelovski et al., 2021). This heterogeneity suggests that the health effects of gentrification depend on individual vulnerability, local context and the specific pathways involved. Reviews and conceptual work indicate that the benefits of neighbourhood upgrading accrue mainly to more privileged groups, while socioeconomically disadvantaged residents are harmed or fail to benefit (Cole et al., 2021; Cole et al., 2024). Age may further shape this vulnerability, as older adults tend to be more exposed to neighbourhood change owing to longer residential tenure, reduced mobility and more constrained financial resources (Torres, 2020).

Because beneficial and adverse influences may operate simultaneously within the same population, and even on the same outcome (Delong, 2023), they may offset one another when effects are estimated for residents as a whole, masking both the concentration of harm among the most disadvantaged and the resulting widening of health inequities (Cole et al., 2024).

Part of this inconsistency may also be methodological. Most studies have relied on cross-sectional designs that cannot distinguish the effects of gentrification from pre-existing neighbourhood differences (Delong, 2023), and the operationalisation of gentrification remains heterogeneous (Bhavsar et al., 2020).

Because gentrification unfolds gradually and its health effects accrue over time, longitudinal and quasiexperimental designs are better suited to disentangling its impact from underlying temporal trends and confounding (Delong, 2023; Schnake-Mahl, Sommers, et al., 2020; Tulier et al., 2019).

Although gentrification is a global phenomenon, its health impacts remain comparatively understudied in European settings (Delong, 2023; Schnake-Mahl, Sommers, et al., 2020). In the city of Porto, northern Portugal, gentrification has intensified rapidly over the past decade and a half, driven by three interlinked factors: the expansion of Francisco Sá Carneiro Airport from 2007 onwards, increased real-estate investment following the 2008 financial crisis, and growing national and international student inflows (Carvalho et al., 2019). Its transnational character, reflected both in the origin of capital and in the international composition of newcomers (Silva et al., 2023; Sousa & Barcón, 2021), makes Porto a particularly relevant European setting in which to study the health impacts of gentrification (Ribeiro et al., 2025).

In Porto, two recent qualitative studies have documented both perceived benefits (e.g., urban renewal, improved transport) and harms (e.g., insecurity, mental health concerns, reduced social ties) attributed to gentrification (Santos, Silva, et al., 2024; Silva et al., 2023), and a cross-sectional analysis reported an association between housing insecurity and several health and well-being outcomes in older adults (Santos, Henriques, et al., 2024). However, no quantitative study has yet assessed the health impacts of gentrification in Portugal. Against this background, we aimed to estimate the health effects of transnational gentrification in Porto, measured between 2011 and 2021, among adults in the EPIPorto cohort. Secondarily, we assessed whether these effects were modified by educational attainment and age.

2. Methods

>Methods

2.1 Study design and participants

A quasi-experimental longitudinal study was conducted within the EPIPorto population-based cohort, which enrolled 2,485 community-dwelling Portuguese adults aged 18 years or older living in Porto between 1999 and 2003 (baseline). Participants were identified through random-digit dialling of household landlines, which covered approximately 97% of households at the time, and one eligible noninstitutionalised adult was randomly selected from each household (Ramos et al., 2004). Residences were georeferenced using ArcGIS Online World Geocoding Service and Google Maps, following rigorous geoprivacy-preserving guidelines (Ribeiro, Dias, et al., 2022), enabling spatial linkage with census block group characteristics.

Follow-up assessments were conducted in five waves (Figure 1). As the current gentrification process in Porto was considered to have started around 2013, after the economic crisis (Carvalho et al., 2019), baseline and wave 1 formed the pre-exposure period and subsequent waves the post-exposure period.

Wave 2 was excluded from the primary analysis because it occurred early in the gentrification process, when cumulative exposure may not yet have been sufficiently established; its re-inclusion was examined in a sensitivity analysis (Supplementary Material Section E). Wave 4 was not included because none of the outcomes was assessed in that wave. All EPIPorto participants were eligible for inclusion. Participants who moved to a census block group with a different gentrification classification during the post-exposure period were excluded from the primary analysis; in a sensitivity analysis, they were retained and classified according to their census block group of residence at wave 3, the first post-exposure wave analysed (Supplementary Material Section G).

Participants were allocated to study groups (exposed vs unexposed) according to the gentrification status of their census block group of residence (gentrifying vs non-gentrifying), and repeated measurements of ten health outcomes obtained between 1999 and 2022 were used to compare outcome trajectories between groups. This study is reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guideline (von Elm et al., 2007).

2.2 Exposure assessment

Neighbourhood-level gentrification was operationalised through a multidimensional index derived by Principal Component Analysis (PCA), previously developed and validated for the Porto Metropolitan Area (Alves et al., 2026). The unit of analysis was the census block group, the second smallest geographical unit of the Portuguese census, which in 2021 had, on average, 1,203 residents and an area of 1.41 km² Fifteen candidate indicators, each expressed as its 2011 value and its absolute change between 2011 and 2021 (30 variables), were considered. Following exploratory analysis of correlations and communalities, 16 standardised (z-score) variables were retained, reflecting both a census block group's baseline status and its temporal trajectory and covering conceptual domains commonly identified in the literature (Bilal et al., 2020; Cocola-Gant et al., 2020): educational attainment, occupational class, employment status, housing (high rents), demographic composition (older adults, young adults and foreign residents from high-income countries) and tourism (short-term accommodation). The variables included in the index and the spatial distribution of gentrifying census block groups are shown in Supplementary Table S1 and Supplementary Figure S1, respectively.

The principal component selected as the basis of the gentrification index showed high positive loadings for increases in higher-education attainment, foreign residents from high-income countries, young adult population, housing units with high rents, and short-term rentals, alongside a negative loading for change in the proportion of residents aged ≥65 years. It was also effective in distinguishing census block groups with lower socioeconomic status at baseline (Alves et al., 2026), known to be more susceptible to gentrification (Freeman, 2005).

The transnational gentrification index was then standardised (mean=0, SD=1) and dichotomised using a threshold of >1 SD, approximately corresponding to the 90th percentile. Compared with alternative thresholds (+0.5 SD, +2 SD and the 75th, 90th and 95th percentiles), this cut-off showed a strong and consistent association with Property Tax Value, one of the external indicators used to validate the index, while classifying approximately 10% of census block groups as gentrifying (Alves et al., 2026), and most closely matched the spatial distribution of areas previously described as undergoing intense gentrification in Porto (Carvalho et al., 2019; Sousa & Barcón, 2021). Participants residing in census block groups above this threshold (gentrifying) were considered exposed, and those residing in the remaining census block groups (non-gentrifying) unexposed. In sensitivity analyses, gentrifying census block groups were alternatively defined as those scoring ≥1.5 SD above the mean or belonging to the upper quartile of the index (Supplementary Material Section F).

2.3 Outcomes

Guided by conceptual frameworks on gentrification and population health (Cole et al., 2021; Cole et al., 2024), ten cardiometabolic and behavioural outcomes with repeated, comparable measurements across follow-up waves were selected. Continuous outcomes were body mass index (BMI, kg/ m² ), total/HDL/LDL cholesterol (mg/dL), systolic and diastolic blood pressure (mmHg), and average nightly sleep duration (hours). Binary outcomes were overweight/obesity, defined as BMI ≥ 25 kg/ m² (World Health Organization, 2019), current tobacco use and current alcohol use (Supplementary Material, Section B). BMI, overweight/obesity, total, HDL and LDL cholesterol, and tobacco use were available at baseline and waves 1, 2, 3 and 5; systolic and diastolic blood pressure and alcohol use at baseline and waves 1, 2 and 3; and sleep duration at baseline and waves 1, 3 and 5.

2.4 Covariates

Based on previous conceptual frameworks linking gentrification and neighbourhood change to health (Bhavsar et al., 2020; Cole et al., 2021; Cole et al., 2024; Ribeiro, 2022), age, sex, educational attainment, employment status and marital status were treated as potential confounders and included in the adjusted models. All covariates entered the models with their pre-exposure values, so that adjustment was restricted to characteristics that could not have been affected by the exposure. Educational attainment was classified according to the International Standard Classification of Education (ISCED 2011) as low (ISCED 0-2), medium (ISCED 3-4) or high (ISCED 5-8); employment status as employed, unemployed or other (e.g., retired, homemaker or student); and marital status as with or without partner.

Covariate categories were harmonised across waves.

Age and educational attainment were additionally examined as potential effect modifiers in stratified analyses. These two roles are not mutually exclusive: adjusting for a variable removes the bias it would otherwise introduce into the overall estimate, whereas examining it as an effect modifier assesses whether the magnitude of that effect differs across its levels (Hernán & Robins, 2020).

Both effect modifiers were selected a priori, given previous evidence that the health impacts of gentrification are unevenly distributed, with benefits accruing mainly to more socioeconomically privileged residents and harms concentrating among socioeconomically disadvantaged and older ones (Cole et al., 2021; Cole et al., 2024). Educational attainment was used as an individual-level marker of socioeconomic position, since lower educational attainment may reflect fewer material and social resources with which to absorb negative consequences of gentrification. Older residents may be particularly susceptible to neighbourhood disruption, owing to longer residential attachment, greater reliance on local social networks and greater baseline health vulnerability (Smith et al., 2018), a pattern also described among older adults in Porto (Santos, Henriques, et al., 2024). Sex was retained as a covariate but was not examined as an effect modifier, as we identified no specific theoretical or empirical basis for expecting differential susceptibility to gentrification-related neighbourhood change by sex, and further stratification would have reduced precision.

2.5 Statistical analysis

Each outcome-specific analytical sample was described using means and standard deviations for continuous variables and counts and percentages for categorical variables. Participants living in gentrifying and non-gentrifying census block groups were compared at each wave using Welch's t-tests and Pearson's chi-squared tests, as were participants included in and excluded from the complete-case analyses, to assess potential selective loss to follow-up and missingness.

Effects were estimated using the Callaway and Sant'Anna difference-in-differences (DiD) estimator for multiple time periods, which provides the average treatment effect on the treated (ATT) at each postexposure wave and an aggregated ATT across post-exposure waves, used as the main effect estimate.

Compared with traditional two-way fixed-effects models, this estimator accommodates heterogeneous treatment effects, multiple pre-and post-treatment periods, and covariate-conditional assessment of the parallel-trends assumption (Callaway & Sant'Anna, 2021).

Inverse probability of censoring weighting (IPCW) was defined as the primary analytical approach, to reduce potential selection bias arising from loss to follow-up and missing outcome or covariate data, under the assumption that censoring was independent of the potential outcomes conditional on measured covariates. Pooled logistic regression was used to estimate the wave-specific probability of remaining under observation conditional on baseline covariates, and stabilised cumulative weights, truncated at the 1st and 99th percentiles, were incorporated into the DiD models (Cole & Hernán, 2008; Hernán & Robins, 2020; Robins & Finkelstein, 2000).

The parallel-trends assumption, which requires that, in the absence of exposure, outcome trajectories would have evolved similarly in the exposed and unexposed groups, was formally assessed over the preexposure period using Wald tests. A non-significant test (p>0.05) was interpreted as consistent with the assumption; otherwise, the corresponding ATT was not interpretable as a causal effect. Complete-case analyses were conducted as a secondary analysis to assess the sensitivity of the findings to the exclusion of participants with incomplete data.

The stability of the complete-case estimates was further assessed through cluster-bootstrap resampling by participant (2,000 and 5,000 replicates), with sign consistency defined as the proportion of replicates yielding an estimate in the same direction as the complete-case point estimate. For the subgroup analyses, participants were stratified by educational attainment (low vs. high) and age at the end of follow-up (<65 vs. ≥65 years), and the Callaway and Sant'Anna estimator was applied separately within each stratum. Given the modest and variable stratum sizes, these analyses were regarded as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than as formal tests of interaction. All analyses were performed in R

3.3 Impact of gentrification on health outcomes: exploratory subgroup analyses

In the adjusted IPCW model, residence in a gentrifying census block group was associated with shorter nightly sleep duration among participants aged ≥65 years (-0.433 h; 95% CI -0.859, -0.007) and those with lower educational attainment (-0.415 h; 95% CI -0.806, -0.024), but not among participants aged <65 years or those with higher educational attainment (Table 4). Nightly sleep duration was the only outcome with statistically significant estimates within any stratum, and the unadjusted IPCW model showed the same pattern (Supplementary Table S20). The parallel-trends assumption was supported for nightly sleep duration in all four strata but not for a few non-significant estimates of other outcomes, mostly systolic blood pressure among participants aged ≥65 years (Supplementary Tables S18-S19).

3.4 Sensitivity and robustness analyses

The results for nightly sleep duration were robust across alternative exposure definitions. Under both the stricter (≥1.5 SD above the mean) and the broader (upper quartile of the index) definitions, nightly sleep duration remained the only outcome significantly associated with gentrification in the adjusted and unadjusted IPCW analyses, with adjusted estimates similar to the primary analysis (-0.349 h; 95% CI -0.658, -0.040 and -0.350 h; 95% CI -0.697, -0.003, respectively), and the parallel-trends assumption was supported for all outcomes (Supplementary Tables S25-S29). The corresponding complete-case estimates for sleep duration were in the same direction but smaller and not statistically significant (-0.129 h; 95% CI -0.445, 0.187 and -0.158 h; 95% CI -0.431, 0.115, respectively; Supplementary Table S27).

Within subgroups, alternative exposure definitions generally reproduced the pattern observed in the primary subgroup analysis, although statistical significance varied across specifications (Supplementary Tables S38-S45). Under the upper-quartile definition, the parallel-trends assumption was supported for nightly sleep duration in all strata (Supplementary Tables S34-S37). The unadjusted IPCW estimates remained statistically significant among participants aged ≥65 years (-0.424 h; 95% CI -0.775, -0.073) and those with lower educational attainment (-0.418 h; 95% CI -0.819, -0.016). The corresponding adjusted estimates were similar in magnitude but no longer statistically significant (-0.382 h; 95% CI -0.820, 0.057 and -0.326 h; 95% CI -0.803, 0.152, respectively).

Under the ≥1.5 SD definition, IPCW estimates among participants aged ≥65 years were larger in both the unadjusted (-0.501 h; 95% CI -0.893, -0.109) and adjusted models (-0.543 h; 95% CI -1.048, -0.038).

However, the parallel-trends assumption was violated in this stratum under both models (p=0.013 and p=0.005, respectively; Supplementary Tables S32 and S33). In the corresponding complete-case analyses, estimates among participants aged ≥65 years were statistically significant and the paralleltrends assumption was supported in both the unadjusted (-0.401 h; 95% CI -0.775, -0.028) and adjusted models (-0.373 h; 95% CI -0.685, -0.061) (Supplementary Tables S30, S31, S38 and S39).

Isolated statistically significant estimates for other outcomes were observed only under the alternative exposure definitions. Under the ≥1.5 SD definition, these involved total and HDL cholesterol and systolic blood pressure among participants with lower educational attainment or aged ≥65 years, and total and LDL cholesterol and diastolic blood pressure among participants aged <65 years. Under the upperquartile definition, they involved systolic blood pressure among participants aged <65 years and diastolic blood pressure among those with higher educational attainment and occurred only in the unadjusted models (Supplementary Tables S38-S42 and S44). None was reproduced in the primary analysis, several were accompanied by violations of the parallel-trends assumption, and their directions were inconsistent across exposure definitions and model specifications. As these subgroup analyses were exploratory, not powered to formally test interaction effects, and not corrected for multiple comparisons, they should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating.

When follow-up wave 2 was re-included as the first post-exposure wave, no statistically significant associations were observed for any outcome in either the IPCW or complete-case analyses, adjusted or unadjusted, and the parallel-trends assumption was supported throughout (Supplementary Tables S21-S24). This analysis was not informative for nightly sleep duration, which was not assessed at wave 2.

Retaining the 40 participants who moved between differently classified census block groups did not materially change the results: nightly sleep duration remained the only outcome significantly associated with gentrification (adjusted IPCW ATT = -0.316 h; 95% CI -0.605, -0.027), and the pattern of paralleltrends pre-test results was largely unchanged (Supplementary Table S46).

In the complete-case analyses of the overall effect, no statistically significant associations were observed for any outcome (Supplementary Tables S9-S12). For nightly sleep duration, the adjusted (-0.252 h; 95% CI -0.509, 0.005) and unadjusted (-0.244 h; 95% CI -0.513, 0.025) estimates were in the same direction as in the primary analysis but smaller, and the parallel-trends assumption was not supported in either model (p=0.030 and p=0.032, respectively). Within subgroups, complete-case sleep estimates were in the same direction as the IPCW estimates, of similar magnitude among participants with lower educational attainment (adjusted -0.351 h; 95% CI -0.766, 0.063) but attenuated among those aged ≥65 years (adjusted -0.188 h; 95% CI -0.529, 0.154), and not statistically significant in any subgroup or model (Supplementary Tables S14-S17).

Cluster-bootstrap resampling of the complete-case estimates yielded confidence intervals that included the null for all outcomes (Supplementary Table S13). Sign consistency was highest for nightly sleep duration (0.93 with 2,000 and 0.92 with 5,000 replicates), whereas the direction of most other estimates was unstable, consistent with their limited precision. Overall, the reduction in sleep duration was consistent in direction across analytical approaches and exposure definitions but, at the overall level, reached statistical significance only in the IPCW analyses, whereas the null findings for the remaining outcomes were consistent across all sensitivity analyses.

4. Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first quantitative quasi-experimental study to estimate the health impacts of gentrification in Portugal and one of the few conducted in Europe. In this population-based cohort of adults from Porto, residence in a gentrifying neighbourhood was associated with a statistically significant reduction in nightly sleep duration of approximately 21 minutes, after adjustment for individual-level covariates and accounting for selective loss to follow-up, whereas no statistically significant effects were observed for the remaining outcomes.

The observed reduction in sleep is consistent with conceptual frameworks linking gentrification and neighbourhood disruption to health. Gentrification may affect sleep through several mutually reinforcing pathways, including increased nocturnal noise, the expansion of nightlife and tourism-related activity, the growth of short-term rental accommodation and greater housing insecurity, all of which have been linked to poorer sleep among urban residents (Anguelovski et al., 2021; Cole et al., 2021; Santos, Henriques, et al., 2024). In Porto, qualitative studies conducted within the same cohort have documented nocturnal noise, perceived insecurity and housing-related stress as resident-reported consequences of tourism-and student-driven neighbourhood change (Santos, Silva, et al., 2024; Silva et al., 2023), for which the present findings provide quantitative support.

Quantitative evidence linking neighbourhood change to sleep remains scarce and has generally addressed neighbourhood reinvestment rather than gentrification itself. In two low-income Pittsburgh neighbourhoods, residents living closest to major public and private investments were protected against the decline in sleep duration observed among those living farther away, a difference of 18.3 minutes per night, although the neighbourhood-level comparison was null (Dubowitz et al., 2021). In contrast with our findings, an ecological analysis of US census tracts reported a small decline in the prevalence of insufficient sleep in gentrifying areas, although its aggregate design cannot separate improvement among remaining residents from population replacement, and its threshold-based outcome is not directly comparable with mean sleep duration (Candipan et al., 2023).

Most longitudinal studies have instead focused on proximal features of neighbourhood change, such as socioeconomic disadvantage, residential instability, housing insecurity and environmental noise (Kim et al., 2022). Neighbourhood disadvantage and disorder have been associated with shorter and more fragmented sleep, while housing insecurity has been prospectively associated with shorter sleep duration among low-income adults (Bozick et al., 2021). Against this background, the reduction observed in this study is potentially important at the population level: its magnitude is comparable to that reported by Dubowitz et al. (2021), which those authors considered policy-relevant, and insufficient sleep is associated with cardiovascular morbidity and mortality (Cappuccio et al., 2011), although whether a change of this magnitude translates into measurable cardiometabolic effects over the period examined remains uncertain.

In exploratory subgroup analyses, the effect on sleep was statistically significant among older adults and participants with lower educational attainment, the groups considered a priori to be most vulnerable to neighbourhood change, but not among younger or more highly educated participants. Although these analyses were not powered to formally test interaction effects nor adjusted for multiple comparisons, this pattern is consistent with previous evidence of greater susceptibility to neighbourhood change and environmental stressors in these groups (Ribeiro, Fraga, et al., 2022; Santos, Henriques, et al., 2024) and with the expectation that the burdens of neighbourhood upgrading fall disproportionately on those with fewer resources to adapt or relocate (Cole et al., 2021; Cole et al., 2024).

The absence of statistically significant effects on most cardiometabolic and behavioural outcomes is consistent with the broader international literature, in which beneficial, adverse and null effects of gentrification on cardiometabolic health have been reported without a consistent pattern in the direction or magnitude of associations (Delong, 2023; Schnake-Mahl, Jahn, et al., 2020; Tulier et al., 2019), including modestly improved hypertension control in Los Angeles County (Chen et al., 2023) and no association with incident metabolic syndrome among Hispanic/Latino adults (Pichardo et al., 2023).

Several non-mutually exclusive explanations may account for these findings. First, gentrification may exert opposing effects on the same outcome (Breyer & Voss-Andreae, 2013), which may cancel out at the population level. Second, the relevant pathways may operate over different time horizons: the psychosocial mechanisms most directly engaged by transnational gentrification, such as nocturnal noise, insecurity and housing-related stress, may act on sleep over a relatively shorter period, whereas body mass index, cholesterol and blood pressure are shaped by cumulative life-course exposures and change more gradually (Lynch & Smith, 2005). The post-exposure follow-up may therefore have been sufficient to detect effects on sleep but not on downstream cardiometabolic risk factors, for which sleep may itself represent an upstream determinant.

Third, and most importantly, the precision of the estimates was limited by the small size of the exposed group. Confidence intervals for the cardiometabolic outcomes were wide and compatible with effects in either direction, and cluster-bootstrap resampling showed that the direction of most estimates was unstable. Because IPCW reduces bias arising from differential loss to follow-up but does not increase the effective sample size, the null estimates, particularly those for blood pressure and cholesterol, should be interpreted as inconclusive rather than as evidence of no effect.

The discrepancy between the IPCW and complete-case estimates for sleep duration warrants consideration. Both approaches yielded estimates in the same direction, and the difference in statistical significance stemmed not from greater precision of the IPCW analysis, whose confidence interval was in fact wider, but from a larger point estimate (-0.346 h vs -0.252 h). Participants retained in the completecase samples were younger, more highly educated and more often employed than those lost to followup or with missing data (Table 1), whereas the effect on sleep appeared to concentrate among older and less educated participants. By up-weighting retained participants who resembled those lost to followup, IPCW restores the contribution of these under-represented groups, which may explain the larger overall estimate; consistently, the complete-case estimate was most attenuated among participants aged ≥65 years (-0.188 h vs -0.433 h in the IPCW analysis). Both complete-case estimates were further limited by violations of the parallel-trends assumption.

Nevertheless, IPCW relies on the assumption that, conditional on measured baseline covariates, loss to follow-up is unrelated to the outcomes; if attrition depended on unmeasured factors, such as health status or residential displacement, residual selection bias cannot be excluded.

4.1 Strengths

This study has several methodological strengths. First, its longitudinal, quasi-experimental design, combined with the Callaway and Sant'Anna estimator, addressed key limitations of previous crosssectional research. Second, combining IPCW to address selective loss to follow-up with clusterbootstrap resampling to assess estimate stability provided a more rigorous evaluation than is common in gentrification research.

Third, the estimated reduction in sleep duration remained similar and statistically significant in the IPCW analyses when gentrifying neighbourhoods were defined using other thresholds, reducing concerns about the arbitrariness of the primary cut-off. Fourth, the PCA-based gentrification index, previously validated against objective indicators of neighbourhood change and residents' perceptions (Alves et al., 2026), integrated multiple dimensions of neighbourhood change within a reproducible and theoretically informed framework. Unlike generic measures of socioeconomic change, it incorporated high-income foreign residents and short-term tourist accommodation, capturing the transnational, tourism-driven character of gentrification particularly salient in Southern European cities such as Porto, which may affect health through mechanisms not captured by a purely socioeconomic index.

Finally, the simultaneous assessment of ten outcomes provided a broad overview of potential health impacts within the same population, and five follow-up waves spanning more than two decades enabled the construction of the pre-exposure trajectories required for valid DiD inference.

4.2 Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the operationalisation of gentrification remains heterogeneous across studies, limiting direct cross-study comparability (Bhavsar et al., 2020; Mujahid et al., 2019), although this was partly addressed by combining domains frequently used in previous research and by empirically assessing the structure and construct validity of the index (Alves et al., 2026).

Second, outcome selection was constrained by the availability of repeated and comparable measurements, which precluded mental health outcomes such as depression and anxiety, for which associations with gentrification have previously been reported (Anguelovski et al., 2021). This is particularly relevant because our results are compatible with an important role of psychosocial pathways, which could therefore only be assessed indirectly.

Third, the post-exposure follow-up period was shorter for blood pressure and alcohol use than for the remaining outcomes, reducing comparability across estimates and potentially limiting the detection of longer-term changes. Fourth, exposure classification was based on residential address and was therefore subject to the Uncertain Geographic Context Problem (Kwan, 2012), as participants' daily exposure to gentrification-related changes may extend beyond their residential neighbourhood. Fifth, the few participants who moved between differently classified census block groups were excluded from the primary analysis, although retaining them did not materially change the estimates. More importantly, the estimates do not capture the health consequences of displacement, a central mechanism through which gentrification may affect health (Anguelovski et al., 2021), also documented in Porto (Silva et al., 2025), and may therefore underestimate its overall health impact.

Sixth, the small size of the exposed group limited the ability to distinguish the absence of an effect from insufficient statistical precision. Finally, although the difference-in-differences design accounts for timeinvariant differences between study groups and the analyses adjusted for measured individual-level covariates, residual confounding due to unmeasured time-varying individual-or neighbourhood-level factors remains possible.

4.3 Conclusions

In this population-based cohort, living in a neighbourhood undergoing transnational gentrification in Porto was associated with a reduction of approximately 21 minutes in nightly sleep duration. This effect was consistent in direction across analytical approaches, robust to alternative exposure definitions, and appeared to be concentrated among older adults and those with lower educational attainment. No effects were detected on body mass index, cholesterol, blood pressure, or tobacco and alcohol use, although limited precision and, for some outcomes, violations of the parallel-trends assumption do not allow such effects to be ruled out. Sleep may thus be an early and sensitive marker of the health impact of gentrification and a plausible pathway through which it may widen health inequities among the most vulnerable residents.

The methodological framework applied in this study provides a reproducible approach for examining urban processes as population health exposures. Replication in cohorts with larger exposed groups and longer post-exposure follow-up is needed to clarify the long-term health consequences of gentrification and to support evidence-informed urban policy.
