No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

Background: Domestic gender-based violence (DGBV) can undermine women's sexual and reproductive health (SRH) and reproductive autonomy. However, community-level evidence examining multiple forms of violence and their relationship with SRH-seeking behaviour in Uganda's informal urban settlements remains limited. This study assessed the prevalence of DGBV, associated factors and its relationship with SRH-seeking behaviour among women in Lusaaze, Kampala.

Methods: A quantitative, community-based analytical cross-sectional study was conducted among 383 women aged 15-49 years. Background

Domestic gender-based violence (DGBV), encompassing physical, sexual, emotional and economic violence within intimate relationships, is a major public health and human rights concern [1, 2]. Data from the World Health Organization (WHO) suggests that approximately one in three women globally (nearly 840 million women aged 15 years and above) has experienced physical and/or sexual violence from intimate partners or non-partners in her lifetime, which has remained roughly constant over the last 20 years. While physical and sexual abuse have historically garnered the most clinical and policy attention, abuse in the form of emotion control and economic control are likely to co-occur with physical violence and, together, render poly-victimisation-which may lead to enhanced mental health outcomes and reduced women's ability to earn a living from the household [2, 3].

In addition to the physical injuries, domestic gender-based violence (DGBV) has detrimental and long-term effects on women's sexual and reproductive health (SRH). There is strong, consistent evidence that women experiencing DGBV are at increased risk of unwanted pregnancy, unsafe abortion, STIs, HIV and adverse pregnancy outcomes [4, 5]. DGBV may also be a structural and interpersonal barrier to accessing health care services. Threatening partner behavior, fear of retribution, financial constraints and internalized stigma may diminish women's confidence to negotiate contraceptive use, to access appropriate ANC, to attend regular HIV testing sessions, or to obtain immediate treatment of STI symptoms [4] [5] [6].

DGBV and negative sexual and reproductive health (SRH) outcomes are especially intertwined in Sub-Saharan Africa in settings where male supremacy, economic instability and infectious disease and endemic violence are prevalent [7]. Meta-analyses at the regional level have demonstrated that lifetime prevalence of intimate partner violence in Sub-Saharan Africa is higher than the global estimate, at 36-44%, for ever partnered women. Ugandan women continue to experience violence towards women in spite of progressive legal frameworks such as the Domestic Violence Act (2010) and the National Policy on Elimination of Gender-Based Violence [7, 8]. In 2022, the Ugandan Demographic and Health Survey (UDHS) reported that 43.7% of all women (aged 15-49) ever in a union experienced physical violence and 16.7% (both physical and sexual violence combined) experienced sexual violence from their current or most recent intimate partner. Prevalence of SRH service gaps such as high levels of unmet need for modern contraception, inadequate attendance to ANC, and postponement of STI/HIV services are likely leading to preventable maternal morbidity and transmission of HIV among women of reproductive age in Uganda [9, 10].

In Uganda, the rapid growth of informal urban settlements lacking essential resources has increased community-level socioeconomic stresses that may influence both household violent interactions and access to SRH services for women and girls. Among the high-density informal settlement residents of Lubaga Division of Kampala, where 78% of the population live in crowded single-room dwellings, informal daily wage labor, and with lack of personal financial independence, women are highly likely to be vulnerable to both household power dynamics and financial strife. In a recent community-based study of suburban Kampala communities, the overall prevalence of violence against women was 53.3% indicating that the population is at substantial risk of both multidimensional domestic gender-based violence (DGBV) and limitations in access to SRH services due to compounded factors such as intense economic hardship, reliance on fee-paying private clinics, and dependence on male partners [10] [11] [12].

However, despite the growing national acknowledgement of DGBV in Uganda, there remain notable evidence gaps at the micro-urban level. Existing evidence provides limited community-level understanding of the co-occurrence of physical, sexual, emotional and economic violence and their relationship with women's sexual and reproductive health-seeking behaviour in informal urban settlements. There is also limited community based quantitative evidence to determine if specific types and cumulative burdens of DGBV can lead to certain SRH-seeking behaviours, namely condom usage, STI treatment-seeking, HIV testing and antenatal care. The following study has been designed to contribute to this gap by reporting: the prevalence of all types of DGBV; associated socio-demographic characteristics; and the relationship between exposure to each of these and all four types of SRH-seeking behaviour in a population of women living within the informal settlement of Lusaaze Zone, Lubaga Division, Kampala, Uganda.

Methodology

Study design

We conducted a quantitative, community-based analytical cross-sectional study rooted in a postpositivist framework of objective measurement and statistical inference. The study design involved the collection of data at one point in time to determine the prevalence and distribution of DGBV (descriptive study component). The study further attempts to examine the relationship between DGBV, sociodemographic correlates, and SRH-seeking behavior (analytical study component). Though this design is ideal for assessing burden of disease and violence, in this context where exposures and outcome are assessed at the same time, it was not able to establish causality.

Study Area

This study was conducted in the Lusaaze Zone, Lubaga Division, Kampala District, Uganda. Lusaaze was chosen because of the suspected domestic and gender-based violence and reproductive health challenges in this population that could be driven by the economic pressures of kampala.

Study population

The study population was women aged 15-49 years living in the Lusaaze Zone. The target population was estimated at 900-1,300 women from local administrative estimates and community records. The accessible population was women who had lived in Lusaaze for at least six months and who consented to take part (or assented with guardian consent, if aged under 18).

The sample population comprised all women in the age range of 15-49 years living in the Lusaaze Zone.

An estimated target population of 900-1300 women was obtained from local administrative and the community registers. The participant population was women who had been residents in the study area for at least six months and who gave informed consent or assent (if aged under 18 years).

Inclusion criteria:

Women aged 15-49 years, residence in the Lusaaze Zone for at least six months, informed consent, or assent with guardian consent for those under 18.

Exclusion criteria: Women who were critically ill at the time of data collection and women who were unable to communicate in English or Luganda during the interview.

Sample size

The sample size for estimating a prevalence was calculated with the Kish and Leslie (1965) formula [13], n = Z²p(1 -p)/d², where Z = 1.96 (95% confidence), p = expected prevalence and d = 0.05 (margin of error).

National data from the 2022 Uganda Demographic and Health Survey show that 43.7% of women have experienced physical violence since age 15 (Uganda Bureau of Statistics, 2023), while a local study in the suburbs of Kampala reported an overall violence prevalence of 53.3% [12]. The local figure was used: n = (1.96)² × 0.533 × (1 -0.533) / (0.05)² = 3.8416 × 0.2489 /0.0025 = 382.5, rounded up to 383.

The calculated minimum sample size was 383 women. A total of 383 women aged 15-49 years were enrolled in the study.

Sampling Method

Participants were recruited using a systematic random sampling technique. The sampling frame was compiled through a systematic enumeration of the residential households in the Lusaaze Zone with the assistance of local leaders and VHTs (Village Health Teams) resulting in 1,120 households identified as eligible for sampling from the Luzaase administrative register. The sampling interval, k, was calculated using the formula: k = N/n (1,120/383 = 2.92). The sampling interval was then rounded to three, k = 3. A starting household was randomly generated, k = 1-3 using a random number generator, and one household was surveyed and sampled along the geographic axis. The first household and subsequent homes were queried until reaching the sample size of 383 women.

Within each sampled household, one eligible woman between the ages of 15 and 49 was selected to participate. If more than one woman was eligible, participants were selected via a simple random ballot method with numbered slips of paper folded and placed in a bowl. If there was no eligible woman in a selected household, the next adjacent household was approached along the original geographic axis while not disturbing the original sampling frame. If a respondent was unavailable at the time of survey, up to two repeated visits were conducted at different points in the day before moving on to the next household.

Data Collection

Using a structured instrument administered through interviews in either English or Luganda, as appropriate, data were obtained. The questionnaire was administered face-to-face and in a private room by trained research assistants, which was likely to minimize researcher bias and potentially improve the accuracy of responses for persons with low literacy levels and to allow for clarification of sensitive questions. The questionnaire covered three broad sections: (a) sociodemographic information, (b) domestic violence (i.e., physical, emotional, sexual, and economic violence) experiences and (c) sexual and reproductive healthseeking behaviors (i.e., antenatal care, family planning, HIV testing, and treatment-seeking for STI symptoms). Data were captured on mobile data-collection forms or printed questionnaires, with audio recordings not utilized.

Data were collected via a structured interview. The violence module asked specific questions about physical, sexual, emotional and economic violence. The questions on sexual, emotional and economic violence specifically asked about violence by the respondent's current or most recent husband/partner and within the last 12 months. Questions on physical violence did not specifically ask about who the violence was from or when it occurred. Non-partner sexual violence was also asked about separately and was not included in the composite measure of DGBV.

Validity and reliability

Content validity was established through a detailed review by academic supervisors and other public health research experts. Before the beginning of data collection, the pretested questionnaire in 20 women of a neighboring community with comparable sociodemographic settings, and the unclear issues were modified.

Also, the interviewers got training on conducting interviews, ethical issues, and assuring privacy of the respondents which is most likely to reduce respondent and interviewer bias.

Exposure: Domestic gender based violence was measured using four categories: physical, sexual, emotional and economic violence. A positive answer to at least one question in each category indicated exposure to that type of violence. Physical and sexual violence were indicated by any answer other than 'never'. Emotional and economic violence were indicated by positive answers. Sexual, emotional and economic violence questions asked about experiences with the respondents current or last husband/partner in the last 12 months. Physical violence questions did not ask about the perpetrators or time frame. Caution should be taken when interpreting this composite measure as there are differences in perpetrator and time frame between categories. Non-partner sexual violence was also measured.

Data management and cleaning

Data were entered and validated at the time of collection; electronic data files were password protected, and paper questionnaires were stored in locked cabinets available only to the research team. The raw data file (n=383) was cleaned prior to analysis in R: (1) duplicate records were checked for and not identified, (2) spelling, capitalization and spacing were standardised, (3) resident eligibility was validated (women who had been in Lusaaze<6 months were excluded, as well as 3 records with missing length of residency, giving a final primary analytical sample of 377 women), (4) eight inconsistent response sets were recorded as missing (e.g., "agree" and "strongly disagree" responses to the same question) along with one implausible value, (5) the exposure, outcome and covariate variables were derived according to sections above, and (6) a single coded column was retained for each item and indicator variables were removed to prevent replication, as well as the "askid" identifier for each item. Missing data at item level was 2% across variables included in the analysis, and was dealt with using available-case analysis (model-by-model complete case analysis).

Data Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed in R (version 4.3), with key outputs independently verified in

Python. Descriptive statistics (frequencies and percentages) summarized respondent sociodemographic characteristics, forms of DGBV, and SRH-seeking indicators, with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for proportions calculated using the Wilson score method.

For bivariate and multivariable analyses, modified Poisson regression with robust (sandwich) standard errors was used to estimate crude and adjusted prevalence ratios (cPR and aPR) with 95% CIs [14]. Modified Poisson regression was selected because all outcomes exceeded 10% prevalence, a setting in which logistic regression odds ratios substantially overestimate prevalence ratios. For Objective 2, each SRH-seeking indicator was modeled on DGBV exposure, adjusting for age group, education, employment status, and partnership status. Partner interference with family planning was analyzed specifically among ever-partnered women, while STI treatment-seeking was evaluated as an exploratory outcome restricted to women reporting at least one STI symptom. For Objective 3, any DGBV exposure was modeled simultaneously against age group, education, employment status, and partnership status. Additionally, a sensitivity analysis was conducted redefining the outcome as non-economic violence (physical, sexual, or emotional violence only) to evaluate its association with partner financial control without circularity. Statistical significance was set at a two-sided 0.05.

Ethical Considerations

The study received ethics approval from the Uganda Heart Institute Research Ethics Committee (REC: UHIREC0192) with institutional clearance by Victoria University and administrative permission from the Lusaaze Zone Local Chairperson in Lubaga Division, Kampala. The researcher adhered to the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki which include respect for persons, voluntary participation, anonymity and confidentiality, beneficence and justice. Written informed consent was obtained from adult participants before conducting interviews; written informed assent was obtained from 15-17 year olds in conjunction with written informed consent from their parents or guardians. Participants were appropriately informed of the purpose of the study, its risks and benefits, and that their participation was voluntary and they could refuse or withdraw at any time, without penalty. Interviews were conducted in private to ensure privacy and confidentiality, names were not recorded and participants were given identifiers on each record. Participants experiencing distress were treated with dignity and referred to local health and psychosocial services. Data was stored securely in password protected files and filing cabinets for use in the research and academic work.

Discussion

This study identified a high prevalence of reported violence across four domains among women living in Lusaaze, Kampala. However, differences in perpetrator attribution and recall periods across the questionnaire domains require caution when interpreting the composite estimate as domestic or intimate partner violence. In general (72.5%) of the surveyed women who had completed the full battery of questions in each of the four DGBV questionnaires reported experiencing at least one form of DGBV. The percentage of women reporting experience of DGBV was significantly higher than previous estimates from national surveys conducted in Uganda, such as Uganda's Demographic and Health Surveys, which estimated that thirty-five to fifty percent of ever married women experienced either physical or sexual violence during the year preceding the survey [15, 16]. Nonetheless, these national survey estimates cannot be compared directly to those found in this study due to many methodological differences. For example, national surveys employ stratified random samples from a large geographic area -commonly including rural areas while this study used systematic sampling of a small geographic area, a single peri-urban informal settlement.

Additionally, national surveys typically include items assessing physical and sexual violence only over a specific time period -e.g., past twelve month while this study included behavior-specific items assessing physical, sexual, emotional and economic violence. The inclusion of behavioral items assessing emotional and economic violence may have contributed to increasing the number of cases detected in this study relative to studies that assess only physical and sexual violence. Evidence exists suggesting that assessment tools focusing on both physical and sexual forms of violence underreport experiences of other types of violence when compared to assessment tools designed to measure all forms of violence [18, 19]. A further contributing factor to the high levels of reported DGBV in informal communities could be the presence of socioeconomic disadvantages and limited access to potential sources of support services. While the current study did not collect sufficient data to determine whether these factors were causal determinants of the high rates of DGBV reported by women in this study [20, 21], they are likely to have been relevant factors.

Emotional abuse was the most frequent type of violence reported at 65.2%. The second highest frequency was for physical abuse at 62.8%, followed by sexual abuse at 59.9% and then economic abuse at 56.7%.

These rates are similar to other reports from sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia that report psychological abuse, either alone or with more than physical abuse. In addition, nearly half (49.7%) of the sample were exposed to all four types of violence, suggesting that women in this sample were likely to experience a pattern of multiple exposures of violence.

When victims of violence experience two or more types of violence simultaneously it can be referred to as polyvictimization. Polyvictimization is a serious issue since experiencing different forms of victimization can have additive negative impacts on mental and physical health [17, 24]. Of note, there were significantly greater percentages of currently partnered women (97.1%) who reported having experienced one or more forms of DGBV compared to previously partnered women (60.4%) and never partnered women (9.4%).

The higher prevalence of reported violence among currently partnered women highlights the importance of examining relationship context, while recognising that the study did not establish whether the current partner was responsible for every reported physical violence experience [25, 26]. Although the rates of abuse among never partnered women are high, they should be interpreted cautiously. There could be several explanations for these high rates, such as being abused by someone else in the same household or being abused in a previous relationship. However, whether these abuses occurred in previous relationships will depend upon how the investigators define and measure abuse in the DGVB survey. Abuse can occur against a woman by many people outside her intimate partner(s) whereas intimate partner violence refers only to violence from an individual's present or past intimate partner(s) [27, 28]. Understanding this difference is key to comparing our results with studies examining only partner specific violence, such as the Kenya DHS examination of intimate partner based sexual violence and the South African examination of physical violence by present or past partner(s) [25, 29].

There was not a consistent relationship found between DGBV and utilization of SRH services. As indicated earlier, we did find a statistically non-significant association between DGBV and use of HIV testing (aPR 0.99, 95% CI: 0.82 -1.20). Our finding contradicts those from an intervention trial in Nairobi, Kenya where screening for intimate partner violence during HIV counseling resulted in increased self-reporting of both HIV testing and use of HIV related services [30]. However, the studies differ substantially in design and setting: the Nairobi study evaluated an intervention among clinic attendees, whereas the present study examined community-level associations. Furthermore, HIV testing coverage in Lusaaze was already high (84.3%), potentially limiting the observable variation needed to identify differences between exposure groups. Violence may also influence testing in opposing directions: fear of partner retaliation may discourage testing, while perceived HIV risk or the need for reproductive health information may motivate it [31] [32] [33]. Consequently, the absence of an association should not be interpreted as evidence that violence has no influence on HIV testing decisions.

DGBV was not significantly associated with ANC attendance (aPR 0.93, 95% CI: 0.84-1.02). This contrasts with evidence from systematic reviews and meta-analyses that intimate partner violence is associated with reduced adequate utilisation of ANC services [34, 35]. However, the near universal attendance of every pregnant woman (98.4%) may explain why this association was not significant due to limited variation. Evidence from a 36 country analysis of DHS data suggests that lifetime intimate partner violence is more consistently associated with reduced completion of recommended ANC visits and delivery at a facility [36]. However, the measure of whether women attended ANC at least once may not capture the full picture of how intimate partner violence impacts on women's ANC. In high initial contact settings, intimate partner violence may impact on women's ability to adequately access and utilise services [36].

Therefore, the high ANC attendance in Lusaaze should not be taken to mean that women are accessing equitable and violence free maternal health services.

A notable finding was the modest positive association between DGBV and current contraceptive use (aPR 1.11, 95% CI: 1.01-1.22). This is contrary to findings showing that women exposed to intimate partner violence have reduced contraceptive use [23, 37]. However, this is consistent with findings from Bawuah et al (38) who found that women experiencing intimate partner violence had higher modern contraceptive use but lower adequate maternal health utilisation from a 25 country DHS analysis in Africa. This suggests that contraceptive use and maternal health utilisation may be impacted in opposing ways by relationship control. One possible explanation for this is that women are using contraceptives discreetly. Studies from Uganda and Bangladesh have shown that women are using discreet contraceptive methods to retain some control over their reproduction within controlling or abusive relationships [39, 40]. This could therefore explain the association seen in Lusaaze. Another factor to consider is whether the association is due to reverse causation. Women using contraception may cause suspicion from their partner. Longitudinal and qualitative studies are needed to determine if women are making autonomous decisions around contraceptive use or if they are using discreet contraceptive methods. It should be noted that only 14 women were non-users so this may have impacted on the stability of the results [44].

The strongest association with an SRH outcome was partner interference with family planning (aPR 3.36, 95% CI: 1.25-9.06). Nearly half of ever partnered women (48.2%) experienced interference with family planning. Women who experienced DGBV had over 3 times higher prevalence of partner interference. This suggests that reproductive coercion is an important aspect of violence experienced in Lusaaze. The reproductive coercion framework explains how women experience sabotage of contraceptive use, pressure to conceive and interference with contraceptive decision making from their partner which limits their reproductive autonomy [41, 45]. This is consistent with qualitative findings from Wakiso District in Uganda where women reported that their partners would break contraceptive pills, refuse to use condoms and pressure them to conceive [39]. Similar findings have been seen in Bangladesh and India where women have reported partner and family resistance including in-laws to contraceptive use [40, 37]. However, the wide confidence interval suggests that there is a lot of uncertainty around the strength of this association.

There were only a small number of ever partnered women who were not exposed (n=18). This is an issue with sparse data and can be particularly problematic in areas where prevalence is high. This reduces the stability of the adjusted estimates [46, 47].

The sociodemographic findings show that DGBV is influenced by a range of individual and relationship factors. Women with no or primary education (aPR 1.13) and secondary education (aPR 1.12) had higher prevalence of DGBV compared to those with tertiary education. This is generally consistent with evidence showing women with lower levels of education are more vulnerable to intimate partner violence [48, 49].

However, there was not a large difference so it cannot be said that women with tertiary education are protected from DGBV. Education may be an indicator of wider socioeconomic differences, economic opportunities and power in relationships. It may also influence whether women are able to identify, report and leave abusive relationships [50, 51]. Women who were self-employed had higher prevalence of DGBV (aPR 1.08). Whilst this may increase women's financial independence it does not necessarily increase their power in relationships. Women may challenge traditional gender norms by becoming economically active which can cause conflict and violence [52, 53]. This shows the importance of understanding violence beyond just individual socioeconomic factors and considering how gendered power and resources are distributed [54, 55].

Age was not significantly associated with DGBV. This is contrary to studies showing that younger women are more likely to experience violence [56, 57]. However, this is not necessarily conflicting as there is evidence that women experience violence throughout their reproductive life. This may also be due to the high proportion of women aged between 35-49 years old (45.4%). There may also be differences in relationship transitions such as separation and widowhood.

The findings regarding STI symptoms should be treated with caution. The high prevalence of symptoms (94.4%) and seeking treatment (94.1%) may not be accurate due to issues with how symptoms were categorised. Symptoms such as discharge and itch are not specific to STIs and could be caused by other gynaecological or physiological issues. This has been seen in other studies using symptoms as a method of screening [59,60]. Therefore, it cannot be confidently said that there is no association between DGBV and seeking treatment for STIs as the outcome may not accurately reflect true STI morbidity or appropriate treatment. Future studies should include lab confirmed STI testing or validated syndromic algorithms with clear time frames [18].

Overall, the findings can be best understood using the social ecological model, feminist perspectives and reproductive coercion. The social ecological model considers that violence occurs due to a combination of individual, relationship and community factors. Feminist perspectives consider power imbalances in relationships to control women's bodies and reproduction [55, 54, 61]. Reproductive coercion explains how women's reproduction is controlled by their partner through interfering with contraceptive use and conception decisions [41, 45]. The high prevalence of contraceptive use combined with high partner interference shows that traditional measures of service use may not fully reflect women's reproductive autonomy. Women may be accessing contraception but still experience limited control over their reproduction. This demonstrates that women may not be empowered, making informed decisions or free from coercion [39, 40, 62].

This should be interpreted with consideration to the limitations. The study was cross-sectional meaning that it is not possible to determine causation or temporality. There was also no clear time frame for violence meaning that the results may not be as accurate. Non-response may have caused bias. There were issues with the validity of the STI questions and the exact measure of violence. There were a limited number of currently partnered women who were not exposed meaning some of the adjusted estimates were not as accurate. Particularly the association between partner interference with family planning. However, the study provides evidence from a community setting in an under-researched informal urban area. It looked at a range of different types of violence and included specific behaviours. The decisions made during analysis were reported meaning the findings are transparent. From a public health perspective, interventions should go beyond increasing women's service use and consider improving safety, confidentiality, reproductive autonomy and identifying partner interference. Women may not be empowered as they have high ANC attendance and contraceptive uptake but relationship coercion is still prevalent [36, 63].

There were different ways of asking about perpetrators of violence. The questions on sexual, emotional and economic violence specifically asked about current or ex-husbands/partners in the last 12 months.

Questions on physical violence were not specific about perpetrators or time frame. This means the 4 domain composite combines different time frames and cannot be viewed as a single measure of intimate partner violence in the last year. Future studies should be longitudinal and include validated reproductive coercion measures. Lab confirmed STI testing should also be included. The quality, continuity and autonomy of care should also be considered. This would provide better evidence to implement integrated gender sensitive interventions to improve women's SRH in informal urban settlements.

Conclusion

Violence exposure across multiple domains was common among women in Lusaaze. The coexistence of contraceptive use and partner interference with family planning highlights the limitations of healthcare utilisation as a standalone indicator of reproductive autonomy. Survivor-centred SRH services should address violence, confidentiality and reproductive decision-making.

Recommendations

SRH services should offer confidential, survivor focussed strategies to identify violence and reproductive coercion in informal urban settings. There should be clear referral routes for psychosocial, legal and protection services. Confidentiality should be maintained whilst supporting women to make their own decisions on contraception. Public health strategies should also be implemented to reduce violence in the community. Longitudinal and mixed methods studies should be conducted to understand the causal pathways. Validated tools should be used to measure violence and reproductive coercion.
