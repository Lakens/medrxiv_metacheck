Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. How StatCheck works

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Objective. Out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) is often unwitnessed, which delays treatment and lowers the chance of survival. A smartwatch could act as a witness by detecting loss of pulse from wrist photoplethysmography (PPG) and subsequently alert emergency services. We aimed to develop and evaluate such a detection model. Approach. As smartwatch data of OHCA are extremely di! cult to acquire, we used in-hospital proxy data to test sensitivity: wrist PPG and accelerometry recorded during baseline and induced loss-of-pulse periods in 31 patients during catheter ablation of ventricular arrhythmias and defibrillation threshold (DFT) testing during subcutaneous implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (S-ICD) implantation (BECA-S2). In total, 33 loss-of-pulse events, annotated using accompanying electrocardiograms and, where available, arterial blood pressure, were included. To count false alarms, we used wrist PPG and accelerometry of 100 healthy volunteers during unsupervised daily activities (BECA-S6). A recurrent neural network was evaluated with cross-validation, with one fold per patient with events. As the electrical VF induction and defibrillation shocks applied during these procedures would not be present in true OHCA episodes, we tested for and mitigated the resulting target leakage. Main results. The Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC-ROC) was 86.8% (95% CI: 79.7 to 92.5%) and the Area Under the Precision-Recall Curve (PR-AUC) was 52.4% (95% CI: 38.6 to 66.2%). At a false alarm rate of zero on BECA-S6, the event-level sensitivity on BECA-S2 was 48.5% (95% CI: 32.1 to 65.5%). Higher sensitivities came at the cost of more false alarms. Without mitigation, target leakage inflated the model scores by 35.3% (95% CI: 11.5 to 61.4%) of the model's discriminative range. Significance. These results show the feasibility of smartwatch-based loss-of-pulse detection. Larger studies with data more representative of OHCA are needed. We recommend that future studies on induced loss of pulse test for and mitigate target leakage.

1 Introduction

Out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) is one of the leading causes of mortality worldwide, with an incidence of emergency medical services-treated OHCA of 35.7-100.0 per 100,000 population per year and survival to hospital discharge rates of only 3.4% to 15.8% [1, 2]. Successful treatment of OHCA consists of early recognition, alerting emergency medical services (EMS), starting cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR), and early application of advanced life support, together known as the chain of survival.

A large proportion (34.4-55.1% according to national registries) of OHCA cases are unwitnessed [1]. Given that victims are unable to call for help themselves, this delays the first step of the chain of survival and therefore the whole treatment. As survival rates of OHCA drop rapidly with delayed treatment (→6% per minute), the chances of survival in unwitnessed cases are poor (<5%) [3, 4].

In the BEating Cardiac Arrest (BECA) project, our aim is to develop a smartwatch-based solution that functions as a witness when no human witness is present [5]. We use wrist-based 1 photoplethysmography (PPG) and accelerometry to detect loss of pulse and loss of motion, the main symptoms of OHCA, which would enable starting the chain of survival in unwitnessed cases.

Several research groups and manufacturers have shown interest in this concept in parallel, converging on largely the same problem statement but di"ering in device, algorithm, and validation approach. Google Research has implemented a loss-of-pulse feature on the Pixel Watch 3, combining digital signal processing with a convolutional neural network across a three-stage detection cascade [6]. In prospective validation, it reached a sensitivity of 67.2% in arterial-occlusion testing, with 1.34 algorithm-level false positives (check-in notifications) per user-year in free-living conditions.

The DETECT research group has validated a rule-based wrist-PPG algorithm in patients with induced and spontaneous circulatory arrest during cardiac procedures, reporting sensitivities of 92-98% at 0.12-0.26 false alarms per hour [7, 8]. The HEART-SAFE consortium has taken a device-independent approach using consumer smartwatches, validating detection across cu"-induced ischemia, cardioplegic cardiac arrest during surgery, and animal models, reaching 90.3% sensitivity but a comparatively modest 94.1% hour-level specificity, and has additionally demonstrated end-to-end alerting through a community-responder network [9]; a subsequent case report documented the first automated detection of a genuine spontaneous cardiac arrest by a consumer smartwatch [10].

Although these studies show cautiously promising sensitivity, specificity in real-world conditions remains the central open problem, as frequent false alarms would burden emergency medical services and community responders, and could lead users to stop wearing the device or to ignore its alarms. Reported false alarm rates vary by orders of magnitude across studies and validation settings [6, 7, 8, 9], evidence on spontaneous (as opposed to induced) cardiac arrest remains limited to small cohorts and case reports [7, 10, 11, 12], and the authors of most studies call for further validation before real-world deployment.

In this work, we take a step toward such validation by presenting a methodology for the detection of loss of pulse using wrist-PPG and accelerometry and evaluating it on both clinical and real-world data, which, to our knowledge, has not been done before. As smartwatch data of true OHCA events are extremely di! cult to obtain in view of their low incidence [1, 2], we approximated such data with two studies. We tested detection sensitivity based on in-hospital measurements during routine cardiac procedures in which ventricular tachycardia or fibrillation (VT/VF) was expected to occur (the BECA-S2 study), and false alarm rate in out-of-hospital measurements in healthy volunteers wearing the device in unsupervised daily life (the BECA-S6 study). As electrical VF induction and defibrillation shocks that start or terminate some of these events leave signal artifacts that would not be present in OHCA, a model could exploit these artifacts instead of detecting the loss of pulse itself (target leakage). Therefore, unlike previous studies, we tested for and mitigated target leakage.

2 Methods

2.1 The BECA-S2 study of intraoperative loss of pulse

To measure detection sensitivity, the prospective observational BECA-S2 study included patients who underwent catheter ablation of ventricular arrhythmias or defibrillation threshold (DFT) testing during subcutaneous implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (S-ICD) implantation between August 2023 and February 2026 at Catharina Hospital (Eindhoven, The Netherlands). These procedures were chosen because of the high probability that VT/VF events would occur. Patients were eligible when they were at least 18 years old, able to provide informed consent, and had no wounds, injuries, infectious diseases, or tattoos on the wrist on which the PPG/accelerometer detection device was placed. The cohort consisted of the patients who presented for these procedures and met these criteria.

During the procedures, data were acquired from the catheterization lab (cathlab) using the GE HealthCare CardioLab EP and Mac-Lab Recording Systems. This included ECG (sampled at 240-977 Hz) and, if available, arterial blood pressure (ABP, sampled at 977 Hz). Furthermore, patients wore a Philips Elan data logger wristband, equipped with a single-channel green light photoplethysmography (PPG) sensor sampling at 32 Hz and a three-axis accelerometer sampling at 128 Hz. For redundancy purposes, patients wore a wristband on each wrist if the procedure allowed for it.

After data collection, the onsets and ends of VT/VF events were independently annotated in the cathlab ECG and ABP data by two electrophysiologists, followed by adjudication of disagreements by consensus-seeking. Episodes of VF visible in the ECG were considered to cause loss of pulse. Episodes of VT were considered to cause loss of pulse only if ABP was available and showed a peak arterial pressure of 50 mmHg or lower. Based on these annotations, we calculated inter-annotator agreement using Gwet's AC1 and Cohen's kappa, and the annotation overlap using the median Intersection over Union (IoU). An expert in photoplethysmography translated these annotations to the Philips Elan data, annotating the onsets and ends of loss of pulse in the PPG signals.

Pre-and postoperative parts of the wristband signals were excluded, leaving only the intraoperative data. Given the precarious nature of the cardiovascular system during catheter ablation, we only included the wristband data of these procedures that were accompanied by ECG and/or ABP signals to confirm both events and absence of events. Since ECG and ABP were not recorded continuously, this split each record into several segments. As the S-ICD implantation procedures were generally uneventful, we included all of their intraoperative wristband data, including parts without ECG or ABP in which the absence of events could not be confirmed.

The records were cut after each occurrence of loss of pulse, after which 10 seconds of data were excluded to allow the cardiovascular system to recover. In some procedures, periodic blood pressure measurements were performed using an automated oscillometric sphygmomanometer, leading to arterial occlusions in the arm that mimic loss of pulse. These episodes were excluded with a 10-second margin to avoid confusing the detection model. Loss-of-pulse episodes shorter than 3 seconds were excluded with a 10-second margin as well. After all cuts, only segments of 30 seconds or longer were included. Loss-of-pulse events that fell within excluded data were not analysed.

2.2 The BECA-S6 study of out-of-hospital free-living conditions

To measure the false alarm rate, we performed the prospective observational BECA-S6 study, in which we collected wrist PPG and accelerometry of healthy volunteers without loss of pulse. Between July 2023 and April 2024, participants were recruited from a pool of university students, employees, and community volunteers. Eligibility required a minimum age of 18 years, absence of wrist wounds, injuries, and tattoos, and unrestricted upper-limb mobility. Participants wore two Philips Elan data logger wristbands, one on each wrist, for approximately one hour in free-living conditions.

2.3 Preprocessing and feature extraction

After data acquisition, the PPG signals were corrected for hardware-controlled amplitude scaling and separated into baseline and pulsatile components by third-order causal low-pass and high-pass Butterworth filters with a cuto" frequency of 0.5 Hz. Accelerometry signals were downsampled by a factor of 4 to match the PPG sampling frequency, and their gravity/DC component was removed by a third-order causal high-pass Butterworth filter with a cuto" frequency of 0.5 Hz.

We extracted 37 time-and frequency-domain features per sample, including PPG derivatives, PPG moments (variance, skewness, kurtosis), the Hjorth parameters [13], zero-crossing rate, peak-to-peak amplitude and its log-ratio against the same quantity 10, 30, 60, and 120 seconds earlier, signal energy, signal-to-noise ratio, spectral entropy and flux, acceleration and jerk magnitude, peak-to-peak acceleration amplitude log-sum and log-ratios against delayed amplitudes, and acceleration moments. 2.4 Detection model

The detection model consists of a recurrent neural network with a convolutional encoder. The shared-weights encoder takes one second of features as input and works it through three convolutional blocks before global average pooling. The encoder outputs of consecutive seconds are then used as input to a long short-term memory (LSTM) network, followed by a shared-weights decoder with a dense layer to make the final logit classification of loss of pulse. Model width and encoder dropout were explored as hyperparameters during model selection (see Section 2.5). An overview of the model is shown in Figure 1 and Appendix B. This detection model was trained using an Adam optimizer (lr = 0.001) on Tversky loss [14] where y ↓ [0, 1] B↑T are the per-second targets in a batch, and p ↓ [0, 1] B↑T the predicted per-second probabilities.

Furthermore, we applied a smoothness penalty penalizing noisy consecutive estimates. For the smoothness function, we define a mask defined only between adjacent non-positive-labeled seconds to avoid penalizing genuine event onsets.

The penalty itself is the mean squared error of consecutive estimates over this mask.

In addition, we applied a gradient penalty GP(z), the squared gradient of the output logits z with respect to all input channels of the gapped input samples, to penalize reliance on the gapped input and thus mitigate target leakage [15]. The Tversky weights ω, ε (false-positive and false-negative weights, respectively), the smoothness weight ϑ, and the gradient-penalty weight w are hyperparameters. The model was fed with batches of 32 arbitrary-length (30-60 seconds) pieces of signal and features, where half of the batch was guaranteed to contain loss-of-pulse-positive seconds. Online amplitude jittering of ±5% was applied. As a device should raise alarms for events rather than for individual seconds, the second-level classifications were converted into event-level alarms. To prevent incidental positive classifications from triggering a false alarm, we used a debouncing mechanism, requiring a certain number of consecutive positive second-level classifications before an alarm was raised. The required number of positive classifications (the debounce length, 1 to 60 seconds) was chosen per fold after model selection (see Section 2.5).

2.5 Evaluation

We used cross-validation to assess detection performance, with one fold for each BECA-S2 patient having at least one loss-of-pulse event. The BECA-S2 patients without events and the BECA-S6 participants (with data from both wrists) were randomly and uniformly distributed over the folds, forming one group per fold. In each fold, one group was used for testing, the next five groups for validation, and the remaining groups for training. Inside each fold, the model hyperparameters (model width, encoder dropout, Tversky loss parameters, smoothness penalty weight) were selected using successive halving on the Area Under the Precision-Recall curve (PR-AUC), starting with 27 Latin-Hypercube-sampled hyperparameter candidates trained for 5 epochs, of which the 9 best were trained for 15 epochs. The 3 best of these were selected as finalists and trained until convergence on validation loss, and the finalist with the highest validation PR-AUC was selected. As hyperparameters were selected within each fold, the reported performance reflects the training procedure rather than a single fixed model. The optimal debounce length was chosen per fold on the validation data. For each candidate length, we determined the event-level false alarm rate (FAR) needed to reach 80% sensitivity, with sensitivity determined on the BECA-S2 validation patients and false alarms counted on the BECA-S6 validation participants. We then selected the length with the lowest 97.5th-percentile upper bound of this FAR, obtained from a cluster bootstrap of 1,000 replicates. Only debounce lengths up to the median duration of the validation events were considered, so that at least half of these events lasted long enough to be detected.

To mitigate target leakage, the training procedure was repeated for all gradient-penalty weights in {0, 1, 10, 10 2 , 10 3 , 10 4 , 10 5 , 10 6 , 10 7 }. The hyperparameter search was performed at weight 0. To reduce computational e"ort, for all other weights, each fold reused the configuration selected for that fold at weight 0.

To determine whether target leakage remained, we probed each trained model with synthetic gaps: if a model relies on gaps as a cue for loss of pulse, inserting a gap raises its scores. For each weight, residual target leakage was estimated by inserting synthetic gaps, copied from real gaps, in event-free data of the test BECA-S2 patients and BECA-S6 participants (arrest-free context; 10 gaps per data segment) and just before loss-of-pulse events (arrest context; 10 gaps per event) and observing the change in model scores in the 30 seconds after the synthetic gap. Only events not already preceded by a gap were eligible for the arrest-context probe. This leakage e"ect was expressed relative to the discriminative range of the model, i.e., the di"erence between the mean model score during loss-of-pulse events and during event-free data. For both contexts, we then tested whether the leakage e"ect lay within a pre-specified tolerance of ±5% of this range (an equivalence test), using a bootstrap over patients and participants (20,000 replicates).

The gradient-penalty weight was then selected as follows. Weights showing model collapse, i.e., with a discriminative range of less than 25% of that of the unmitigated model (w = 0), were excluded from weight selection. Subsequently, fixed-sequence testing was applied to find the smallest gradient-penalty weight that, together with all larger non-collapsed weights, passed both tolerance tests: weights were tested one at a time from the largest non-collapsed weight downward, each at a significance level of 0.05, stopping at the first weight failing either test. As the tests are performed in a fixed order, no correction for multiple testing is needed. The PR-AUC of the selected weight was compared with that of the unmitigated model (w = 0) by a paired patient-cluster bootstrap (20,000 replicates) of the di"erence in macro-averaged PR-AUC over the test patients.

For each selected model, the second-level classifications on the test patient were generated, from which the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) and PR curves were inferred by threshold-sweeping. A 500-point grid was used to interpolate the curves of each patient before macro-averaging across patients. Patients without loss-of-pulse events have no defined curves and do not contribute to the average. We inferred 95% confidence intervals using patient-cluster bootstrap with 2,000 replicates, resampling all patients. The same procedure was used to determine Area Under the ROC curve (AUC-ROC) and PR-AUC.

We also tested event-level performance, where an event-level alarm was defined as a sequence of second-level positives reaching the debounce length and was considered a true positive whenever its onset was within a detection window of 10 seconds before a loss of pulse onset or 30 seconds after it, reflecting that a device alarms on events rather than individual seconds. Sensitivity was determined on the BECA-S2 events, and false alarms were counted on BECA-S6 data. The false alarm rate is the number of alarms divided by the number of scored hours, pooled over the BECA-S6 participants in a test fold. As each wrist was recorded and scored separately, an hour of recording on both wrists counts as two wrist-hours. In a secondary analysis, false alarms were also counted on BECA-S2, to compare false alarm rates between clinical and real-world conditions. After applying debouncing on the test patients, the resulting Free-response ROC (FROC) curves were weighted based on event count (micro-averaging) to mitigate the low count of events per patient. Bootstrapping over cross-validation folds (each holding one BECA-S2 patient with events and the BECA-S6 participants of that fold) was used to infer 95% confidence intervals. Each FROC curve was summarized by four pre-specified points: the FAR at sensitivities of 60%, 80%, and 95%, and the sensitivity at a FAR of zero.

Lastly, we examined whether the algorithm's performance di"ered by participant characteristics, separately for each cohort. In BECA-S2, we examined whether per-patient PR-AUC di"ered by procedure type (catheter ablation vs. DFT testing), sex (male versus female), age (decades), BMI (kg/m 2 ), Fitzpatrick skin tone (ordinal), or wrist circumference (cm). Because PR-AUC is bounded between 0% and 100%, we used fractional-logit regression (binomial family, logit link) [16] with all six characteristics entered simultaneously. E"ects are reported as average marginal e"ects in percentage points. In BECA-S6, we examined whether the number of false alarms per participant di"ered by sex, age (decades), BMI, Fitzpatrick skin tone, or wrist circumference (averaged over both wrists). We used a Poisson regression of the false alarm count of both wrists summed, with the logarithm of the monitored hours as an o"set and all five characteristics entered simultaneously. False alarms were counted at the threshold and debounce length that each cross-validation fold selected for 80% validation sensitivity. E"ects are reported as average marginal e"ects in false alarms per hour. For both models, confidence intervals and two-sided p-values were obtained by bootstrapping, resampling patients in BECA-S2 and cross-validation folds in BECA-S6 (as participants within a fold share a threshold), with Bonferroni correction per cohort (six and five comparisons, respectively; ω = 0.05). Collinearity was assessed with generalized variance inflation factors (GVIF), with GVIF < 5 considered safe [17].

3 Results

3.1 Analysis datasets

The BECA-S2 study recruited 32 patients, for whom 42 events of loss of pulse were annotated. The annotators showed substantial agreement [18] (Cohen's ϱ of 0.722), and Gwet's AC1, which is less a"ected by the low prevalence of loss of pulse, was 0.999. The overlap between the annotations is captured by a median Intersection over Union of 0.891. After data preparation, the analysis population consists of 31 patients with 33 loss-of-pulse events and 31 hours of data: one patient had no monitored interval of 30 seconds or longer, and 9 events were excluded because they lasted shorter than 3 seconds (6), occurred in monitored intervals shorter than 30 seconds (2), or lacked ECG/ABP coverage (1). An example of an annotated loss-of-pulse event, preceded by electrical VF induction, is shown in Figure 2.

The BECA-S6 study recruited 102 participants, of whom 2 were excluded due to general health concerns and limited mobility, leaving 100 participants in the analysis population contributing 156.7 wrist-hours (median of 1.59 wrist-hours per participant). The population flowcharts and patient and participant characteristics for both studies can be found in Figure 3 and Table 1, respectively. The BECA-S2 patients were predominantly male (80.6%) and had light skin tones (Fitzpatrick type I or II in 27 of the 28 patients with a recorded skin tone), whereas the BECA-S6 participants were more balanced in sex (56.0% male) and mostly had Fitzpatrick type III (75.0%).

The 22 BECA-S2 patients with events were used to define a 22-fold cross-validation procedure, over which the 9 BECA-S2 patients without events and the 100 BECA-S6 participants were distributed. In each fold, 16 BECA-S2 patients with events (plus 3-9 without events) and 70-76 BECA-S6 participants were used for training, 5 patients with events (plus 0-5 without events) and 20-25 participants for validation, and 1 patient with events (plus 0-1 without events) and 4-5 participants for testing.

3.2 Target leakage mitigation analysis

Of the 33 events, 13 events in 7 patients were not preceded by a gap and were therefore eligible for the arrest-context probe. With no target leakage mitigation, i.e., a gradient-penalty weight of w = 0, injecting a synthetic gap in the arrest context shifted the model's per-second score by a mean of 4.273 logit units (n = 7), corresponding to 35.3% (95% CI: 11.5 to 61.4%) of the discriminative range. In the arrest-free context, the shift in score was 0.809 logit units or 6.69% (95% CI: 4.78 to 9.88%) of the discriminative range (n = 131 patients and participants). The tolerance tests for both contexts could not show that these shifts fall within the 5% tolerance (p tol = 0.990 and p tol = 0.955, respectively), indicating the need for mitigation. As the models at gradient-penalty weights 10 6 and 10 7 showed discriminative ranges (1.43 and 0.17 logit units) below 25% of that of the unmitigated model (12.09 logit units), we considered these models as collapsed, excluding them from the weight selection. For the remaining weights, we observed a decrease in target leakage e"ects in both arrest-and arrest-free contexts with increasing weight, indicating that the gradient penalty a"ects target leakage (see Figure 4(a) and (b)).

The subsequent fixed-sequence testing led to the adoption of 10 5 , which passed both tolerance tests, whereas the next weight in the sequence (10 4 ) failed the arrest-context test and ended the sequence. At 10 5 , we observed an arrest-context shift in score of ↑0.045 logit units, corresponding to ↑1.23% (95% CI: ↑3.46 to ↑0.09%) of the discriminative range. In an arrest-free context, the mean shift in score is ↑0.008 or ↑0.23% (95% CI: ↑0.49 to ↑0.03%) of the discriminative range. Both fall within the tolerance (p tol = 0.003 and p tol < 0.001, respectively). The reduction in leakage was accompanied by a lower test-set PR-AUC point estimate, from 68.7% (95% CI: 54.7 to 81.7%) at gradient-penalty weight 0 to 52.4% (95% CI: 38.6 to 66.2%) at weight 10 5 (see Figure 4(c)). The paired di"erence of ↑16.3 pp (95% CI: ↑34.1 to 1.8) was not statistically significant (two-sided paired bootstrap, p = 0.079).

4 Discussion

In this work, a model detected loss of pulse in wrist-PPG and accelerometry during routine cardiac procedures (BECA-S2) and event-free free-living conditions (BECA-S6) with a second-level AUC-ROC of 86.8% (95% CI: 79.7 to 92.5%) and PR-AUC of 52.4% (95% CI: 38.6 to 66.2%). At event level, with false alarms counted in free-living conditions, the false alarm rate remained zero up to a sensitivity of 48.5% (95% CI: 32.1 to 65.5%), after which it increased to 7.36 false alarms per hour at a sensitivity of 80%; the 95% sensitivity target was not reached within the evaluated false alarm rate range (up to 100 alarms per hour). In other words, about half of the loss-of-pulse events were detected without any false alarms in the roughly 157 wrist-hours of daily-life data, but higher sensitivities came at the cost of many false alarms.

While this work evaluates false alarm rate on a separate dataset capturing free-living conditions, counting false alarms on the BECA-S2 data captured during cardiac procedures instead gave a higher sensitivity at a false alarm rate of zero (63.6%, 95% CI: 48.3 to 78.9%, versus 48.5%) and slightly lower false alarm rates at 60% and 80% sensitivity (0.00 versus 0.39 and 6.47 versus 7.36 false alarms per hour), with overlapping confidence intervals throughout; neither reached 95% sensitivity. The di"erence at low false alarm rates could be explained by the state of the wearer: the free-living conditions in BECA-S6 may form a bigger challenge than the anesthetized and supine conditions in BECA-S2.

The DETECT studies used induced arrests in patients during cardiac procedures, including VT ablation and implantable defibrillator implantation, and validated their algorithm in independent patients. They reported sensitivities of 92-98% at 0.12-0.26 false alarms per hour [7, 8]. At comparable sensitivity (90%), our model produced more false alarms: 14.16 per hour (lower 95% confidence bound: 7.36). This may relate to the di"erences between the datasets and algorithms: DETECT used a fixed rule-based algorithm developed on a considerably larger number of in-hospital patients (205 in DETECT-1), whereas our model was trained and evaluated on a combination of 31 in-hospital patients and 100 participants in free-living conditions.

The HEART-SAFE results (90.3% sensitivity at roughly 0.06 false alarms per hour) cannot be compared with ours, as their algorithm was developed and tested on the same data [9], so that overfitting to these data cannot be ruled out and their performance estimates may be optimistic. Google Research's algorithm was developed and tested mainly outside the clinical setting, on healthy volunteers and stunt performers with tourniquet-induced peripheral pulselessness and on free-living data. It reported a sensitivity of 67.2% at 1.34 false alarms per user-year, equivalent to about 1.5 ≃ 10 ↓4 false alarms per hour [6]. This false alarm rate was based on 21.67 user-years of free-living data, whereas our 157 wrist-hours of BECA-S6 data cannot resolve false alarm rates with such resolution. However, given the need for an acceptably low false alarm rate, our data agree with Google's choice of a low-sensitivity operating point.

A methodological point of wider relevance is target leakage. In studies where loss of pulse is induced or terminated by a procedure, such as cu" inflation, electrical VF induction, or defibrillation shocks, the procedure leaves signal artifacts that coincide with the label, and a model can exploit these artifacts instead of detecting the loss of pulse itself. In our study, not dealing with induction and shock artifacts as a form of target leakage would have increased the model's scores: inserting a synthetic gap, which stands in for an induction or shock artifact, in the arrest context shifted the unmitigated model's scores upward by 35.3% of its discriminative range, far outside the 5% tolerance. A gradient penalty on the model's dependence on gapped input reduced this shift to ↑1.23% (95% CI: ↑3.46 to ↑0.09%) at a penalty weight of 10 5 . While the PR-AUC point estimate was lower with the penalty (68.7% versus 52.4%), the paired di"erence (↑16.3 pp, 95% CI: ↑34.1 to 1.8) was not statistically significant (p = 0.079). To our knowledge, this is the first study of smartwatch-based loss-of-pulse detection to mitigate target leakage. We recommend that future studies with induced or procedure-related events either avoid developing models on such data, as Google Research did with their electrophysiology-laboratory recordings of induced VF [6], or adopt both the synthetic-gap test, which can be applied to any trained model without retraining, and a mitigation strategy. Without mitigation, performance estimates risk being overly optimistic.

Exploratory subgroup analyses found no evidence that PR-AUC in BECA-S2 or the false alarm rate in BECA-S6 di"ered by participant characteristics. These analyses had limited power, in particular for sex and procedure type in the small BECA-S2 cohort and for the darker Fitzpatrick skin tones (IV-V, with no type VI), and therefore cannot establish equal performance across subgroups; larger and more diverse cohorts are needed.

4.1 Limitations

First, while false alarm rates were tested in out-of-hospital conditions, sensitivity was tested only on loss-of-pulse events that occurred in clinical settings during cardiac procedures, in patients who were typically sedated or anesthetized, lying supine, and largely motionless. Real OHCA occurs in free-living conditions with di"erent posture, motion, and circulatory dynamics, so our results cannot be directly translated to real OHCA.

Second, sensitivity and false alarm rate were measured in cohorts with di"erent demographics: the BECA-S6 cohort is relatively young and healthy (median age 31) compared to the BECA-S2 cohort with older patients (median age 60) who had cardiac disease.

Third, the BECA-S2 cohort is small (33 events in 22 patients), so all estimates have wide confidence intervals, and the statistical tests had limited power. This applies in particular to the target leakage test, which could only use the 13 events in 7 patients not preceded by a gap, and to the subgroup analysis, which included 20 patients, of whom only three were female. Similarly, darker skin types were underrepresented: only one BECA-S2 patient had a Fitzpatrick skin type darker than type II. Future studies should include more data, more female patients, and more participants with darker skin types to produce narrower confidence intervals.

Lastly, the loss-of-pulse events were shorter (median 10 s) than the loss of pulse in most OHCA cases, leading to shorter selected debounce lengths. Longer debounce lengths are likely to have led to fewer false alarms, making our performance estimations conservative. Future studies should include longer loss-of-pulse events to arrive at more realistic performance estimates.

5 Conclusion

We evaluated smartwatch-based detection of loss of pulse in cohorts of patients undergoing cardiac procedures and participants in free-living conditions. The model reached an AUC-ROC of 86.8% and a PR-AUC of 52.4%. About half of the loss-of-pulse events (48.5% sensitivity) were detected without any false alarms in daily-life data of healthy volunteers. Higher sensitivities came at the cost of more false alarms. Target leakage a"ected the model scores; a gradient penalty mitigated it, and we recommend that future studies test for target leakage and mitigate it. These results show the feasibility of smartwatch-based loss-of-pulse detection and provide a basis for larger studies with data more representative of real OHCA events.
