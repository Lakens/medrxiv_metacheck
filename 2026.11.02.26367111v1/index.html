Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Introduction

Cataract and glaucoma frequently coexist. Lens extraction alone lowers intraocular pressure (IOP) by approximately 2-4 mmHg in glaucomatous eyes, with greater reductions associated with higher preoperative IOP [1] [2] [3] [4] [5]. Phacoemulsification combined with minimally invasive glaucoma surgery (MIGS) is routinely performed when additional IOP control or a reduction in medication burden is warranted. The incremental efficacy of the most widely used trabecular and canalicular devices over phacoemulsification alone is modest: pivotal randomized trials demonstrated between-group differences of 1.6-2.3 mmHg in unmedicated diurnal IOP reduction at 24 months [6, 7]. Canal interventions are mostly proximal to the collector channels and episcleral veins, and their pressure-lowering efficacy is therefore constrained by distal outflow resistance and episcleral venous pressure.

The uveoscleral pathway permits aqueous humor to traverse the ciliary interstitium into the supraciliary and suprachoroidal spaces, without the lower pressure limit imposed by episcleral venous pressure. Surgical cyclodialysis accesses this pathway, but its outcomes have historically been unpredictable. Cyclodialysis clefts are susceptible to spontaneous postoperative closure, which can cause IOP spikes and diminish the pressure-lowering effect. Cleft reinforcement is therefore indicated to maintain patency and provide sustained IOP reduction.

Scleral allograft-reinforced cyclodialysis entails creation of a controlled ab-interno cyclodialysis cleft and placement of shaped segments of minimally modified homologous, non-resorbable human scleral allograft within the supraciliary space to maintain cleft patency [8]. Published studies have reported first-in-human results, 12-month multicenter outcomes, safety outcomes in a large clinical series, single-center 24-month outcomes, and 12-month outcomes following standalone surgery [8] [9] [10] [11] [12]. However, multicenter safety and efficacy outcomes at 24 months have not yet been reported.

We report 24-month outcomes from two parallel cohorts of the CREST registry: phacoemulsification combined with allograft-reinforced cyclodialysis in phakic eyes (Cohort 1), and standalone allograft-reinforced cyclodialysis (Cohort 2). The cohorts answer different clinical questions and differ in lens status, baseline IOP, and referral pattern, so they are characterized in parallel and not compared with each other. Because IOP dependence on baseline pressure is a recurrent theme in MIGS reporting [5, 13], we examined it in the combined Cohort 1 as an exploratory analysis [14]. Enrollment numbers in the standalone cohort did not support subgroup stratification in the standalone Cohort 2.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

CREST (NCT05506423) is a prospective, multicenter, observational real-world evidence study that enrolls recipients of allograft-reinforced cyclodialysis using the AlloFlo Uveo™ system (Iantrek, Inc., White Plains, NY) at 17 sites in the United States and Central America. The study includes broad real-world inclusion criteria (diagnosis of open-angle glaucoma and bioreinforced cyclodialysis was attempted) with no exclusion criteria. Per-protocol, eyes are enrolled into one of two cohorts: Cohort 1 comprises eyes that underwent the study procedure concurrent with phacoemulsification in a single surgical session; Cohort 2 comprises eyes that underwent a standalone study procedure. As a real-world study, there is no medication washout, and treating surgeons have postoperative discretion to medicate eyes according to need and clinical best practice. Likewise, there are no postoperative medication or treatment policies in the CREST protocol.

Eyes in the present analysis were required to have had at least 24 months of post-surgical followup to allow for adequate long-term analysis of efficacy and safety. The present analysis reports outcomes as of the most recent data cutoff (31 January 2026). The CREST study is conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, under oversight of Advarra IRB (approval MOD02608603). All participants gave written informed consent.

Surgical Procedure

The ab-interno bio-interventional surgical (BIOS) technique has been previously described [8, 9]. Briefly, under topical or intracameral anesthesia, a cyclodialysis cleft is created in the supraciliary space and then acellular scleral allograft bio-scaffolds are placed in the cleft. The allograft spacer is composed of microtrephined scleral donor tissue (5 mm × 500 µm each), rather than a rigid polymer, and the procedure preserves the conjunctiva and does not result in a filtering bleb. Allograft tissue is processed in a minimally modified standard and comes preloaded in a handheld delivery cannula. The number of segments and quadrant were at the treating surgeon's discretion. Postoperative medications and the decision to withhold or continue glaucoma medications followed each surgeon's best practice for the individual patient and eye.

Measurements

IOP was measured by Goldmann applanation tonometry. Baseline IOP was the medicated IOP at the preoperative visit on the patient's current regimen. Medications were counted by active ingredient, with fixed combinations counted as two and oral carbonic anhydrase inhibitors counted as one. Best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) was converted to logMAR. When performed per clinician judgement, endothelial cell density (ECD) was captured and measured by specular microscopy. No reading center was employed, and measurements were verified for quality in the study's data management process. Allograft position was assessed by gonioscopy.

Outcomes

Effectiveness outcomes were: mean changes in medicated IOP and number of medications from baseline to Month 24 in each cohort, the proportions of eyes with ≥ 20% IOP reduction and with IOP ≤ 18 and ≤ 15 mmHg, and composite responders achieving the same IOP thresholds without increase in medications from baseline or secondary IOP-lowering procedures. Additional outcomes assessed were rates of secondary IOP-lowering procedures, change in visual acuity, and change in ECD. Time to reoperation was estimated by the Kaplan-Meier method with complementary log-log confidence intervals. Eyes without an event were censored at their last recorded study visit. Safety outcomes were prospectively defined adverse events: clinical hypotony, choroidal effusion or folds, hyphema > 2 mm, loss of ≥ 2 lines of visual acuity at ≥ 90 days, IOP elevation ≥ 10 mmHg above baseline > 30 days after surgery, macular edema, confirmed visual field mean deviation worsening ≥ 2.5 dB, retinal complications, uveitis, and unplanned surgical reintervention, in addition to all other ocular adverse events.

Handling of Secondary Procedures

Two definitions are distinguished and reported separately throughout. A secondary IOP-lowering procedure is any procedure performed to provide sustained IOP reduction and is the event that governs censoring for efficacy assessments. Anterior chamber paracentesis and bleb needling or revision are not counted as censoring procedures; all other IOP-lowering procedures result in censoring at the time they are performed. Reoperation counts incisional surgeries only, excluding laser procedures, and is the endpoint of the Kaplan-Meier analysis.

The primary analysis used a while-on-treatment strategy, excluding values recorded after a secondary procedure, so efficacy estimates describe eyes managed with the index procedure and medication without confounding from subsequent procedures. Composite responder analyses were calculated on all eyes attending Month 24, with eyes undergoing a secondary procedure counted as non-responders. A sensitivity analysis was performed in which eyes with a secondary IOP-lowering procedure were assigned their baseline IOP and medication count and classed as non-responders.

Statistical Analyses

Continuous variables, including number of medications, are summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and IOP is reported as mean with 95% confidence interval (CI). Within-cohort changes from baseline were tested with the paired t-test, the change in the proportion of medication-free eyes with McNemar's exact test, and confidence intervals for mean changes and for mean percentage changes are t-based. Both eyes of some patients were included, and all data were analyzed at the eye level without adjustment for inter-eye correlation [15].

Because baseline medicated IOP in Cohort 1 ranged from 9 to 32 mmHg, eyes were stratified by baseline medicated IOP and analyzed in three tiers: low (≤ 15 mmHg), moderate (16) (17) (18) (19) (20) and high (≥ 21 mmHg). A single pooled mean would otherwise blend eyes operated to lower pressure with eyes already at target operated to reduce medication burden. Missing data were handled by complete-case analysis without imputation. Analyses were performed in R version (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Cohort 2: Standalone Allograft-Reinforced Cyclodialysis

In the 23 eyes of the primary analysis, mean IOP decreased from 20.0 mmHg (18.0, 22.1) at baseline to 13.7 mmHg (12.1, 15.2) at Month 24, a change of -6.4 mmHg (-9.0, -3.8; p < 0.001), a relative reduction of 27.1% (-40.4%, -13.7%; Fig. 1). Medications decreased from 2.8 ± 1.3 at baseline to 1.6 ± 1.5 at Month 24, a reduction of -1.2 ± 1.7 medications (p = 0.004); 52.2% of eyes were using fewer medications at Month 24 than they were at baseline, and medication-free eyes rose from 4.3% to 30.4% (p = 0.031), with 6 eyes becoming medicationfree and none losing that status. A total of 16 eyes (69.6%) achieved a ≥ 20% reduction, 13 (56.5%) a ≥ 30% reduction, and 21 (91.3%) had IOP ≤ 18 mmHg and 17 (73.9%) had IOP ≤ 15 mmHg (Fig. 3). On the composite definition applied to all 32 eyes, the corresponding proportions were 40.6%, 31.3%, 56.3% and 43.8%. Kaplan-Meier freedom from reoperation at 24 months was 84.4% (66.5%, 93.2%; Fig. 2).

In the sensitivity analysis, 9 eyes with a secondary procedure were counted as failures and their baseline IOP and medication counts carried forward. The Month 24 IOP change was -4.6 mmHg (-6.7, -2.5; -19.5%; p < 0.001) with 50.0% achieving a ≥ 20% reduction.

Safety

Adverse events (AEs) are summarized in Table 3 for the 208 eyes that attended Month 24. At least one ocular AE occurred in 32 Cohort 1 eyes (18.2%) and 9 Cohort 2 eyes (28.1%). Rates of procedurally related events were low and comparable between the cohorts. No eye developed clinical hypotony or choroidal effusion or folds. One Cohort 1 eye had anterior rotation of the ciliary body with anterior displacement of the intraocular lens, assessed as probably related to the allograft and managed with atropine. There was one event each of corneal abrasion and retained cortical fragment requiring removal. Loss of ≥ 2 lines of acuity at ≥ 90 days occurred in 8 eyes (3.8%) and confirmed visual field worsening ≥ 2.5 dB in 9 eyes (4.3%). BCVA improved from 0.38 ± 0.50 logMAR at baseline to 0.08 ± 0.18 at Month 24 in Cohort 1 (N = 127; mean change: -0.30 ± 0.48; p < 0.001) and from 0.43 ± 0.66 logMAR at baseline to 0.27 ± 0.28 at Month 24 in Cohort 2 (N = 23; mean change: -0.16 ± 0.64; p = 0.23).

Serious ocular AEs occurred in 6 eyes: 4 (2.3%) in Cohort 1 and 2 (6.2%) in Cohort 2. These comprised 4 eyes with best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) loss of ≥ 2 lines measured ≥ 90 days postoperatively, 1 with early BCVA loss, and 1 with IOP elevated ≥ 10 mmHg above baseline.

The four serious events in Cohort 1 resolved without visual sequelae; the two serious events in Cohort 2 remained unresolved at the time of analysis. In one, BCVA fell from 20/30 to 20/100 in association with an epiretinal membrane and was judged unrelated to the allograft. In the other, an eye with a baseline IOP of 35 mmHg recorded 45 mmHg on the day after a second cyclophotocoagulation, then settled to 7 mmHg on two medications by Month 24 with the event remaining open on the case report form.

Seriousness was assigned by the investigator using standard regulatory criteria and not by the magnitude or timing of the acuity change. Therefore, the 4 serious BCVA losses appear twice in Table 3: once in the "any ocular AE" category (8 eyes [3.8%]) and again the "any serious ocular AE" category (4 eyes [1.9%]). Likewise, the 1 serious IOP elevation appears twice in Table 3.

There were no reports of secondary allograft migration throughout the analysis period. At Month 24, gonioscopy was performed in 156 eyes. Of these, the allograft was optimally positioned in 140 (89.7%), the cleft was open around the allograft in 18 (11.5%), and no eye had the allograft touching the cornea at any visit, including Month 24. Partial peripheral anterior synechiae (PAS) were recorded in 9 eyes (5.8%) and total synechiae in 6 (3.8%).

In the limited subset of evaluable patients with real-world specular microscopy, an exploratory analysis revealed a rate of ECD loss below the 10% threshold anticipated for phaco-MIGS [16, 17]: 8.4% by Month 24 in the 34 Cohort 1 eyes (from a baseline of 2,415 ± 385 to 2,219 ± 430 cells/mm²) and 3.0% in the 3 Cohort 2 eyes (from a baseline of 2,547 ± 373 to 2,458 ± 338 cells/mm²). Corneal edema occurred in 3 eyes, all resolved, and no eye developed corneal decompensation.

Discussion

Efficacy

In this multicenter registry, allograft-reinforced cyclodialysis lowered both IOP and medication burden on average in both cohorts. The IOP-lowering effect was more pronounced in the standalone population (Cohort 2), where baseline pressure was much higher than in the phacocombined cohort (Cohort 1), in which eyes were mostly controlled and the therapeutic objective is often medication reduction.

When combined with phacoemulsification, the procedure lowered medicated IOP by 3.1 mmHg and medication use by 0.5 from a mean baseline of 18.0 mmHg; performed standalone, it lowered IOP by 6.4 mmHg (27.1%) and medication use by 1.2 from 20.0 mmHg in eyes managed without further surgery. Nearly half of phaco-combined eyes and three in ten standalone eyes were controlled on no glaucoma medication at 24 months. No eye developed clinically significant hypotony or choroidal effusion. More eyes in the standalone cohort needed secondary incisional surgery for optimal IOP control.

The two cohorts represent distinct, clinically selected real-world populations. At baseline, the standalone population (Cohort 2) had higher mean medicated IOP than the phaco-combined population (Cohort 1; 20.8 versus 17.9 mmHg) despite approximately twice as many glaucoma medications (2.9 versus 1.4 agents); 56.3% versus 23.9% of eyes had baseline IOP ≥21 mmHg.

These differences fit a paradigm in which intervention during cataract surgery often reduces medication burden while maintaining pressure control, whereas standalone intervention addresses the need for additional IOP lowering despite substantial medical therapy. Similar selection patterns are reported for other MIGS procedures: in the iStent inject series of Guedes et al., standalone eyes had higher baseline IOP and medication use (17.8 mmHg on 2.60 medications versus 16.0 mmHg on 2.14) [18], and Hengerer et al. reported greater baseline pressure, medication burden, and prior glaucoma procedures in standalone eyes [19]. Sieck et al.

found the same pattern with Kahook Dual Blade goniotomy: 20.4 mmHg on 3.1 medications in standalone eyes versus 16.7 mmHg on 1.9 in combined eyes [20]. Outcomes also depend on baseline pressure: in the phaco-Hydrus study of Ang et al., normal-tension eyes starting at 13.5 mmHg sustained medication reduction without significant further IOP lowering [21]. In the phaco-combined OMNI canaloplasty-trabeculotomy arm of ROMEO, eyes with baseline IOP ≤18 mmHg maintained pressure control (14.1 to 13.4 mmHg) while medications fell from 1.6 to 0.9, whereas eyes above 18 mmHg reduced both IOP (21.9 to 15.1 mmHg) and medications (2.0 to 1.1) at 12 months [22]. Collectively, these findings support interpreting the CREST cohorts according to their differing baseline treatment needs, rather than attributing their differing outcomes to surgical setting alone.

Safety was comparable to canal-based interventions (e.g., iStent, canaloplasty, goniotomy) for postoperative hyphema, IOP spikes, and other key AEs, including the low rates of serious AEs, which, as anticipated, were more frequent in the more severe standalone group (Cohort 2) than the mild-to-moderate phaco-combined group (Cohort 1). Table 4 places the CREST findings alongside published 24-month MIGS outcomes; medicated real-world studies are the most relevant comparators. CREST measured medicated IOP, whereas pivotal randomized trials used medication washout and higher untreated baseline pressures. In the iStent inject and HORIZON trials, phacoemulsification alone reduced unmedicated diurnal IOP by 5.4 and 5.3 mmHg, respectively, with incremental device effects of 1.6 and 2.3 mmHg [6, 7]. Medication status materially affects observed outcomes: a meta-analysis of phacoemulsification-alone trial arms reported 12-month reductions of 5.25 mmHg with washout versus 3.13 mmHg without, falling to 1.81 mmHg after excluding an outlier [3]. Because medication status, baseline pressure, and patient selection influence measured reductions, subtracting a cataract-surgery effect from another population would not isolate AlloFlo's contribution. Its incremental efficacy in combined surgery therefore remains undetermined.

Within medicated real-world data, Cohort 1's 3.1-mmHg reduction from 18.0 mmHg, with a 0.5agent medication reduction, is numerically consistent with established phaco-MIGS outcomes. In a 340-eye multicenter phaco-iStent inject series, IOP decreased from 16.4 to 13.7 mmHg and medications from 1.49 to 0.49 at 24 months [23]. Propensity-matched Fight Glaucoma Blindness registry cohorts showed reductions of 3.1 mmHg with iStent inject and 2.3 mmHg with Hydrus, with medication reductions of 1.0 and 0.5 agents [24]. Larger reductions are reported in canaloplasty populations starting higher: Gallardo's mixed standalone and phaco-combined iTrack series showed IOP falling from 20.0 to 13.5 mmHg and medications from 2.77 to 1.7 [25]. In the two-year ROMEO extension, OMNI canaloplasty-trabeculotomy reduced IOP by 6.1 mmHg in combined eyes and 7.4 mmHg in standalone eyes starting above 18 mmHg, compared with 0.6 and 2.3 mmHg in the respective lower-pressure groups; medications decreased across all four subgroups [26]. These observations support interpreting pressure lowering jointly with baseline IOP and medication reduction, while recognizing that baseline-stratified changes may also reflect regression to the mean.

Cohort 2 addressed a different treatment context, with higher baseline medicated IOP despite greater medication use. Its primary 24-month reduction of 6.4 mmHg from 20.0 mmHg, with a 1.2-agent medication reduction, falls within the numerical range reported for real-world standalone canal-based surgery. IOP and medication measurements recorded after secondary IOP-lowering procedures were excluded from efficacy analyses, so subsequent interventions did not contribute to the reported reductions. Secondary procedures occurred in 28.1% of standalone versus 5.1% of combined eyes, while Kaplan-Meier estimates of incisional reoperation were 15.6% and 1.1%, respectively. A similar direction of association was observed in the IRIS Registry, where two-year reoperation estimates were 24% after standalone goniotomy/canaloplasty versus 6% with concurrent phacoemulsification [27]. Differences in patient selection, ascertainment, and reoperation definitions preclude direct comparisons of these rates.

Standalone eyes requiring further intervention had higher baseline IOP than those remaining in the primary efficacy analysis (22.8 versus 20.0 mmHg). The primary estimate consequently describes outcomes while eyes remained managed with the index procedure and medications, rather than outcomes across the entire original cohort. In the failure-inclusive sensitivity analysis, these eyes were assigned their baseline IOP and medication counts, yielding a more conservative standalone reduction of 4.6 mmHg, with 50% achieving ≥20% reduction. The findings therefore support sustained IOP and medication reductions through 24 months in eyes managed without further procedures, alongside a clinically relevant need for additional intervention in about onequarter of standalone eyes. Comparisons with other MIGS provide context but do not establish superiority or equivalence, and the combined-surgery findings cannot distinguish the contribution of allograft-reinforced cyclodialysis from that of phacoemulsification.

Safety

The observed safety profile of allograft-reinforced cyclodialysis was broadly consistent with established canal-based MIGS, for which hyphema, transient IOP elevation, and anteriorsegment inflammation are characteristic postoperative events. A meta-analysis of 95 studies encompassing 9,733 eyes found no increase in sight-threatening events with phaco-combined MIGS compared with phacoemulsification alone, although pooled hyphema rates varied by mechanism: 3.5% for trabecular bypass, 15.5% for non-GATT goniotomy, and 27.7% for GATT [28]. Phaco-combined OMNI canaloplasty-trabeculotomy produced hyphema, IOP spikes, and mild inflammation in 4%, 5%, and 11% of eyes [22], compared with 4%, 6%, and 13% after standalone OMNI surgery [29]. Kahook Dual Blade goniotomy was associated with transient Day-1 hyphema in 17.3% and Week-1 IOP elevations >10 mmHg in 10.2% [20], whereas the iStent inject pivotal trial demonstrated overall safety similar to cataract surgery alone through 24 months [6].

In the present CREST analysis, no clinically significant hypotony, choroidal effusion or folds, or corneal decompensation occurred, and no allograft-cornea contact was recorded at Month 24 gonioscopy. Prior allograft-reinforced cyclodialysis reports provide supportive context: among 243 eyes, postoperative hyphema occurred in 2% and resolved conservatively; one prolonged iritis event and one numeric hypotony event without maculopathy also resolved [10]. A separate 12-month standalone CREST analysis reported no persistent inflammation, allograft rejection, clinically significant hyphema, or scaffold migration, although IOP elevations ≥10 mmHg occurred in 17%, most resolving within 30 days [12]. These earlier findings should not be extrapolated to imply absence of such events throughout the present 24-month cohort.

Overall ocular adverse events, serious ocular adverse events, and protocol-defined IOP elevations were numerically more frequent in standalone than phaco-combined CREST eyes (28.1% versus 18.2%, 6.2% versus 2.3%, and 9.4% versus 4.0%, respectively). Six eyes experienced serious ocular events, including two standalone events unresolved at the analysis cutoff. Although procedure-related event rates were low and similar between cohorts, the contribution of baseline disease burden to these differences cannot be determined from this unmatched observational analysis. The findings therefore support a favorable observed safety profile, without establishing equivalence to other MIGS. In a review of 401 studies encompassing 39,381 eyes, inconsistent adverse-event definitions and incomplete capture of late endothelial loss and peripheral anterior synechiae materially limited cross-procedure comparisons; standardized reporting and longer prospective follow-up remain necessary [30].

Protocol-defined IOP elevations clustered early, and the mechanisms most likely to account for them are retained ophthalmic viscosurgical device, postoperative inflammation with fibrin occluding the cleft, and corticosteroid response, rather than the allograft itself. At Month 24, partial and total synechiae were recorded in 9 (5.8%) and 6 (3.8%) of 156 evaluable eyes, and no allograft-cornea contact was recorded at any time. For comparison, PAS occurred in 14.6% of Hydrus eyes during five-year HORIZON follow-up, without serious sequelae attributable to the Hydrus-associated adhesions [31]. These comparisons remain descriptive because cyclodialysis-cleft adhesions may differ anatomically and functionally from implant-associated PAS, and ascertainment and follow-up were not harmonized. In the CREST specular-microscopy subset, endothelial cell density declined by 8.4% at 24 months in 34 phacocombined eyes, with no detectable change in standalone eyes. This loss was numerically lower than the 13.1% with phaco-iStent inject and 12.3% with phacoemulsification alone in the pivotal trial, although the small, real-world CREST subset, without a reading center or protocolized specular microscopy, precludes inference of superior endothelial preservation [6]. The absence of corneal decompensation and of recorded allograft-cornea contact may partly reflect the compliant, homologous acellular scleral tissue and its placement within the supraciliary cleft, clear of the corneal endothelium. Compared with exogenous hardware, this tissue-based construct offers a plausible rationale for reduced mechanical irritation and foreign-body response; however, neither mechanism was directly measured, and material composition cannot be separated from positioning, geometry, or surgical technique. The results therefore support the biological rationale for allograft reinforcement without establishing superior biocompatibility or corneal and angle safety. Optional endothelial measurements, small evaluable subsets, and nonstandardized synechiae characterization warrant longer follow-up with serial specular microscopy and anatomically defined gonioscopic grading. The cleft was recorded as open around the allograft in 18 eyes (11.5%) at Month 24. Because no eye had allograft-cornea contact and the allograft was optimally positioned in 89.7%, this appearance is more consistent with a cleft that remains patent beyond the allograft footprint, and therefore with continued uveoscleral flow, than with displacement of the tissue. The converse finding of partial or total synechiae in 15 eyes may represent gradual remodeling of the cleft margins. Both readings are descriptive because gonioscopic appearance was not graded against a predefined anatomic scale.

No secondary allograft migration was reported through 24 months. This may reflect the compliant scleral tissue conforming to the endoscleral wall, with close tissue apposition and frictional engagement as plausible mechanisms of retention, although surface friction and fixation strength were not measured, and greater resistance to migration than metal or polymer implants has not been established.

Strengths and Limitations

This is the largest multicenter report of 24-month safety and effectiveness for allograftreinforced cyclodialysis, and it draws on prospectively collected data with protocol-defined adverse event categories.

Several limitations qualify these findings. The design is observational and single-arm, so in the combined cohort the contribution of the allograft procedure cannot be separated from that of phacoemulsification; only a randomized comparison can do that. IOP was measured on medication without washout, so these values are not comparable with pivotal trials that used washout. The two cohorts differed substantially in baseline pressure, medication burden and disease severity, were not matched, and are therefore reported separately without a formal between-cohort test of effectiveness. Stratification by baseline pressure was exploratory and unadjusted for multiplicity, and because tier membership is defined by the same measurement from which change is calculated, tier-level changes remain susceptible to regression to the mean.

The standalone cohort comprised 23 eyes in the primary analysis, which limits the precision of every estimate derived from it, and the 9 eyes that underwent a secondary procedure had higher baseline pressure, so the failure-inclusive estimate is the more conservative description. Specular microscopy was optional, as real-world standard of care does not include ECD biometry for glaucoma procedures, so endothelial estimates rest on a limited subset of eyes. Lastly, both eyes of 50 patients were included and analyzed at the eye level without adjustment for inter-eye correlation (baseline IOP r = 0.73, Month 24 r = 0.56), so confidence intervals may be modestly narrow. Follow-up beyond 24 months is not yet available, and the durability of cleft patency over 469 longer intervals remains unknown. 470
