Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 2 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Neuroinflammation is increasingly recognized as an early contributor to Alzheimer's disease and related dementias (AD/ADRD), yet accessible blood-based biomarkers of early brain microstructural changes remain limited, particularly in African Americans, who are at elevated risk and underrepresented in biomarker research. We examined associations between plasma inflammatory markers and diffusion MRI measures in 41 cognitively unimpaired older African Americans (mean age, 68.7 years). Plasma chemokines were quantified using a multiplex immunoassay, and voxel-wise general linear models and cluster-based analyses were performed to evaluate associations with mean diffusivity (MD), radial diffusivity (RD), mean kurtosis (MK), and radial kurtosis (RK), adjusting for age, sex, and education. Higher plasma TARC/CCL17 levels were associated with widespread increases in MD and RD across gray and white matter, whereas Eotaxin-3/CCL26 showed similar but more spatially restricted associations. Associations with kurtosis metrics were limited, and neither marker was associated with global cognition measured by the Montreal Cognitive Assessment. These findings indicate that diffusivity measures are more sensitive than kurtosis metrics for detecting inflammation-related microstructural alterations. Peripheral chemokines, particularly TARC/CCL17, may serve as accessible biomarkers of preclinical brain microstructural vulnerability relevant to AD/ADRD risk in older African Americans.

1 Background

Converging evidence from genetics, molecular neuropathology, and neuroimaging implicates immune dysregulation as an implicated contributor of neural vulnerability in Alzheimer's disease (AD) and ADrelated dementias (AD/ADRD) (Heneka et al., 2025; Jack et al., 2018). Genome-wide association studies have identified immune-related genes-including TREM2, CD33, and complement pathway components as key modulators of AD risk (Bellenguez et al., 2022), while postmortem and in-vivo studies demonstrate early microglial activation preceding overt neurodegeneration (Hansen et al., 2018). Importantly, inflammatory processes appear to emerge decades before clinical symptom onset, suggesting that neuroimmune signaling may contribute to early microstructural brain alterations rather than simply reflecting downstream pathology (Banks, 2005; Racine et al., 2016).

Peripheral inflammation may influence central nervous system structure through multiple mechanisms, including blood-brain barrier modulation, cytokine-mediated microglial activation, and oligodendrocyte dysfunction (Franklin and ffrench-Constant, 2017; Zheng et al., 2016). Chemokines represent a special class of cytokines that regulate immune cell trafficking and activation during inflammatory responses. Several chemokines have been implicated in neurodegenerative processes and AD pathology. Thymus and activation-regulated chemokine (TARC/CCL17) has been reported to increase in AD and other inflammatory conditions (Campbell et al., 1999; Imai et al., 1998).

Similarly, elevated cerebrospinal fluid levels of C-C motif chemokine ligand 26 (CCL26; also known as eotaxin-3) have been reported in prodromal AD (Westin et al., 2012). However, the microstructural substrates linking systemic immune activity to early brain vulnerability remain poorly characterized.

Diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) provides sensitive, non-invasive quantitative measures of cellular organization by characterizing water diffusion in relation to biological barriers. While mean diffusivity (MD) reflects overall water mobility, mean kurtosis (MK) captures deviations from Gaussian diffusion reflecting microstructural complexity and radial diffusivity (RD) and radial kurtosis (RK) are particularly sensitive to myelin integrity and microstructural complexity respectively (Plank et al., 2025; Wen et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2023). Because these diffusion metrics are influenced by tissue geometry, axonal organization, and microstructural complexity, the direction of their alterations may vary depending on the underlying biological processes and stage of pathology. Notably, increases in radial diffusivity have been linked to myelin disruption and inflammatory processes in both preclinical AD and related conditions (Chu et al., 2022; Dean et al., 2017; Racine et al., 2019), suggesting that diffusion MRI may capture early signatures of immune-mediated brain vulnerability.

Understanding these processes is particularly important in populations that are disproportionately affected by AD/ADRD yet underrepresented in biomarker research. African Americans are almost twice at risk of developing AD/ADRD compared to non-Hispanic whites (Barnes, 2022). Characterizing how systemic inflammation relates to early brain microstructural alterations in this population is critical for addressing health disparities and identifying modifiable risk pathways.

In the present study, we examined associations between plasma inflammatory markers and diffusion MRI metrics, including MD, MK, RD, and RK, in cognitively unimpaired older African Americans. We utilized both voxel-based and ROI-based approaches to characterize systemic inflammatory profiles. We identified robust associations between diffusion-derived measures of brain microstructure and two chemokines in particular: TARC and Eotaxin-3. Higher levels of these chemokines were consistently associated with increased MD and RD across both gray and white matter regions, suggesting reduced microstructural integrity. In contrast, associations with MK and RK, which are sensitive to tissue complexity and microstructural heterogeneity, were more regionally variable, indicating that the effects of systemic inflammation on brain microstructure may depend on local tissue organization and underlying biological processes 2 Methods and Materials 2.1 Participants 41 participants (29 females) were drawn from the longitudinal Pathways to Healthy Aging and Brain Health Alliance study at Rutgers University-Newark.

The study was approved by the Rutgers Institutional Review Board (eIRB) and conducted in accordance with the principles of the 1975 Helsinki Declaration. Inclusion and exclusion criteria are brought in Appendix. Forty-one participants met the inclusion criteria.

Cognitively unimpaired status was defined using education-adjusted Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) cutoffs validated for non-Hispanic Black individuals (Milani et al., 2018). Demographic and biomarker characteristics are summarized in Table 1.

2.2 Plasma Inflammatory Biomarkers

Fasting EDTA plasma samples were thawed on ice and centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 5 minutes at 4°C. All samples were assayed in duplicate and blinded to imaging data.

Inflammatory markers (Eotaxin-3, IL-1β, IL-5, IL-6, IL-7, IL-10, MCP-1, MIP-1α, TARC, TNF-α) were quantified using a customized multiplex U-PLEX (catalog# K15067M-2) assay (Meso Scale Discovery, Rockville, MD).

Samples were run at neat dilution. The plates were read on the MESO QuickPlex SQ 120 instrument and analyzed using the Discovery Workbench software. Concentrations were derived from an 8-point calibration curve using a 4-parameter logistic model with 1/y² weighting. Quality control (QC) was assessed by adding an in-house pooled plasma sample to each plate to measure the percentage coefficient of variance across plates. Inter-plate assay precision for the internal QC sample was as follows: Eotaxin-3 -8%, IL-10 -6.9%, IL-1β -10%, IL-5 -11.5%, IL-6 -9.3%, IL-7 -6.3%, MCP-1 -9.4%, MIP-1α -9.7%, TARC -5.4%, and TNFα -9.9%, indicating acceptable assay reliability.

2.3 MRI Acquisition MRI data were acquired on a 3T Siemens Prisma scanner (32-channel head coil) at the Rutgers University Brain Imaging Center. High-resolution T1-weighted images were obtained using an MP-RAGE sequence (1 mm isotropic resolution; TR = 2300 ms; TE = 2.98 ms; TI = 900 ms). Diffusion-weighted images were acquired with 2.0 mm isotropic resolution (TR = 3400 ms; TE = 71 ms), including 7 b0 volumes and 126 diffusion-weighted volumes across three shells (b = 500, 1000, 2000 s/mm²). Detailed MRI acquisition and curation information are brought in supplementary document. 2.4 MRI Data Processing

2.4.1 Anatomical Preprocessing

T1-weighted (T1w) images were corrected for intensity non-uniformity using N4 bias field correction (Tustison et al., 2010) implemented in ANTs 2.4.3 and used anatomical references throughout the workflow. Images were reoriented to AC-PC alignment and nonlinearly registered to the MNI152NLin2009cAsym template using symmetric normalization (SyN) (Avants et al., 2008). Brain extraction was performed using SynthStrip (Hoopes et al., 2022), and automated tissue segmentation was conducted using SynthSeg (Billot et al., 2023a (Billot et al., , 2023b) ) implemented in FreeSurfer (version 7.3.1).

2.4.2 Diffusion Preprocessing

Diffusion data were preprocessed using QSIPrep (version 1.1.1) (Cieslak et al., 2021), a robust and reproducible pipeline, which is built on Nipype (version 1.9.1) and follows the NiPreps framework. The pipeline integrates tools from FSL, MRtrix3, and ANTs within a standardized framework. Details are explained in supplementary materials.

2.4.3 Diffusion Model Fitting and Metric Derivation

Diffusion tensor and kurtosis models were estimated using DIPY (Garyfallidis et al., 2014). For each subject and session, a diffusion kurtosis imaging (DKI) model was fit to the preprocessed multi-shell DWI data within a brain mask using the corresponding gradients tables.

Maps of MD, RD, MK, and RK were derived from the fitted model. All derived maps were visually inspected to ensure data quality. For group-level analysis, diffusion metric maps were spatially normalized to MNI space using SPM12 in MATLAB 2024b.

2.5 Statistical Analyses

2.5.1 Whole-Brain Voxel-Wise Analyses

Voxel-wise associations between plasma inflammatory markers and diffusion metrics were examined using general linear models in SPM12.

Separate regression models were estimated for each diffusion maps and each inflammatory biomarker. In each model, the diffusion metric served as the dependent variable, with the inflammatory marker as the predictor of interest. Age, sex, and years of education were included as covariates of no interest.

Continuous variables were mean-centered prior to analysis. GM and WM explicit masks-created as explained in Appendix-were used to assess tissue-specific effects in all analyses.

Statistical inference was performed using a voxel-defining threshold of p < .001 (uncorrected), followed by cluster-level family-wise error (FWE) correction at p < .05. We tested for positive associations between MD and RD and inflammatory markers, while a negative association was tested between MK and RK and inflammatory markers.

2.5.2 Cluster-Based Analyses

To further characterize the direction and magnitude of significant voxel-wise effects, a secondary regionof-interest (ROI) analysis was performed based on clusters identified in the whole-brain analyses.

Significant clusters from each voxel-wise model were extracted from the SPM-generated cluster maps and converted into binary masks.

For each subject and diffusion map, mean diffusion values were extracted within each cluster mask. These cluster-level mean values were then used in follow-up regression analyses, with the corresponding inflammatory marker as the predictor of interest, while controlling for age, sex, and years of education.

From these models, standardized effect sizes and confidence intervals were estimated for each cluster.

3 Results

3.1 Whole-Brain Voxel-Wise

Among all markers tested, TARC and Eotaxin-3 demonstrated the most robust and spatially consistent associations with microstructural measures, surviving correction for multiple comparisons. In contrast, the remaining inflammatory markers showed more limited or non-significant effects at the whole-brain level.

For completeness and transparency, results for all tested markers are provided in the Supplementary Materials, while the main Results section focuses on findings related to TARC and Eotaxin-3.

Anatomical localization of gray matter (GM) clusters was determined using Harvard-Oxford Cortical atlas, Julich Histological atlas and AAL3 atlas, WM tracts were identified using the JHU ICBM-DTI-81 atlas and JHU white matter tractography atlas (Oishi et al., 2008). Detailed peak coordinates and cluster statistics for all significant findings are provided in the Supplementary Materials. Tables A-1-10, in the supplementary materials, present the summary statistics and cluster peak coordinate information for each voxel-wise analysis within GM and WM.

No clusters survived cluster-level FWE correction for the association between mean and radial kurtosis and Eotaxin-3 in either gray or white matter indicating a lack of significant relationships with microstructural complexity.

Exploratory analyses of the remaining inflammatory markers revealed a heterogeneous pattern of associations with diffusion MRI metrics. No significant associations were observed for TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-10, or MCP-1. In contrast, higher IL-5 levels were associated with increased MD, RD, and MK within localized gray matter regions, including the insula and occipital cortex, as well as the left superior longitudinal fasciculus (Supplementary Tables A11-A13). IL-6 demonstrated positive associations with MD and RD within bilateral frontal cortical regions (Supplementary Table A14), whereas IL-7 showed more widespread associations with increased MD and RD involving the temporal pole, hippocampus, middle frontal cortex, occipital cortex, and cerebellum (Supplementary Table A15 and Figure A1). Among these markers, MIP-1α exhibited the most extensive effects, showing widespread positive associations with MD across multiple cortical and subcortical gray matter regions and major white matter tracts (Supplementary Figure A2, Table A16), including the superior longitudinal fasciculus, inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus, corticospinal tract, anterior thalamic radiation, and posterior limb of the internal capsule.

3.1.1 Mean Diffusivity and TARC

Within gray matter, higher plasma TARC levels were associated with widespread increases in mean diffusivity, indicating reduced microstructural integrity. Posterior cortical regions showed the most prominent associations, including the lateral occipital cortex, angular gyrus, and superior parietal lobule. As presented in Figure 1 (panel B), in white matter, higher TARC levels were similarly associated with widespread increases in mean diffusivity, after FWER correction. The most prominent effects were observed in anterior white matter pathways, including the bilateral anterior corona radiata and forceps minor. Additional clusters were identified in major projection and association tracts, including the internal capsule, anterior thalamic radiation, and corticospinal tract. Posterior white matter regions were also involved, including the posterior corona radiata, posterior thalamic radiation, and occipital pathways such as the forceps major and inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus. Midline structures, including the corpus callosum and cingulum bundle, were consistently implicated.

3.1.2 Radial Diffusivity and TARC

Within gray matter, higher TARC levels were associated with widespread increases in radial diffusivity in a spatial distribution closely mirroring that observed for mean diffusivity, spanning posterior cortical, medial, frontal, temporal, limbic, subcortical, and cerebellar regions (Figure 1, Panel C; peak coordinates in Supplementary Materials).

In white matter, higher TARC levels were similarly associated with widespread increases in radial diffusivity, paralleling the mean-diffusivity findings across the same anterior and posterior projection, commissural, and limbic pathways (Figure 1, Panel D).

3.1.3 Mean Diffusivity and Eotaxin-3

In contrast to TARC, associations between Eotaxin-3 and mean diffusivity were more spatially restricted in both gray and white matter. Within gray matter, higher Eotaxin-3 levels were associated with increased mean diffusivity in a limited number of clusters surviving cluster-level FWER correction. Significant effects were observed in sensorimotor regions, including the precentral and postcentral gyri, as well as in frontal and limbic regions, including the frontal pole and temporal pole with extension into the parahippocampal gyrus and amygdala. Additional clusters were identified in posterior regions, including the inferior parietal lobule, as well as in cerebellar structures.

3.1.4 Radial Diffusivity and Eotaxin-3

Within gray matter, radial-diffusivity effects mirrored the mean-diffusivity distribution, involving sensorimotor, frontal, and limbic regions with smaller posterior parietal and cerebellar clusters (Figure 2, Panel C).

Within white matter, Eotaxin-3-related increases in radial diffusivity were minimal and restricted to a single small cluster in anterior regions, with limited anatomical specificity close to the right middle frontal gyrus with the cluster peak at (27, 30, 25). Figure 2 (Panel D) shows the corresponding statistical parametric map in WM.

3.1.5 Mean Kurtosis and TARC

Associations between TARC and mean kurtosis were more spatially restricted than those observed for diffusivity-based metrics and showed distinct patterns across gray and white matter.

Within gray matter, higher TARC levels were associated with limited alterations in mean kurtosis, with only two small clusters localized to sensorimotor and posterior regions, including the precentral gyrus and adjacent medial parietal areas, as well as occipital regions encompassing the calcarine and precuneus.

After correcting for the FWER at voxel-level, only two voxels within each cluster, survived.

In contrast, white matter exhibited more consistent and spatially distributed associations. Higher TARC levels were associated with significant alterations in mean kurtosis across major projection and commissural pathways, including the internal capsule, corona radiata, and corpus callosum. Additional involvement was observed in anterior white matter regions, including the anterior corona radiata and fronto-occipital pathways. Figure 3 shows the corresponding statistical parametric maps in WM.

3.1.6 Radial Kurtosis and TARC

Associations between TARC and radial kurtosis were limited and spatially restricted compared to those observed for diffusivity-based metrics.

Within gray matter, only a single small cluster survived cluster-level FWER correction at cluster-level, localized to the precentral region extending into medial parietal areas, however, this cluster did not survive FWER correction at voxel-level.

In white matter, a small number of clusters demonstrated significant associations, primarily within midline and projection pathways. These included the genu of the corpus callosum, as well as regions corresponding to the corona radiata and corticospinal tract. These effects did not survive the FWER correction at voxellevel.

3.2 Cluster-Based

Cluster-based ROI analyses were performed using masks derived from significant clusters (pFWE<0.05 at cluster-level) identified in the whole brain analyses. Tables A17-A28 present the cluster-based ROI analyses parameters.

3.2.1 TARC

Cluster-based analyses confirmed robust associations between plasma TARC levels and diffusion MRI measures in both gray and white matter. In gray matter, higher TARC levels were consistently associated with increased MD and RD across multiple cortical and subcortical regions, with the strongest effects observed in temporal, parietal, and occipital areas. Similar positive associations were observed in major white matter pathways, including the corona radiata, internal capsule, thalamic radiations, and corpus callosum. In contrast, kurtosis metrics (MK and RK) demonstrated weaker and less consistent associations, with effect estimates generally centered near zero (Supplementary Figure A3). Figure 4, panels A and B, shows the standardized regression coefficients (β) and 95% confidence intervals for the association between plasma TARC levels and diffusion MRI metrics (MD and RD) extracted from significant clusters identified in voxel-wise analyses.

3.2.2 Eotaxin-3

Cluster-based analyses revealed more limited associations for Eotaxin-3 compared with TARC. Higher Eotaxin-3 levels were associated with increased MD and RD in a small number of gray matter regions, including frontal, parietal, and limbic areas, as well as in select white matter tracts such as the anterior thalamic radiation and inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus. Effect sizes were generally smaller and confidence intervals wider than those observed for TARC, indicating greater variability and less extensive microstructural involvement. Panels C and D in Figure 4 present the standardized regression coefficients and 95% confidence intervals for significant Eotaxin-3-diffusion metric associations identified in the voxelwise analyses.

4 Discussion

This study examined the relationship between circulating inflammatory markers and brain microstructure in cognitively unimpaired older African Americans using a voxel-wise diffusion MRI framework followed by cluster-based ROI modeling. TARC and Eotaxin-3 showed the most consistent associations with diffusion metrics, with TARC demonstrating the strongest and most widespread effects. Many of the identified regions overlap with the default mode network, known to show early vulnerability in preclinical AD and to support episodic memory, suggesting that TARC-associated microstructural alterations may preferentially target networks at risk for early AD-related change (Brier et al., 2014; Racine et al., 2019).

These chemokines were dominantly positively associated with MD and RD across multiple cortical and subcortical regions, indicating reduced microstructural integrity and potential myelin vulnerability (Chu et al., 2022; Racine et al., 2019). These effects were confirmed by cluster-based analyses and were observed both in gray and white matter, suggesting a diffuse rather than regionally localized pathology, aligning with emerging models of early network-level vulnerability in preclinical neurodegenerative disease states (Jack et al., 2018). These associations are also consistent with prior DTI and other quantitative MRI studies in aging and AD, showing increased demyelination and reduced tissue integrity (Kantarci et al., 2014; Moallemian et al., 2026 Moallemian et al., , 2023)).

TARC (CCL17) is a chemokine involved in Th2 lymphocytes and regulatory T cell recruitment and plays a central role in immune cell trafficking and systemic inflammatory responses (Campbell et al., 1999; Imai et al., 1997). Although incompletely studied, elevated TARC levels have been reported in individuals with mild cognitive impairment and AD, and it has been included in blood-based biomarker panels for neurodegenerative disorders (Campanelli et al., 2025). Peripheral inflammatory mediators may contribute to neuroinflammation through blood-brain barrier interactions and immune-cell trafficking, while also disrupting oligodendrocyte function and myelin maintenance, processes that are critical for preserving white matter integrity and efficient neural communication (Banks, 2005; Franklin and ffrench-Constant, 2017; Heneka et al., 2015; Varatharaj and Galea, 2017). These mechanisms provide a biologically plausible explanation for the observed associations between higher TARC levels and increased diffusivity metrics.

Eotaxin-3/CCL26, which signals through CCR3 to recruit eosinophils and Th2 cells, shares the broader Th2 immune polarization context of TARC. Circulating eotaxins increase with aging and have been implicated in neurodegenerative processes (Huber et al., 2018). Although its associations were less spatially extensive than those of TARC, which may reflect differences in receptor distribution, signal abundance, or the regional specificity of CCR3-mediated neuroinflammatory processes in aging -both chemokines showed convergent relationships with diffusivity measures across overlapping frontal and limbic regions, suggesting a shared inflammatory pathway underlying early microstructural vulnerability for Th2-polarizing chemokines.

In contrast, kurtosis-based metrics showed fewer and more spatially selective associations, primarily involving TARC. Higher TARC levels were associated with lower radial kurtosis in WM regions, potentially reflecting subtle alterations in microstructural complexity, axonal organization, or myelin integrity (Fieremans et al., 2013).

Neither TARC nor Eotaxin-3 was associated with MoCA performance (TARC: β=-0.020 p=0.902; Eotaxin-3: β=-0.094 p=0.561), suggesting that inflammation-related microstructural alterations may precede detectable cognitive decline in this cognitively unimpaired cohort. Given evidence of elevated inflammatory marker levels among Black individuals across the lifespan (Wiley et al., 2025), these findings may reflect population-specific mechanisms of early brain vulnerability that merit investigation in larger and racially diverse cohorts. A major strength of this study is its focus on older African Americans, a population at elevated risk AD/ADRD risk that remains underrepresented in neuroimaging research (Barnes, 2022).

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design limited causal inference, and the modest sample size (n=41) precludes statistical power for subgroup analyses despite correction for multiple comparisons. In addition, plasma cytokines provide indirect measures of neuroinflammation and may not fully capture central immune processes. We did not adjust for vascular or cardiometabolic risk factors (e.g., hypertension, diabetes) or white-matter-hyperintensity burden, which influence both systemic inflammation and diffusion metrics and may contribute to the observed associations. Because the cluster-based analyses extracted effect estimates from clusters defined as significant in the same voxelwise models, these estimates are descriptive and non-independent rather than an independent validation.

In addition, multiple inflammatory markers, diffusion metrics, and tissue compartments were examined, so the TARC and Eotaxin-3 findings should be regarded as hypothesis-generating pending replication in larger, independent cohorts. Finally, restricted variability in MoCA scores (range 19-29, mean 23.4, SD 3.1) likely reduced sensitivity to detect associations between inflammatory markers and cognition.

In summary, higher circulating TARC and Eotaxin-3 levels were associated with diffusion MRI markers of reduced microstructural integrity, particularly increased MD and RD, in the absence of detectable cognitive impairment. Suggesting reduced microstructural integrity and myelin vulnerability in relation to systemic

Th2-polarizing inflammatory signaling. This combined voxel-wise and cluster-based framework provides a robust approach for linking peripheral immune activity to early brain changes relevant to aging and AD/ADRD risk.
