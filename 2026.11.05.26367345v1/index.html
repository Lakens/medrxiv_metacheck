Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Introduction: Mobile poultry vending and slaughtering practices in Bangladesh pose potential public health risks due to poor hygiene, unsafe waste disposal, and close human-animal interactions. Limited data exist on these practices and feasible interventions to improve biosecurity among mobile poultry vendors.

Methods: This mixed-method study was conducted in three phases across four sub-districts in three districts. Phase 1 involved 416 hours of structured observation, 40 in-depth vendor interviews, and 40 informal customer interviews. Phase 2 involved the development and pilot testing of an intervention package, including poultry killing cones, hand sanitizers, disinfectants, and a hygiene pamphlet, with 10 vendors. Phase 3 implemented the intervention among 20 vendors for one month, followed by 94 hours of observation and 17 customer interviews.

Result: Phase 1 found 1,959 slaughtering events with no use of personal protective equipment; slaughtering occurred in plastic jars (58%), directly on roads (21%), and roadside drains (11%).

Seventy percent of vendors reported discarding slaughtering waste directly into the environment, while 78% reported not knowing how poultry diseases could spread to other animals or humans.

During Phase 2, 9/10 vendors considered the killing cone useful, although bicycle users reported challenges related to weight and balance; the cone was subsequently modified. During Phase 3, 294 poultry were observed being slaughtered, with 79% using killing cones. Waste was stored in cone-attached boxes in 97% of cone uses, and vendors used hand sanitizer before eating in 75% of observed instances. Among 17 interviewed customers, 88% recognized the cone's role in safer waste disposal and 53% considered it useful overall.

Conclusion: This study highlights major hygiene and biosecurity gaps and demonstrates that low-cost, targeted interventions may improve biosecurity practices among mobile poultry vendors and reduce opportunities for environmental contamination in informal slaughter settings.

Introduction

In Bangladesh, Avian Influenza A (H5N1) virus is endemic in poultry (1). Since 2007, nine human cases of H5N1 have been reported, including one death (2). All cases had a history of exposure to poultry or poultry products and waste before illness (3, 4). For example, on March 15, 2011, urban surveillance by icddr,b in Kamalapur, Dhaka, identified a human case infected with highly pathogenic avian influenza (HPAI) H5N1 virus (4). Seven days before the onset of illness, the patient's mother purchased seven chickens from a mobile poultry vendor. The vendor slaughtered, de-feathered, and skinned the chickens. The patient's father further processed them inside the household, while the patient was present at the slaughtering site (4).

Mobile poultry vendors commonly sell chickens, pigeons, geese, and ducks using bicycles, a pedal-powered tricycle van, or by carrying birds by hand. They may source birds of different species from multiple locations and keep them together in close confinement during transport and sale, creating opportunities for avian influenza circulation, inter-species transmission, and viral reassortment (5) (6) (7) (8). Vendors often slaughter poultry at the point of sale and discard blood, feathers, and other waste directly into the environment. Improper disposal of slaughter waste and carcasses into the environment is a potential source of exposure to avian influenza or other pathogens for dogs, cats, and other domestic animals (9). A community-based ethnographic study in Bangladesh reported that during poultry slaughter, humans, domestic animals, and scavenging birds are frequently exposed to poultry blood, feathers, internal organs, and other processed byproducts discarded into the open environment (10). Beyond avian influenza, mobile poultry vending may contribute to broader zoonotic and environmental health risks, including bacterial contamination and the spread of antimicrobial-resistant organisms, particularly in settings with limited sanitation and biosecurity (11, 12). Avian influenza viruses circulate naturally among wild waterfowl and can spill over to domestic poultry through shared water sources and trading networks (13, 14). In Bangladesh's densely populated poultry sector, small-scale and backward farms dominate, while rapid urbanization has increased the informal and peri-urban poultry marketing system (10).

Surveillance databases and outbreak reports from the World Health Organization (WHO), World Organization for Animal Health (WOAH), and the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations (FAO) have documented recurrent avian influenza activity in Bangladesh and across South Asia, highlighting the public health relevance of informal poultry trading and onsite slaughtering practices (15) (16) (17). Most existing research has focused on live bird markets and formal slaughterhouses; comparatively fewer studies have examined the practices, slaughter locations, and waste disposal behaviors of mobile poultry vendors, particularly in urban and periurban settings (10, 18). The live-bird market system in Bangladesh comprises fixed markets, backward sellers, and mobile vendors; together, these systems sustain poultry demand and employment but also create a continuum of potential exposure pathways. Mobile vendors are uniquely important because they connect rural production systems with urban consumers, often moving between communities without formal biosecurity oversight (10, 19, 20).

Despite the importance, there is limited empirical evidence on the day-to-day practices of mobile poultry vendors, including sourcing, transport, slaughtering, hygiene, and waste management behaviors. Our previous scoping work identified gaps in hygiene practices and risk awareness during on-site slaughtering and carcass disposal, highlighting the need for contextspecific interventions. While mobile poultry vending is an essential component of the poultry value chain, the close interaction between humans, animals, and the environment in these settings underscores the importance of improving hygiene and reducing exposure pathways.

This study aimed to assess existing poultry handling, slaughtering, and waste disposal practices among mobile poultry vendors; develop and pilot a practical, low-cost hygiene and biosecurity intervention package; and evaluate its feasibility, acceptability, and potential to improve hygiene and biosecurity practices and reduce potential exposure risks.

Materials and methods

Study site

The team, consisting of social scientists, medical technicians, and a veterinary epidemiologist, each with several years of experience in zoonotic disease research, conducted this study in Mirpur and Mohammadpur (sub-districts of Dhaka) and in Mymensingh Sadar and Netrokona Sadar (sub-districts outside Dhaka) (Fig. 1). The study areas were selected purposively based on the availability of mobile poultry vendors, logistical feasibility due to the presence of ongoing avian influenza surveillance in nearby live-bird markets, and the opportunity to leverage existing research infrastructure and staff. Avian influenza surveillance in nearby live-bird markets facilitated our access to both the communities and the mobile poultry vendors. Because surveillance was limited to live bird markets while this study focused on mobile poultry vendors, this introduced bias is unlikely, as mobile poultry vendors and live bird market workers operate independently, sell poultry in different ways, and at different locations.

Study design and data collection

Between April 1, 2016, and March 31, 2017, we conducted this formative mixed-method study in three phases (Fig. 2). The study was guided by a grounded theory approach, allowing themes to emerge inductively from interviews and observations to explain factors influencing the acceptability of the poultry killing cone. Phase 1 included line listing of vendors, structured observation, in-depth interviews with vendors, and informal interviews with customers. A comprehensive list of mobile poultry vendors was developed in collaboration with community leaders and live bird market workers, and through snowball sampling to identify additional participants. To ensure adequacy of the sample size, we relied on data saturation. From the sampling frame, ten vendors were purposively selected from each of the four sub-districts to represent different transport types (bicycle, tricycle, and hand-carry) and diverse selling locations. We conducted 416 hours of structured observation of 40 vendors, observing each vendor from poultry collection through the end of daily sales over two consecutive days. This approach allowed us to capture variability in trading practices and to build rapport with participants, facilitating more open discussion during interviews. Trained social science researchers documented the number of poultry carried, the presence of apparently sick poultry, interactions with the customers, direct handling of poultry, slaughtering practices (who, where, and how), and disposal of blood and other waste. Poultry showing visible lethargy, ruffled feathers, nasal discharge, closed eyes, or reluctance to move were considered as apparently sick poultry during (21).

In Phase 1, we conducted 40 face-to-face in-depth interviews with the vendors observed to explore poultry trading, slaughtering, and waste disposal practices, and to clarify observation findings. Additionally, 40 informal interviews were conducted with purposively selected customers to understand purchasing behavior, slaughtering preference, waste disposal practices, knowledge on avian influenza, and suggestions for risk reduction. Customers were approached immediately after purchasing poultry, provided with study information, and interviewed upon consent using a standardized guide. The interview guides were developed collaboratively by the research team and pre-tested to ensure clarity and comprehension. Interviews were conducted in participant-preferred locations and audio-recorded with consent.

In phase 2, we developed and piloted an intervention package that included a poultry killing cone co-designed with vendors. Two cone sizes were developed to fit tricycles (Size-1: upper end 6", lower end 1") and bicycles (Size-2: upper end 6", lower end 1"). The cones were complemented with a waste collection box (20" × 14" × 4") and an underlying tray (20" × 10" × 3") to collect blood, with sand or sawdust used for absorption. The intervention package also included hand sanitizer, soap, detergent, sodium hypochlorite, a spray bottle, and an informational pamphlet illustrating hand hygiene and poultry waste disinfection practices. Before piloting, the intervention team demonstrated proper cone use, preparation of disinfectant solutions, and handwashing practices. The intervention was pilot-tested among 10 vendors (eight using tricycle vans and two using bicycles) for five consecutive days to assess its practicality, acceptability, and usability. During this period, an independent observation team conducted unannounced structured observations to document operational practices. Although brief, the fiveday pilot provided rapid, actionable feedback that informed refinement of the cone design, disinfection procedures, and training messages. Semi-structured interviews were conducted with vendors to assess feasibility and gather recommendations for design improvement.

Based on the feedback, the cone design was subsequently modified before Phase 3. The upper opening was widened from 6" to 8" to accommodate larger poultry, and the frame was replaced with a lighter metallic structure to reduce weight and improve stability, particularly for bicycle users.

In phase 3, the refined intervention package was provided to 20 mobile poultry vendors for one month. Vendors were purposively selected from the phase 1 sampling frame to ensure diversity in location, vendor type, and scale of operation. Vendors who participated in the baseline and pilot phases were excluded to avoid prior exposure influencing outcomes. Each vendor received a killing cone appropriate to their transport mode (bicycle or tricycle van). In locations with temporary selling sites, shared cones were installed, and one vendor, selected by peers, was responsible for maintaining hygiene, disinfection, and waste management. All vendors were briefed on the study objectives, and participation was voluntary. To minimize the potential selection bias, all eligible vendors in the study areas were invited to participate. During the one-month implementation period, a separate trained observation team conducted 94 hours of unobtrusive observations, defined as non-participatory observation of behavior in natural settings. This approach is particularly useful for evaluating the effectiveness of environmental interventions that aim to influence health-related behavior (22). Observers were present but did not interfere with vendor activities. The team systematically recorded practices related to slaughtering, cleaning, and waste management as public behavior. In addition, we conducted 20 in-depth interviews with participating vendors and 17 informal interviews with customers who purchased poultry from these vendors. Customers were recruited using the same approach as in earlier phases: they were approached immediately after purchase, informed about the study, and interviewed upon providing written consent. These interviews explored perceptions, satisfaction, and feedback regarding improvements in slaughtering and waste management practices associated with the intervention.

Data analysis

All quantitative data were entered into and analyzed in Microsoft Excel using descriptive statistics. For qualitative data, the research team expanded field notes and transcribed all interviews verbatim in Bengali using Microsoft Word. A preliminary coding framework was developed based on the initial research questions and study objectives, and subsequently refined through iterative reading and discussion among the investigators to incorporate emerging themes.

The transcripts were then manually coded using the finalized coding framework. Coded segments were organized thematically and summarized across key domains aligned with the study objectives. Data triangulation was performed by comparing findings across different sources, including interviews, field notes, and structured observations, to enhance the credibility and depth of interpretation. We collaboratively reviewed the coding process and thematic synthesis to improve reliability and minimize individual bias.

Ethics statement

We obtained informed written consent from all participants before conducting observations and interviews. We also sought written consent from the customers before conducting informal interviews. Poultry slaughtering and waste disposal practices

Eighty percent of vendors reported slaughtering both healthy and sick poultry.

Slaughtering was commonly conducted in customers' yards, bathrooms, inside a plastic jar, along roads, in drains, or near water sources, findings that were also confirmed through observation. We observed 1,959 slaughtering events. Personal protective equipment (PPE) was not used in any observed event. In 442 events (23%), vendors sought assistance from consumers during slaughtering. Slaughtering occurred in plastic jars (1,139 events; 58%), directly on the road (415 events; 21%), in roadside drains (214 events; 11%), and on newspapers (39 events; 2 %). Seventy percent of vendors reported discarding slaughtering waste directly into the environment. The remaining 30% reported storing waste in containers (e.g., plastic drums, buckets, polythene bags, or sacks) sometimes for sale to fisheries or low-income consumers.

Observation data showed that waste or dead poultry was discarded on roads, agricultural land, drains, open areas, bushes, ditches, ponds, or rivers. Vendors were also observed feeding slaughtering waste to crows and stray dogs.

Hygiene practices and use of personal protective equipment

Nearly 90% of vendors reported being unaware of proper hygiene practices and had never used PPE, such as face masks, aprons, gloves, or head covers. Almost all vendors (38/40) reported not washing their hands after handling poultry. Vendors reported cleaning their tricycle vans or bicycles approximately once per week, while poultry cages were cleaned daily or once every two days. Cages were washed in rivers, ponds, ditches, or at home. Slaughtering equipment was wiped with cloths or rinsed with water; however, most vendors reported cleaning plastic jars used for slaughtering with detergent. All participants reported washing their clothes daily with soap. During observation, vendors repeatedly wiped their hands with cloths (e.g., handkerchiefs) and consumed snacks without washing their hands.

Risk perception of avian influenza

Nearly half of the vendors were unfamiliar with the term "bird flu". Some could describe symptoms observed in poultry. About one-third recognized that poultry diseases could be contagious. Most vendors (78%) reported not knowing how diseases could spread from poultry to other animals or humans, and were unaware of preventive measures. Vendors were unaware of their potential role in disease transmission and/or the importance of safe slaughtering practices.

Phase 2: Intervention piloting and feedback

During the piloting (Table 1), most vendors (9/10) reported that the killing cone was useful for restraining birds, reducing mess, and improving hygiene during the slaughtering.

Vendors noted improved working comfort and environmental cleanliness, including reduced flies and odors. One vendor stated, "The provided killing cone has helped us a lot. Now, blood and waste no longer spread over the road. We can store the slaughtered poultry and waste properly; it's a real advantage".

Another vendor added, "It looks neat and attracts people. Earlier, customers used to scold us for spreading blood and waste in the environment, but now they appreciate this system".

Vendors also reported that the informational pamphlet helped reinforce hand hygiene and waste management practices. However, bicycle users reported challenges, including noise, added weight, and reduced balance and speed. One vendor also reported difficulty carrying disinfectant due to a lack of storage space. Although most vendors accepted the cone positively, some noted challenges in maintaining it. One vendor noted, "Blood dries quickly, and we do not always have enough water to wash it properly, even though we try with soap and disinfectant".

Another vendor stated, "It takes extra time to clean after each slaughter, but it keeps the place neat and stops the bad smell". Some vendors highlighted trade-offs: One said, "It does not increase or reduce my profit, but it adds extra labor". Based on this feedback, we made two key refinements to the cone design: (1) the upper opening was widened from 6″ to 8″ to better accommodate larger poultry, and (2) the frame was replaced with a lighter metallic structure to reduce weight and improve stability (Fig. 3). These modifications improved usability and stability before full implementation.

Phase 3: Implementation and assessment

Acceptability of the intervention

Vendors (Table 1) generally perceived the killing cone and waste box as acceptable and useful tools that improved cleanliness and reduced odor. Many reported that the intervention made their practices appear more hygienic to customers. One vendor noted, "After using this cone, customers said the setup looks clean and hygienic. Blood doesn't scatter on the road, and the environment stays clean." Another vendor shared a similar experience: "People used to avoid my tricycle because it looked dirty, but now they come and buy more poultry".

Some vendors reported perceived increases in sales. As one explained, "After using this, my daily income increased from 400 to 600 BDT; customers like clean work, so sales have increased". These were vendor-reported perceptions rather than objectively measured changes in sales.

During implementation, vendors acknowledged that hand sanitizer and waste disinfectants could help prevent disease transmission. Consistent with findings from the pilot phase, vendors using bicycles reported similar challenges with the killing cone; two participants recommended using lighter materials for cones. During participant observation, 294 poultry were slaughtered. Of these, 232 (79%) were slaughtered using a killing cone. Other slaughtering locations included roadside areas (11%), drains (2%), and other locations (7%). Waste was stored in the cone-attached waste boxes in 228 instances (97% of cone uses). Vendors were observed consuming snacks during trading; in 75% of such instances, they cleaned or rubbed their hands using sanitizer before eating. Vendors applied sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl) to waste 39 times, and 75% of vendors disposed of final waste in city corporation dustbins. Vendors did not use PPE at baseline but partially adopted hygiene materials, such as hand sanitizers, during implementation. Among 17 interviewed customers, 88% (15/17) recognized the cone's role in safer waste disposal, and 53% (9/17) considered it a useful tool overall. These percentages represent proportions of coded responses. Additional perceived benefits included access to freshly slaughtered poultry (29%; 5/17), convenience (24%; 4/17), and reduced exposure to previous slaughter waste (18%; 3/17). Customers also noted environmental cleanliness. One customer stated, "It's good that the road stays clean, and we are not afraid of any germs".

Discussion

The study findings indicate that mobile poultry vending involves multiple practices that may increase opportunities t for exposure to zoonotic pathogens at the human-animalenvironment interface. These practices included sourcing poultry from multiple locations, keeping different poultry together in confined cages, slaughtering poultry in open or poorly controlled settings, inadequate cleaning and hand hygiene, unsafe waste disposal, and handling of sick or dead birds (7, 10, 18). These practices, along with limited awareness of disease transmission and preventive measures, highlight important biosecurity gaps among mobile poultry vendors. The intervention package, including the killing cone, waste box, hand sanitizer, disinfectants, and educational pamphlet, was well accepted by vendors and customers and was associated with improved observed slaughtering, waste-management, and hand -hygiene practices during the implementation period. behaviors among. However, because the study did not measure pathogen transmission or environmental contamination, these findings should be interpreted as evidence of improved practices and reduced potential exposure pathways rather than confirmed reductions in infection or transmission.

Since the introduction of HPAI H5N1, 585 poultry outbreaks have been reported across 54 of Bangladesh's 64 districts. (23). Our study found that vendors sourced multiple poultry species from various locations and housed them together in cages. These cages were often poorly ventilated and lacked regular cleaning or disinfection. A companion study detected influenza A viruses in 17% of poultry samples, including H5 in 4% of chickens and 57% of ducks and H9 in 82% of chickens and 29% of ducks. This companion study also reported an association between door-to-door vending and virus detection (24). Although these findings were from a separate study and were not generated by the present intervention study, they provide relevant context for the poultry-handling practices observed here. Mixing poultry from different sources and species may create opportunities for circulation of avian influenza and other pathogens (18). In addition, slaughtering poultry along roads, in drains, or near water sources may increase opportunities for environmental exposure among people and animals such as dogs and crows. These practices are relevant in the context of recurrent detection of HPAI H5N1in scavenging birds in Bangladesh (25).

Our findings also highlight potential poultry-to-human exposure pathways. Live-bird market trading can provide opportunities for avian influenza virus exposure through the movement and handling of live poultry, slaughtering of sick birds, and inadequate use of protective measures (26). Vendors in our study transported poultry to residential areas and handled and slaughtered birds at customers' homes and other informal locations (7). During the slaughtering and bleeding, flapping poultry can generate droplets that may contaminate nearby surfaces (27).

Daily movement of vendors and poultry, together with potentially contaminated equipment, may also create opportunities for indirect exposure (7). Limited knowledge of avian influenza transmission pathways, combined with the lack of handwashing after handling poultry and before eating, further highlights potential occupational exposure among vendors. Similar exposure pathways have been reported among poultry market workers in Bangladesh (28, 29).

International guidelines discourage slaughtering poultry outside designated facilities; however, in low-resource settings, on-site slaughtering may remain common because of consumer preferences for freshly slaughtered poultry, cultural practices, and economic constraints (30, 31).

Within this context, the intervention was designed as a practical and low-cost approach to improve hygiene and biosecurity during informal poultry slaughtering. During the pilot phase, most vendors reported that the killing cone helped restrain birds, reduce mess, and improve hygiene. The intervention was further refined based on vendor feedback, including widening the upper opening and replacing the frame with a lighter metallic structure. This co-design process was particularly relevant for mobile vendors because the intervention needed to accommodate different transport modes and operating conditions. During Phase 3, 79% of observed slaughtering events used the killing cone, and waste was stored in the cone-attached waste box in 97% of cone-use events. These observations indicate that the intervention could be incorporated into routine slaughtering practices during the study period. The use of a containment device, with blood collected in a tray and waste stored in a dedicated container, may help reduce direct contact with poultry by-products and opportunities for environmental exposure (32). This approach is consistent with recommendations to strengthen hygiene and biosecurity in poultryhandling environments (33) and with previous research emphasizing improved slaughtering environments, sanitation, and waste management to reduce zoonotic disease risks (34). The intervention also addressed personal hygiene and waste disinfection. At baseline, almost all vendors reported not washing their hands after handling poultry, and no PPE use was observed during the 1,959 slaughtering events. During implementation, vendors used hand sanitizer before eating in 75% of observed eating instances, and sodium hypochlorite was applied to poultry waste on 39 occasions. Evidence from Bangladesh indicates that poultry workers who do not practice hand hygiene may have a higher risk of H5N1 infection (35). Our baseline findings are also consistent with previous studies among poultry workers and small-scale producers, where individuals were observed wiping blood with cloths rather than washing their hands (36).

Alcohol-based hand sanitizers containing 62-70% alcohol have been shown to rapidly inactivate HPAI H5N1 viruses (37). These findings support the inclusion of accessible hand-hygiene materials within interventions for informal poultry vendors. However, the observed use of sanitizer and disinfectants should be interpreted as a change in reported or observed practices, rather than evidence that the intervention reduced viral contamination.

Poultry waste is a recognized potential source of pathogens that can affect humans and animals; therefore, appropriate waste management is an important component of environmental hygiene and biosecurity (38, 39). Safe disposal and treatment of poultry waste may also provide environmental benefits through reduced pollution and resource recovery (40). In our study, vendors used sodium hypochlorite to treat waste and disposed of final waste in city corporation dustbins. The use of chlorine-based disinfectants is consistent with evidence on their effectiveness against HPAI H5N1 contamination in poultry environments (41). However, the intervention did not address all potential exposure pathways. In particular, it did not address airborne exposure, the broader occupational risks associated with handling live and sick poultry, or the movement of vendors and poultry between multiple locations. These factors require complementary measures, including appropriate infrastructure, regulatory engagement, risk communication, surveillance, and broader One Health approaches.

Customer feedback further supports the intervention's practical acceptability. Among the 17 interviewed customers, 15 (88%) recognized the killing cone's role in safer waste disposal, and 9 (53%) considered it useful overall. Customers also described environmental cleanliness, convenience, and reduced exposure to previous slaughter waste as perceived benefits. These findings suggest that visible improvements in cleanliness may support consumer acceptance of safer slaughtering practices. However, these findings were based on a small purposively selected group of customers and should be interpreted as perceptions rather than evidence of populationlevel acceptability or changes in purchasing behavior.

The findings have implications for improving biosecurity in informal poultry-vending systems. Mobile vendors operate at the intersection of poultry production, transportation, household consumption, and the environment, yet they often work without access to dedicated slaughtering facilities or formal biosecurity infrastructure. A context-specific intervention that combines a physical containment device with hand hygiene, waste management materials, and simple information may therefore provide a practical option for reducing opportunities for exposure in settings where formal slaughtering facilities are not readily available. However, the intervention should complement rather than replace broader measures addressing poultry movement, surveillance, slaughtering infrastructure, waste disposal systems, and regulatory oversight (42, 43). Further research should assess whether these changes can be sustained over longer periods and whether similar approaches are feasible in other urban and peri-urban settings.

Limitations

Our study has several limitations. First, it was conducted in only four sub-districts and did not employ random sampling or randomization; therefore, the findings may not be generalizable to all mobile poultry vendors in Bangladesh, and changes observed during Phase 3 cannot be attributed solely to the intervention. Second, a potential Hawthorne effect may have influenced vendor behavior because participants knew they were being observed, although the use of unobtrusive observation during implementation was intended to minimize this effect. Third, some activities may have been missed because observers could not capture all interactions occurring simultaneously. Fourth, the study was conducted over one year, but the number of observed sick or dead poultry may still have varied over time. A 10-year study found no strong seasonal trend in avian influenza among poultry in Bangladesh (44). In addition, the slaughtering and waste-disposal practices observed in our study were consistent with findings from previous studies conducted at different times and locations in Bangladesh (10, 36, 45). Nevertheless, seasonal and temporal variation in vendor practices cannot be completely excluded.

Finally, we did not conduct microbiological or environmental testing for H5N1 or other avian influenza viruses as part of this study. Therefore, our assessment of transmission and exposure risk was based on observed and reported behaviors rather than confirmed viral detection. The companion study detected H5 virus in poultry and influenza A viruses in samples collected from poultry (24), but these findings were generated independently of the present intervention study and should not be interpreted as evidence that the intervention reduced viral contamination or transmission. Similarly, the present study did not include a comparison group or longitudinal follow-up after the intervention period. We therefore cannot determine the extent to which observed changes were sustained or establish the intervention's effectiveness in reducing infection or environmental contamination.

Conclusion

This study identified important hygiene and biosecurity gaps among mobile poultry vendors, including the handling of apparently sick poultry, slaughtering in open or poorly controlled locations, inadequate hand hygiene, lack of PPE use, and unsafe disposal of poultry waste. A low-cost intervention package combining a poultry killing cone, waste collection system, hand-hygiene materials, disinfectants, and an informational pamphlet was feasible and generally acceptable to vendors and customers. During the one-month implementation period, vendors adopted the killing cone in most observed slaughtering events, used the associated waste boxes in most cone-use events, and adopted some additional hygiene and waste-management practices.

The findings support the potential use of practical, context-specific interventions to improve biosecurity practices among mobile poultry vendors where formal slaughtering facilities are limited. However, the study did not measure pathogen transmission or environmental contamination and therefore cannot demonstrate a reduction in avian influenza transmission or infection. Future research should include microbiological and environmental assessments, longer follow-up, and broader implementation across different settings to determine the sustainability, effectiveness, and scalability of such interventions. These measures should be integrated with broader One Health strategies addressing surveillance, infrastructure, waste management, poultry movement, and engagement with relevant regulatory authorities.
