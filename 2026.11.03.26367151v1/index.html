Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Full text

Abstract

Emotion regulation is critical to mental health, and its disruption can lead to psychiatric conditions such as major depressive disorder (MDD). MDD encompasses diverse symptoms, including anhedonia and anxiety, which may be associated with distinct patterns of altered neural activity. Interventions that address the neural mechanisms underlying these symptom dimensions may improve treatment efficacy. We investigated an adaptive, naturalistic real-time functional magnetic resonance imaging neurofeedback paradigm designed to differentially target anhedonia and anxiety through bidirectional amygdala regulation. Unlike conventional abstract feedback, our approach used emotional faces whose expressions changed dynamically according to ongoing amygdala activity, embedding neural selfregulation within a socially relevant emotional context. In this parallel-group study, 21 patients with MDD received one of two neurofeedback conditions. In the Happy-up group, patients attempted to increase amygdala activity by making a neutral face happier to enhance positive emotional responding. In the Fear-down group, patients attempted to decrease amygdala activity by making a fearful face less fearful to reduce negative emotional reactivity. Both groups showed the expected pattern of neural regulation during neurofeedback, with greater amygdala activity during Happy upregulation and decreasing amygdala activity during Fear downregulation. Following the intervention, patients showed reductions in depression severity, anxiety, and anhedonia, and more positive evaluations of neutral and happy faces. However, improvements in anhedonia and anxiety did not differ significantly between groups. These findings show bidirectional amygdala regulation through naturalistic neurofeedback in MDD. Although symptom-specific clinical effects were not established, the observed clinical improvements underscore the therapeutic potential of this approach.

Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a complex affective disorder associated with high rates of comorbidity and substantial global burden [1] [2] [3] [4] . Although psychotherapy and pharmacotherapy approaches are effective for many patients, approximately one third do not respond adequately to firstline treatments 5 . Developing safe, non-invasive and non-pharmacological interventions that target clinically relevant neural mechanisms could therefore offer a valuable complement to existing treatments.

MDD is characterized by substantial clinical and neurobiological heterogeneity. Patients meeting the same diagnostic criteria can differ considerably in the type and severity of their symptoms, such as anhedonia, anxiety, dysphoria, cognitive impairment, and altered emotional reactivity 1, 6 . This variability has motivated therapeutic approaches that focus on specific symptom dimensions and their underlying neurobiological substrates rather than assuming a uniform pathophysiology across MDD 1, 3, 7 . The Research Domain Criteria (RDoC) initiative, for instance, reoriented psychiatric research toward dimensional systems of functioning, including positive and negative valence processes, rather than categorical diagnoses 8 . This distinction is particularly relevant to MDD, in which both positive and negative affective processing are altered. In particular, reduced responsiveness to positive emotional information has been linked to anhedonia 9, 10 , whereas heightened or sustained responses to negative and threatening information may contribute to anxiety and dysphoria 11, 12 . Both forms of altered affective processing involve the amygdala. This subcortical region contributes to the evaluation of emotional salience and to affective learning, and plays a particular role in processing facial expressions 13 . Consistent with this, studies and meta-analyses of MDD report alterations in amygdala responses in both directions, with blunted responses to positive information and heightened responses to negative stimuli [14] [15] [16] [17] . These findings make the amygdala a plausible target for a neurofeedback intervention aimed at modulating affective processes relevant to anhedonia and anxiety.

Real-time functional magnetic resonance imaging neurofeedback (rtfMRI-NF) provides a non-invasive means of training voluntary regulation of spatially defined neural signals using feedback derived from ongoing brain activity [18] [19] [20] [21] . Prior work has established rtfMRI-NF as a feasible method for modulating neural systems involved in emotion regulation in healthy participants 22, 23 and in patients with MDD 24- 31 . Studies targeting the amygdala have demonstrated upregulation through positive autobiographical memory recall 26, 27, 32 and downregulation during exposure to aversive stimuli in healthy participants 22,33- 35 . Such approaches have been associated with improvements in depressive symptoms and emotional processing in patients with MDD 26, 27, 32, 36, 37 , as well as changes in prefrontal-limbic functional connectivity during downregulation in healthy participants 34 . Related work has shown that neurofeedback targeting prefrontal activity during cognitive reappraisal can also reduce amygdala responses to aversive stimuli 38.

Previous rtfMRI-NF studies in MDD have largely relied on internally generated emotional or cognitive states, such as positive autobiographical memory recall or mental imagery, paired with abstract visual feedback displays [24] [25] [26] [27] . The processing of social cues has thus received little attention in MDD neurofeedback research, although social functioning is a core aspect of MDD, with impairments that can persist beyond symptomatic remission 39, 40 . Reduced responsiveness to social reward and difficulties interpreting others' emotions and mental states may contribute to a cycle of social disengagement and diminished opportunities for positive interpersonal experiences 39, 41 . Facial expressions are central to these interactions, yet MDD is associated with negatively biased interpretations and reduced recognition accuracy of positive, neutral, and ambiguous expressions [42] [43] [44] . Experimental training studies have further demonstrated that biases in the interpretation of ambiguous facial expressions can be modified 45 , while changes in facial-affect processing have been associated with improvements in depressive symptoms 43, 46 . Socially meaningful stimuli such as emotional faces may therefore offer a complementary approach to neurofeedback in MDD. Quevedo et al. 47 previously incorporated smiling self-faces into neurofeedback training in depressed adolescents, combining facial stimuli with positive autobiographical memory recall and abstract feedback. We previously developed a dynamic face-based amygdala neurofeedback paradigm and tested it in healthy participants 23 . In the present study, we adapted this paradigm for patients with MDD to investigate amygdala regulation in positive and negative social-affective contexts.

In this paradigm, the neurofeedback signal was conveyed dynamically through the face itself. Rather than representing ongoing amygdala activity through a separate visual display such as a bar or thermometer 19, 35, [48] [49] [50] , changes in amygdala activity were translated into gradual changes in the emotional intensity of the face stimulus (Fig. 1). The facial expression thus constituted both the affective context in which regulation occurred and the neurofeedback signal itself. This dynamic format may strengthen the affective impact of the feedback, as dynamic expressions, relative to static ones, can facilitate emotion perception, elicit facial mimicry, and evoke stronger affective and physiological responses 51.

The study paradigm included positive (Happy) and negative (Fear) emotion regulation conditions. In the Happy condition, successful amygdala upregulation caused a neutral face stimulus to become progressively happier. Positive facial feedback therefore depended on successful regulation, and this contingency was intended to reinforce amygdala upregulation and sustain engagement with positive emotional information. In the Fear condition, successful amygdala downregulation caused a fearful face to become gradually more neutral, reducing negative emotional input. In both conditions, the resulting change in affective input was intended to further shift amygdala activity towards the targeted regulatory direction, forming a self-reinforcing closed loop between amygdala activity and the emotional stimulus. Importantly, the feedback thereby became an active element of the regulatory loop rather than a passive display of brain activity.

Patients with MDD were assigned to either a Happy-up or Fear-down group. The Happy-up group received real neurofeedback for amygdala upregulation in the Happy condition, whereas the Fear-down group received real neurofeedback for amygdala downregulation in the Fear condition. Both groups additionally performed the opposite regulation task using yoked sham feedback, in which changes in facial expression were generated artificially based on the participant's performance during real neurofeedback rather than their concurrent amygdala activity. Thus, both groups performed both regulation tasks but received real neurofeedback only for their assigned regulation direction. This study addressed three main questions. First, we examined whether dynamic face-based amygdala neurofeedback was associated with clinical improvement, including changes in overall depression severity, anhedonia, anxiety, mood, and perceived sense of control in MDD patients. We hypothesized that depressive symptoms would improve across training, with greater reductions in anhedonia in the Happy-up group and greater reductions in anxiety in the Fear-down group.

Second, we tested whether patients could successfully regulate amygdala activity during neurofeedback and whether these effects generalized beyond the training context and persisted over time. We hypothesized that amygdala activity would be higher in the Happy condition with real feedback and lower in the Fear condition with real feedback than in the other regulation conditions, and that comparable regulation would be observed during a No-Feedback Transfer Task at post-training and follow-up. We additionally examined whether regulation changed across training runs.

Third, we examined whether neurofeedback affected emotional processing and subjective experience. We hypothesized that the Happy-up group would show greater changes in responses to positive emotional faces, whereas the Fear-down group would show greater changes in responses to negative faces. We further expected participants to report higher perceived regulation performance during real than sham feedback. Finally, in exploratory analyses, we examined whether individual differences in amygdala regulation were associated with clinical improvement.

Methods

Preregistration

The study protocol, intervention design, and outcome measures were registered in the ISRCTN trial registry (Ref. No. ISRCTN71745131). The registration specified depression severity as the primary clinical outcome and included measures of anhedonia, anxiety, mood, sense of control, emotional-face processing, and amygdala responses as secondary outcomes. The comparison of perceived regulation performance during real versus sham feedback was not preregistered and was therefore considered exploratory. Analyses examining associations between individual differences in amygdala regulation and clinical improvement were likewise exploratory.

Participants

Twenty-two patients with a current episode of mild to moderate unipolar depression, diagnosed according to ICD-10 criteria (F32-F33), were enrolled in the study. Power calculations for the sample size are provided in the Supplementary Material. Patients who took antidepressants or oral contraceptives were required to be on a stable regimen. All patients were recruited by an experienced clinician according to established procedures at the Psychiatric University Hospital, Zurich, from internal depression wards and outpatient clinics. Recruited patients were between 18-65 years of age, fluent in German, right-handed, with normal vision, and without MRI contraindications such as pregnancy, brain surgery, or metal implants. Based on the Mini-International Neuropsychiatric Interview (MINI) 52 and a semi-structured clinical interview, patients with Axis-I psychiatric disorders other than MDD (e.g., organic disorders, psychoactive substance use, psychotic and bipolar disorders, posttraumatic stress disorder, panic disorder, personality disorder, etc.) and other neurological comorbidities such as epilepsy, stroke, brain tumour, and traumatic brain injury were excluded from the study (N = 1). Eligible participants (N = 21) were alternately assigned to the Happy-up group (N = 10) and the Fear-down group (N = 11). Participants signed an informed consent form following the Declaration of Helsinki before participation. No compensation was offered for participation in the study. The Cantonal Ethics Committee of Zurich (Kantonale Ethikkommission Zürich), Switzerland, gave ethical approval for this work (BASEC No. 2017-01306). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and all participants provided written informed consent prior to participation.

Study Overview

The study was conducted at the MR Centre of the Psychiatric University Hospital Zurich over four consecutive visits (V1-V4), with an additional follow-up visit (V5) scheduled 60 days after the first visit (Fig. 1). On the first visit (V1), the participants completed pre-training measurements, including a clinical assessment, a Face Rating Task, and a No-Feedback Transfer Task inside the MRI scanner. On the second (V2) and third (V3) visit, participants completed four neurofeedback (NF) training runs per day. On the fourth session (V4), participants completed a set of post-training assessments, which were identical to those administered at V1. The same assessments were repeated at the follow-up visit (V5), scheduled 60 days after V1, which corresponds to approximately eight weeks after the neurofeedback training. One participant from the Happy-up group completed the post-training clinical assessment and Face Rating Task at V4 but dropped out before the post-training MRI session and did not attend the follow-up visit (V5).

The current study followed the guidelines provided by the Consensus on Reporting and Experimental Design of Clinical and Cognitive-Behavioural Neurofeedback Studies (CRED-nf) 53 and included the checklist summarizing the study design in the Supplementary Table 1.

Region of Interest (ROI)

A high-resolution, probabilistic mask of the bilateral amygdala was derived from the California Institute of Technology CIT168 human brain atlas 54 . Using the normalization parameters estimated in SPM12 (Wellcome Centre for Human Neuroimaging, University College London, London, UK), this atlasbased ROI was nonlinearly transformed from Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space into each participant's native anatomical space by inverse warping (Fig. 1B).

Face Stimuli

Thirty gender-balanced faces of Caucasian models depicting neutral, fearful, and happy emotions were selected from the Radboud Face Database 55 . The No-Feedback Transfer Task and the Face Rating Task used the original static face stimuli. For the neurofeedback training, dynamic emotional faces were generated using a Python-based face morphing algorithm 56 . Each face was morphed along a continuum from a neutral expression to either a happy or fearful expression, depending on the regulation condition. The resulting morphs enabled smooth, video-like transitions between emotional expressions, with Morph level 0 corresponding to a fully neutral expression and level 30 to the maximum emotional intensity of the happy or fearful expression. During neurofeedback, the displayed emotional intensity was adjusted in real time according to the mean blood-oxygen-level-dependent (BOLD) signal extracted from the participant's bilateral amygdala.

Naturalistic Face Feedback

Neurofeedback signals were estimated and presented using Open NeuroFeedback Training (OpenNFT), an open-source neurofeedback framework implemented in Python and MATLAB 57 . The feedback was scaled to the normalized amygdala time-series using OpenNFT's default dynamic range to estimate the maximum and minimum limits of the scaling by using the average of the 5% highest and lowest signal intensities observed so far. The preprocessed amygdala signal was continuously mapped onto the emotional intensity of the face stimulus (morph levels 0-30), such that changes in amygdala activity produced smooth, dynamic transitions in facial expression rather than discrete changes between static images.

Task Description

Neurofeedback Task Participants completed eight NF runs spread over two training sessions (four runs per session). A NF training run consisted of four 40-s regulation blocks, interleaved with four 20-s baseline blocks in which participants mentally counted digits backwards while fixating a dot.

Each run included two Happy condition blocks and two Fear condition blocks, presented in alternating order. During the Happy condition, participants viewed a dynamic face varying along a neutral-tohappy continuum and were instructed to "make the face happier." During the Fear condition, they viewed a face varying along a neutral-to-fearful continuum and were instructed to "make the fearful face neutral." Facial expression intensity was updated once per second, and a different face identity was used in each block to reduce habituation (Fig. 2). Before training, participants were informed about the amygdala's role in emotional processing and that changes in the facial expressions reflected their ongoing amygdala activity. Participants were free to select their own emotion-regulation strategies and were asked to verbalize their intended strategies before training. They were instructed to remain focused on the regulation task throughout each run and could adapt or change their strategies between runs.

The regulation blocks provided either real or yoked sham feedback to the participant, which allowed for a within-subject control comparison 58 . During real feedback, the participant's ongoing amygdala activity directly controlled the emotional intensity of the face. In the Happy condition with real feedback (Happy Real), increases in amygdala activity shifted the face toward a happier expression. In the Fear condition with real feedback (Fear Real), decreases in amygdala activity shifted the face toward a more neutral expression. In both conditions, the system was designed to establish an adaptive, self-reinforcing closed loop between amygdala activity and the emotional expression of the face stimulus. Changes in amygdala activity dynamically altered the facial expression, which in turn was intended to reinforce amygdala regulation towards the targeted direction.

The participants were assigned to either of two groups. The Happy-up group received real feedback during the Happy condition only (Happy Real), and sham feedback during the Fear condition (Fear Sham) (Fig. 2A). The Fear-down group received real feedback during the Fear condition only (Fear Real), and sham feedback during the Happy condition (Happy Sham) (Fig. 2B). Importantly, this double-dissociation design allowed both groups to complete both the Happy and Fear conditions while receiving contingent neurofeedback in only the condition corresponding to their assigned training group.

At the end of each NF training run, participants rated their perceived performance in Happy and Fear conditions on a scale from 1 (= bad) to 10 (= good). Mean performance ratings were used to compare perceived regulation success during real and sham feedback.

No-Feedback Transfer Task

Participants completed the No-Feedback Transfer Task at pre-training, post-training, and follow-up (V1, V4, and V5). The task followed the same block structure as the neurofeedback task, with four 40s regulation blocks (without feedback) interleaved with four 20-s baseline blocks. Each regulation block comprised five static face trials presented for 8 s each.

During the Fear condition, participants viewed fearful faces and applied emotion regulation strategies to downregulate amygdala activity. During the Neutral condition, participants viewed neutral faces and applied positive regulation strategies with the aim of upregulating amygdala activity. Importantly, no neurofeedback was provided during this task, and the facial expressions therefore remained static rather than adapting as a function of ongoing amygdala activity.

Face Rating Task

Participants' perceptual responses (affective perception) and arousal levels (affective arousal) in response to emotional expressions were assessed using a face rating task outside the MRI scanner at all three assessment timepoints. The task consisted of 60 gender-balanced face trials (20 trials/emotion), which were presented in a pseudo-randomized fashion using PsychoPy (version 3.1) software 59 . Each trial began with a fixation dot presented for 1 s, followed by the emotional face presented for 3 s. The faces were either happy, neutral, or fearful. Participants were then asked, "What do you think this person is feeling?" (German: "Was denken Sie fühlt diese Person?"), which was used as a measure of affective perception, and "How do you feel when you look at this face?" (German: "Wie fühlen Sie sich bei der Betrachtung dieses Gesichts?"), which was used as a measure of affective arousal. Participants answered these questions by clicking on a scale from 0 to 100, with the lowest and highest anchors corresponding to 'anxious' and 'happy' (German: 'ängstlich' and 'glücklich') for affective perception, and 'relaxed' and 'excited' (German: 'entspannt' and 'aufgeregt') for affective arousal. No time limit was specified to answer these questions. The task took approximately ten minutes to complete.

Brain Image Acquisition

Brain scans were acquired with an Achieva 3-Tesla MRI scanner (Philips Healthcare, Best, The Netherlands) using the manufacturer's 32-channel head coil at the MR Centre of the Psychiatric University Hospital Zurich. At the beginning of the scanning session, a whole-brain T1-weighted structural scan (TR/TE = 9.3/4.6 ms, 160 sagittal slices, reconstructed voxel size = 1 × 1 × 1 mm 3 , field of view = 240 × 240 × 160 mm 3 , flip angle = 8°, acquisition time = 3 min 44 s) was acquired for anatomical reference.

A single whole-brain echo-planar imaging (EPI) volume was then acquired using the same EPI contrast and in-plane resolution as the subsequent neurofeedback acquisition, but with extended slice coverage, for coregistration of the partial-volume neurofeedback images to the T1-weighted anatomical image (TR/TE = 6000/35 ms, 70 axial slices, voxel size = 2 × 2.04 × 2 mm 3 , matrix size = 112 × 110, field of view = 224 × 224 × 140 mm 3 , flip angle = 65°, SENSE factor = 2, acquisition time = 42 s).

For each neurofeedback run, 215 volumes were acquired using a partial-volume gradient-echo T2*weighted EPI sequence restricted to the target regions, including the medial orbitofrontal cortex, temporal lobe, amygdala, fusiform gyrus, and parts of the visual cortex (TR/TE = 1250/35 ms, 16 axial slices, voxel size = 2 × 2.04 × 2 mm 3 , slice gap = 1 mm, matrix size = 112 × 110, field of view = 224 × 224 × 47 mm 3 , flip angle = 65°, SENSE factor = 2, acquisition time = 4 min 33 s per run). The first five acquired volumes were excluded from subsequent analysis.

Neural data were exported in real time to a high-performance computer using the proprietary Philips DRIN export system. Feedback from the bilateral amygdala was presented continuously using OpenNFT 57.

Clinical Assessment

Clinical changes were evaluated using standardized psychometric questionnaires at pre-training, posttraining, and follow-up. Depression severity was assessed using the 21-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAM-D) 60 and the Montgomery-Åsberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS) 61 as primary outcome measures. Anxiety was assessed using the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI) 62 and anhedonia using the German Snaith-Hamilton Pleasure Scale (SHAPS) 63 , summed across 14 items. Mood was assessed using the 12-item short form of the Multidimensional Mood State Questionnaire (German: MDBF) 64 , with item responses summed after reverse scoring to yield good-mood (MDBF-GS), wakefulness (MDBF-WM), and calmness (MDBF-RU) subscale scores. Competence and control beliefs were assessed using the FKK-SKI-PC composite of the Competence and Control Beliefs Questionnaire (German: FKK) 65 , expressed as standardized T-scores (normative mean = 50, SD = 10), hereafter referred to as "sense of control." Higher scores on the HAM-D, MADRS, STAI, and SHAPS indicate greater symptom severity, whereas higher scores on the MDBF subscales and FKK-SKI-PC indicate better mood, greater wakefulness and calmness, and stronger internal relative to external control beliefs, respectively.

Data Analysis

Online preprocessing and neurofeedback signal calculation

An SPM12-based spatial transformation pipeline was implemented to align each participant's structural scan, whole-brain EPI volume, test EPI volume, and a bilateral amygdala mask into the subject's space that served as a volume of interest during NF training. Online fMRI data preprocessing and feedback signal calculation were performed using OpenNFT's default pipeline which includes realignment for motion correction and spatial smoothing with a 5 mm full width at half maximum (FWHM) Gaussian kernel. Spike removal using a Kalman filter and drift removal using a cumulative General Linear Model (GLM) were performed to process temporal NF data 66 . The resulting preprocessed amygdala time series were extracted and used as a neurofeedback signal.

Offline Brain Data Analysis

Offline data processing was performed with SPM12 and included slice-timing correction, realignment, coregistration, normalization to MNI space, and spatial smoothing with a 6 mm FWHM Gaussian kernel.

Separate first-level GLMs were fitted for each neurofeedback run, with two regressors of interest for the real-and sham-feedback regulation blocks. The real-feedback regressor represented the Happy condition in the Happy-up group and the Fear condition in the Fear-down group, with two 40-s blocks beginning at 20 and 140 s. The sham-feedback regressor represented the opposite condition, with two 40-s blocks beginning at 80 and 200 s. Separate GLMs were also fitted for the pre-training, post-training and follow-up runs, with regressors for the Fear and Neutral conditions. Block durations and onsets were identical to those of the neurofeedback runs. In the Fear-down group, fear blocks began at 20 and 140 s and neutral blocks at 80 and 200 s; this order was reversed in the Happy-up group. All task regressors were convolved with SPM's canonical hemodynamic response function.

All models included six realignment parameters and the mean white-matter signal as nuisance regressors. Volumes with framewise displacement (FD) above 0.5 mm were censored via spike regression, with a separate nuisance regressor for each affected volume. A 128-s high-pass filter was applied, and temporal autocorrelation was modelled with an AR(1) process. Models were estimated with SPM12's classical estimation procedure.

Runs were excluded from the analysis if more than 20% of volumes were censored or head displacement exceeded 3 mm in any direction.

Statistical Analysis

1. Clinical and symptom-specific effects

The primary outcome of interest was the change in depression severity from pre-to post-training, assessed with HAM-D and MADRS scores. Separate linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) were fitted in R using the lme4 and lmerTest packages with restricted maximum likelihood estimation. Each model included time (pre-training, post-training, follow-up), group (Happy-up, Fear-down) and their interaction as fixed effects, with a random intercept for participants. Time was dummy-coded with pretraining as the reference, and group was effect-coded (Happy-up = +0.5, Fear-down = -0.5). Time coefficients therefore represent changes from pre-training averaged across the two groups, and interaction coefficients represent between-group differences in change (Happy-up minus Fear-down). The pre-training to follow-up contrast assessed longer-term changes. All available data were included (n = 21); one participant from the Happy-up group did not complete the follow-up assessment and contributed pre-and post-training data only.

Equivalent LMMs assessed changes in anhedonia (SHAPS), trait and state anxiety (STAI-T and STAI-S), mood (MDBF good mood, wakefulness and calmness) and sense of control (FKK-SKI-PC). Holm-Bonferroni correction was applied separately to the pre-post contrasts for anhedonia and anxiety (three tests) and for mood and sense of control (four tests). HAM-D and MADRS p-values were reported without adjustment. Time × group interactions tested the hypothesized symptom-specific effects; these and the follow-up contrasts are reported with unadjusted p-values. Effect estimates are presented as unstandardized regression coefficients (b) with 95% confidence intervals, and two-sided p-values were obtained from t-tests with Satterthwaite-approximated degrees of freedom.

2. Neural regulation and transfer

2a. Neurofeedback regulation performance and improvement Neurofeedback performance and neurofeedback improvement were analysed separately, following the terminology of Haugg et al. 67 . Neurofeedback performance referred to the extent to which amygdala activity differed between the four regulation conditions in the predicted direction, whereas neurofeedback improvement referred to changes in regulation performance over the course of training. All neural LMMs were fitted in R using the lme4 and lmerTest packages with restricted maximum likelihood estimation, and degrees of freedom for all tests were estimated using the Satterthwaite approximation.

Mean amygdala beta values were extracted for each run and condition from the SPM first-level beta maps. Runs with excessive head motion were excluded (18 runs), leaving 300 observations from 21 participants. Condition was modelled as a four-level categorical factor comprising Happy Real, Happy Sham, Fear Real and Fear Sham. Participants in the Happy-up group completed the Happy Real and Fear Sham conditions, and participants in the Fear-down group completed the Fear Real and Happy Sham conditions; comparisons between conditions completed by different groups were therefore between-subjects. Run was entered as a continuous, mean-centred predictor.

For neurofeedback performance, beta values were analysed using an LMM with condition and run as fixed effects and a random intercept for participants. We tested two hypotheses: Happy Real should elicit higher amygdala activity than each of the other three conditions, and Fear Real should elicit lower amygdala activity than each of the other three conditions.

For neurofeedback improvement, condition-specific linear changes across the eight neurofeedback runs were estimated using an LMM with condition, run and their interaction as fixed effects and a random intercept for participants, allowing a separate run slope for each condition. We tested two hypotheses: the Happy Real slope should be greater than the slope of each of the other three conditions, and the Fear Real slope should be lower than the slope of each of the other three conditions. Each condition's slope was additionally tested against zero (one-sided in the predicted direction for the real conditions, twosided for the sham conditions).

Each prediction was tested with three one-sided contrasts comparing the target condition with each of the other three conditions, using Satterthwaite-approximated degrees of freedom. A prediction was considered supported when the three contrasts were significant.

2b. Transfer of amygdala regulation

Transfer effects of NF training were assessed in the No-Feedback Transfer Task by comparing changes in amygdala activity from the pre-training baseline to the post-training transfer assessment and the follow-up. Mean amygdala beta values were extracted for the Fear and Neutral conditions, and the Fear -Neutral difference was computed for each participant and session. An LMM was fitted with time (baseline, post-training, follow-up; baseline as reference), group (Happy-up, Fear-down; effect-coded as +0.5/-0.5) and their interaction as fixed effects, and a random intercept for participants. Time coefficients represent changes from baseline averaged across groups, and interaction coefficients represent between-group differences in change (Happy-up minus Fear-down). Sessions excluded for excessive head motion (n = 2) were omitted, and all remaining available data were included (21 participants, 59 sessions). Effect estimates are reported as unstandardized regression coefficients (b) with 95% confidence intervals, and p-values were obtained from t-tests with Satterthwaiteapproximated degrees of freedom.

3. Emotional perception and subjective performance

3a. Emotional perception and arousal Behavioural changes in affective perception (or valence) and affective arousal were assessed based on the participant's subjective ratings of emotional face stimuli in the Face Rating Task. An LMM analysis was performed for each emotion (neutral, fear, and happy), with affective perception and affective arousal ratings as dependent variables, time (pre-, post-training, and follow-up), group (Happy-up and Fear-down) and their interaction as fixed effect factors with a random intercept for each participant. Time was dummy-coded with pre-training as the reference, and group was effect-coded (Happy-up = +0.5, Fear-down = -0.5).

3b. Subjective neurofeedback performance

Subjective self-regulation performance was assessed using participants' ratings of their perceived regulation success after each NF run. Ratings were analysed using an LMM with feedback (real, sham), emotion (happy, fear), run, and their interactions as fixed effects and a random intercept for participants. Feedback and emotion were sum-coded, and run was entered as a continuous, mean-centred predictor. A directional contrast compared ratings in the real-feedback conditions with ratings in the shamfeedback conditions. Subjective improvement was assessed with the Feedback × Run interaction and by estimating condition-specific linear changes in ratings across runs. Positive slopes were expected for both real-feedback conditions, reflecting increasing perceived regulation success over training.

All behavioural LMMs were fitted in R using the lme4 and lmerTest packages with restricted maximum likelihood estimation, and p-values were obtained from t-tests with Satterthwaite-approximated degrees of freedom.

4. Brain-clinical associations

Associations between amygdala activity during NF training and clinical change were examined separately for each group. For each participant, amygdala beta values were averaged across all motioneligible runs of the group-specific real-feedback condition: Fear Real for the Fear-down group and Happy Real for the Happy-up group. Clinical change scores were calculated as post-training minus pretraining scores for HAM-D, MADRS, STAI-T, STAI-S, SHAPS, FKK-SKI-PC, and the three MDBF subscales. For each clinical measure, a Spearman partial correlation tested the association between mean amygdala activity and the clinical change score, controlling for the pre-training score of the same measure. As a control analysis, the same correlations were computed for the group-specific sham condition. Two-sided p-values were adjusted within each group and condition across the nine clinical outcomes using the Holm-Bonferroni procedure.

Results

Sample characteristics

Participants were 21 patients with MDD, of whom 10 were assigned to the Happy-up group and 11 to the Fear-down group. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The groups did not differ significantly in sex distribution or in any baseline clinical measure (all p > .16), with comparable levels of depression severity, anhedonia, trait anxiety, and sense of control.

Discussion

This study investigated whether a naturalistic face-based neurofeedback paradigm could engage distinct affective processes involved in MDD and produce symptom-specific clinical changes. The paradigm, previously tested in healthy participants 23 , was designed as an adaptive, self-reinforcing closed-loop system in which the feedback was embedded in the emotional stimulus itself. In the Happy condition, amygdala upregulation shifted the face toward a happier expression, whereas in the Fear condition, amygdala downregulation shifted the face toward a less fearful, more neutral expression. These changes in facial expression were in turn intended to evoke amygdala activity towards the targeted regulatory direction. Participants in the Happy-up and Fear-down groups trained amygdala upregulation and downregulation, respectively, to target different affective processes. Specifically, upregulation in response to happy faces aimed to enhance responsiveness to positive emotional information, a process relevant to anhedonia, whereas downregulation in response to fearful faces aimed to attenuate responsiveness to negative emotional information, a process relevant to anxiety.

At the neural level, both training conditions showed evidence of amygdala regulation in the predicted direction, although their temporal profiles differed. Amygdala upregulation in the Happy condition was characterized by higher amygdala activity during real feedback compared with both sham feedback conditions and the Fear Real condition across training, without a systematic increase across runs. In the Fear condition, the overall real-versus-sham difference was not significant across the full training period, but downregulation became progressively stronger over successive runs, with amygdala activity decreasing significantly during real feedback and more steeply than during the two sham conditions, as well as the Happy Real condition. Thus, both conditions showed amygdala regulation in the intended direction through different temporal trajectories. Upregulation in the Happy condition appeared to be present from early training and was subsequently maintained, whereas downregulation in the Fear condition improved progressively with practice.

The same pattern was observed in our previous study in healthy participants 23 , in which downregulation in the Fear condition improved across runs, whereas upregulation in the Happy condition showed no significant run-wise change. Its recurrence in patients with MDD suggests that this pattern is not specific to the disorder. It remains unclear, however, whether these different training trajectories reflect differences in the difficulty of amygdala upregulation versus downregulation or the influence of emotional valence, since upregulation was always trained with happy faces and downregulation with fearful faces. With respect to regulation direction, up-and downregulation of emotion recruit partly distinct neural systems, both during amygdala neurofeedback 68 and without neurofeedback 69 , with downregulation engaging dorsolateral prefrontal control regions more strongly 70 . With respect to emotional valence, regulating highly salient negative emotional information may place greater demands on cognitive control and strategy selection 71 , while cognitive-control training has been shown to facilitate subsequent regulation of negative emotion 72 . Together, these findings suggest that Fear downregulation may have been more demanding and therefore required more practice to achieve effective regulation.

Embedding the feedback signal into the emotional stimulus is the central design feature of the paradigm. Previous research has shown that the format of neurofeedback can influence neural regulation, with socially meaningful feedback, such as a smiling avatar, associated with greater activation of the targeted brain region than conventional bar feedback 73 . Beyond the social relevance of the feedback, the present paradigm integrated the neurofeedback signal directly into the emotional stimulus being regulated. This approach is consistent with the process-based neurofeedback framework proposed by Lubianiker et al. 74 , which advocates linking neural targets and feedback environments to clinically relevant psychological processes. In the present study, participants showed amygdala regulation during training and reported greater perceived regulation success during real than sham feedback. Although these findings support the use of dynamic emotional faces as a neurofeedback interface in patients with MDD, the extent to which the changing expressions themselves facilitated regulation remains unknown.

Despite the observed neural regulation, the intervention did not produce the hypothesized symptomspecific clinical effects. Depression severity, anhedonia, and anxiety improved over the course of the study, but these changes did not differ significantly between the Happy-up and Fear-down groups. This pattern can be considered in light of clinical research showing that interventions targeting distinct affective processes may produce overlapping benefits. In randomized trials comparing positive-affect treatment targeting reward sensitivity with negative-affect treatment targeting threat sensitivity, Craske et al. 75, 76 found that enhancing positive affect improved not only positive-affect outcomes but also depression and anxiety symptoms. These findings suggest that interventions targeting positive and negative affect do not necessarily produce improvements restricted to their respective symptom dimensions. However, the absence of between-group differences in the present study does not establish that the two neurofeedback approaches have equivalent clinical effects.

The reductions in depression severity were notable given the brief training period, with mean decreases of 4.04 points (27.1%) on the HAM-D and 6.52 points (31.7%) on the MADRS. These changes were observed after only two neurofeedback training sessions on consecutive days, with post-training assessments conducted three days after baseline. The magnitude of the reductions was comparable to conventional definitions of partial clinical response 77 , although these definitions apply to individual patients rather than changes in group means. The MADRS reduction also exceeded published estimates of minimal important differences 78 , although these estimates were developed to evaluate antidepressant treatment effects rather than uncontrolled pre-post changes. The limited training duration may have been insufficient for the distinct trajectories of neural regulation to translate into differential clinical outcomes. Future studies with extended training protocols could examine whether additional sessions lead to greater clinical improvements or more selective effects on depressive symptoms.

Whether the observed clinical improvements were specifically attributable to neurofeedback remains unclear. Previous randomized controlled trials in MDD have reported greater clinical improvements following amygdala neurofeedback compared with control feedback 26 , whereas other studies found comparable improvements following neurofeedback targeting emotion-related and control brain regions 25 . In the latter study, clinical improvement was associated with increased self-efficacy, suggesting that the experience of successful self-regulation may contribute to therapeutic benefits independently of the specific neural target. Comparable clinical improvements between active and control neurofeedback conditions have also been reported in alcohol dependence and posttraumatic stress disorder 79, 80 . This literature suggests that shared psychological processes, such as perceived control, expectancy, and emotion-regulation practice, may contribute to clinical improvement. In the present study, all participants received real feedback in one condition and sham feedback in the other, and group assignment alternated rather than being randomized. Without a sham-only clinical control group, alternative explanations for improvement over time, including regression to the mean, cannot be excluded. Exploratory analyses also revealed associations between amygdala activity during neurofeedback and clinical improvement. In both the Happy-up and Fear-down groups, higher mean amygdala activity during real feedback was associated with greater reductions in depression severity.

Given the small group-specific samples and the exploratory nature of these analyses, these findings should be interpreted cautiously despite the large observed correlations.

Changes in facial-affect perception provide an additional indication that participants' emotional processing changed over the course of the intervention. Across groups, neutral and happy faces were rated more positively following training, and these effects persisted at follow-up. This pattern is potentially relevant to MDD, in which neutral and ambiguous facial expressions are often interpreted with a negative bias 41, 43 . However, because these effects did not differ between the Happy-up and Feardown groups, they cannot be attributed to the condition-specific neurofeedback mechanisms. They may instead reflect repeated engagement with emotional facial expressions, emotion-regulation practice, or broader changes occurring during the intervention. These behavioural changes are therefore informative, but should be distinguished from the evidence for condition-specific neural regulation.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered. The bilateral amygdala target was defined anatomically despite substantial functional heterogeneity within the amygdala 81, 82 . Individualized or functionally defined targets may offer greater neurofeedback specificity, although whether such approaches consistently improve neurofeedback learning remains unclear 50 . Regarding clinical outcomes, relatively mild baseline anhedonia may have limited the sensitivity to detect improvements following Happy-up training. Although the Happy-up and Fear-down conditions were designed to target processes relevant to anhedonia and anxiety, respectively, participants were assigned to training independently of their individual symptom profiles. The study therefore evaluated symptom-targeted neurofeedback without investigating personalized treatment allocation. Future studies could examine whether matching neurofeedback interventions to individual symptom profiles improves clinical outcomes.

Small sample sizes represent a broader challenge in rtfMRI-NF research 83, 84 , where extensive repeated scanning provides rich within-subject data but cannot fully compensate for limited statistical power at the participant level. Here, each patient underwent five scanning sessions, enabling detailed characterization of neural regulation despite the relatively small sample.

Evidence for transfer beyond the neurofeedback context was also inconclusive. Regulation effects observed during training were not reproduced during the No-Feedback Transfer Task. However, this absence of a significant transfer effect should be interpreted cautiously, as successful regulation during feedback does not necessarily generalize to conditions in which feedback is absent 50 . Moreover, the transfer assessment consisted of a single, relatively short fMRI run, which may have limited sensitivity to detect group-by-time effects across repeated assessments 85 . The present data therefore do not provide strong evidence either for or against generalization of the acquired regulation skills beyond the training context.

Conclusion

Patients with MDD demonstrated amygdala upregulation and downregulation using dynamic facial expressions as the feedback signal, with each regulation direction trained in a separate group. Upregulation in the Happy condition was present from early training, whereas downregulation in the Fear condition improved progressively across runs. Depression severity, anhedonia, and anxiety decreased following training, but these improvements did not differ between the Happy-up and Feardown groups. More broadly, the results support dynamic social-emotional stimuli as a feasible means of embedding the neurofeedback signal within the affective context of regulation. Future studies could test whether symptom-or biomarker-guided treatment allocation can translate differential neural regulation into more selective clinical benefit.
