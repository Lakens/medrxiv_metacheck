In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. Abstract

Objective: Published Sepsis-3 detection rates range from 3.4 % to 65.2 % in MIMIC-III and from 9.8 % to 47.9 % in eICU-CRD. We tested whether data preprocessing decisions alone produce differences of this magnitude when data, cohort and sepsis definition are held fixed.

Using the MIMIC-III and eICU-CRD databases we developed and applied a configurable pipeline (medalabel) that stepwise maps the databases into the OMOP Common Data Model, standardizes and cleans the data, and preprocesses them to compute Sepsis-3 labels. Each setting of the preprocessing represents one decision of published Sepsis-3 implementations, and settings can be freely combined. On fixed cohorts of first ICU stays (13,388 in MIMIC-III, 94,782 in eICU-CRD), we changed one setting of a reference configuration at a time (51 variants in five preprocessing domains) and compared detection rates and septic stays (Jaccard index) with the reference. We approximately reconstructed six published pipelines and combined the settings with the largest effects in pairs to test whether their effects add up. The time a value is measured can differ from the time it is documented and thus available for automated analysis. Beyond the usual retrospective view based on measurement time, the pipeline optionally combines both time points to simulate a real-time system.

Results: The reference configuration labelled 31.5 % of ICU stays in MIMIC-III and 8.2 % in eICU-CRD as septic. Changing a single setting resulted in sepsis detection rates of 15.3-58.6 % in MIMIC-III and 4.6-17.2 % in eICU-CRD. The largest shifts from the reference, all in the same direction in both databases, came from the SOFA baseline, up to +27.1 percentage points (pp) in MIMIC-III and +9.0 pp in eICU-CRD, from disabling imputation (-16.3 and -2.5 pp), the temporal window (+12.6 and +5.3 pp) and aggregation (-10.1 and -0.8 pp); clinician-validated parameter sets had little effect (-4.8 and -1.1 pp). Even variants that changed the rate by less than 2 pp labelled partly different patients, with Jaccard indices as low as 0.67 in MIMIC-III and 0.56 in eICU-CRD (1 = identical septic stays). Reconstructions deviated from the published rates by 2-10 pp. Two settings applied together deviated from the sum of their single effects by 0.9-11.8 pp in MIMIC-III and by up to 4.9 pp in eICU-CRD. Simulating a real-time system lowered the MIMIC-III rate by 5.0 pp and delayed the label in 45 % of the stays that remained septic.

Discussion: With the infection criterion held fixed, preprocessing decisions alone can produce a considerable part of the variation between published sepsis detection rates. Similar rates can stem from different patients, which makes studies and prediction models difficult to compare, and retrospective labels rely on values that a real-time system would not yet have.

Conclusion: Sepsis-3 labels from health records are only comparable if their data preprocessing is known. Configurable pipelines allow quantifying the effect of these decisions, computing alternative labels on the same data and, in future, validating them against clinician-adjudicated cases. Introduction

Sepsis caused an estimated 11 million deaths in 2017, corresponding to 19 % of all deaths worldwide [1, 2], and it causes substantial in-hospital treatment costs [3]. In intensive care units, 29.5 % of patients are affected, with a hospital mortality of 35.3 % [4, 5]. The Sepsis-3 consensus defines sepsis as lifethreatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection and identifies organ dysfunction as an acute increase in the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score [6] of at least 2 points [7].

Sepsis research makes use of openly accessible ICU databases such as the single-centre database MIMIC-III [8] and eICU-CRD, which pools 208 US hospitals [9]. Studies on these databases range from observational analyses to prediction models [10], treatment-effect estimates [11] and comparisons of applied methods [12]. Generally, sepsis-related research depends not only on correctly identifying sepsis cases but also on precise dates of sepsis onset. The Sepsis-3 definition makes both possible, since the sepsis label and its onset can be computed directly from recorded clinical measurements. In contrast, diagnosis codes are unreliable, as sepsis codes in US hospitals identified only 32.3 % of patients with severe sepsis or septic shock (as defined prior to Sepsis-3) confirmed by medical record review [13].

However, the Sepsis-3 definition leaves many implementation details open, such as which measurements to use, over which time windows to evaluate them and how to handle missing values. Consequently, different implementations applied to the same data can yield substantially different sepsis labels. This variability is illustrated by our recent review, which covered 64 Sepsis-3 implementations on MIMIC-III and eICU-CRD [14]. Sepsis detection rates ranged from 3.4 % to 65.2 % in MIMIC-III and from 9.8 % to 47.9 % in eICU-CRD, even among studies with identical cohort definitions. Study characteristics such as the database, the unit of analysis and the infection detection method explained only about 9 % of this heterogeneity. Based on the publications and the source code analysed in the review, we grouped data preprocessing and label computation methods into six domains: parameter selection for each SOFA component (D1), temporal windows (D2), aggregation (D3), handling of missing values (D4), SOFA calculation (D5) and identification of suspected infection (D6). Most studies did not fully report how they implemented these six domains, and few published their source code. As a result, neither the effect of individual decisions nor the reproducibility of published rates could be assessed [14].

Recent studies have also shown that varying components of the Sepsis-3 definition itself, such as the infection criterion or the timing of onset, changes both labels and prediction performance [15, 16]. One of the studies also varied the imputation method and the SOFA version [16]. In contrast, parameter selection, temporal windows and aggregation have not yet been varied on identical data. To our knowledge, no study has compared single preprocessing decisions with a fixed reference, and no openly available pipeline exposes the decisions of domains D1-D5 as settings. A further gap concerns time, because labels are computed retrospectively from the complete record, whereas a real-time system only sees values that have already been documented.

To address these gaps, we developed medalabel, a pipeline that maps ICU data to the OMOP Common Data Model [17] and exposes the preprocessing decisions of domains D1-D5 as configurable settings.

We hypothesized that single preprocessing decisions shift Sepsis-3 detection rates by an amount comparable to the variation between published studies, and that variants with similar rates label partly different patients. In addition, we examined whether reconstructions of published pipelines reproduce their published rates, whether the effects of decisions from different domains add up and how much the labels rely on values that a real-time system would not yet have.

Materials and Methods

Data Processing Pipeline

We developed medalabel (Figure 1), a configurable data processing pipeline based on the medallion architecture of data engineering, in which data pass through three layers of increasing quality called Bronze, Silver and Gold [18, 19]. It combines the preprocessing settings of published Sepsis-3 implementations in MIMIC-III and eICU-CRD in a single pipeline, so that each of them can be applied to either database. The pipeline thus serves as a test bench for label computation, in which individual decisions can be varied while data, cohort and definition remain fixed and the labelling pipelines of published studies can be reconstructed. Only the first layer is specific to a data source, and only the SOFA and label computation are specific to sepsis. Each layer ends with an automated profiling step for quality control. The technical specification is given in Supplement S1. The Bronze layer maps the source data into the OMOP CDM v5.4 [17] without changing their content.

To simplify the following mapping to standard concepts, it fills the OMOP source_to_concept_map table with each source identifier, its name and, where the source data dictionary provides one, a standardized terminology code (LOINC, ICD-9, NDC). Bronze profiling calculates statistics for each source parameter, such as the number of observations, value distributions and measurement frequency, and reports them separately for each recorded unit (e.g., FiO 2 in percent and as a fraction). These statistics show clinical experts what a source parameter contains, even where its name is ambiguous or its unit is missing. On this basis, the experts complete the mapping with standard OMOP concepts, unit rules, plausibility ranges and analytical tags, so that every standardization decision is clinically validated. The Silver layer applies this annotated mapping through the assignment of standard concepts, normalization of units and removal of implausible values.

The Gold layer selects the cohort, stores it in the OMOP cohort tables and retrieves only the concepts tagged for the analysis, which determines the parameter selection (D1). After normalizing vasopressor doses to body weight, deriving ventilation episodes and identifying suspected infection, the Gold layer applies four configurable stages, namely temporal aggregation by window size, step and per-parameter aggregation function (D2/D3), imputation of missing values (D4), SOFA subscore computation (D5) and Sepsis-3 label computation. Imputation proceeds in three levels of increasing uncertainty: derivation from related parameters by established clinical relationships (e.g., total GCS from its components), estimation from earlier values of the same stay (e.g., forward filling) and, where no information exists, constant default values (e.g., normal values). All settings are controlled through configuration files, so variants require no code changes.

On the technical side, the pipeline is started from a command line or scheduled with the workflow management software Apache Airflow, and it is packaged in Docker containers so that it runs unchanged on other systems. To keep test cycles short, the pipeline was developed on the MIMIC-III and eICU-CRD demo datasets and then applied unchanged to the full databases. More than 3,000 automated unit and integration tests (pytest test cases) check data integrity, transformation correctness and filter logic across all three layers.

Data and Cohorts

In both databases, inclusion was restricted to adults (≥ 18 years) with an ICU stay of more than 24 h, and only the first ICU stay per patient was kept. For MIMIC-III (v1.4) [8], the analysis was further restricted to stays documented in MetaVision (iMDsoft), because microbiology cultures are underrepresented in the older CareVue system (Philips) [20]. The eICU-CRD (v2.0) [9] was used without further restriction.

The primary outcome is the sepsis detection rate, i.e. the proportion of ICU stays labelled as septic. All times are counted from ICU admission (𝑡 0 ).

Sepsis-3 Label and Reference Configuration

In the reference configuration, the pipeline aggregates the data of each ICU stay into 24 h windows that advance in 1 h steps (D2), takes the worst value of each parameter within a window, except that SpO 2 is averaged and urine output is summed (D3), fills missing values with the three imputation levels described above (D4) and computes a SOFA score for every window (D5), giving one score per hour. A stay is labelled septic if its total SOFA score rises by at least 2 points above a baseline between 48 h before and 24 h after the onset of suspected infection [21]. The baseline is not a pre-illness value but the lowest total SOFA within the preceding 24 h. In MIMIC-III, suspected infection (D6) is defined as an antibiotic prescription combined with a microbiology culture obtained within 72 h before or 24 h after it, with onset at the earlier of the two [21]. We preferred this rule to ICD codes, which lack precise onset times and depend on coding practice [14]. In eICU-CRD, microbiology cultures are too sparse for this rule. Suspected infection is therefore taken from sepsis diagnosis records (ICD codes and the APACHE admission diagnosis), as in the two eICU-CRD repositories analysed, with onset at the time the diagnosis was entered. Because the two databases differ in how infection is identified, the comparison between them concerns the direction rather than the size of effects.

For parameter selection (D1), the reference combines the clinical parameters for SOFA calculation of the six public repositories analysed previously [14], four for MIMIC-III [11, 12, 22, 23] and two for eICU-CRD [24, 25], into one union set (repositories in Table 2; parameter sets in Supplement S2, Section S2.1).

For MIMIC-III, we then searched the data dictionary for additional source codes of these parameters, and clinicians validated the resulting sets of both databases (Supplement S1, Section S1.3). Parameter selection (D1) was examined with the six repository-specific parameter sets, each restricted to the validated union (Supplement S2, Section S2.3). Infection identification (D6) was held fixed within each database.

Variants were derived from our previous analysis of the implementation decisions in the six repositories and the methods of the 64 reviewed studies [14]. Their consolidation and the decisions not modelled are described in Supplement S2 (Section S2.1). Each variant is labelled D𝑑-S𝑛, for domain 𝑑 and setting 𝑛. In each domain, the reference is the method reported most often. The exception is the SOFA baseline, which few studies reported [14]. Its 24 h minimum follows [26], so that an acute deterioration can be detected at any hour of the stay. The Sepsis-3 consensus assumes a baseline of zero only for patients without known organ dysfunction [7]. With a zero baseline for all stays, patients with chronic organ dysfunction would meet the SOFA criterion throughout their stay, so this setting is examined as a variant (D5-S15) and not used as the reference.

Information time.

Like all repositories analysed, every configuration uses each value from the time it was measured (charttime). For bedside observations, MIMIC-III also records when a value was entered into the clinical information system and thus became available (storetime). Because the OMOP CDM holds a single timestamp per event [27], we extended its measurement and drug exposure tables with a storetime column and re-ran the reference with documentation-time availability, in which each value counts at its charttime but only once it has been documented (Supplement S1, Section S1.2, and Supplement S2, Section S2.4). The contrast is restricted to MIMIC-III because eICU-CRD records no storetime. Within MIMIC-III, results from the laboratory and microbiology tables carry none either, except laboratory values that are also charted at the bedside. The contrast therefore underestimates how many values a real-time system would not yet have.

Further contrasts. Supplement S2 (Section S2.4) reports three additional settings, namely the first measurement instead of ICU admission as time origin, the inclusion of CareVue stays in MIMIC-III and the stricter infection rule.

Statistical Analysis

The effect of each variant was expressed as the signed difference between its detection rate and that of the reference in percentage points, and each domain was characterized by the difference between the highest and the lowest rate among its variants. Two variants that do not correspond to a published method, the best-case aggregation (D3-S4) and the additional variant D2-S10, were reported but not counted in this difference (rationale in Supplement S2, Section S2.1). Because all configurations run on the same fixed cohort, differences between them are exact and were compared without significance tests. Because the cohorts are large, the sampling uncertainty of each rate is small (95 % Wilson confidence interval within ±0.9 pp of the rate), so confidence intervals are not shown for individual rates.

Shifts in SOFA total and subscores were summarized as mean differences from the reference (Supplement S2, Section S2.3).

Whether two configurations labelled the same stays was quantified with the Jaccard index, which is the number of stays labelled septic by both divided by the number labelled septic by at least one. It is 1 when both label exactly the same stays and 0 when they share none, independent of whether the rates differ. The index alone does not show whether a variant adds septic stays, removes them or exchanges them. For every variant, Supplement S2 (Section S2.3) therefore also lists how many stays gained and how many lost the label compared with the reference. Tier 3 interactions were computed as the observed effect of a combination minus the sum of the two single effects, in percentage points.

An interaction of 0 pp means that the effects simply add. As an alternative, we compared the observed rate with the product of the relative changes of the two settings. Agreement between databases was described with the Spearman rank correlation of the D2-D5 variant rates and with the direction of variants that shifted the rate by at least 1 pp in both. Tier 2 rates were compared with the published rates as absolute differences, and all rates with the range of published rates [14]. Analyses used Python with pandas and SciPy.

Results

After applying the inclusion criteria, the reference cohorts comprised 13,388 ICU stays in MIMIC-III and 94,782 in eICU-CRD, with reference detection rates of 31.5 % and 8.2 %, respectively. The catalogue of the 51 Tier 1 variants is provided in Supplement S2 (Section S2.1).

Source Data

Before preprocessing, the Bronze layer held 21,813 source codes with about 365 million records in MIMIC-III, of which 349 codes were mapped to 39 OMOP concepts for the Sepsis-3 label. In eICU-CRD, 255 of 14,276 codes with about 985 million records were mapped to 27 concepts. Both databases recorded the same parameter under several names, codes and unit spellings. In MIMIC-III, urine output had to be determined from 12 source codes named after the collection route (e.g., "Foley" and "Ileoconduit"). Mechanical ventilation was derived from 78 source codes. In eICU-CRD, norepinephrine appeared under 19 names, some of which include the concentration, and a single platelet code carried 55 spellings of its unit. Within an ICU stay, MIMIC-III recorded vital signs about hourly and laboratory values every 8 to 24 h on average, whereas eICU-CRD recorded SpO 2 about every 6 min and mean arterial pressure every 15 min, but laboratory values only every 15 to 37 h (Supplement S2, Section S2.2).

Discussion

With data, cohort and Sepsis-3 definition held fixed, single preprocessing decisions shifted the detection rate from 31.5 % to between 15.3 % and 58.6 % in MIMIC-III and from 8.2 % to between 4.6 % and 17.2 % in eICU-CRD. Variants with similar rates labelled partly different patients, reconstructions of published pipelines deviated from the published rates, the effects of two decisions rarely added up, and labels computed from measurement times relied on values that were documented only later. A considerable part of the variation between published studies can therefore arise from the preprocessing decisions of domains D1-D5 alone, even with the infection criterion held fixed, which supports our first hypothesis.

Together with the reconstructions of published pipelines, which reached 25.9 % in eICU-CRD, our rates covered 70 % of the range published for MIMIC-III (3.4-65.2 %) and 42 % of that for eICU-CRD (9.8-47.9 %) [14]. These percentages compare only the lowest and the highest rate on each side. In our analyses, these extremes come from single variants, in MIMIC-III from disabling imputation and from the zero baseline, and in eICU-CRD the highest rate comes from a reconstruction with its own cohort.

They should therefore not be read as the share of the published differences that preprocessing explains.

They show that preprocessing alone can move the rate across a similarly wide range. The remaining range stems from studies that differ in what our design holds fixed, such as additional cohort filters, admissions instead of ICU stays as the unit of analysis, or an unreported infection definition [14], and from implementation details that our 51 variants do not cover.

The decisions that mattered most are rarely reported [14]. In MIMIC-III, the baseline of the SOFA increase, the handling of missing values, the aggregation within a window, the window granularity, the restriction to the window around suspected infection and the exclusion of early-onset cases each shifted the rate by more than 10 pp, whereas the clinician-validated repository parameter sets mattered little (at most 4.8 pp). In percentage points, eICU-CRD was less sensitive, but its reference rate is also lower.

Relative to the reference rate, the zero baseline and the 1 h windows had larger effects in eICU-CRD than in MIMIC-III (+110 % against +86 % and +65 % against +40 %), whereas disabling imputation and mean aggregation had smaller ones (-30 % against -52 % and -10 % against -32 %). The direction of effects agreed between the databases whenever a variant mattered in both, but effect sizes did not transfer one-to-one. Effects of decisions from different domains rarely combined additively. When one setting dominated, such as the zero baseline or 1 h blocks, the other lost much of its effect. This is partly inherent to the scale, because a stay can be labelled septic only once and two settings that act on the same stays cannot add their effects. Effects of individual decisions therefore cannot simply be summed.

Our reconstructions approximate the published pipelines with the settings available in our pipeline.

Run on the same database version as the original studies, they yielded rates that deviated from the published ones by 2.2-9.5 pp in both directions, and these deviations cannot be attributed to a single cause. Part of these deviations lies in the comparison itself. Only one repository (Table 2, No. 1) reports both the number of septic cases and the cohort size, whereas for the other three the denominator had to be inferred from the size of the database [14], which tends to understate the rate when the published cohort was filtered further. Yet even this repository, with both numbers reported, deviated by 5.7 pp. The reconstructions also cover most, but not all, implementation decisions of the repositories (Supplement S2, Section S2.1), and published rates further depend on undocumented choices, software versions and extraction details that the code does not capture.

These results add a layer to known reproducibility problems. Machine learning for health suffers from persistent reproducibility gaps [28, 29], and systematic reviews of sepsis prediction could not pool performance across studies because of varying sepsis definitions [30, 31]. Open ICU databases record no reliable sepsis timestamps [32], and implementing Sepsis-3 in AmsterdamUMCdb raised the same operational questions [33], so every label has to be reconstructed by preprocessing. Our results indicate that this reconstruction is itself a source of variability. Varying the definition itself changes labels and predictive performance [15]. Closest to our work, Mackensen et al. [16] crossed 2,520 variants of the Sepsis-3 definition and attributed most label variability to the suspected-infection criterion, with imputation and window length contributing little. Where the two designs overlap, the results agree, as adding the four-day antibiotic rule of the CDC Adult Sepsis Event [13] lowered our MIMIC-III reference rate from 31.5 % to 19.1 %. They diverge on imputation, which contributed little in their design but shifted our rates by up to 16.3 pp when disabled, and on evaluating the SOFA increase over the whole stay, which their design did not include and which shifted our rates by 12.3 pp. Harmonization frameworks such as ricu [34] provide a harmonized implementation across databases, whereas our pipeline exposes the decisions behind it, so that their effect can be measured.

In line with our second hypothesis, similar detection rates did not imply that the same patients were labelled. Most variants relabelled only a small part of the stays, but some replaced about a fifth of the septic stays while leaving the rate almost unchanged, and the subscore analyses showed that variants act on specific organ systems. Two studies with similar rates may therefore identify sepsis through different organ dysfunction patterns in partly different patients. For prediction models, this may limit the comparability of reported performance and the transfer of published models to practice. In observational and treatment-effect studies, the label decides which patients are analysed as septic, so estimates of incidence, outcome and treatment effect may inherit the same differences.

Every published label we examined, and our reference, also uses each value from its measurement time, as if it were available the instant it was recorded. Counting values only once documented lowered the MIMIC-III rate by 5.0 pp. About one in five septic stays of the reference lost the label, and in almost half of the remaining ones it was met later, so these labels depend on values that a real-time system would not yet have. Because results from the laboratory table carry no documentation time, this is a lower bound. A related problem was observed after deployment, where a widely implemented proprietary sepsis model reached an AUROC of 0.63 in external validation [35] and 0.47 once predictions made after clinical recognition were excluded [36]. Prospective evaluation can succeed [37, 38], but its results transfer only if the label pipeline is reported as precisely as the model.

The Lancet Commission on Sepsis counts the lack of consensus on definitions and surveillance criteria

among the central problems of the field [39]. Our results suggest that even a consensus definition does not yield consistent labels unless its operationalization is agreed and reported. A common data model is necessary but not sufficient, because identical OMOP schemas can hold semantically different content [27] and identical data still yield different labels. Mapping data into the model is itself demanding.

Even within one hospital, the same clinical concept was recorded under many source codes, and the 208 hospitals of eICU-CRD added local names, unit spellings and dosing conventions. Such heterogeneity can be expected to grow with every additional site, and recording the mapping in a reviewable file, as in the Silver layer, makes this step transparent and reusable.

Labels would become comparable if studies reported at least the SOFA baseline, the handling of missing values, the aggregation rule, the window and observation period, the handling of early-onset cases, the information time and the parameter set of each SOFA component [14]. Reporting guidelines such as TRIPOD [40], STROBE [41] and CONSORT [42] could cover these decisions for database-derived labels. For deployed models, these decisions also belong in the documentation that reaches clinicians, for which clinician-facing model cards offer a place [43]. How such information is presented matters as well, because the way predictive uncertainty is shown in sepsis decision support changed physicians' advice uptake, accuracy and trust [44]. Publishing the preprocessing code with the article and testing the decisions with the largest effects identified here in sensitivity analyses, ideally in more than one database, would allow readers to judge how robust a result is. Configurable pipelines make such analyses feasible without rewriting code. The underlying architecture is not specific to sepsis and has a low entry barrier. In a master's course, 11 student groups each built a simplified medallion pipeline on the MIMIC-IV demo dataset for a disease-specific research question [45].

In a follow-up study, we plan to apply the pipeline to the ICU data of our institution, whose extraction infrastructure is in place [46]. Because the Gold layer reads only the standardized OMOP representation, the analyses can run on institutional data without their developers accessing it, following the code-to-data model of OMOP network studies [47] that avoids the barriers to sharing routine clinical data [48, 49]. There, we intend to compare our configurations with sepsis diagnoses and onset times adjudicated by intensivists, extending comparisons of Sepsis-3 definitions with expert labels [16, 50] to the preprocessing that precedes the definition. Where structured infection records are sparse, sepsis-related features extracted from clinical notes [51] could complement the infection criterion. The clinician-validated annotations of this study can further serve as a reference for automated concept mapping, where large language models still require expert review [52].

Limitations

Both databases originate from the US, and validation in other healthcare systems remains to be done.

The variants form a structured sample of the decision space rather than a complete enumeration. Parameter selection was limited to the six repository sets, infection identification was held fixed apart from one contrast, and Tier 3 combined four selected variants in pairs only, without higher-order combinations. All effects are relative to the reference, whose 24 h minimum baseline is not the most common choice. Tier 3 indicates how they change with the zero baseline as starting point, which most of the repositories use [14]. No derivation still lowered the rate by 9.9 pp and mean aggregation by 3.7 pp, whereas the 1 h blocks added only 0.9 pp. The direction of the effects was thus unchanged, but their size depends on the reference. Because cultures are too sparse in eICU-CRD, suspected infection had to be taken from diagnosis records there, although such records miss many septic patients [13].

The eICU-CRD rates are therefore likely to underestimate sepsis, absolute rates are not comparable between the databases, and eICU-CRD serves to test whether the direction of the effects is replicated.

The eICU-CRD reference rate of 8.2 % also lies below the lowest published rate, for which we see two reasons. The reference requires a SOFA increase in addition to the diagnosis-based infection criterion, and in our reconstructions of the two eICU-CRD repositories this requirement lowered the rates by 2.5 and 4.0 pp. The cohort differs as well, because the reference is restricted to first ICU stays of more than 24 h, and under the same requirement the cohorts of the two repositories yielded 16.8 % and 23.4 %.

The documentation-time contrast is restricted to MIMIC-III and excludes results from the laboratory table. When PaO 2 is missing, the respiratory subscore depends on the chosen SpO 2 /FiO 2 equivalence, of which we tested one alternative [53] but not others [54, 55]. Results refer to the original SOFA score, and SOFA-2 [56] will add further decisions, which the pipeline can represent through configuration. Finally, our analyses show how much preprocessing changes the label, not which label is clinically correct, because neither database provides a clinical reference standard.

Conclusion

With data, cohort and infection criterion held fixed, the preprocessing decisions of domains D1-D5 changed how many and which patients were labelled septic by Sepsis-3 in two ICU databases. The SOFA baseline had the largest effect in both databases, and the handling of missing values and the temporal window followed. The effects of two decisions rarely added up, and in MIMIC-III retrospective labels relied on values that a real-time system would not yet have. Sepsis-3 labels are therefore comparable only if their preprocessing is known.

We advocate routine sensitivity analyses of preprocessing and standardized reporting of the implementation decisions in domains D1-D6. The configurable pipeline presented here makes these decisions explicit. Which of the resulting labels is clinically correct remains to be tested against clinician-adjudicated cases.
