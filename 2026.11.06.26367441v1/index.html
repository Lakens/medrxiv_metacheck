Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Alzheimer's disease and related dementias (AD/ADRD) are clinical disorders characterized by declines in cognitive function to levels at which everyday living is meaningfully impaired. Genetic variants may confer risk for AD/ADRD by way of steeper rates of aging-related cognitive declines or their association with lower premorbid levels of cognitive function that place individuals closer to a clinically severe threshold of cognitive function. We applied joint longitudinal-hazard models to 26-year data from an ancestrally diverse US sample of N = 11,518 adults to estimate effects of multi-ancestry polygenic indices (PGIs) for a range of established risk factors on dementia hazard and the degree they operate via premorbid cognitive function and longitudinal cognitive decline. APOE e4 and PGIs for ADRD, type 2 diabetes, stroke, LDL cholesterol, and hearing loss operated via rate of change, whereas PGIs for educational attainment, cognitive function, physical activity, BMI, smoking, loneliness, schizophrenia, and externalizing were associated with dementia hazard via premorbid differences in cognitive function. Some results varied by continental ancestry. Our implementation of joint longitudinal-hazard models allowed us to distinguish how different genetic risk factors for AD/ADRD operate via degenerative processes that might be therapeutically targeted during later life and via premorbid levels of cognitive function that would require intervention earlier in the life course.

Introduction

Alzheimer's Disease and Alzheimer's Disease Related Dementias (AD/ADRD) are major neurocognitive disorders of aging whose core clinical feature is cognitive deterioration that interferes with independence in everyday activities (Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 2022). Recent estimates of the global prevalence of AD/ADRD indicate increases from approximately 1% at age 65 years to upwards of 30% at age 90 years (Nichols et al., 2022). However, these statistics grossly underestimate the prevalence of neurocognitive changes underlying AD/ADRD, as prodromal neurodegeneration occurs long before individuals meet clinical criteria for mild cognitive impairment (MCI) or AD/ADRD (Jack et al., 2018). As put by the National Institute on Aging-Alzheimer's Association workgroups, "the pathophysiological process of [AD/ADRD] begins years, if not decades, before the diagnosis of clinical dementia" (Sperling et al., 2011). Indeed, aging-related cognitive declines in the general population of apparently healthy adults are strongly linked to declines in everyday functions and eventual clinical AD/ADRD diagnosis (Tucker-Drob, 2011 , 2019).

Genetic risk for AD/ADRD is characterized by moderately large effects of common variants within the APOE region, highly penetrant rare variants, and a more distributed polygenic background (Andrews et al., 2023; de la Fuente et al., 2022; Scheltens et al., 2021). Several risk factors for AD/ADRD have been established in the epidemiological literature (Livingston et al., 2024), many of which are known to be heritable, and may therefore contribute to genetic risk for AD/ADRD, either through vertical pleiotropy (in which genetic effects are transmitted through a causal chain from the risk factor to AD/ADRD) or horizontal pleiotropy (in which the genetic effect on the risk factor and AD/ADRD operate through overlapping biological pathways). Either form of pleiotropy, however, may confer risk for dementia either via effects on neurodegenerative process that affect rate of cognitive decline from an earlier level of functioning, or premorbid process that affect the attained level of cognitive function prior to decline. This distinction is critical for understanding AD/ADRD etiology and determining targets for both therapeutics and public health policy. Because AD/ADRD is clinically diagnosed when an individual's cognitive function has declined to a threshold below which their adaptive behaviors are meaningfully impaired, both genetic and epidemiological risk factors for AD/ADRD can operate via associations on premorbid levels of cognitive function that place individuals closer to the threshold prior to experiencing normal decline without those risk factors being associated with steeper rate of decline (Lövdén et al., 2020; Tucker-Drob, 2019). Processes affecting neurodegeneration and premorbid cognitive function may each be valuable targets for prevention efforts, the latter likely requiring interventions at earlier points in the life course (Walhovd et al., 2023).

Our analyses capitalize on genetic, cognitive, and epidemiological data from the Health and Retirement Study (HRS), a prospective, multiethnic longitudinal study of over 30,000 middleaged and older US adults assessed on cognitive function approximately every 2 years for up to 25 years and prospectively ascertained for incident dementia. We compute polygenic indices (PGIs) from independent multi-ancestry GWAS discovery data corresponding to modifiable risk factors identified in the 2024 Lancet Commission on Dementia report (Livingston et al., 2024) along with a selection of other genetic predictors implicated in AD/ADRD. To directly estimate the extent to which a given risk factor for dementia hazard operates via rate of cognitive decline vs. premorbid cognitive function, we employ joint longitudinal-hazard models that integrate a growth curve approach to modeling cognitive change over time with a hazard modeling approach to predict timing of dementia onset. The advantages of the HRS in terms of sample size, representativeness, and longitudinal span are tempered by the lack of in-person assessments. Therefore, to establish the validity of inferences based on the HRS, we first present data confirming strong agreement between results from analyses of educational attainment and APOE genotype (two of the most well-established risk factors for AD/ADRD) in the HRS to results from the Lothian Birth Cohort of 1936 (LBC1936), a prospective longitudinal study of cognitive aging with gold standard cognitive assessments and clinical ascertainment of AD/ADRD. We then report results from the large-scale HRS for an expanded range of genetic and epidemiological risk factors.

Results

Overview of joint longitudinal-hazard modeling

We modelled trajectories of change in cognitive function in relation to dementia hazard using joint longitudinal-hazard models (Online Methods). The joint longitudinal-hazard model integrates a longitudinal mixed model, which decomposes repeated measures of cognitive function into a component capturing variation in baseline level and a component capturing subsequent rate of longitudinal cognitive change, with a hazard model of dementia onset. By incorporating a time-invariant predictor (measured at or prior to baseline) into the model, the joint model allows us to estimate the extent to which a predictor's association with dementia hazard acts via effects on baseline cognitive level and longitudinal rate of cognitive change. We employed this approach to estimate the extent to which a given risk factor for dementia operated via premorbid adult cognitive function (cognitive level) and prodromal cognitive decline (cognitive slope). To estimate how rates of prodromal cognitive change related to subsequent dementia onset, cognitive data from timepoints concurrent with or after dementia onset were set to missing for all analyses.

Risk for dementia onset via Child Cognitive Function, Educational Attainment, APOE, and Comparison across the LBC1936 and HRS

The HRS cohort is a nationally representative, highly powered study with expansive survey data (N = 30,509; age 51+ at baseline and 65+ for full cognitive measurements (see Online Methods and Supplementary Note); 13 waves of assessment spanning a total of 26 years; Sonnega et al., 2014). However, the survey nature of the study necessitates brief, remotely administered, cognitive assessments and algorithmically-(as opposed to clinician-) determined ascertainment of dementia. To establish the validity of inferences based on the HRS, we conducted preliminary longitudinal-hazard analyses for two well-established predictors of dementia, educational attainment and number of APOE e4 alleles. We then compare these HRS-based results to results of analyses of the LBC1936, a prospective longitudinal study of cognitive aging with gold-standard cognitive assessments and clinical ascertainment of dementia (N=989 participants; age 70 years at baseline; 5 waves of assessment spanning a total of 12.54 years; Corley et al., 2023; Mullin et al., 2023; Taylor et al., 2018). LBC1936 also has a high-quality measure of child cognitive function assessed at 11 years, which allows us to confirm that variation in child cognitive function is related to dementia hazard via growth curve level (baseline adult cognitive function) (Scottish Council for Research in Education, 1933). Despite being well-positioned for these validation purposes, the LBC1936 is too small to test the broader set of predictors that we seek to test in the larger HRS sample and, unlike the HRS, it is neither diverse nor nationally representative. We first report results in the HRS European-like (EUR) ancestry subsample for more direct comparisons to the Lothian Birth Cohort (Figure 1, Table S1), with results for the full, ancestrally heterogenous HRS sample and other ancestry subsamples presented in Figures 3 and 4 (Table S2-S8).

We found consistent results across LBC1936 and EUR-like ancestry participants in HRS (Figure 1). Consistent with meta-analyses and reviews implicating educational attainment in baseline cognitive function, but not rate of longitudinal cognitive decline (Lövdén et al., 2020; Seblova et al., 2020), educational attainment was strongly associated with lower dementia hazard via baseline adult cognitive function in both samples (HRHRS = .43 [.40-.45], HRLBC=.44 [.27-.72]) but negligibly associated with dementia hazard via longitudinal slope (HRHRS = .90 [.83-.98], HRLBC=1.22 [.89-1.68]). Conversely, consistent with a very large body of empirical research (e.g. Caselli et al., 2009; Deary et al., 2002), APOE e4 allele count was strongly associated with dementia hazard via longitudinal cognitive slope (HRHRS = 1.88 [1.59-2.23], HRLBC=1.73, [1.31-2.15]) but not baseline cognitive function (HRHRS = 0.99 [.92-1.06], HRLBC=1.05, [.99-1.12]). Genetic Predictors of Dementia in HRS

We assessed APOE e4 status and multi-ancestry polygenic indices (PGIs) in three major ancestry groups available in HRS; European-like ancestry (EUR, N=8,728), African-like ancestry (AFR, N=1,608), and Admixed American ancestry (AMR, N=1,182). We identified large scale GWAS of risk factors corresponding to the Lancet commission report, which we used to construct polygenic indices (Modifiable risk factors outlined in Table S9, GWAS described in Table S10 and Online Methods). We also included PGIs for general cognitive function, AD and for two additional phenotypes not included in the Lancet report, but for which very highly powered GWAS are available, and have been implicated in risk for AD/ADRD: externalizing (NEUR=1,492,085), which describes a broad dimension of health-relevant risk-taking behaviors; and schizophrenia (NEUR=67,390ca/94,015ctrl), polygenic load for which has been linked with cognitive aging in unaffected individuals. Source of GWAS are listed in the Online Methods. These PGIs have several desirable properties that position them to provide complementary information to the measured epidemiological risk factors: a) they can be used to proxy risk factors that were not directly measured or only measured using self-report in the prediction sample, b) they index latent risk that may not be fully realized in a measurable risk factor, and c) they do not change during disease progression (and only index genetic effects on pre-morbid factors when trained using GWAS of cognitively healthy individuals). Nevertheless, it is important to note that genetics only account for a portion of total variation in each exposure, and PGIs only index a portion of that genetic variation. Thus, the effect sizes for PGI analyses are expected to be considerably lower than those for direct epidemiological measures. Epidemiological Predictors of Dementia in HRS

The Lancet 2024 Commission report identified 14 modifiable risk factors for dementia risk: education, hearing loss, high LDL cholesterol, depression, traumatic brain injury, physical inactivity, diabetes, smoking, hypertension, obesity, excessive alcohol consumption, visual loss and air pollution. The HRS contains measures of nearly all these modifiable risk factors (Supplementary Table S9) except traumatic brain injury. Measures of air pollution are not publicly available in the HRS so we were unable to include them in these analyses. Importantly, we use measurements at baseline for epidemiological risk factors, where participants are age 51 or older (Mage = 68), although risk factors may be most modifiable earlier in the life course. We examined whether the measure of each modifiable risk factor was associated with dementia hazard via premorbid cognitive differences and differences in cognitive change. Results for epidemiological factors are shown in Figure 4 and complete parameter estimates are reported in Supplementary Table S5-S8. We focus here on results for the full sample (right panel of Figure 4) and report results separately by demographic group (European-like ancestry sample, selfreported black sample, self-reported Hispanic sample) to evaluate the consistency of estimates across these groups (first, second, and third panels of Figure 4). It is of note that, because subgroup analyses are necessarily lower powered, it is most sensible to compare overall patterns of effects, as opposed to comparing the consistency of significance levels, across subgroups. Patterns were generally consistent across these subgroups, but we point out some notable differences. All epidemiological risk factors were prospectively related to dementia hazard, yet displayed considerable heterogeneity in the extent to which effects were mediated by baseline levels or longitudinal rates of cognitive change. Beyond educational attainment and APOE genotype, Type 2 diabetes was among the strongest predictors of dementia hazard, with effects occurring similarly across both baseline levels and rate of change. A Type 2 diabetes diagnosis was associated with an approximately 50% increase in dementia hazard via lower baseline cognitive function and an additional 50% increase in dementia hazard via steeper rate of cognitive decline (HRi = 1.59 [1.5 -1.69], HRs=1.44 [1.29-1.62]). One SD higher BMI was associated with an approximately 10% increase in dementia risk via lower baseline cognitive function and an additional 6% increase in dementia hazard via steeper rate of cognitive decline (HRi = 1.10 [1.06 -1.14], HRs=1.06 [1.01-1.11]). In the Black subsample higher BMI was protective against dementia, with effects occurring via baseline levels, possibly reflecting a survivor bias or what has sometimes been referred to as the obesity paradox of aging (e.g., Childers & Allison, 2010, Table S7). Self-reported smoking history at baseline was associated with a 25% increase in dementia hazard via lower baseline cognitive function, and an additional 15% increase in dementia hazard via steeper cognitive decline (HRi = 1.26 [1.2 -1.32], HRs=1.16 [1.08-1.24]). One SD higher levels of physical activity was associated with an approximately 15% reduction in risk via higher baseline cognitive function and an additional 5% reduction in risk via shallower rates of cognitive decline. Higher blood pressure, depression, and stroke occurrence prior to baseline assessment were primarily associated with dementia hazard via lower baseline cognition, with some additional mediation by steeper rates of change. Occurrence of stroke prior to baseline assessment was associated with an 80% increase in dementia hazard via lower baseline cognitive function, and an additional 30% increase in dementia hazard via steeper cognitive decline (HRi = 1.81 [1.62 -2.02], HRs=1.29 [1.03-1.61]). Diagnosis of high blood pressure at baseline was associated with a 40% increase in dementia hazard via lower baseline cognitive function, and an additional 10% increase in dementia hazard via steeper cognitive decline (HRi = 1.4 [1.34 -1.46], HRs=1.09 [1.02-1.16]). Reporting one SD higher depression symptoms was associated with an approximately 30% increase in dementia hazard via lower baseline cognitive function, and an additional 8% increase in risk via steeper cognitive decline (HRi = 1.33 [1.3 -1.36], HRs=1.08 [1.05-1.12]). Finally, greater alcohol consumption, higher loneliness, and poorer self-ratings of hearing and eyesight were generally only associated with dementia hazard via lower premorbid cognitive function, although there was some evidence that poorer self-ratings of eyesight were also associated with dementia hazard via steeper rate of cognitive decline in the Hispanic subsample. Although the PGI for hearing loss was associated with dementia hazard via steeper rate of cognitive decline in the EUR subsample, phenotypic poorer self-rating hearing was associated with dementia hazard via lower premorbid cognitive function across all groups. In the full sample, one SD greater self-rating of hearing and eyesight were each associated with an approximately 25% reduction in dementia hazard via baseline levels of cognitive function. One higher SD self-reported drinks per week and one SD higher levels of self-reported loneliness were associated with a 15% and 25% increase in dementia hazard via lower baseline cognitive function, respectively. Discussion Clinically impaired levels of cognitive functioning characteristic of AD/ADRD can arise from a combination of steep rates of cognitive decline to a diagnostic threshold of low cognitive function and lower premorbid levels of cognitive functioning that place individuals closer to the diagnostic threshold prior to the aging process. Here, we have capitalized on the joint analysis of longitudinal and time-to-event data to discern between these time-ordered pathways for a range of genetic and epidemiological predictors of AD/ADRD hazard. We found that the APOE e4 allele and PGIs for AD, type 2 diabetes, stroke, LDL cholesterol, and hearing loss were associated with dementia hazard via rates of cognitive change, and PGIs for educational attainment, cognitive function, physical activity, BMI, smoking, loneliness, schizophrenia, and externalizing were associated with dementia hazard via premorbid differences in cognitive function. History of stroke, Type 2 diabetes, BMI, smoking, and physical activity exhibited associations with dementia hazard via both baseline level and longitudinal change. Both depression and high blood pressure were also associated with dementia hazard via longitudinal change, although their associations via baseline levels were appreciably stronger. Hearing, eyesight, loneliness, and alcohol intake were associated with dementia exclusively via baseline levels. These findings have considerable implications for understanding the mechanisms of AD/ADRD risk and determine the most promising periods of the lifespan for application of interventions targeting each of these factors. Notably, effects varied by ancestry group. For example, PGIs for AD and Type 2 Diabetes were similarly associated with dementia hazard via cognitive change in the EUR and AMR groups, but were not significantly associated with dementia hazard via cognitive change in the AFR group. Given the relatively lower samples sizes of the AFR GWAS contributing to the multiancestry PGIs, lower PGI prediction may be at least partially explained by issues of portability related to differences in minor allele frequency and linkage disequilibrium structure across ancestry groups (Wang et al., 2020). Similarly, some PGIs (e.g. macular degeneration, white matter hyperintensities) were computed based on GWAS with limited sample size in all ancestries, including EUR. This may explain why PGIs for some risk factors had null or small associations with to dementia hazard. While we validated our analyses by demonstrating very consistent results for educational attainment and APOE in HRS and the deeply phenotyped LBC1936, the sample size of LBC1936 did not permit a highly powered examination of the broader range of risk factors that we examined in the HRS. Langa-weir scores have been extensively validated and used in other research (Crimmins et al., 2011; Langa et al., 2005 Langa et al., , 2017;; Wei et al., 2018), but may have key differences from clinically diagnosed AD/ADRD. Future work in large deeply phenotyped cohorts or consortia may provide more mechanistic insight into the links between modifiable risk factors and cognitive aging processes over time. An association between a baseline risk factor and dementia hazard via subsequent cognitive change may arise, not because that risk factor causally affects cognitive decline, but because it represents an early indicator of disease progression that forecasts but does not causally affect decline. Lower cognition in early life may itself be a risk factor for subsequent changes in lifestyle, health behaviors, and physical health (Deary et al., 2021; Twig et al., 2018). Although the analyses reported here excluded individuals with dementia at baseline, it is likely that many participants experienced undetected Alzheimer's pathology-related neurocognitive change for years prior to the baseline assessment. Thus, although we consider the risk factors identified to be promising targets for future research efforts to prevent dementia, their causal status and biological mechanisms of action currently remain ambiguous. Randomized controlled trials are needed to obtain decisive conclusions regarding the effects of these risk factors and their utility as intervention targets to prevent, delay, or treat AD/ADRD.

Online Methods

Participants and Measures

The Health and Retirement Study

The Health & Retirement Study (HRS) is a nationally-representative (of the USA) sample of adults over age 50 years (and their spouses, regardless of age) that includes oversamples of Hispanic and Black participants (Sonnega et al., 2014). The sample for these analyses (N=30,509) contains adults that participated in cognitive testing in at least one of 14 HRS timepoints (every two years from 1996-2020) and those that participated in cognitive testing in the AHEAD sample in 1995, yielding up to 25 years of longitudinal cognitive data per person. Participants aged 50 years and younger were excluded. Data from the 1992 and 1994 waves of HRS were excluded because stimuli for some cognitive tasks administered in these waves were not the same as those from later waves. Participants receiving Langa-Weir scores corresponding to MCI or dementia at baseline were excluded. Supplementary Table S11 reports basic descriptive statistics for participants at their first wave of cognitive testing. Cognitive Measures. The HRS cognitive battery includes 7 measures of cognitive function: Immediate and delayed word recall, serial 7s, backwards counting, dates, naming, and vocabulary. Details on administration of each cognitive task can be found in Herzog & Wallace (1997). We used total cognition score (ranges from 0-35) which is the sum of scores on the immediate recall (0-10 points), delayed recall (0-10 points), serial 7s (0 to 5 points), backwards counting (0-2 points), dates (0-4 points), and president and object naming tasks (0-2 points each). HRS participants completed all 7 cognitive tasks at baseline and at follow-up time points at which they were 65 years and older. Additionally, the telephone interview for cognitive status (TICS) score includes serial 7s, backwards counting, date naming, object naming, and president/vice president naming tasks, which are assessed for all participants at all time points, regardless of age. Primary models were fit to the total cognition composite (a continuously distributed score without evidence for ceiling or floor effects; Figure S1). We also conducted a set of sensitivity analyses using immediate recall scores (also normally distributed with minimal evidence of ceiling or floor effects) to test sensitivity of results to modeling changes specifically associated with episodic memory and a set of sensitivity analyses using the TICS score to test the sensitivity of results to the availability of more frequent follow-up measures between ages 51 and 65 (Supplementary Note). Cognitive assessment data from time points concurrent with or after a dementia onset were set to missing to specifically estimate how rates of premorbid cognitive change relate to subsequent dementia hazard. We did not include imputed values for the cognitive measures or cognitive data from proxy respondents. As Langa-Weir scores were only available in 1995 and later timepoints, we did not include cognitive scores from 1993. Participants whose original first testing date was in 1992, 1993, or 1994 were assigned a baseline testing date as the earliest cognitive testing they participated in that was included (e.g. 1995 or 1996).

Dementia Ascertainment.

Dementia is classified in the HRS using Langa-Weir scores, described extensively in previous publications (Crimmins et al., 2011; Langa et al., 2005 Langa et al., , 2019)). Langa-Weir scores were developed by mapping scores on the HRS cognitive measures to cognitive states ascertained in the Aging, Demographics, and Memory Study (ADAMS) sample by a consensus panel based on 3-4 hour structured home-based interviews by a team consisting of a nurse and neuropsychology technician. When a participant is unable to respond for themselves, Langa-Weir scores are based on information from proxy respondents, who complete a direct assessment of memory and an assessment of limitations in five instrumental activities of daily living and an interviewer assessment of difficulty completing the interview due to cognitive limitation. Langa-Weir classifications range from 1-3, where 1 indicates normal cognitive functioning, 2 indicates cognitive impairment but not dementia, and 3 indicates dementia. Langa-Weir classifications in HRS correspond with the gold standard assessments made in ADAMS, particularly for individuals that are cognitively normal and those diagnosed with dementia, and Langa-Weir scores are routinely used in epidemiological research (e.g. Davydow et al., 2015; Langa et al., 2017; Wei et al., 2018). We classify participants that receive a Langa-Weir score of 3 as meeting criteria for incident dementia, and thus code all future Langa-Weir scores for that participant as 3. For hazard analyses, participants are considered right-censored if they dropped out of the study prior to receiving a Langa-Weir score of 3. Across all years of measurement, 30-40% of all dementia cases in that year were based on proxy data, and 30-40% of initial instances of dementia classification were based on proxy data (Figure S2, Figure S3). Note that we also perform a validity analysis that compares results for APOE e4 status and educational attainment (two canonical predictors of dementia hazard) to those from the LBC1936 in which AD/ADRD is ascertained by a team of clinical dementia experts, and cognitive function is measured using an extensive in-person neurocognitive test battery.

Confirmatory Predictors

We selected two well-established predictors of dementia risk to validate the longitudinal-hazard approach: Educational attainment and number of APOE e4 alleles. Education was coded as number of completed years of schooling (Supplemental Table S12), to account for differences between school systems in the United States and Scotland (Miller, 2007), and divided by four such that one unit corresponds to a 4-year degree. The e4 allele of the APOE gene is well-known risk factor for AD/ADRD that occurs commonly within the general population (Corder et al., 1993; Serrano-Pozo et al., 2021; Strittmatter & Roses, 1996; William Rebeck et al., 1993). APOE status was coded where 0 indicated the participant had no e4 alleles, 1 indicated the participant had one e4 allele and 2 indicated the participant had 2 e4 alleles. Of the 16,802 participants with APOE data, 4288 (26%) carried 1 e4 allele and 373 (2%) carried 2 e4 alleles. The majority of participants had APOE directly genotyped, with APOE genotyped imputed from SNP array data for small proportions of participants. APOE was imputed for 1,090/8,796 of EUR participants, 225/2,329 of AFR participants, and 132/1,766 of AMR participants.

Polygenic Predictors

Polygenic indices (PGIs; Becker et al., 2021) are individual summary scores of weighted associations between genetic variants and a given outcome, weighted by effect size estimates derived from a large external Genome Wide Association Study (GWAS) that did not include the prediction sample (here, the HRS; we use summary statistics that exclude the HRS). We selected the most recent EUR GWAS corresponding to the modifiable risk factors identified in the 2024 Lancet Commission on Dementia report (Livingston et al., 2024) in addition to other factors that have been separately implicated in dementia risk (i.e. externalizing, schizophrenia). We selected corresponding AFR and AMR GWAS with sample sizes greater than 3,000 or, for traits where EUR N < 100,000, we use GWAS data where AFR or AMR sample size is greater than 5% of the EUR sample size. We use the following GWAS: educational attainment (NEUR=3,026,656; Okbay et al., 2022), general cognition (NEUR= 274,617; Davies et al., 2018), hearing loss (NEUR=147,997ca/575,269ctrl; Trpchevska et al., 2022); LDL cholesterol (NEUR=1,097,570, NAFR=262,383, NAMR=58,398; Graham et al., 2021); depression (NEUR=658,808ca/4,364,225ctrl; Adams et al., 2025, NAFR=9.649ca/122,347ctrl, NAMR=22,927ca/340,403ctrl; Meng et al., 2024); stroke (NEUR=110,182ca/1.5Mctrl, NAFR=3,691ca/20,030ctrl, NAMR=1,516ca/4,146ctrl; Mishra et al., 2022), white matter hyperintensities (NEUR=50,970; Sargurupremraj et al., 2020); moderateto-vigorous physical activity (NEUR=608,595; Wang et al., 2022); type 2 diabetes (NEUR=428,452ca/2.1Mctrl, NAFR=50,251ca/103,909ctrl, NAMR=29,375ca/59,368ctrl; Suzuki et al., 2024); smoking (NEUR=557,337ca/674,754ctrl, NAFR=8,571ca/13,427ctrl; Saunders et al., 2022); systolic and diastolic blood pressure (NEUR=1,028,980; Keaton et al., 2024, NAFR=119,274, NAMR=57,988; Verma et al., 2024), BMI (NEUR=681,275; Yengo et al., 2018, NAFR=118,993, NAMR=57,793; Verma et al., 2024); problematic alcohol use (NEUR=903,147, NAFR=40,116ca/82,455ctrl; Zhou et al., 2023), alcohol consumption (N=2,428,851, NAFR=7,072, NAMR=5,162; Saunders et al., 2022); loneliness (NEUR=456,422; Jiang et al., 2019); early age related macular degeneration (NEUR=14,034ca/91,214ctrl; Winkler et al., 2020, NAFR=7,126ca/109,352ctrl, NAMR=4,642ca/52,515ctrl; Verma et al., 2024); Alzheimer's Disease (excluding the APOE region (Chr19:45,000,000-45,800,000, GRCh37/hg19), as APOE genotype is entered as a separate predictor (NEUR=100,806ca/2,026,274ctrl, NAFR=4,379ca/66,731ctrl; Uffelmann et al., 2026). We also created PGIs for two additional behavioral/psychiatric phenotypes not included in the Lancet report, but for which very highly powered GWAS are available, and have been implicated in risk for AD/ADRD (Cai & Huang, 2018; Ritchie et al., 2020; Solares et al., 2023): externalizing (NEUR=1,492,085; Karlsson Linnér et al., 2021), which describes a broad dimension of health-relevant risk-taking behaviors; and schizophrenia (NEUR=67,390ca/94,015ctrl, NAFR=6,152ca/3,918ctrl; Trubetskoy et al., 2022), polygenic load for which (even among unaffected individuals) has been linked with cognitive aging. Although GWAS data for AFR and AMR groups fitting our criteria were available for hearing loss (Verma et al., 2024), these data were primarily from the Million Veteran Program (MVP), a cohort of military members, which may be especially more likely to experience hearing loss due to non-aging related factors. The EUR GWAS of hearing loss from Verma et al., 2024 and the Trpchevska et al., (2022) GWAS of hearing loss were genetically correlated at 0.63, suggesting that there may be meaningful differences in the etiology of hearing loss between these two cohorts. As a result, we elected to only use the EUR GWAS data from Trpchevska et al., (2022) and did not compute multi-ancestry PGIs for hearing loss. Genotype array data necessary for computing PGIs are available for n=8,728 non-Hispanic White participants, n=1,608 AFR participants and n=1,182 AMR participants. Primary global ancestry was determined in HRS using self-reported race and ethnicity in addition to individual principal component loadings from principal component analysis of all study subjects. The EUR sample includes self-reported non-Hispanic whites with PC loadings within 1 standard deviation of the mean for eigenvectors 1 and 2 in PC analysis. The AFR sample includes self-reported African Americans within 2 standard deviations of the mean of all self-identified African Americans for eigenvector 1 and 1 standard deviation of the mean for eigenvector 2 in PC analysis of all subjects. The AMR sample includes all self-reported Hispanic participants with outliers removed that fell outside 6 standard deviations of the mean for PCs 1-10. For prediction in EUR samples, or in cases where only EUR GWAS data was available, we use SBayesRC to derive PGI weights. SBayesRC (Zheng et al., 2024) is a Bayesian method that incorporates functional annotation data to distinguish likely causal SNPs from non-causal SNPs. SBayesRC uses a low-rank model to model the LD among common variants, then uses a multicomponent annotation-dependent mixture prior to model the distribution of SNP effect sizes. For each trait, we estimated PGI weights with SNPs sorted into five using probability starting values of 95%, 2%, 2%, and 1%, with effect sizes scaled at .00, .01, .10, and 1.00. We used BaselineLD v2.2 genomic annotation data (Gazal et al., 2017) and approximately 1 million common SNPs from HapMap3 for LD reference (Zheng et al., 2024). The MHC region was excluded from these analyses. We used the SBayesRC weights to estimate a score for each participant using PLINK2 (Chang et al., 2015). PGIs computed from European-like ancestry (EUR) summary statistics typically produce attenuated predictive performance in other ancestries. However, GWAS data in non-EUR ancestries is often limited (Privé et al., 2022; J. Y. Wang et al., 2026). To maximize prediction in AFR and AMR samples where AFR and AMR GWAS data were available (according to specifications outlined above), we generated multi-ancestry PGI weights using PRS-CSx, a Bayesian method which couples genetic effects across populations by estimating a shared continuous shrinkage prior (Ruan et al., 2022). We used PRS-CSx auto to derive multi-ancestry PGI weights integrating data from the EUR GWAS and relevant ancestry group (AFR or AMR) corresponding to each non-EUR participant's primary global ancestry. PRS-CSx auto derives PGI weights from continuous shrinkage priors shared across the two ancestry groups (i.e. EUR and AFR or EUR and AMR). The "auto" option develops global shrinkage priors directly from GWAS summary statistics, bypassing the need for a tuning sample. We used the "meta" option, where PRS-CSx combines SNP posterior effect sizes from both ancestry groups using an inverse-variance-weighted meta-analysis to derive trans-ancestry PGI weights. We used the PRS-CSx weights to estimate PGIs for each HRS participant using PLINK2 (Chang et al., 2015). In all models with genetic factors, we included principal components of ancestry to avoid confounds associated with population stratification. PGIs were standardized prior to analysis.

Epidemiological Predictors of Dementia Hazard.

We selected epidemiological predictors available in the HRS that correspond with the modifiable risk factors identified in the most recent Lancet Dementia Commission Report (Livingston et al., 2024), which include education, hearing loss, high LDL cholesterol, depression, physical inactivity, diabetes, smoking, hypertension, obesity, excessive alcohol use, social isolation, visual loss, and air pollution. We use the measurement of each predictor concurrent with or prior to the baseline cognitive assessment to afford prospective prediction of change and dementia hazard. For measures introduced to the HRS at later waves, we removed cognitive data from waves preceding that measurement (Years each epidemiological predictor was assessed are noted in Table S9). Hearing and Vision are self-rated (from1 = "poor" to5 = "excellent"). Depression is measured by the 8-item CESD (Karim et al., 2015). Physical activity is measured using selfreport of frequency of engagement in vigorous physical activity (1 = "hardly ever or never" to5 = "every day"). Alcohol consumption is calculated as the number of days per week a participant reports drinking alcohol multiplied by the average number of drinks consumed per day. Social Isolation is measured using the sum of 3 items from the UCLA Loneliness scale (Russell, 1996) that assesses the degree to which participants lack companionship, feel isolated from others, and feel left out. Stroke (implicated in the Lancet report, but not included among the list of 14 modifiable risk factors), hypertension, smoking, and type 2 diabetes are measured by participant self-report. Obesity is derived from BMI, based on self-reported height and weight. We were unable to assess air pollution in the HRS as these data were not publicly available.

Validation Sample: The Lothian Birth Cohort of 1936

Participants. The Lothian Birth Cohort of 1936 (LBC1936) is a narrow-age longitudinal study of aging of community-dwelling adults born in 1936 living in Edinburgh and the Lothians area of Scotland (Deary et al., 2012). All LBC1936 participants are of European-like ancestry. The present study comprises a total of 989 participants from the first five assessments (wave 1, at a mean age about 70 years, to wave 5, at a mean age about 82 years) spanning a total of 12.54 years (Taylor et al., 2018). Of the 1,091 LBC1936 participants measured at baseline, we restricted analyses to participants that had known dementia status and age data for at least one assessment wave, death, or dementia diagnosis, resulting in N=989 participants. Cognitive Measures. LBC1936 participants underwent a battery of 13 cognitive tests representing visuospatial ability, processing speed, verbal memory, and crystallized knowledge (Deary et al., 2012). Cognitive test scores were standardized by subtracting the mean of all data points at the baseline occasion and dividing by the standard deviation of all the data points at baseline. Because we were specifically interested in estimating how rates of premorbid cognitive change related to subsequent dementia onset, 33 data points from cognitive assessments administered concurrent with or subsequent to a dementia diagnosis were set to missing.

Dementia Ascertainment.

As detailed in Mullin et al. (2023), incident dementia was ascertained based on consensus review board meeting by a multidisciplinary team of clinical dementia experts, drawing on data from electronic health records, death certificates, and home visit clinician assessments. A total of 145 participants in the LBC1936 study received a diagnosis of dementia (15% of the sample), with 46.21% of diagnosed participants being female. Medical records and death certificates were obtained approximately concurrent with wave 5. Thus, for participants who were not classified as meeting criteria for dementia and participated in wave 5, their censoring age is their individual age at wave 5. For participants who were not classified as meeting criteria for dementia and survived until wave 5, but did not participant in wave 5, their censoring age was set as the mean age at wave 5 (82 years). For individuals who were deceased prior to wave 5, but who were not classified as meeting criteria for dementia, we used age at death as the age at censoring. The absolute frequencies of dementia-free participants, dementiapositive participants, and censored cases across age groups are presented in Supplementary Figure S4. APOE status. APOE-e4 alleles are a key risk factor for dementia and cognitive decline in older age (Belloy et al., 2019; Blacker et al., 1997; Petersen et al., 1995; Strittmatter & Roses, 1996). Participants had blood samples taken for DNA extraction. APOE-e4 status was determined by genotyping the single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), rs429358 and rs7412 using TaqMan technology at the Wellcome Trust Clinical Research Facility Genetics Core at the Western General Hospital in Edinburgh (Luciano et al., 2009). The genotyping was used to determine the presence or absence of APOE-e4 alleles. Out of the 934 with available genotyping data, 262 were carriers of one copy of the APOE-e4 allele (28%) and 19 were carriers of two copies of the APOE-e4 allele (2%). Years of education. Education was coded as number of completed years of formal full-time schooling, to account for differences between school systems in the United States and Scotland (Miller, 2007), and divided by four such that one unit corresponds to a 4-year degree.

Child cognitive function.

Almost all LBC1936 participants underwent the Scottish Mental Survey of 1947 at approximately 11 years of age, which included the Moray House Test No. 12, a measure of general cognitive function (Deary et al., 2000 (Deary et al., , 2004)). We expected that early life cognitive function would be related to dementia hazard via the growth curve intercept (baseline level of adult cognitive function) and not the slope. The availability of child cognitive function allowed us to explicitly test this key prediction.

Joint Longitudinal-Hazard Models

We modelled trajectories of change in cognitive function in relation to dementia hazard using joint longitudinal-hazard models (Ghisletta et al., 2006; Henderson et al., 2000; McArdle et al., 2005). By incorporating a time-invariant predictor (measured at or prior to baseline) into the model, the joint model allows us to estimate the extent to which a predictor's association with dementia hazard acts via effects on baseline cognitive level and longitudinal rate of cognitive change.
