Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

The World Health Organization conditionally recommends mass drug administration (MDA) for short-term reductions in malaria burden, including in emergencies. We assessed the association of a time-limited MDA and indoor residual spraying (IRS) campaign with malaria prevalence and mortality up to 14 months after its last MDA round in Angumu, a malaria holoendemic and conflict-affected health zone in the Democratic Republic of the Congo.

Methods Fourteen health areas were allocated non-randomly to MDA+IRS (those with the highest malaria incidence), IRS alone (those with the least MSF support for case management), or routine care (Routine). Malaria prevalence by rapid diagnostic test was measured at 0, 7, 13, 18, and 24 months from March 2022, and under-five and crude mortality at 0, 13, and 24 months. Within each survey, zones were compared by mixedeffects regression adjusted for area-level baseline values and individual displacement status. Routine areas were excluded after receiving interventions, leaving one at 24 months.

At every later survey, the odds of malaria in the MDA+IRS zone were approximately a quarter to a third of those in the Routine zone. This difference persisted to the last survey, 14 months after the last MDA round, with prevalence of 24.4% compared with 56.4% (odds ratio [OR] 0.31, 95% CI 0.19-0.50). IRS alone was associated with lower prevalence during the campaign, but not statistically significantly afterwards. Prevalence was lower in the MDA+IRS than in the IRS zone at 18 and 24 months (ORs 0.37 and 0.47); during the campaign, the difference was not statistically significant after adjustment for baseline prevalence. Mortality differences were not statistically significant, except lower crude mortality in the MDA+IRS than the Routine zone at 13 months. In 2023, the sporozoite rate was lower in the intervention zones than the Routine zone, and facility test positivity was lowest in the MDA+IRS zone at every later survey.

In holoendemic, conflict-affected settings, malaria programmes and humanitarian organisations may consider co-timed MDA and IRS where high coverage is achievable, with prevalence monitored in repeated surveys to decide when to repeat the campaign.

Background

Malaria caused an estimated 610,000 deaths worldwide in 2024, approximately 95% in the World Health Organization (WHO) African Region [1], with most malaria deaths occurring among children under five [1]. Among people in humanitarian crises, crowding, malnutrition, and disrupted case management increase malaria incidence and case fatality [2, 3]. The Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC) alone accounted for approximately one-eighth of global cases and deaths in 2024 [1].

Mass drug administration (MDA) is the treatment of the whole population of a defined area with a full antimalarial course at approximately the same time, regardless of symptoms or infection status [4]. WHO conditionally recommends MDA for short-term reductions in malaria burden in moderate-to-high transmission and in emergencies [5]. In a Cochrane review, MDA lowered Plasmodium falciparum prevalence one to three months after treatment in very-lowto-low transmission settings, but the effect was attenuated by four to six months [6]. There was no evidence of an effect in moderate-to-high transmission settings [6, 7].

MDA clears infection in those treated, which reduces the human parasite reservoir, while indoor residual spraying (IRS) of insecticide on inner house walls shortens the survival of mosquitoes that rest on them, which reduces the number that survive until they become infectious. Transmission models predict that vector control, by IRS or insecticide-treated nets (ITNs), lengthens the effect of MDA [8, 9]. In one review, 63% of MDA studies had less than six months of follow-up, and sustained reductions were reported mainly in low-transmission, island, or remote settings, usually in combination with vector control [10]. No evaluation in high-transmission sub-Saharan Africa had measured malaria prevalence more than six months after the last round of a time-limited MDA and IRS campaign.

Angumu health zone, in Ituri province, eastern DRC, is holoendemic for malaria [11]. Since December 2017, violence within Ituri province has created mass displacement of populations into and within Angumu [11]. A mortality survey in January 2020 found a crude mortality rate (CMR) and an under-five mortality rate (U5MR) above emergency thresholds [11]. Between 2019 and 2021, households reported malaria or anaemia as the cause of 66% to 72% of under-five deaths [11].

From September 2020 to January 2021, the Ministry of Health and Médecins Sans Frontières (MSF) delivered three MDA rounds in four health areas [11]. This 2020 MDA campaign was associated with a halving of U5MR over approximately 10 weeks of follow-up [11]. Neither how long the lower U5MR lasted nor any association with malaria infection was measured [11]. The present study measured both after a second campaign.

From late June 2022 to May 2023, the Ministry of Health and MSF delivered four MDA rounds and two IRS rounds in the health areas with the highest malaria incidence in routine data (MDA+IRS zone). Other areas received the two IRS rounds alone (IRS zone) or neither intervention (Routine zone) (Figure 1). We assessed the association of these interventions with malaria prevalence and mortality. Five population-based surveys were conducted: survey 1 before the campaign, surveys 2 and 3 during it, and surveys 4 and 5 approximately 8 and 14 months after the last MDA round. The primary outcome was malaria prevalence, compared within each survey between each intervention zone and the Routine zone. The MDA+IRS zone was also compared with the IRS zone, to estimate the difference associated with adding MDA where IRS was also delivered. Secondary outcomes were U5MR and CMR at surveys 1, 3, and 5. Entomological and routine facility surveillance were analysed as supporting evidence.

Methods

Study setting and design

Angumu health zone comprises 22 health areas, eight of which were excluded from the study for insecurity [12]. The remaining 14 health areas were assigned to three intervention zones: Routine (Abia, Ara, Awasi, Cawa, and Linga), MDA+IRS (Apala, Gengere, Lanyi, Ugudo-Zii, and Uyandu), and IRS (Anzika, Pakala, Panyadong, and Udongo Abira) (Figure 1). Zone assignment was an operational decision and was not randomised: MDA+IRS was allocated to the health areas with the highest malaria incidence in routine data, and IRS alone was allocated to those health areas most lacking MSF support for routine case management. Gengere lies in a river delta on the shore of Lake Albert, where the lake spread inland between 2019 and 2021 [13], and its swamps provide breeding sites for Anopheles.

The 2020 MDA campaign had been delivered in health areas of both intervention zones but not in any Routine area [11]. Abia (Routine) and Gengere (MDA+IRS) had received IRS in 2021 (Additional file 1: S1 Text).

Interventions

The MDA+IRS zone received four MDA rounds: rounds 1 and 2 with pyronaridine-artesunate in July and August 2022, and rounds 3 and 4 with dihydroartemisinin-piperaquine (DHApiperaquine) in January and February 2023. Two IRS rounds with SumiShield 50WG (clothianidin) [14] were delivered in the MDA+IRS and IRS zones, from June to November 2022 and from February to May 2023. The campaign extended from late June 2022 to May 2023. Routine care in every zone comprised facility-based testing and treatment, and the distribution of ITNs, including a health-zone-wide distribution in December 2023 (Figure 2A). MSF also distributed ITNs in Awasi in August 2023 (MSF Operational Centre Geneva, unpublished programme data).

Four of the five Routine health areas later received interventions (crossover areas; Figure 2B). In the primary, as-treated classification, a crossover area contributed to the Routine zone before its intervention and was excluded from the zone comparison from the following survey onwards. Abia and Cawa (IRS, August and September 2023) were excluded from survey 4 onwards, and Ara and Linga (MDA, January and February 2024) from survey 5. At survey 5, the Routine zone comprised Awasi alone, the only health area not to have received IRS or MDA up to survey 5.

Surveys and outcomes

Five surveys were conducted between March 2022 and May 2024. Survey 1 (March to April 2022) was the baseline (Figure 2A). Months from baseline were calculated from the midpoint of each survey: 0, 7, 13, 18, and 24 at surveys 1 to 5. Each cluster comprised one rooftop, selected at random from a frame of rooftops geocoded on a satellite image dated February 2022, and its four nearest neighbours. Sample size was calculated separately for each survey (Additional file 1: S1 Text).

All household members aged 2 months or older present at the visit were tested for malaria at surveys 1, 2, and 4, and those in a random subset of households at surveys 3 and 5. Participants were tested with a rapid diagnostic test (RDT) for histidine-rich protein 2 (HRP2; SD Bioline Ag P.f., Standard Diagnostics, Republic of Korea). Malaria prevalence was the number of RDT-positive participants divided by the number with a valid result. Displacement status was recorded for each household member as displaced or resident (Additional file 1: S1 Text).

Mortality was assessed among all eligible household members by retrospective recall at surveys 1, 3, and 5, which followed the low-transmission season (the December to February dry months). The recall period ran from 1 January 2021, 18 April 2022, and 9 April 2023 at surveys 1, 3, and 5, respectively, to the interview. At survey 1, it ended before the campaign began. At survey 3, it began 10 weeks before IRS round 1 and therefore included all four MDA rounds, IRS round 1, and most of IRS round 2. At survey 5, it began after the last MDA round, in the final weeks of IRS round 2 (Figure 2A). CMR and U5MR were the number of deaths (at all ages and among children under five, respectively) divided by their person-time, expressed as deaths per 10,000 person-days.

Coverage of MDA rounds 1 and 2 and IRS round 1 was measured at survey 2 (November and December 2022), and that of the later rounds at survey 3. Intervention coverage was the proportion who had received a round (MDA) or whose dwelling had been sprayed (IRS) (Additional file 1: S1 Text).

Entomological surveillance

Entomological surveillance data were collected from May 2022 to December 2024 with miniature Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) light traps operated indoors (Additional file 1: S3 Figure ). Entomological indicators were compared descriptively between zones, to assess whether the zones ranked in the same order on these indicators as on survey malaria prevalence. From May to October 2022, the design placed 15 traps in each zone, 10 in houses selected at random from the rooftop frame and five in purposively selected traditional houses (cob walls, thatched roofs). Each trap operated for four consecutive nights a week and was moved to a different house each week, within four-week sampling cycles. In October 2022, to maintain catches in the intervention zones after IRS, five traps were withdrawn from the Routine zone and four of them were redeployed, two to each intervention zone. Anopheles were identified morphologically to species complex, and tested for Plasmodium falciparum sporozoites by circumsporozoite enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) [15]. Mosquitoes for testing were selected at random within each trap-week's collection, and where catches were large, mainly in Gengere, only part of the catch was tested. Sporozoite results were used from January 2023 only, because the 2022 ELISA results were judged unreliable [15].

The sporozoite rate was the number of sporozoite-positive Anopheles divided by the number with a recorded laboratory result. Estimation of the human biting rate (HBR) assumed that every Anopheles caught in a CDC light trap was seeking a human host. HBR was the number of Anopheles caught in working traps (one trap in one house for one night on which it functioned) divided by the number of occupant-nights: the people who sleep in the trapped house, summed over its working trap-nights. The estimated entomological inoculation rate (EIR) was HBR multiplied by the sporozoite rate and by 365, and was reported as infective bites per person per month (the annual rate divided by 12). Because trapping was continuous, a crossover health area was excluded from the zone comparison from the month of its first intervention, rather than from the next survey as in the survey analysis. Indicators were estimated by half-year from July 2022 to November 2024, which matches the intervals between surveys. The protocol specified 2-month periods, but most held too few sporozoitepositive mosquitoes for a stable EIR, and the 2-month series is reported as supplementary (Additional file 1: S5 Figure). Residual efficacy of IRS round 2 was assessed by monthly WHO cone bioassays with local Anopheles on four mud walls in Panyadong (IRS zone) and four plastic-sheeting shelters in Gengere, from April to December 2023 (Additional file 1: S1 Text). Except for the pooled sporozoite rate, each was calculated per health area and averaged over a zone's areas with population weights (Additional file 2).

Routine facility surveillance

We analysed the monthly test positivity rate (TPR; positive RDTs divided by tests performed) in national malaria programme (PNLP) facility reports, October 2021 to October 2024. TPR was used as an indicator of malaria infection in the population, which it reflects where testing practice, care-seeking, and RDT supply are similar between zones and stable over time. Zone TPR was the unweighted mean of its health areas (13 of the 14 had data). As in the survey analysis, a crossover area was excluded from the zone comparison from the month of its first intervention. We counted the surveys at which the same zone had the lowest TPR and the lowest survey prevalence, and at which the three zones ranked identically on TPR and on survey prevalence (Additional file 1: S1 Text).

Statistical analysis

Crude malaria prevalence, crude mortality rates, and crude odds ratios (ORs) and rate ratios (RRs) were estimated at each survey with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Estimates were survey-weighted, with the cluster as the only sampling stage and zone as stratum. Surveyweighted malaria prevalence was also estimated for each health area, to compare areas across zones (Additional file 1: S8 Table).

In the primary model, each participant's RDT result was modelled by mixed-effects logistic regression over the five surveys, with a zone-by-survey interaction and random intercepts for survey-specific cluster and for household within cluster. The model was adjusted for age, sex, displacement status, and area-level malaria prevalence at baseline (Additional file 1: S1 Text). Baseline prevalence was included because zones were assigned partly by prior malaria incidence. Displacement status was included because the proportion displaced at baseline was highest in the MDA+IRS zone. The primary contrast was the within-survey OR, comparing each intervention zone with the Routine zone at the same survey. The modelstandardised prevalence difference between zones, in percentage points (pp), was also estimated, because an OR lies further from 1 than the prevalence ratio when prevalence is high.

The difference-in-differences (DiD) ratio of ORs, from the zone-by-survey interaction, divided the change in the odds of malaria since survey 1 in an intervention zone by the corresponding change in the Routine zone. It therefore removed any difference between zones at survey 1 that remained after adjustment. This remaining difference, the residual survey-1 OR, was the OR between zones at survey 1 after adjustment for baseline prevalence. The within-survey OR retains it.

The MDA+IRS zone was also compared with the IRS zone within each survey to estimate the difference associated with adding MDA where IRS was also delivered.

U5MR and CMR were modelled by mixed-effects Poisson regression with a log person-time offset, adjusted for sex, displacement status, and, at surveys 3 and 5, the area-level baseline value of the same rate. CMR was modelled over surveys 1, 3, and 5 together, with the zoneby-survey interaction, adjustment for age, and the random intercepts of the prevalence model, with the DiD ratio of RRs for CMR defined in the same way. U5MR was modelled separately at each survey with a cluster random intercept only, because a pooled model did not fit reliably on the small number of under-five deaths (Additional file 1: S1 Text). Within each survey, RRs were estimated for each intervention zone compared with the Routine zone, and, at surveys 3 and 5, for MDA+IRS compared with IRS.

We conducted six sensitivity analyses of prevalence. First, the Routine comparator was held fixed at Awasi at every survey, because the Routine zone fell from five health areas to three and then one. Second, a health-area random intercept was added, because zone was assigned to only 14 health areas and intervals based on clusters may be too narrow. Third, because zone was assigned to health areas, an area-level permutation test treated each health area as one observation, without adjustment for covariates. It compared the difference in mean area prevalence between two zones with the differences obtained when the health areas of those two zones were divided into two groups of the same sizes in every possible way. Fourth, E-values were calculated for the ORs comparing the MDA+IRS zone with the Routine zone at surveys 2 to 5 and with the IRS zone at surveys 4 and 5, to assess the strength of unmeasured confounding needed to explain them [16]. Fifth, two models added net use, because it differed between zones and affects malaria risk: the proportion of RDTtested participants in each health area who slept under a net at survey 1, and, at surveys 1, 3, and 5, whether each participant slept under a net the night before. Sixth, a post-hoc analysis treated Gengere as a stratum separate from the other four MDA+IRS health areas, because its lakeside swamps supplied most of the Anopheles caught in the MDA+IRS zone and, unlike the other four areas, its prevalence changed little after survey 1. For mortality, CMR was re-estimated with Awasi as the fixed comparator, with Gengere as a separate stratum, and, at survey 3, with person-time from MDA round 1 (11 July 2022), because the survey-3 recall period began before the campaign. U5MR and CMR were also re-estimated by survey-weighted quasi-Poisson regression with design-based variance, because the mixed models assumed Poisson variance and overdispersion was not modelled. These analyses are in Additional file 1 ( S2 and S3 Tables).

The pooled sporozoite rate was given an exact binomial 95% CI, and the other entomological indicators bootstrap 95% CIs from resampling of trapping sites within health areas (Additional file 1: S1 Text). No hypothesis tests were applied to the entomological indicators. Analyses used R version 4.5.2. The study is reported following the STROBE guideline for crosssectional studies (Additional file 3).

Results

Study population and intervention coverage

The five surveys enrolled 621 to 3,053 households (2,597 to 15,990 household members). After exclusion of crossover areas, the zone comparison included 516 of the 621 households (83.1%) at survey 4 and 2,267 of the 3,028 (74.9%) at survey 5 (Additional file 1: S4 and S6 Tables). In the MDA+IRS zone, MDA coverage decreased from 91.2% (95% CI 87.9-93.8) at round 1 to 84.8% (95% CI 82.9-86.5) at round 4. IRS coverage was at least 94.5% (95% CI 92.2-96.1) in the MDA+IRS and IRS zones at both rounds (Additional file 1: S7 Table).

At survey 1, before the campaign, CMR was highest in the MDA+IRS zone, whereas malaria prevalence was lowest in that zone and highest in the IRS zone. CMR was 0.84 (95% CI 0.60-1.07) deaths per 10,000 person-days in the Routine zone and 1.38 (95% CI 1.15-1.60) in the MDA+IRS zone, an RR of 1.78 (95% CI 1.25-2.56) (Table 2). The CMR difference between the IRS and Routine zones, and both U5MR differences from the Routine zone, were not statistically significant. Malaria prevalence was 36.1% (95% CI 32.9-39.3) in the Routine zone, 31.4% (95% CI 28.4-34.3) in the MDA+IRS zone, and 46.8% (95% CI 43.8-49.9) in the IRS zone (Table 1). Before adjustment for baseline prevalence, the OR comparing the MDA+IRS zone with the IRS zone was 0.43 (95% CI 0.33-0.55) (Additional file 1: S1 Text). The proportion of participants who were displaced was 18% in the Routine zone, 33% in the MDA+IRS zone, and 20% in the IRS zone (Additional file 1: S1 Table).

Malaria prevalence

Malaria prevalence in the Routine zone was higher at every later survey than at survey 1, whereas in the MDA+IRS zone it was below its survey-1 level at surveys 3 and 5 (Table 1; Figures 3 and 4). In the Routine zone, prevalence was 53.2% (95% CI 48.4-58.0) at survey 3 and 56.4% (95% CI 49.2-63.6) at survey 5, above Awasi's survey-1 level of 44.1% (Additional file 1: S2 Table, panel B). In the MDA+IRS zone, prevalence was 27.0% (95% CI 23.0-31.0) at survey 3 and 24.4% (95% CI 20.5-28.3) at survey 5.

At each of surveys 2 to 5, survey-weighted malaria prevalence was lower in every MDA+IRS health area than in every Routine health area (Additional file 1: S4 Figure ). At survey 1, the ranges overlapped: 20.4% to 42.0% in the MDA+IRS areas and 28.8% to 44.1% in the Routine areas (Additional file 1: S8 Table). In the area-level permutation test, the p-value for the MDA+IRS zone compared with the Routine zone was 0.008 at surveys 2 and 3 and 0.018 at survey 4 (Additional file 1: S2 Table, panel D). At survey 5, it was p=0.167, the smallest value attainable with six health areas.

The odds of malaria in the MDA+IRS zone were a quarter to a third of those in the Routine zone at each survey after the campaign began (OR 0.24 to 0.35; p<0.001 at each survey; Table 1), including 0.31 (95% CI 0.19-0.50) at survey 5, 14 months after the last MDA round. Model-standardised prevalence was 25.3 to 34.3 pp lower than in the Routine zone. The difference was larger than at survey 1, when the residual survey-1 OR was 0.87 (95% CI 0.67-1.11), and the DiD ratio of ORs was 0.27 to 0.41, with its 95% CI excluding 1 at every survey (Additional file 1: S2 Table, panel A).

In the IRS zone, the odds of malaria were about half those in the Routine zone at survey 2 (OR 0.50, 95% CI 0.32-0.80) and a third at survey 3 (OR 0.36, 95% CI 0.26-0.50), during the campaign, and the differences were not statistically significant after the campaign (OR 0.64, 95% CI 0.36-1.14, at survey 4; 0.66, 95% CI 0.41-1.04, at survey 5; Table 1). In the area-level permutation test, the difference was not statistically significant at any survey (Additional file 1: S2 Table, panel D). The residual survey-1 OR was 1.22 (95% CI 0.93-1.61), and the DiD ratio of ORs was 0.52 (95% CI 0.30-0.91) at survey 4 and 0.54 (95% CI 0.33-0.86) at survey 5, with its 95% CI excluding 1 at every survey (Additional file 1: S2 Table, panel A).

With Awasi as the fixed comparator or a health-area random intercept, the MDA+IRS ORs (0.20 to 0.35) were statistically significant at surveys 2 to 5, and the IRS ORs at surveys 2 and 3 only (Additional file 1: S2 Table, panels B and C). Adjustment for area-level or individual net use changed the MDA+IRS ORs by 0.02 or less and the IRS ORs by 0.10 or less, and did not change which contrasts were statistically significant (Additional file 1: S2 Table, panel G; S11 Table). With Gengere as a separate stratum, the MDA+IRS ORs at surveys 2 to 5 were 0.19 to 0.37 in the other four health areas and 0.31 to 0.49 in Gengere, and the difference between the two strata was not statistically significant (joint test p=0.057; Additional file 1: S2 Table, panel H).

After the campaign, the odds of malaria in the MDA+IRS zone were less than half those in the IRS zone (OR 0.37, 95% CI 0.23-0.60, at survey 4; 0.47, 95% CI 0.33-0.68, at survey 5; p<0.001 at each), compared with a residual survey-1 OR of 0.71 (95% CI 0.53-0.94). At surveys 2 and 3, during the campaign, the difference between the two zones was not statistically significant in the primary model (Additional file 1: S2 Table, panel E). In the arealevel permutation test, mean area prevalence in the MDA+IRS zone was 15.4 and 16.7 percentage points below that in the IRS zone at surveys 2 and 3 (p=0.024 at each), close to the 15.1-point difference at survey 1, and 28.3 and 22.7 points below at surveys 4 and 5 (p=0.016 and 0.024) (Additional file 1: S2 Table, panel D). The E-values for the MDA+IRS OR were 2.75 to 3.52 compared with the Routine zone at surveys 2 to 5 (2.05 to 2.68 for the 95% CI limits closest to 1) and 2.67 and 2.28 compared with the IRS zone at surveys 4 and 5 (1.91 and 1.73), against 1.36 and 1.66 for the residual survey-1 ORs (Additional file 1: S2 Table, panel F).

Mortality

At surveys 3 and 5, U5MR was 0.61 to 1.27 and CMR 0.26 to 0.61 deaths per 10,000 person-days across the three zones (Table 2; Additional file 1: S2 Figure ). U5MR in the MDA+IRS zone was 0.68 (95% CI 0.37-0.98) at survey 3 and 1.27 (95% CI 0.78-1.76) at survey 5, the highest of the three zones at that survey.

The differences in U5MR between each intervention zone and the Routine zone were not statistically significant at survey 3 or survey 5 (Table 2). At survey 3, the RR was 0.58 (95% CI 0.27-1.28) in the MDA+IRS zone and 0.81 (95% CI 0.39-1.68) in the IRS zone. Only four under-five deaths were recorded in the Routine zone at survey 5, when the RR was 2.40 (95% CI 0.66-8.78) in the MDA+IRS zone and 1.14 (95% CI 0.29-4.47) in the IRS zone. CMR in the MDA+IRS zone was approximately two-thirds of that in the Routine zone over the survey-3 recall period (RR 0.64, 95% CI 0.41-0.99; p=0.042; Table 2), and the difference was not statistically significant over the survey-5 recall period (RR 0.94, 95% CI 0.50-1.78). The DiD ratio of RRs was 0.46 (95% CI 0.29-0.73) at survey 3 and 0.68 (95% CI 0.36-1.27) at survey 5 (Additional file 1: S3 Table, panel A). In the IRS zone, the CMR differences from the Routine zone were not statistically significant at either survey. The differences in U5MR and CMR between the MDA+IRS and IRS zones were not statistically significant at survey 3 or survey 5 (Additional file 1: S3 Table, panel D). In the design-based analysis, every CMR contrast and every survey-3 U5MR contrast was on the same side of 1 as in the primary analysis (Additional file 1: S3 Table, panel E). No mortality contrast changed statistical significance. With Gengere as a separate stratum, the survey-3 CMR RR was 0.69 (95% CI 0.41-1.16) in the other four MDA+IRS health areas and 0.60 (95% CI 0.27-1.32) in Gengere, and the difference between the strata in the change from survey 1 was not statistically . significant over surveys 3 and 5 (joint test p=0.157; Additional file 1: S3 Table, panel F). U5MR in Gengere was based on 3 and 8 deaths at surveys 3 and 5.

Entomological indicators

In 2023, the sporozoite rate was 2.3% to 4.4% in the intervention zones, below the 10.8% (95% CI 8.6-13.3) and 12.0% (95% CI 9.1-15.4) in the Routine zone (Table 3; Figure 5C). From July to November 2024, it was lowest in the MDA+IRS zone, at 0.3% (95% CI 0.1-0.7; 7 of 2,062), based almost entirely on mosquitoes from Gengere (96% of those tested) (Additional file 2: S5 Table). In the IRS zone, the sporozoite rate rose in each half-year from January 2023.

Estimated HBR was lowest in the IRS zone in each of the five half-years from July 2022, and highest in the MDA+IRS zone in four of them (Table 3). In each half-year, 75% to 98% of the Anopheles caught in the MDA+IRS zone were caught in Gengere. HBR was lower from July to November 2024 than from July to December 2022 in 9 of the 10 health areas trapped in both periods, and unchanged in Udongo Abira (Figure 5B; Additional file 2: S5 Table). Awasi received neither IRS nor MDA, but received an MSF ITN distribution in August 2023 and the health-zone-wide distribution in December 2023. In Awasi, HBR from July to November 2024 was 0.43 times (95% CI 0.22-0.77) its level from July to December 2022.

Estimated EIR was lower in both intervention zones than in the Routine zone from January to June 2023 (Table 3). It was 0.51 (95% CI 0.31-0.78) infective bites per person per month in the Routine zone, 0.15 (95% CI 0.031-0.27) in the MDA+IRS zone, and 0.012 (95% CI 0-0.026) in the IRS zone. From July to December 2023, when the catch in Gengere was approximately five times that of the first half, EIR was 0.54 (95% CI 0.050-1.0) in the MDA+IRS zone and 0.63 (95% CI 0.35-1.5) in the Routine zone. EIR was lowest in the IRS zone in each half-year from January 2023 to June 2024, and lowest in the MDA+IRS zone from July to November 2024. In WHO cone bioassays with local Anopheles after IRS round 2, Abbott-corrected 72-hour mortality on plastic sheeting in Gengere remained at or above 80% up to 245 days after spraying, whereas on mud walls in Panyadong (IRS zone) it was below 80% in the valid tests from day 179, before survey 4 (Additional file 1: S12 Table).

Routine facility surveillance

Facility TPR and survey malaria prevalence were both lowest in the MDA+IRS zone at each of surveys 2 to 5 (Figure 6). The three zones ranked in the same order on both measures at surveys 3 and 5. At survey 1, TPR was lowest in the IRS zone, whereas survey malaria prevalence was lowest in the MDA+IRS zone. Before the campaign, from October 2021 to May 2022, monthly TPR in the MDA+IRS zone was 0.1 to 16.9 pp below that in the Routine zone, and it fell from 79.3% to 48.4% between June and August 2022, while TPR in the other two zones changed little (Additional file 1: S1 Figure, S10 Table). TPR in the MDA+IRS zone was above that in the Routine zone in four of the five months from July to November 2023 and below it in seven of the nine months from February to October 2024, when the Routine zone comprised Awasi alone.

Discussion

Principal findings

Malaria prevalence was lower in the MDA+IRS zone than in the Routine zone at every survey after baseline, a difference persisting to the last survey, 14 months after the last MDA round. IRS alone was associated with lower prevalence during the campaign in the primary model, but not when health areas were compared as units without adjustment for baseline prevalence, and the association weakened after spraying ended. Prevalence was lower in the MDA+IRS zone than in the IRS zone at surveys 4 and 5, the difference associated with adding MDA where IRS was also delivered. CMR was lower in the MDA+IRS zone than in the Routine zone at survey 3 only, and the differences in U5MR between zones were not statistically significant.

Persistence of lower prevalence with MDA+IRS

Lower malaria prevalence in the MDA+IRS zone lasted far longer than the protection that the drugs give. The effect of MDA wanes within one to three months [5], and DHA-piperaquine, used in MDA rounds 3 and 4, protects against reinfection for several weeks [17]. Survey 3, approximately two months after the last MDA round, may partly reflect that protection, but surveys 4 and 5 were beyond it. One explanation consistent with this pattern is that MDA cleared the parasite reservoir while IRS shortened mosquito survival, which reduced the number that survived until they became infectious. After spraying ended, the reservoir would then have refilled slowly, because fewer infected people remained to infect mosquitoes than in the zones without MDA. In a transmission model, co-timed IRS and MDA had approximately twice the impact of sequential delivery, because IRS protected the cleared reservoir from reinfection [8]. Consensus modelling in low-transmission settings predicted that the effect of MDA would be temporary unless vector control was scaled up [18]. However, prevalence in the MDA+IRS zone was still lower at survey 5, approximately 11 months after spraying ended.

Chance at the level of assignment is an unlikely explanation of the MDA+IRS contrast with the Routine zone at surveys 2 to 4, when prevalence was lower in every MDA+IRS health area than in every Routine health area (area-level permutation test). Part of the contrast from survey 3 onwards reflects a rise in Routine prevalence that the data do not explain, which occurred in all five Routine health areas (Additional file 1: S8 Table). Season does not explain the rise, because surveys 1 and 3 both followed the low-transmission season. If the rise was unrelated to the campaign, it made the MDA+IRS zone appear further below the Routine zone. With Awasi as a fixed comparator, the contrast was similar, which excludes the changing Routine composition but not the rise, which Awasi shared.

Regression to the mean is also unlikely, because the MDA+IRS zone started with lower prevalence than the Routine zone. Residual confounding cannot be excluded. Because the models were adjusted for baseline prevalence, such a confounder would have had to change prevalence differently in the two zones after baseline. Both the within-survey OR and the DiD ratio of ORs were below 1 at every survey after baseline, meaning that the contrast does not depend on how the baseline difference is handled.

IRS alone and adding MDA

The difference between the IRS and Routine zones was not statistically significant at any survey in the area-level permutation test, which does not adjust for the higher baseline prevalence of the IRS zone. After the campaign, the within-survey OR for the IRS zone was not statistically significant, whereas the DiD ratio of ORs was, because the IRS zone retained higher odds than the Routine zone at survey 1 after adjustment. If part of that difference was temporary, regression to the mean would lower the DiD ratio of ORs without any effect of IRS. We therefore give more weight to the within-survey OR. Season is an unlikely explanation of the weakening, because surveys 2 and 4 both followed the high-transmission season. The 2023 ITN distributions may have contributed at survey 5, although adjustment for individual net use changed the IRS ORs little. Chance cannot be excluded, because the IRS intervals at surveys 4 and 5 were wide. Survey 4 took place 4.5 to 8.5 months after IRS round 2, within or before the 6.5 to 11 months of clothianidin activity reported in bioassays with susceptible laboratory strains [14, 19, 20]. However, in Uganda, mud walls retained the least clothianidin [21]. Resurgence there after the switch to clothianidin also coincided with tolerance in Anopheles funestus [21]. In this study, residual activity on mud walls in the IRS zone was lost before survey 4, but not on plastic sheeting in Gengere. In Apac District, Uganda, facility test positivity rose back to pre-spraying levels over the 4 to 18 months after IRS stopped [22]. The IRS-zone sporozoite rate rose in each half-year from January 2023 (8 of 107 by July to November 2024), in the direction of the weakening. The weakening may therefore reflect faster loss of clothianidin activity on mud walls than in the published laboratory bioassays, or reduced susceptibility of the vectors in the IRS zone. With one test site per wall type, the two could not be separated.

In the primary model, which adjusts for baseline prevalence, the difference between the MDA+IRS and IRS zones appeared only after the campaign. In the area-level permutation test, which does not, the MDA+IRS zone was below the IRS zone at every survey, but during the campaign by approximately the same margin as at survey 1. MDA may have prolonged the lower prevalence observed with IRS during the campaign. However, the two zones already differed at survey 1 before adjustment for baseline prevalence. An unmeasured confounder as strong as that baseline difference could explain the whole survey-5 difference, and could make the survey-4 difference not statistically significant (Additional file 1: S2 Table, panel F).

Comparison with published evaluations

The lack of a statistically significant difference between the MDA+IRS and IRS zones during the campaign is consistent with two cluster-randomised trials in moderate-to-high transmission, in which MDA did not reduce prevalence within six months [23, 24]. In Zambia, where every arm received insecticide-treated nets and targeted IRS, as both zones did in the Angumu comparison of MDA+IRS with IRS, MDA lowered the odds of infection in children by 72% where baseline prevalence was below 10%, but not in the higher-transmission stratum that corresponds to Angumu [23]. In The Gambia, one round of MDA did not lower prevalence at four to six months [6, 24].

In a controlled pre-post evaluation in Katakwi District, a high-transmission area of Uganda, adding co-timed DHA-piperaquine MDA to four rounds of IRS was associated with lower odds of infection, with DiD ORs of 0.28 to 0.66 at surveys during and up to approximately six months after the rounds [25]. In Angumu, the equivalent DiD ratio of ORs was not statistically significant during the campaign and was 0.52 (95% CI 0.33-0.83) at survey 4 and 0.66 (0.46-0.96) at survey 5 (Additional file 1: S2 Table, panel E), within the Katakwi range. In other humanitarian emergency contexts, MDA delivered without IRS was followed within weeks by less self-reported fever in Monrovia, Liberia [26], fewer confirmed malaria cases in Sierra Leone [27], and mixed changes in malaria cases at health facilities in the Central African Republic [28]. In Sierra Leone, where transmission was intense, the fall in cases waned approximately four weeks after each round [27]. Prevalence was not measured more than six months after the last round in the trials [6], in Katakwi [25], or in these emergency evaluations, whereas in Angumu the lower prevalence in the MDA+IRS zone persisted to the last survey, 14 months after the last MDA round.

Mortality

At surveys 3 and 5, CMR and U5MR in every zone were below the January 2020 levels in Angumu [11], and U5MR was below the rate assumed in the sample size calculation, meaning that only large differences could be detected. In a verbal autopsy study of those deaths reported at survey 1, clinicians attributed 35.9% (95% CI 27.0-45.2; 41 of 115) of under-five deaths to malaria [29], which limits how far a campaign against malaria could lower U5MR. The proportion was similar across zones: 38.2% (13 of 34) in the Routine zone, 41.9% (18 of 43) in the MDA+IRS zone, and 37.5% (9 of 24) in the IRS zone. Mortality was therefore a less sensitive measure of the campaign than malaria prevalence.

The lower CMR in the MDA+IRS zone at survey 3 may partly reflect a difference that predated the campaign, because the survey-3 recall period began 10 weeks before IRS round 1. With person-time restricted from MDA round 1 onwards, to exclude the pre-MDA period, the RR moved towards 1 with a much wider interval (0.78, 0.19-3.17; Additional file 1: S3 Table, panel C). Regression to the mean cannot be excluded, because the MDA+IRS zone started with the highest CMR and had the largest fall. A cause mix favouring malaria does not explain that fall, because households attributed fewer survey-1 deaths to malaria and/or anaemia in the MDA+IRS zone (50.2%) than in the Routine (62.3%) and IRS (58.5%) zones (Additional file 1: S9 Table).

At survey 5, the U5MR point estimate in the MDA+IRS zone was higher than at survey 3, and the CMR and U5MR point estimates were higher than in the IRS zone. The CMR difference from the IRS zone was not statistically significant but close to the threshold (RR 1.69, 0.99-2.88; p=0.057; Additional file 1: S3 Table, panel D). The design-based estimate of this RR was further from the threshold (Additional file 1: S3 Table, panel E). The cause of this higher mortality is not known, but a rebound in malaria deaths is unlikely, because malaria prevalence at survey 5 was lower in the MDA+IRS zone than in the IRS zone.

Entomological and routine surveillance evidence

In 2023, the sporozoite rate was lower in both intervention zones than in the Routine zone, the direction of the prevalence difference, consistent with shorter mosquito survival after IRS. HBR and EIR did not consistently follow the prevalence ranking, and neither can serve as a zone comparator in this study. Zone HBR in the MDA+IRS zone largely reflects one health area, because 75% to 98% of the Anopheles caught there came from Gengere, and without a pre-intervention baseline, zone differences cannot be separated from pre-existing ones. EIR, the product of HBR and the sporozoite rate, inherits both problems. HBR also fell between 2022 and 2024 in 9 of the 10 health areas trapped in both periods, Awasi included, so the fall over time cannot be attributed to the campaign. The health-zone-wide ITN distribution of December 2023, and in Awasi the MSF distribution of August 2023, may have contributed to this fall.

The lower prevalence in the MDA+IRS zone was present both in Gengere and in the other four MDA+IRS health areas, with ORs closer to 1 in Gengere at surveys 3 to 5, but the difference between the two strata in the change from survey 1 was not statistically significant. Besides its swamps, Gengere differed from the other areas in population movement, as reported by programme staff. It has a lake port with boat traffic from Uganda and elsewhere in Ituri. It is also the entry point for displaced people arriving in Angumu and hosts a displacement camp. Gengere had also received IRS in 2021. Because Gengere is the only health area of this kind, its ecology cannot be separated from these differences. The MDA+IRS contrast was therefore not confined to Gengere, but the Gengere estimates cannot be attributed to any one of its features.

The MDA+IRS zone had both the lowest facility TPR and the lowest survey malaria prevalence at every survey after baseline. TPR in the MDA+IRS zone was already below that in the Routine zone before the campaign. It then fell between June and August 2022, during IRS round 1 and MDA rounds 1 and 2, while TPR changed little in the other two zones. From July to November 2023, however, TPR in the MDA+IRS zone was above that in the Routine zone in four of five months, whereas prevalence at survey 4, in October 2023, was approximately half that in the Routine zone. TPR measures positivity among attendees who were tested, so it changes with who attends and who is tested as well as with infection in the population. The agreement in zone ranking at the surveys and the timing of the 2022 fall support the survey finding, but monthly TPR is not a reliable measure of prevalence differences between zones.

Limitations

The main limitation is that zones were not randomly assigned. MDA+IRS was allocated to the health areas with the highest malaria incidence according to surveillance data, yet malaria prevalence at survey 1 was lowest in this zone. Facility incidence reflects care-seeking and reporting as well as infection, and the 2020 MDA in Lanyi and Ugudo-Zii and IRS in Gengere in 2021 may have lowered prevalence before survey 1. The zones also differed at baseline in mortality, in the proportion displaced, and in receipt of earlier interventions. The models were adjusted for the area-level baseline outcome and for individual displacement status, but the zone contrasts remain associations rather than estimates of the campaign's effect. E-values indicate that removing the MDA+IRS contrast with the Routine zone at surveys 2 to 5 would require confounding stronger than the baseline imbalance before or after adjustment (Additional file 1: S2 Table, panel F). For the contrast with the IRS zone at surveys 4 and 5, the E-values for the CI limits (1.91 and 1.73) exceeded that of the baseline imbalance after adjustment (1.66) but not before it (2.42), so confounding as strong as the unadjusted baseline difference between those zones could make that contrast no longer statistically significant. However, E-values are based on participant-level ORs and do not address the assignment of zones to 14 health areas. Four of the five Routine health areas later received IRS or MDA, which removed them from the comparison. Future evaluations could randomise the order in which comparison areas receive the intervention, in a stepped-wedge design.

Because follow-up ended at the last survey, 14 months after the last MDA round, the duration of the lower prevalence beyond that point is unknown. At survey 5, the Routine comparator was Awasi alone, the Routine health area with the highest baseline prevalence.

The models allowed for Awasi's higher starting level, but not for anything that changed malaria in Awasi alone after baseline, so the survey-5 contrasts could be overestimated or underestimated. Spill-over between neighbouring zones and the August 2023 ITN distribution in Awasi would bias the contrasts at surveys 4 and 5 towards the null, although adjustment for net use changed the ORs little.

Recall of deaths over more than a year may have omitted some deaths, with an effect of unknown direction. Overdispersion was not modelled in the mixed models, although designbased estimates, which do not assume Poisson variance, led to the same conclusions (Additional file 1: S3 Table, panel E). The small number of under-five deaths in the Routine zone at survey 5 makes the survey-5 U5MR contrasts imprecise.

No pre-intervention entomological baseline was available, because trapping began approximately four weeks before the first spray round (Additional file 2). Zone differences in the entomological indicators therefore cannot be separated from pre-existing differences. From January 2024, the entomological indicators for the Routine zone were based on Awasi alone, meaning that zone comparisons in 2024 depend on one health area. Mosquitoes were selected for sporozoite testing at random within each trap-week, but with unequal probabilities of selection between trap-weeks, meaning that the zone sporozoite rates may differ from those of the whole catch. Weighting each tested mosquito by the number caught divided by the number tested in its trap-week changed the zone rates by less than 0.4 pp in 11 of 12 zone-half-years. It lowered the MDA+IRS rate from July to December 2023 from 2.4% to approximately 1.3%, which places the MDA+IRS zone further below the Routine zone (Additional file 1: S1 Text).

Care-seeking may have differed between zones, because the IRS zone was allocated partly for its lack of MSF support for routine case management. Differences in RDT supply would change which attendees were tested. Attendance at facilities in another zone, including by displaced people, and HRP2 persisting after MDA in recently treated attendees [30, 31] would both make the zone series more similar, towards the null. Abia had no facility data, so the Routine series lacks one of its five health areas. TPR therefore supports the zone ranking from the surveys but does not measure an effect of the campaign.

Conclusions

In a conflict-affected, holoendemic health zone, co-timed MDA and IRS were associated with lower malaria prevalence to the last survey, 14 months after the last MDA round. This lower prevalence lasted far longer than the protection the drugs give, whereas the association with IRS alone weakened after spraying ended. Where high coverage of both interventions can be achieved, national malaria programmes and humanitarian organisations may consider cotimed MDA and IRS in similar settings, with malaria prevalence monitored in repeated surveys to decide when to repeat the campaign. Ethics approval and consent to participate

The MSF Ethics Review Board approved the study protocol (protocol 2216) on 22 March 2022 and its amendments on 26 August 2022, 5 October 2023, and 25 March 2024. The protocol was also approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the University of Kisangani, DRC (UNIKIS/CER/007/2022, March 2022; amendments CER/005/GEAK/2023 and CER/002/GEAK/2024). Household representatives gave verbal informed consent to the questionnaire. Each person tested with an RDT gave written consent, by signature or fingerprint, with an independent witness for illiterate participants. Parents or guardians consented for minors, and children over 10 years of age also gave assent (protocol v3.2, section 6.7.3). Households in the entomological surveillance gave verbal consent to trap placement. Interviewers recorded first names only, and individual information was linked only to a household number during analysis. Electronic databases were password protected and stored on MSF secure servers, with access limited to the study co-investigators and the medical coordinator (protocol v3.2, section 9.3). RDT-positive participants received artemether-lumefantrine free of charge under national protocols.
