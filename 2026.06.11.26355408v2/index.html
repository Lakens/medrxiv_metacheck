How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Abstract

Nontuberculous mycobacteria (NTM) are opportunistic pathogens of growing concern in drinking water, yet the phages and plasmids associated with them remain uncharacterized. We used genome-resolved quantitative metagenomics in three buildings on a chloraminated distribution system. Total bacterial metagenome-assembled genome (MAG) and viral population concentrations averaged 8.41 × 10 7 and 8.00 × 10 8 copies/L, respectively, while the total concentration of the seven NTM MAGs averaged 4.01 × 10 5 copies/L. Converging genomic evidence supported the identification of four viral MAGs (vMAGs) as putative mycobacteriophages. Three of these vMAGs encoded lysin B, a mycolylarabinogalactan esterase that cleaves the linkage between mycolic acids and arabinogalactan in the cell envelope, and three encoded a WhiB-family transcriptional regulator. NTM concentrations were highest at the site with the lowest bacterial and viral diversity. Combined four-vMAG concentrations were positively associated with total NTM concentrations within sites, although the association across all nine samples was not significant. NTM plasmids carried ESX secretion and metal efflux genes at higher proportions than NTM chromosomes. NTM carried more steroid degradation genes than co-occurring Actinomycetota. These findings reveal site-specific NTM-phage concentration relationships and identify phage-and plasmid-associated functions with potential implications for NTM-phage interactions and environmental adaptation.

Introduction

Drinking water can harbor opportunistic pathogens that pose risks to human health and contribute to substantial economic burdens. In the United States, drinking water exposures account for approximately 40% of hospitalizations and 50% of reported deaths associated with waterborne disease, with annual healthcare costs estimated at $1.39 billion 1 . Nontuberculous mycobacteria (NTM) alone account for approximately 46% of this total economic burden 1.

Specific NTM species, including Mycobacterium abscessus and Mycobacterium avium, can cause pulmonary and soft tissue infections 2 . These species were included in the U.S.

Environmental Protection Agency's Contaminant Candidate List 5 (CCL 5) 3 , which prioritizes unregulated drinking water contaminants for monitoring, research, and possible future regulation. Draft CCL 6 further added the "pathogenic waterborne mycobacteria group", including nine NTM species 4 . NTM persistence is supported by a robust, mycolic acid-rich cell envelope 5 , the capacity to form biofilms 2 , and associations with free-living amoebae that provide protection from environmental stress 6 . NTM are detected more frequently and at higher concentrations in chloraminated drinking water distribution systems than in those using free chlorine 2, 7 . This proliferation is further amplified within building plumbing, where decay of disinfectant residuals, high surface-to-volume ratios, prolonged stagnation, and higher water temperatures are conducive to microbial growth and biofilm formation. Because building plumbing represents the final critical point of human exposure, quantifying NTM and characterizing the genetic traits they carry at this point is essential for managing NTM in drinking water.

Beyond the intrinsic cellular traits that enable survival and growth in building plumbing, NTM persistence may also be influenced by phages and plasmids. Phage-host interactions range from lytic infection to lysogeny and can have divergent consequences for bacterial populations 8 . Lytic infection can reduce bacterial abundance through cell lysis, and some mycobacteriophages (viruses that infect Mycobacterium) encode lysis-associated enzymes, including LysB, which facilitates disruption of the mycobacterial cell envelope 9 . Phage infection can also induce metabolic reprogramming of infected cells (virocells), altering host nutrient acquisition, energy metabolism, and stress responses 10, 11 . In drinking water, phages have been reported to carry genes associated with oxidative and nitrogen stress responses 12 . Lysogeny may also support bacterial persistence through prophage-encoded accessory genes 13, 14 and protection against infection by related phages 15 . Plasmids represent another source of accessory genes, including those associated with antimicrobial resistance, virulence, and Type VII secretion (ESX) systems 16, 17 . Although putative mycobacteriophages have been detected in chlorinated drinking water 9 , research on these viruses has focused primarily on clinical diagnostics and therapy, including treatment of drug-resistant M. abscessus infections [18] [19] [20] [21] . However, the prevalence, genetic diversity, and potential ecological roles of NTM-associated phages and plasmids in drinking water systems remain poorly understood.

Existing culture-based methods, amplicon sequencing, and targeted quantitative PCR (qPCR) generally provide either taxon-specific measurements or community-level relative abundances, but rarely link genome-resolved identity to absolute concentration. Because relative abundances depend on community composition, they may not track the absolute concentration of a population, and two populations can appear to change together even when their absolute concentrations do not 22 . A previous study of a Mycobacterium-dominated, chlorinated drinking water distribution system characterized viral community composition based on relative abundance 9 . Recently developed qPCR assays targeting major capsid protein genes of selected Caudoviricetes phages infecting Mycobacterium spp. demonstrate increasing interest in their environmental quantification, but targeted assays cannot capture the broader diversity of uncharacterized phages or resolve their genomic associations with environmental hosts 23 . NTM, viral populations, and the broader bacterial community have not been quantified simultaneously in absolute terms in the same drinking water samples. Quantitative metagenomics 24 using spikedin standards can address this gap by enabling estimates of their absolute concentrations.

Here, we combined genome-resolved metagenomics with absolute quantification to investigate NTM, viral populations, and plasmids in chloraminated building plumbing water. First, we characterized viral populations in drinking water predicted to interact with NTM and tested our hypothesis that mycobacteriophage concentrations would be positively correlated with host concentrations, as greater host abundance may support increased phage replication 25 . Second, we examined how the functional gene content of NTM differs from that of other Actinomycetota in the same samples. Third, we investigated whether NTM-associated plasmids carry genes that expand the functional potential of NTM chromosomes. By integrating absolute concentration measurements, predicted phage-host associations, and functional gene annotations, this study characterizes NTM-associated viral and plasmid gene pools and identifies potential ecological interactions and functional potentials that warrant further investigation.

Methods

Source water and water treatment plant.

The Ann Arbor water treatment plant has been described previously by Dowdell et al. (2024) and Potgieter et al. (2025) 26, 27 . In summary, the plant supplies drinking water to approximately 125,000 residents and produces an average of 15 million gallons per day (5.7 × 10 4 m 3 /day). The source water consists of a seasonal blend of surface water (80-85%) and groundwater (15-20%).

The treatment process involves two-stage lime softening, followed by coagulation, flocculation, and sedimentation. Ozone is applied as the primary disinfectant (approximately 2 mg•min/L) prior to biologically active dual-media filtration using granular activated carbon and sand.

Monochloramine (about 3 mg/L as Cl 2 ) is applied as the secondary disinfectant before the treated water is distributed.

Sampling location and sample collection.

This study was conducted at three building locations in Ann Arbor, Michigan, U.S. (Sites A, B, and E). These sites are used by the City of Ann Arbor for water quality monitoring and have been included in previous studies 26, 27 . Site A was selected because it is located close to the water treatment plant. Sites B and E were selected as they were previously found to have relatively high NTM diversity and concentrations 26 . Site A is a municipal building, whereas Sites B and E are private buildings. Samples were collected from a janitorial closet tap at Site A, a bathroom sink at Site B, and a handwashing sink at Site E (Table S1).

Samples were collected on June 7 (Day 1), June 9 (Day 3), and June 13 (Day 7) in 2022 at approximately 9:00 AM on each sampling day, following the same site order (Site A, then Site B, then Site E). The first 2 L of tap water were collected in sterile polypropylene bottles to represent water from the fixture and the building plumbing (first-draw samples). First-draw sampling was selected to characterize water at the point of use before flushing and to obtain microbial biomass for genome-resolved quantitative metagenomics. All three buildings are nonresidential (Table S1) and generally unoccupied overnight and on weekends. Because June 13 was a Monday, the Day 7 samples likely followed weekend stagnation, whereas the Day 1 and Day 3 samples reflected overnight stagnation under routine building operation. However, the exact duration of stagnation and the preceding water-use history at each fixture were not recorded.

From each 2 L sample, 500 mL was concentrated using an automated Concentrating Pipette Select (InnovaPrep, Drexel, MO, USA) with 100 kDa ultrafilters, and the concentrate was eluted with 500 µL phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The recovery of these filters for bacteriophage T4 was 81 ± 9.7%, measured by droplet digital PCR (Bio-Rad) following a previously described approach 28 . After filtration and elution, the nine first-draw samples were stored at -80 °C. Physicochemical parameters were measured immediately after sampling, as described by Potgieter et al. (2025) 27 (Table S2). Temperature, pH, electroconductivity, ORP, and total dissolved solids were measured using a Hanna probe (HI98129 and HI98121; Hanna Instruments, Woonsocket, RI, USA). Total chlorine, monochloramine, free ammonia, total ammonia, orthophosphate, nitrite, and dissolved iron concentrations were quantified using the Hach parallel analyzer with corresponding Chemkeys®. Samples for total organic carbon (TOC) were collected in carbon-free glass vials, acidified with 85% phosphoric acid, and analyzed as non-purgeable organic carbon using a Shimadzu TOC analyzer (TOC-V, Japan).

DNA extraction.

DNA was extracted using a modified protocol of the DNeasy PowerWater Kit (QIAGEN). The modifications included enzymatic treatments with Lysozyme and Proteinase K, the use of chloroform:isoamyl alcohol (24:1), and altered bead-beating steps 29 (dx.doi.org/10.17504/protocols.io.66khhcw). The extracted DNA was quantified using the Qubit dsDNA High Sensitivity Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in conjunction with the Qubit 4

Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). DNA extracts (100 µL) were stored at -80 °C until sequencing (Table S1).

Short-read sequencing and preparation of metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs).

Short-read sequencing was performed at the University of Michigan Advanced Genomics Core.

Initial quality control was performed with Qubit to measure DNA concentration, NanoDrop for purity assessments, and Agilent Tapestation for fragment size estimation. Library prep was done using the xGen™ ssDNA & Low-Input DNA Library Prep Kit (Integrated DNA Technologies, Inc., Coralville, Iowa). One sample (SiteA_UF_fd_7) was sequenced on a NextSeq P2 600-cycle flow cell due to low biomass. The remaining samples were sequenced on a NovaSeq 6000 SP 500-cycle flow cell. Both resulted in 250 bp reads. Sequencing data were processed using the workflow established by Vosloo et al. (2021) 30 , including sequence processing, de novo coassembly, and MAG reconstruction, as detailed in Text S1.

Long-read sequencing and processing.

Long-read sequencing of the first-draw samples was unsuccessful because of insufficient DNA yield. Instead, three full-flush samples from Site A, collected on Days 1, 7, and 8, were used for long-read sequencing (Text S2). For first-draw samples, 500 mL of each of the 2 L water samples was processed for microbial concentration, yielding DNA concentrations of 0.21-2.06 ng/µL (Table S1). In contrast, the three full-flush samples, collected on 0.22 µm Sterivex filters, yielded DNA concentrations of 29.6, 25.9, and 32.7 ng/µL.

The long-read data were used to provide sequence-level support for regions of the short-read viral assemblies. Only long-read assemblies also detected in the short-read data were retained for downstream analysis. Long-read sequencing, sequence processing, and assembly are detailed in Text S3.

Quantitative metagenomic analysis.

Before library preparation, synthetic dsDNA and ssDNA standards were spiked into each sample at known abundances to relate observed relative abundances to absolute abundances 24, 31 . For all samples except SiteB_UF_fd_7, Sequins dsDNA metagenomics mix B 31 and a mix of five ssDNA standards 24 were added at 1.0% and 0.05% of total DNA mass, respectively (Table S3).

In SiteB_UF_fd_7, only 0.2% of Sequins dsDNA metagenomics mix B was spiked into the sample.

The absolute abundances of MAGs and vOTUs were determined using QuantMeta, with previously established thresholds for detection and read-depth variability 24 . Individual MAG absolute abundances were calculated as the mean absolute abundance of each contig normalized by length, with zeroes for contigs below detection. Values were then converted to copies per liter (copies/L) based on the DNA extraction elution volume (100 µL) and the filtered sample volume (Table S1). vOTUs are composed of individual contigs, so the concentrations directly calculated by QuantMeta were accepted as the vOTU concentrations (copies/L).

Viral sequence identification and host prediction.

Viral contigs were identified and clustered into species-level vOTUs, followed by taxonomic and lifestyle characterization. Virus-host associations were predicted using VHIP and iPHoP.

Detailed procedures are provided in Text S4.

Plasmid and prophage identification.

Plasmid and prophage sequences associated with NTM MAGs were identified and characterized using sequence-based approaches. Detailed procedures are provided in Text S5.

Metabolic potential analysis.

Functional potential of MAGs, associated plasmids and prophages, and vOTUs was evaluated using KEGG-based annotation and complementary analyses of virulence, antimicrobial resistance, and horizontal gene transfer. Gene and KO abundances were estimated using metagenomic read coverage and QuantMeta-derived MAG abundances. Detailed procedures are provided in Text S6.

Data analysis.

All statistical analyses and visualizations were conducted in RStudio (2023.09.1+494) with R (v4.4.0) unless otherwise noted. Figures were generated using ggplot2 (v3.5.2) unless otherwise noted. Alpha diversity was calculated using Shannon and Simpson indices, and community composition was analyzed by PERMANOVA, distance-based redundancy analysis (dbRDA), and variance partitioning, all using vegan (v2.6-10). Relationships between concentrations were evaluated using Pearson and Kendall rank correlations and, to account for the nested sampling design, linear mixed-effects models fitted with site as a random intercept. Differences among sites were tested using the Kruskal-Wallis test, and gene content was compared between groups of MAGs and between genomic compartments using Wilcoxon and Fisher's exact tests. All tests were two-sided, statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05, and Benjamini-Hochberg corrected values are reported as q. The variables entered into each test, the transformations applied, the software versions, and the families over which corrections were applied are detailed in Text S7.

Results and Discussion

Quantitative metagenomics enabled absolute quantification of bacterial and viral populations in drinking water

First-draw samples capture water that has stagnated in building plumbing and provide an exposure-relevant representation of microorganisms present at the point of use. We collected first-draw samples from three buildings 26 (Sites A, B, and E) within a chloraminated drinking water distribution system on Days 1, 3, and 7 (Table S1). Sampling within a single distribution system over one week enabled examination of short-term variation in microbial community composition and abundance while limiting differences associated with seasonal change. Shortread sequencing recovered 522 medium-quality bacterial MAGs 32 (completeness ≥50% and redundancy <10%), as well as 9,377 viral operational taxonomic units (vOTUs; >3 kb), representing metagenomic proxies for distinct viral populations clustered by 95% average nucleotide identity (ANI). Of these vOTUs, 414 with a completeness ≥75% were designated as vMAGs (Fig. S2).

We converted the relative abundances of bacterial MAGs and vOTUs to absolute concentrations (copies/L) using a quantitative metagenomics framework 24 with Sequins dsDNA standards added at 1.0% of library input DNA mass in eight samples and 0.2% in one sample (Table S3). Across the nine samples, absolute concentrations varied more widely than relative abundance (RPKM) for both bacterial MAGs (10.3-fold vs. 1.3-fold) and vOTUs (28.4-fold vs. 9.5-fold; Fig. S3), demonstrating the value of quantitative metagenomics in revealing variation in microbial concentrations that cannot be quantified from relative abundance data alone. The median cumulative concentration of individual viral populations across the nine samples was higher for vMAGs than for all vOTUs (3.54 × 10 5 vs. 1.10 × 10 5 copies/L; Fig. 1A), consistent with the greater assembly completeness generally achieved for more abundant viral populations. Total bacterial MAG concentrations averaged 8.41 × 10 7 ± 5.51 × 10 7 copies/L, whereas total vOTU concentrations averaged 8.00 × 10 8 ± 5.39 × 10 8 copies/L. Bacterial MAG concentrations were approximately 1.6 orders of magnitude higher than the previously reported total bacterial 16S rRNA gene copies measured by qPCR in the same system 33 (~2 × 10 6 copies/L). These differences reflect methodological variation, including differences in DNA extraction methods and metagenomics-based vs. qPCR-based quantification approaches, as well as differences in sampling time and location.

Bacterial MAG and vOTU concentrations were positively associated across the nine samples (Pearson r = 0.75, p = 0.021; Fig. 1B). Positive associations were also observed within each building, and the relationship remained significant when sites were included as a random effect (linear mixed-effects, p < 0.001). However, the small number of observations per site (n = 3) limits the interpretation of these relationships. The viral-to-bacterial ratio, calculated as the total vOTU concentration divided by the total bacterial MAG concentration, averaged 10.1 ± 5.9 (range: 2.4-17.5; Fig. 1C).

To our knowledge, this is one of the first metagenomics-based estimates of an absolute viral-to-bacterial ratio in drinking water. The observed ratios show convergence with the range reported for some marine environments based on particle-based viral-to-bacterial ratios 34 , although direct comparisons are constrained by differences in viral recovery and quantification approaches (particle-based vs. our genome equivalents). Previous drinking-water metagenomic work found that viral contigs represented 9% of contigs longer than 3 kb but did not quantify viral abundance relative to bacterial abundance 12.

We compared the short-read viral assemblies with long-read assemblies generated from fullflush samples from Site A. The most abundant short-read vMAG, vMAG_7795 (65.4 kb; Tables S4 and S5; 1.22 × 10 7 ± 8.31 × 10 6 copies/L), was also represented in the long-read dataset, with a 22.2 kb long-read contig mapped to vMAG_7795, supporting the continuity of the aligned region. Overall, approximately one-third of the short-read viral populations were detected in the long-read assemblies (Fig. S2). Long-read sequencing generally requires higher DNA quality 35 , whereas the extraction protocol used here was optimized to enhance lysis of hard-to-lyse cells in low-biomass drinking water samples, resulting in substantial DNA shearing and no long reads >25 kb. The limited overlap may reflect both DNA shearing and differences in viral community composition between first-draw (short-read) and full-flush (long-read) samples, although their relative contributions cannot be determined from these data. For this reason, we have focused only on long-read contigs found in the short-read data, as this provides independent support for the integrity of selected assembled regions.

Most vOTUs (98.6%) were assigned to the class Caudoviricetes, consistent with a recent drinking water virome study, in which 96% of vOTUs were assigned to the same class 9 . The second most abundant class was Megaviricetes (0.89%). Most vOTUs were unclassified at the family level (Fig. S4), consistent with studies of aquatic and drinking-water viromes 9, 12, [36] [37] [38] and indicating that drinking-water viral diversity remains poorly represented in current reference databases. Among classified vOTUs, Zobellviridae and Inoviridae were abundant (Fig. S4).

Detection of Inoviridae, a family of single-stranded DNA viruses, demonstrates that our library preparation approach recovered both ssDNA and dsDNA viruses: to our knowledge, the first report of ssDNA viruses in drinking water.

Among the 522 bacterial MAGs, seven were assigned to NTM by GTDB-Tk (Table S6). Of these, five were included in a maximum-likelihood phylogenetic tree constructed from singlecopy marker genes using GToTree (Fig. S5), as the remaining two lacked sufficient marker genes. We categorized these seven NTM MAGs according to clinical risk group definitions 39.

Three were categorized as Risk Group 2 (opportunistic pathogens capable of causing disease but mostly treatable): M. avium, Mycobacterium phocaicum, and Mycobacterium arupense. The remaining four, Mycobacterium gordonae, Mycobacterium paragordonae, Mycobacterium gadium, and Mycobacterium llatzerense, were classified as Risk Group 1 (species rarely associated with human or animal disease). Host genomic GC content contributes to broad VHIP-predicted NTM-phage associations

Virus-Host Interactions Predictor 40 (VHIP) was applied to predict virus-host interaction networks. VHIP is a machine-learning tool with a reported MAG-level accuracy of 87.8% for classifying virus-host pairs as infection or non-infection. It uses bacterial, archaeal, and viral genome sequences to compute coevolutionary and sequence-based features, including k-mer distances, GC content differences, and sequence homology. VHIP predicted extensive host associations across the microbial community. All 414 vMAGs were predicted to interact with at least one bacterial MAG in our dataset (Fig. S7); 92% of vMAGs were predicted to interact with at least one of the seven NTM MAGs, and 28% were predicted to interact with all seven (Fig. 2A). vMAGs predicted to interact with more NTM MAGs also tended to be linked to more bacterial hosts overall (Pearson, r = 0.54, p < 0.001; Fig. 2B). These patterns suggest that predicted NTM-associated vMAGs are not necessarily NTM-specific, as many were also linked to diverse non-NTM bacterial hosts. Cross-phylum infection has been experimentally demonstrated for several phages and has also been inferred using proximity-ligation approaches in environmental communities [41] [42] [43] [44] . As VHIP's predictions are based on genomic features that may persist longer than infectivity, our predictions may indicate that these vMAGs have evolved to rapidly change hosts rather than indicate current broad infectivity, an ability that could be advantageous in low-biomass, oligotrophic building-plumbing environments. However, experimental or proximity-based validation will be needed to establish cross-phylum infection.

Four vMAGs carry genomic evidence of NTM association

Given the extensive host sharing predicted by VHIP, we examined additional genomic evidence to identify vMAGs with stronger support for NTM association. We used an additional virus-host prediction tool, iPHoP, a machine-learning-based tool that integrates multiple prediction approaches and provides high-confidence genus-level host predictions from viral genome sequences, with an estimated false discovery rate below 10%. Using its default database (Text S4), iPHoP produced 137 genus-level predictions for 121 of the 414 vMAGs, including four assigned to Mycobacterium (confidence scores 91.4-99.4; Fig. S9). With the custom database, five high-confidence predictions (scores >90) linked vMAG_4615 to the M. arupense MAG and vMAG_8907 to four NTM MAGs in our study (Fig. S9). Fifteen vOTUs shared at least 1 kb of genomic sequence with known mycobacteriophages in the Actinobacteriophage database 46 (71-80% nucleotide identity; Table S7). Based on these results, we highlight four vMAGs (vMAG_1010, vMAG_4615, vMAG_8907, and vMAG_8917) as putative mycobacteriophages (Table S4). All four were detected in more than half of the samples (Table S5) and had cumulative concentrations exceeding 10 5 copies/L (Fig. 1A). In addition, all four retained their predicted NTM host interactions after excluding VHIP-predicted virus-host pairs with GC differences below -20%, as their differences from the seven NTM MAGs ranged from -7.1% to +2.9%. Among the four candidates, vMAG_1010 and vMAG_4615 had the strongest genomic evidence of NTM association. vMAG_1010 shared sequence similarity with the mycobacteriophages JustASigh and Weirdo19, encoded an Arg tRNA identical to that of the M. llatzerense MAG, and was assigned to Mycobacterium by iPHoP using the default database (Table S4). vMAG_4615 corresponded to a 54-kb prophage region within the M. arupense MAG and shared 99.8% nucleotide identity across the aligned region, and iPHoP predicted its host to be M. arupense using both databases (Table S4). vMAG_4615 was the only nearly complete prophage found in an NTM (54.2 kb, 76 genes, seven viral hallmark genes, 97% completeness); four other prophage regions were detected, but their sizes were small, consistent with partial or decaying prophages (Fig. S10). vMAG_8907 and vMAG_8917 also had NTM hosts predicted by iPHoP (vMAG_8907: M. phocaicum, M. arupense, M. llatzerense, and M. gadium MAGs, and vMAG_8917: M. paragordonae MAG). They also had sequence similarity to known mycobacteriophages (vMAG_8917 to mycobacteriophage Soul22 and vMAG_8907 to mycobacteriophage Ramsey) (Table S4). Notably, vMAG_8907 encoded a tRNA matching the non-NTM MAG_218 (Sphingopyxis sp030145715), which VHIP also predicted as a host, further suggesting the potential broad host range of putative mycobacteriophages. These four vMAGs carried 77 ± 10 predicted genes each, of which 39 ± 5% received functional annotations (Table S8). All exhibited a canonical siphovirus-like genome organization 47 (Fig. 3).

In addition, 17 ± 5% of their VOG-annotated genes had best hits from mycobacteriophage L5 or D29 (Table S8), compared with 4 ± 6% among the other vMAGs (Wilcoxon rank-sum test, p = 0.001).

Two genes identified in these four vMAGs, but not in the other vMAGs, were lysin B and a WhiB-family transcriptional regulator (Fig. 3). Three vMAGs encoded lysin B (vMAG_4615, vMAG_8907, and vMAG_8917), a mycolylarabinogalactan esterase that cleaves the linkage between mycolic acids and arabinogalactan in the mycobacterial cell envelope 48 . Its presence is consistent with the potential to infect mycolic acid-containing bacteria. vMAG_4615 encoded adjacent lysin A and lysin B genes, whereas vMAG_8907 and vMAG_8917 also carried peptidoglycan-hydrolyzing enzymes or lysin A near lysin B (Fig. 3). LysB was also recently reported in three viral populations from a chlorinated drinking water distribution system, including two classified as mycobacteriophages 9 . Furthermore, LysB from mycobacteriophage D29 has demonstrated activity against drug-resistant Mycobacterium tuberculosis 49 . Three vMAGs (vMAG_1010, vMAG_8907, and vMAG_8917) also encoded WhiB-family regulators implicated in host cell division and superinfection exclusion in mycobacteriophages 50 (Fig. 3). A WhiB homolog encoded by mycobacteriophage TM4 has been shown to interfere with host cell division and promote superinfection exclusion 50 . The presence of lysin B and WhiB-family regulators suggests potential applications in phage-based biocontrol of NTM through cell envelope degradation and interference with host cell division, although their specific functions and efficacy remain to be experimentally validated.

Three vMAGs (vMAG_4615, vMAG_8907, and vMAG_8917) encoded an integrase together with a putative repressor or antirepressor (Table S8), consistent with the potential for a temperate lifestyle. In particular, vMAG_4615 encoded a CI-like repressor 51 , which may contribute to the maintenance of lysogeny and immunity against infection by related phages 15 (Fig. 3). DNA methyltransferases were also identified in vMAG_1010, vMAG_4615, and vMAG_8907 (Table S8), potentially contributing to protection against host restriction systems 52 . No antibiotic resistance or virulence genes were identified in the four vMAGs.

These results provide complementary genomic evidence supporting the interaction of four candidate mycobacteriophages with NTM, with the strongest evidence for vMAG_4615 through its integration into the M. arupense genome. However, their actual host ranges, infectivity, and potential contributions to NTM survival or control remain to be experimentally validated. NTM MAGs encode a steroid degradation pathway largely absent from co-occurring Actinomycetota

Functional potential of the Actinomycetota MAGs in this system was compared to assess how NTM gene content differed from that of other species within the same phylum. The seven NTM MAGs and the eight other Actinomycetota MAGs recovered in this study (Table S6) yielded 2,216 and 2,333 distinct KEGG Orthology IDs (KOs), of which 1,558 were shared. The NTM MAGs had larger genomes than the other Actinomycetota (5.8 ± 1.4 vs. 3.6 ± 1.5 Mb; Wilcoxon rank-sum, p = 0.01) despite comparable genome completeness (86.0 ± 18.2 vs. 86.4 ± 9.4%; Wilcoxon rank-sum, p = 0.46; Table S6). However, average coding density was comparable between NTM and other Actinomycetota (1,037 ± 122 vs. 1,039 ± 52 predicted protein-coding genes per Mb; Wilcoxon rank-sum, p = 0.34).

The 20 KOs with the highest overall mean concentrations in NTM MAGs were primarily associated with transport and secretion, lipid metabolism, central metabolism, and stress response and regulation (Fig. S18; Table S12). Seventy-seven KOs were present in all seven NTM MAGs and absent from all eight Actinomycetota MAGs (Table S13): 14 in steroid degradation (discussed below), 13 in envelope glycolipid and mycolic acid synthesis, and 5 in the ESX (type VII secretion) system. These lipids contribute to envelope stability and reduced permeability and have been associated with antimicrobial resistance and pathogenicity [56] [57] [58] [59] . ESX systems have been implicated in mycobacterial intracellular survival and pathogenicity [60] [61] [62].

Depending on the ESX subtype, they can contribute to phagosomal membrane disruption and intracellular survival in both amoebae and macrophages 62, 63 , modulate host immune responses through PE/PPE protein secretion 63 , and mediate metal acquisition and homeostasis 17 . Notably, the enrichment of ESX-related genes in NTM MAGs may reflect their association with plasmids rather than their chromosomal gene content, as discussed in the following section.

A screen of chlorine-and oxidative-stress-related functions detected 39 distinct KOs across the seven NTM MAGs (Table S14), including the thioredoxin and peroxiredoxin system, glutaredoxins, mycothiol synthase, peptide-methionine (R)-S-oxide reductase (msrB), and the OxyR and SoxR regulators. While NTM had more distinct KOs (31 ± 4 vs. 24 ± 4; Wilcoxon rank-sum, p = 0.009) and more genes (54 ± 10 vs. 32 ± 6; Wilcoxon rank-sum, p = 0.002), the other Actinomycetota had a similar number of distinct KOs per Mb (5.6 ± 0.9 vs. 7.2 ± 2.1 per Mb; Wilcoxon rank-sum, p = 0.12) and the two groups carried the same number of genes per Mb (9.5 ± 1.7 vs. 9.5 ± 2.5 per Mb; Wilcoxon rank-sum, p = 0.87). In addition, the NTM MAGs carried an average of 2.9 × 10 6 ± 2.5 × 10 6 stress gene copies/L against 3.1 × 10 6 ± 2.3 × 10 6 in the eight other Actinomycetota MAGs (paired Wilcoxon signed-rank, p = 0.65), showing that NTM encoded no greater stress mitigation-related gene concentrations than other Actinomycetota in the same chloraminated water. No antimicrobial resistance genes were identified using AMRFinderPlus.

Among the 45 KEGG level-B categories, KOs related to xenobiotic biodegradation and metabolism showed the clearest difference: an average of 8.2 ± 1.8 KOs per Mb in NTM, compared with 2.6 ± 1.8 KOs per Mb in the other Actinomycetota (Wilcoxon rank-sum, Benjamini-Hochberg q = 0.002). The steroid degradation pathway explained most of this difference (6.0 ± 1.4 KOs per Mb in NTM vs. 0.8 ± 0.5 in the other Actinomycetota; Fig. S19; Wilcoxon rank-sum; Benjamini-Hochberg q = 0.003; Fig. 5). Additionally, the NTM MAGs carried substantially more steroid degradation gene copies (3.5 × 10 6 ± 3.0 × 10 6 copies/L vs. 2.1 × 10 5 ± 1.5 × 10 5 in the eight other Actinomycetota MAGs; Wilcoxon signed-rank, q = 0.026; Fig. S20). Each NTM MAG carried 31 to 36 of the 37 detected steroid degradation KOs, compared with 1 to 11 in the other Actinomycetota, and 14 KOs covering all four steps of the pathway were found in all seven NTM MAGs but none of the eight Actinomycetota MAGs (Fig. 5; Table S13). These results indicate that NTM disproportionately contained steroid degradation genes among Actinomycetota in this system.

Steroid catabolism in mycobacteria is associated with cell envelope maintenance 64 , redox homeostasis 65 , and metabolic flexibility 66 , suggesting potential roles in adaptation to environmental stress. In M. tuberculosis, cholesterol utilization is required for persistence during chronic infection and growth within activated macrophages 67 . Moreover, steroid metabolism provides NTM with a competitive advantage: not only can NTM grow on cholesterol as a sole carbon source, but cholesterol amendment of sewage influent enriched mycobacteria 4.7-fold within seven days relative to the background bacterial community 68 . Although sterols are generally present at low concentrations in drinking water, steroid degradation may contribute to NTM survival within environmental hosts. Free-living amoebae have sterol-containing membranes and could potentially provide a source of sterols for intracellular NTM 69 . However, whether NTM utilize these sterols or whether steroid degradation contributes to their survival in drinking water remains unknown. Additionally, there is no evidence that these genes are being transferred horizontally. While 30 putative horizontal gene transfer events were identified between NTM and co-occurring bacterial taxa (Table S15), none of these were related to steroid metabolism.
