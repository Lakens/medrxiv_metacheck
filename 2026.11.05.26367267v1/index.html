Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Abstract

Communication failures are repeatedly implicated in harmful experiences and outcomes in pregnancy care, especially in contexts of loss, yet little is known about how communication itself is represented in the institutional texts that shape expectations of care. This study examines the extent to which communication is discussed, and how patient-healthcare professional interaction is framed, in publicly accessible UK discourse about pregnancy loss.

Two corpora comprising texts from seven medical organisations (120,383 words) and eight charities (224,481 words) were divided into healthcare professional-facing and patient-facing sub-corpora. Frequency, semantic keyness, keyword and concordance analyses were conducted using Wmatrix7.

In medical discourse, language and communicative interaction were underrepresented, while speech acts associated with unidirectional professional activities such as advising, recommending and referring were overrepresented. Compared with charity texts, medical outputs foregrounded clinical evidence, policy, risk and professional knowledge. Characteristic forms including "confirmed", "advised", "recommended" and "offered"

1. Introduction

Recent decades have seen increasing acknowledgment of the impact of language and communication on patient experience across healthcare contexts (Arora, 2003; Slade et al 2008; Street et al., 2009). In pregnancy care, and especially contexts involving the loss of a baby during pregnancy, efficient and sensitive communication has been repeatedly highlighted as contributing to the experiences of individuals and families, as well as their longer-term recovery and mental health (Lacci-Reilly et al., 2023). Despite recommendations for communication in this context (e.g. Johnson et al., 2020; RCOG, 2022), detrimental impacts of poor communication around pregnancy loss continue to be highlighted internationally, both by clinicians (e.g. Kraus, 2022; Vimalesvaren et al., 2021) and members of the public (e.g. Peanut, 2023). In a recent empirical study, qualitative data from people with lived experience of pregnancy loss showed that poor communication contributes to a lack of perceived clarity, compassion, and transparency in the care received, and can exacerbate psychological trauma (Malory, 2025; Malory & Parr, 2025).

That communication is a significant factor in determining patient experience in this context has been recognised by the outputs of the UK's National Maternity and Neonatal Services Investigation (MNSI), led by Baroness Valerie Amos, which concluded in 2026.

One way to begin to answer this question is by looking at how communication between patients and professionals is currently represented in contemporary texts about pregnancy loss produced by organisations such as the NHS, and other influential public health institutions in the UK. Employing purpose-built datasets, or corpora, comprising large numbers of publicly accessible texts on this subject, we use computer software to identify recurring, or statistically salient, language choices -an approach known as corpus linguistics (McEnery and Hardie, 2012). We combine this with qualitative analysis drawing on concepts from critical discourse analysis (CDA) and communication studies, such as framing (Entman, 1993), in order to identify the understandings and perceptions that these linguistic patterns could reflect and reinforce in this context. This combination of corpus linguistic and CDA approaches has previously been shown to offer a 'useful methodological synergy' through which to explore specific discourses, including in health communication contexts (Baker et al. 2008; see also Baker et al. 2020; Partington et al. 2013).

This article therefore adopts a corpus-informed, critical linguistic approach to investigating how communication between healthcare professionals and patients is framed in institutional discourses relating to pregnancy loss in the UK. Two corpora were built which aimed to be representative of the publicly accessible institutional language of a) UK medical organisations; and b) UK-based charities, specific to the topic of loss during pregnancy. These corpora were then analysed in terms of the following research questions: 1) To what extent is health communication (including language) discussed within these institutional discourses?

2) How is communication between patients and healthcare professionals framed?

2. Background

This research is situated in the context of the growing body of work on health communication in applied linguistics and related fields. As defined by Harvey and Adolphs (2011: 470), health communication covers 'a huge and diverse range of communicative activities touching on health and healthcare, ranging from personal accounts of health and illness and encounters with medical professionals through to health policy documentation and side effects information presented on drug packaging'. This article examines written language used by prominent UK medical institutions and charities on their websites as examples of health discourse. We examine these discourses from a critical standpoint, drawing on the CDA understanding of 'discourse' as a form of social practice which not only reflects underlying attitudes, or ideologies, but also serves to construct and reinforce them (Brookes, 2026: 323-4; Fairclough, 2003; Jones, 2013). In this view, discourse 'constitutes a central activity to the practices of institutions related to health' (Brookes and Hunt 2021: 4).

As will be described in more detail in the next section, the discourses considered for the purpose of the present research are made up of written texts produced by UK organisations which a) share a common focus on pregnancy loss, and which b) are easily accessible to the public on their websites. They can thus be compared with other forms of public health communication such as care guidelines (Pattison, 2006), charity reports (Abnett et al., 2025) and public health campaigns (Brookes et al., 2016). Such texts are predicted to have implications for their target audiences in terms of the perceptions they construct or reinforce. Public-facing health information texts may influence patients' expectations of, and engagement with, healthcare through their representation of health conditions and patients themselves (Brookes et al., 2016; Stanley et al., 2025). At the same time, public health communication texts also represent healthcare practitioners and the institutions in which they work. This representation can influence professional identities and self-concepts of healthcare practitioners, including understandings of how they should communicate with each other and with patients (Pattison, 2006; Sun and Chalupnik, 2022). This article explores how both patients and practitioners are represented in the institutional discourses in question, specifically in terms of the roles they take in communication, and considers the implications of these representations for different target audiences.

Before outlining the data in the following section, we first introduce a useful theoretical concept underpinning this research. The notion of 'framing' has been used differently in various fields including sociology, semantics, artificial intelligence and cognitive linguistics, but might broadly be defined as an effect or function of 'particular lexical and grammatical choices' which 'generates expectations and inferences in communication and action' (Semino et al. 2016: 3). In their study of representations of cancer on social media, Semino et al. (2016) build on the account of framing given in Conceptual Metaphor Theory (Lakoff and Johnson, 1980) as 'a bias of conceptualisation' which has the power to influence how we think about and act on a situation. While Semino et al. (2016) focus on how framing effects are achieved through metaphorical expressions, the definition they cite from Entman (1993) presents a broader perspective on this linguistic effect: Framing essentially involves selection and salience. To frame is to select some aspects of a perceived reality and make them more salient in a communicating text, in such a way as to promote a particular problem definition, causal interpretation, moral evaluation, and/or treatment recommendation for the item described. (Entman 1993: 52; italics in original) In this article, we extend this notion of framing beyond metaphor to include nonmetaphorical linguistic choices which contribute to how communication is construed more broadly in the health discourses we examine.

According to Semino et al. (2016: 11), analysis of framing should pay attention to not only entities, roles and relations being represented, but also 'aspects such as agency, (dis)empowerment, evaluations, and emotional associations'. These framing implications are particularly significant in healthcare contexts (see also Baker et al. 2020; Demmen et al, 2015), where the choice of different descriptions of illness can have both positive and negative implications for the general well-being of people already in a vulnerable position.

Here the emphasis is on the potential consequences for individuals of different (assumed) framings, and the goal is to develop guidelines or recommendations on what kinds of language should or should not be employed (Semino et al., 2016: 7) This vulnerability is certainly true of those experiencing, who have previously experienced, or who fear pregnancy loss. The psychological and behavioural impacts of different ways of describing pregnancy loss using diagnostic terminology can be understood theoretically as framing effects (Malory 2025; Malory & Parr, 2025). In this article, rather than focusing on how the experience of pregnancy loss is framed, we focus on the framing of communicative interactions between patients and healthcare professionals about pregnancy loss. Our goal is to consider the potential ramifications of the framings under examination, for how practitioners and patients approach these communicative situations.

3. Data and methods

In order to investigate institutional discourse around pregnancy loss in the UK, two corpora were constructed. The first of these -the 'Medical corpus' -consisted of web content and online documents about pregnancy loss from 7 prominent UK medical organisations (see Appendix 1; Table 1). Since the research aims to identify possible implications of the language for public and practitioner conceptions of pregnancy loss care, texts were sampled on the basis of ease of accessibility and hence potential reach and impact.

Texts were sourced by searching for key terms in the websites of medical organisations such as the National Health Service (NHS), the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) and the Royal Colleges of healthcare professions likely to be involved in pregnancy loss care. Key terms (miscarriage, ectopic, stillbirth, pregnancy loss, baby loss, fetal loss, fetal death, termination) were chosen on the basis of previous research into common terminology surrounding pregnancy loss in the UK (Malory, Nuttall and Heazell, 2025). Only publicly accessible texts were collected, meaning that any content requiring professional membership and/or password access were not included in the corpus.

Where a webpage or document covered broader topics such as maternity care or reproductive medicine, only sections relevant to the focus of the analysis were collected. This relevance was determined by the title and/or inclusion of two or more instances of at least one key search term in the body of the text. The same sampling criteria were applied to texts covering related topics, such as the abortion of unwanted pregnancies and neonatal death (the loss of a baby in the first 28 days after birth). The collected texts include patient information pages/leaflets, clinical guidelines, training materials/curricula for healthcare professionals, best practice papers, and reports from audits of clinical services. This range is representative of this discourse-type and the multiple registers typically contained with it, including research findings, (fictional) case studies, FAQs, resource lists and contact details. However, since our focus is on written text data, any videos or podcasts were not included.

In order to provide a point of comparison, a second corpus -the 'Charity corpus'was built using similar methods. This corpus consisted of web content from 8 prominent UK charities on the subject of pregnancy loss (See Appendix 1; Table 2). The search terms listed above were used to collect publicly accessible written content on this topic, with the resulting texts including both patient information guides or booklets, and guidelines for healthcare professionals, and again featuring case studies and contact lists along with detailed information and advice. While this corpus does not contain the same range of text-types as the Medical corpus, this is again representative of this discourse, which is primarily focused on information and guidance for patients and their families.

As Appendix Tables 1 and 2 show, our corpora do not contain equal amounts of text from each organisation sampled. The number of texts from each organisation varies, as does the length of these texts, reflective of the range of eligible content that was relevant to our topic from each source, as defined by our sampling criteria. The analysis does not make claims about specific organisations, but rather the dataset as a whole, and findings must be interpreted in relation to the make-up of each corpus. Finally, these samples are not exhaustive of the institutional discourse on pregnancy loss published online. However, since our aim was to collect texts most likely to reach the widest audience, sampling prioritised accurate representation of the most prominent, or influential organisations.

The two resultant corpora contain 120,383 words (Medical corpus) and 224,481 words (Charity corpus), respectively. While relatively small, these datasets are intended to be representative of these two types of health discourse for the purposes of corpus analysis.

However, the different audiences targeted within each corpus (see Appendix 1; Tables 1 and 2) poses challenges for comparison. The role of third sector organisations means that the Charity corpus is dominated by texts aimed primarily at patients, families and the general public, with only a smaller number targeting healthcare professionals specifically. By contrast, the Medical corpus is dominated by texts aimed at healthcare professionals, trainees, or commissioners, with a smaller number targeting patients and the general public. To address this challenge, both corpora were divided into two sub-corpora, with texts categorised based on their function of information sharing: 'healthcare professional-facing' or 'patient-facing'. Phase 1 focused on the representation of communication in the Medical corpus. After an initial examination of word frequencies for specific terms, the Medical corpus was tagged using Wmatrix7's USAS Semantic Tagger, which automatically assigns the semantic domains expressed by words based on a pre-existing lexicon (Archer et al., 2002). This was then compared to the semantic domains tagged in a reference corpus, 'BE21': a sample of 1 million words of general written British English published in 2021 (Baker, 2023). Results were sorted by effect size (LogRatio) and the top 150 results in terms of effect size sampled.

In Phases 3 and 4, keyword analyses were again conducted, this time comparing the pairs of sub-corpora matched in terms of target audience (Table 1). Owing to the small size of the Charity-HP and Medical-P sub-corpora (see Table 1), these were each selected as the target corpus in their respective comparisons. Both positive and negative keywords (those that are significantly more and less frequent in the target corpus, respectively) were identified relative to the larger audience-matched reference corpus. Keywords were collected, sorted and sampled using the same procedures as Phase 2foot_1 . For these comparisons, the small size of the sub-corpora means that caution must be exercised in the interpretation of statistical significance. Here, effect size (based on relative frequency of use) offers a useful measure of linguistic difference.

Finally, concordance lines for each (sub)corpus were manually examined for those positive and negative keywords relating to communication, with a focus on the specific ways in which interactions between patients and healthcare professionals are framed in context.

4. Analysis

These corpus linguistic methods allowed us to assess the extent to which communication is a topic within the institutional health discourses, and the way in which it is framed when it does appear. Results from each phase of analysis are outlined in the following four sections.

4.1 Communication without dialogue?

In order to assess the extent to which the topics of language and communication are discussed in the Medical corpus (RQ1), a basic word frequency analysis was conducted using Wmatrix7. The lemmas communic* and lang* are seen relatively infrequently in the 120,383word Medical corpus, with words such as communicate, communicating, communication appearing 32 times and mentions of language(s) appearing just 17 times. This may reflect the under-privileging of communication as a factor in perinatal healthcare (see Malory, 2025).

However, references to these topics can come in many other forms and raw frequencies do not tell us whether this usage is high or low relative to general British English.

Framing of interaction therefore seems to exhibit key differences between the two patient-facing sub-corpora. Firstly, patient-facing texts produced by charities more frequently frame the patient as an agentive communicator who shares, questions or describes their experiences; often through the same communicative processes attributed to the healthcare professional (e.g. talk, tell, say). Patient-facing texts produced by medical organisations, on the other hand, typically attribute agency or power to the professional, except for in specific scenarios such as the reporting of risk. This provides evidence that charities' discourses are more empowering in their more balanced framing of communication as reciprocal and bidirectional.

Secondly, medical organisations' outputs focus primarily on conveying a diagnosis, treatment or advice, with the professional often backgrounded, or implicit, as the communicator in a transfer of information to the patient (e.g. 'the results confirm'). By contrast, the charities' outputs regularly foreground individual healthcare professionals (e.g. sonographers, Section 4.2) as active communicators in specific interactions with patients (e.g. 'a clinician will talk you through'). In this way, patient-facing texts produced by charities can be seen as more transparent and accessible in their representations of typical interactions and what can be expected from professionals involved in care.

Comparing these two patient-facing sub-corpora has therefore revealed differences in both the types of communication being represented and the extent to which participants, as active communicators and respondents, are foregrounded through the language used. This could have ramifications for patients and other members of the public who access these texts to better understand the experience of loss.

This study examined the extent to which communication is discussed in UK institutional discourses around pregnancy loss (RQ1), and how communication between patients and healthcare professionals is framed within these discourses (RQ2). The findings reveal a consistent contrast between texts produced by medical organisations and those produced by charities. In the Medical corpus, semantic domains relating to spoken communication and language were significantly under-represented relative to general written British English.

Across healthcare professional-facing texts, those produced by charities again featured a greater emphasis on forms of written and spoken communication as part of clinical practice, including discussion of wording or delivery, and the needs and preferences of parents. Charity outputs represented both patients and professionals as communicatively active and more frequently identified (likely) individual participants in particular interactions. Patient agency was most clearly foregrounded in relation to reporting symptoms or concerns. Taken together, these patterns frame medical communication as a largely unidirectional, and often impersonal, transfer of information, while charity discourse more often frames it as interpersonal, reciprocal, and emotionally consequential. Implications for healthcare professionals and professional bodiesInstitutional discourse contributes to defining what healthcare professionals are expected to know and do. The Medical corpus constructs the professional's communicative role primarily as the transmission of clinical knowledge: someone who diagnoses, advises, recommends and offers treatment. Far less attention is given to the professional as a relational communicator who listens, responds to emotion, explains terminology sensitively, elicits preferences, and checks understanding. Communication is consequently positioned as peripheral to clinical expertise rather than as one of the means through which safe and compassionate care is delivered.

healthcare professionals themselves communicate in these ways, but it means that the institutional texts intended to guide them provide limited discursive support for recognising communication as a core component of their professional role. Medical organisations should therefore integrate specific communication guidance throughout clinical guidelines, curricula, and patient pathways; rather than treating it as a separate or optional "soft skill". Guidance should identify who is responsible for communicating information, specify the importance of listening and responding as well as explaining, address the emotional and interpersonal effects of wording; and require professionals to elicit language preferences, invite questions, and check understanding. The greater attention paid to these practices in charity-produced professional guidance demonstrates awareness within that sector of the need for this guidance. Charities should not, however, be left to compensate for omissions in authoritative medical discourse: responsibility for establishing communication as a clinical standard rests with the institutions that define and regulate professional practice.

framing identified in patient-facing medical discourses also has implications for what patients understand themselves to be entitled and expected to do. When communication is represented principally through being advised, offered or told, patients are positioned primarily as recipients of professional knowledge. Their communicative agency is most visible when they are instructed to report symptoms or risk indicators. This creates a restricted model of participation in which the patient's role is to supply clinically relevant information and receive professional decisions, rather than to question, interpret, express preferences, or contribute experiential knowledge. The prominence of risk and lifestyle discourse may further individualise responsibility for preventing adverse outcomes, while the backgrounding of professional communicators makes institutional responsibility less visible. By contrast, charity discourses provide patients with a more explicit account of what interactions may involve, who will communicate with them and what they may wish to ask, say, share, or know. Patient-facing information from medical organisations should therefore represent communication as an active, two-way process. It should name the professionals involved, explain what patients can expect from them and legitimise questions and preferences.
