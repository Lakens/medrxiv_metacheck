Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Abstract

Background: Models based on administrative population health records can help improve understanding of disease and hospitalisation risk. This understanding can support evidence-based population health policy and processes. Influenza and other acute respiratory diseases place a large, inequitable burden on the New Zealand population and health care resources. We have developed and evaluated such models using retrospective New Zealand hospitalisation records linked to other administrative data to estimate individual-level risk of hospitalisation due to acute respiratory infection.

We analysed linked annual records for approximately 4•85-5•36 million people in 2017-2024. Six machine learning algorithms and eight variable sets were assessed using five-fold cross-validation grouped by demographic profile. Variables comprised demographics, continuous hospital-based (M3) and pharmaceuticalbased (P3) morbidity indices, and influenza vaccination status. Models trained on one year were also evaluated on data from the following year. Performance measures were the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) and the average precision score (APS). SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) described model contributions, including paired vaccination-input changes in 2024. Findings: Annual national hospitalisation prevalence ranged from 1•01% to 1•60%. With all variables, mean cross-validation AUROC and APS were 0•8430 and 0•1283 for LightGBM (LGB), and 0•8415 and 0•1285 for XGBoost (XGB). Adding both morbidity indices to the demographics increased LGB APS from 0•0720 to 0•1158; adding vaccination further increased it to 0•1283. Next-year AUROC and APS averaged 0•8429 and 0•1267 for LGB and 0•8423 and 0•1277 for XGB. Age and morbidity were the most influential variables. Among vaccinated individuals, SHAP analysis indicated that vaccination contributed most strongly towards lower modelestimated hospitalisation risk in the oldest age group.

Interpretation: Combining historical morbidity and vaccination information in gradient boosting models improved the ability to distinguish between people who experienced respiratory hospitalisation and those who did not in annual New Zealand population data. Similar next-year performance and interpretable variation across age and morbidity profiles support further development toward practical risk assessment, including calibration and evaluation across population groups.

Research in context

Evidence before this study Our focused narrative review considered population-based respiratory hospitalisation models, respiratory disease burden in New Zealand, and the development of the M3 and P3 morbidity indices. Existing studies include population models for COVID-19 and infant respiratory syncytial virus, risk-factor analyses of broad respiratory admissions, and evidence that hospital and pharmaceutical morbidity measures provide complementary information.

Added value of this study

This is the first evaluation of national hospitalisation data linked to other national administrative data to model the risk of acute respiratory infection admission across eight annual datasets, comparing six algorithms and eight variable sets. Together with the benchmark results, these findings illustrate how gradient boosting applied to population-scale records can represent and explain combinations of routinely recorded health information.

Implications of all the available evidence

Quantifying historical morbidity and including vaccination information improved respiratory hospitalisation classification, with gradient boosting providing the strongest overall performance. The explanatory analyses further showed that vaccination-related contributions depended on the individual's age and morbidity profile. Representing these clinically plausible patterns supports the use of flexible, non-linear models, such as tree-based algorithms, and rich population-scale records for developing interpretable respiratory risk assessments. The study establishes a basis for a New Zealand respiratory risk model by identifying effective algorithms and useful combinations of variables.

Introduction

Respiratory infections are a substantial cause of hospitalisation, and their burden is unevenly distributed across the population. New Zealand studies have documented inequalities in serious infectious disease admissions and respiratory hospitalisation burden associated with age, ethnicity, and deprivation 1, 2 . Identifying people at greater risk could support prevention and guided discussions between patients and general practitioners about modifiable risk factors and mitigation strategies. Routinely collected population records allow us to investigate this vulnerability across a broad range of respiratory hospitalisation outcomes without requiring new clinical measurements and participant burden.

Many respiratory models concern outcomes after infection or presentation to healthcare 3, 4 . Population-based examples include QCOVID for COVID-19 hospitalisation and mortality among adults, and a registry-based model of respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) hospitalisation among infants 5, 6 . A Scottish national study also identified age, deprivation, existing conditions, and previous admissions as risk factors for a broad group of acute respiratory infection admissions 7 . While forecasting aggregate respiratory admissions to predict healthcare demand in New Zealand has been addressed 8 , an individual-level model to identify people at increased risk of hospitalisation is lacking. An individual model can help discern baseline risk due to unmodifiable characteristics, such as age, from modifiable factors and identify relative contributions of different factors to overall risk.

The integration of variables was informed by international literature on risk factors for severe influenza, which highlights age and underlying health conditions as important determinants of severe outcomes 9 . Representing the combined burden of long-term conditions is therefore an important consideration in developing an individual risk model. In New Zealand, the Measuring Multimorbidity (M3) and Pharmaceutical Prescribing Profile (P3) indices summarise historical morbidity using hospital diagnoses and pharmaceutical dispensing, respectively 10, 11 . These indices offer a practical way to incorporate information about multiple conditions from routinely collected records and to assess whether the two sources provide complementary information. Influenza vaccination is also relevant as an established preventive measure against an important cause of respiratory hospitalisation and, unlike age and most medical conditions, represents a modifiable factor 12 . Together with demographic and deprivation variables, these measures provide a starting point for establishing a population model using routinely available information. Investigating the contribution of these variables provides a foundation for subsequent models incorporating more detailed clinical, behavioural, and environmental variables.

Using New Zealand population data, we aimed to develop and evaluate a model that distinguishes between people with and without a hospital admission for respiratory infectious disease. Using linked annual population records from 2017 to 2024, we compared modelling approaches and quantified the extent to which models improved by incorporating historical morbidity and influenza vaccination data, in addition to demographic variables. We also examined whether models maintained their performance when applied to the following year and explored how age, morbidity, and vaccination contributed to model estimates.

Methods

Study design and data

We conducted a retrospective population-level classification study using linked records from the Stats NZ Integrated Data Infrastructure (IDI), a secure research database linking de-identified administrative and survey records about people and households in New Zealand. Annual datasets covered 2017-2024. Sources included population and address records aggregated to the mesh block level, hospital discharge information, immunisation records, and pharmaceutical dispensing records. The unit of analysis was the person within an annual dataset; individuals could appear in several years. People with missing required information were excluded before modelling, and no imputation was applied.

Outcome and variables

The binary outcome indicated at least one qualifying hospitalisation during the calendar year, identified using a broad set of respiratory disease codes from the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, Tenth Revision, Australian Modification (ICD-10-AM). The complete code list is provided in Table S1 (supplementary material). Codes were selected to achieve some specificity for influenza and based on previous literature 1, 13 , clinical advice and codes most commonly associated with influenza-positive SARI cases from the SHIVERS study 14.

Demographics comprised age, sex, and area-level deprivation score 15 and five non-exclusive ethnicity indicators: Asian, European, Māori, Pacific peoples, and Middle Eastern, Latin American, and African (MELAA). Individuals could identify with more than one ethnic group. All five binary ethnicity variables were retained simultaneously, giving eight demographic input columns. In tables and plots, Europe and Pacific denote European and Pacific peoples, respectively; DEP denotes the deprivation score, FV denotes the influenza vaccination status, and Demo denotes the demographic variable set.

To incorporate information on individual multimorbidity, we used the M3 and P3 indices, developed by Stanley and colleagues using New Zealand population health data 10, 11 . M3 and P3 indices were derived from linked IDI records and included as continuous variables. M3 used hospital diagnoses from the five years before each annual snapshot, whereas P3 used pharmaceutical dispensing records from the preceding year. Neither index included information from the outcome year. For hospitalised individuals, FV was one (FV=1) if vaccination occurred in the same calendar year at least 14 days before the qualifying admission. For people without hospitalisation, FV was one if vaccination occurred at any time during that year; otherwise, it was zero (FV=0).

Eight variable sets were evaluated: demographics alone; demographics with FV, M3, or P3 separately; demographics with M3 and P3; demographics with FV and either morbidity index; and demographics with all three. The full set contained 11 input columns.

Algorithms and cross-validation

We treated hospitalisation as a binary classification task: each annual record was labelled as having at least one qualifying hospital admission (1) or not (0). During training, machine learning classification algorithms learned relationships between the recorded variables and these labels. Each fitted model then produced a continuous score between 0 and 1 for the hospitalisation class, with higher scores indicating greater model-estimated hospitalisation risk. We evaluated how well these scores distinguished people with and without hospitalisation, without selecting a threshold to assign individuals to either class. We used scores from the class-balanced models to rank individuals by hospitalisation risk and did not assess calibration.

We compared logistic regression (LR), random forest (RF), LightGBM (LGB), XGBoost (XGB), and single-tree versions of LightGBM and XGBoost, labelled Tree (lgb) and Tree (xgb) [16] [17] [18] . RF combines the outputs of multiple decision trees, whereas LGB and XGB build trees sequentially, with each additional tree refining the combined model. The ensembles used 100 trees (the default parameter), and the single-tree versions used 1 boosting iteration. LR included linear terms for continuous variables on the log-odds scale and no interaction terms, serving as a linear baseline model. Decision-tree models can accommodate nonlinear relationships and interactions without specifying these patterns in advance. Analyses were conducted on the raw individual-level data in secure Stats NZ Data Labs. Computational constraints in the Data Lab informed the choice of algorithms and fixed settings; no hyperparameter tuning was performed (see supplementary material Table S2).

Five non-overlapping test folds were formed within each year using demographic-profile groups. A profile combined age band, sex, the five ethnicity indicators, and a 10-step deprivation band. One representative was randomly selected from each profile, and a five-fold stratified allocation was applied to these representatives based on their outcomes. All individuals with a given profile were assigned to the same fold. Each fold was held out once, with approximately 80% of annual records used for training and 20% for testing. This profile grouping was applied to ensure that multiple samples with the same or very similar combinations of variable values are represented in both the training and testing sets, which can be an issue for the model when using only demographic variables, as these are mainly categorical. The same folds were used across algorithms and variable sets within the cross-validation.

Variables were standardised using means and standard deviations fitted to each training set, then transformed in the held-out data. Class balancing was applied during training, using the appropriate algorithm parameters. LR used balanced class weights; XGB used the negative-to-positive training count ratio; LGB used its classimbalance option; and RF used corresponding observation weights.

Performance and temporal evaluation

Hospitalisation caused by respiratory infection, whilst making a large contribution to seasonal hospital admissions, is infrequent at the population level. In the annual datasets, approximately 1•5% to 1•6% of people had a qualifying admission in most years (Table 2). Most people do not have a hospital admission. This is termed a class imbalance, which makes overall classification accuracy uninformative on its own. For example, a naïve model that always predicts no hospitalisation achieves an accuracy of 98•5% at a prevalence of 1•5%. Therefore, we use threshold-independent performance measures to evaluate model performance and focus on the average precision score (APS), as the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) can be misleading when class imbalance is high 19.

Fold scores were averaged within each year, and annual means were weighted equally. These summaries average evaluation scores rather than pooling individual model outputs. The algorithms were first compared using the full variable set; variable-set comparisons then focused on LGB and XGB. Increments from adding FV were matched by year and fold against the corresponding set without FV.

For next-year evaluation, models trained on complete annual datasets from 2017 through 2023 were applied to the following year's population. Training-year transformations and class weights were retained. Scores were averaged equally across seven year-pairs and compared with cross-validation results for the same evaluation years. This assesses temporal transfer within the national data system, where individuals can overlap between years. Overall performance summaries included all eight cross-validation years (2017-2024) and all seven next-year evaluations (supplementary Tables S3-S6). Figure 2 compares variable sets after excluding the evaluation years 2020 and 2021, which were influenced by pandemic-related changes in respiratory virus circulation and markedly lower hospitalisation prevalence.

Variable importance and SHAP

For LGB and XGB, gain importance represented the total split gain attributable to each variable, and split importance represented its frequency of use 17, 18 . Values were normalised separately within each fitted model and importance type, then averaged across folds and years and expressed as percentages.

SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) describe how each variable contributes to an individual model-estimated hospitalisation risk. All variable contributions, together with a model-specific reference value, sum to the model estimate (on the log-odds scale) 20 . For this analysis, the original models and real records inside the Data Lab were used. SHAP values were derived for a subset of 1,000,000 people randomly selected from the full dataset for the same year or the next year. A subset was used to reduce computational resource requirements. Based on the small variation observed in model performance across cross-validation folds, we expect that a random subset covering ~20% of the data reflects the distributions of the full dataset.

To visualise the SHAP analyses of the models, we used beeswarm plots (Figure 5 and Supplementary Figure S1). These allowed us to examine the magnitude and direction of variable contributions on the log-odds scale.

Note that we selected LGB for the SHAP analysis, as this is the model for which we, together with Stats NZ, found a reliable workflow to release trained models after confidentiality processing. Besides XGB, LGB achieved the highest model performance scores. We used TreeExplainer, an efficient implementation for deriving SHAP values from tree-based models 20.

Paired vaccination SHAP analysis

Among people with observed FV=1 in 2024, we conducted a descriptive paired analysis using the full-variable LGB model fitted to the complete 2024 analysis dataset. Each individual was evaluated twice with TreeExplainer, with FV set to one and zero, holding all other variables, the fitted model, and explainer settings fixed. Differences were defined as SHAP with FV=1 minus SHAP with FV=0. Negative values indicate lower contributions under FV=1. We summarised FV and age differences by medians and quartiles within five age groups, labelled 0-1, 1-5, 5-15, 15-65, and 65+, overall and separately for M3=P3=0 and M3 ≠ 0 or P3 ≠ 0. Both indices remained continuous model variables. This analysis describes the fitted model's response in the training records and does not estimate causal effects of vaccination.

Results

Population and algorithm performance

Annual analysis populations ranged from 4,851,537 in 2017 to 5,355,516 in 2024 (Table 2). Between 51,057 and 85,575 people had a qualifying hospitalisation each year. Prevalence was 1•51-1•60% in 2017-2019 and 2022-2024, compared with 1•01% in 2020 and 1•19% in 2021.

Characteristics of the 2024 analysis population are presented overall and by respiratory hospitalisation status in Table 1. Compared with individuals without qualifying hospitalisation, hospitalised individuals were more frequently aged 65 years or older (39•4 % versus 16•8 %) and belonged to the highest deprivation quintile (29•2 % versus 20•5 %). A greater proportion also had M3 > 0 (41•7 % versus 11•5 %) or P3 > 0 (66•7 % versus 42•5 %).

LGB and XGB had the strongest average performance with the full set of variables (Figure 1; supplementary Table S3).

Contribution of morbidity and vaccination

Both morbidity indices improved performance across all years (Figure 2 and supplementary material Tables S4 and S5). For LGB, demographics alone yielded an AUROC of 0•7856 and an APS of 0•0720. Adding M3 increased these to 0•8259 and 0•1034; adding P3 increased them to 0•8297 and 0•0998. Including both indices yielded an AUROC of 0•8400 and an APS of 0•1158. XGB followed the same pattern: AUROC increased from 0•7830 to 0•8382 and APS from 0•0713 to 0•1154 with both indices. Relative APS increases were 60•8% for LGB and 61•9% for XGB.

The added value of FV depended on the other variables. For LGB, FV increased APS by 0•0054 with demographics alone and by 0•0124 when both indices were included. XGB increments were 0•0055 and 0•0131, respectively. Gains with either index separately were intermediate (Table S6, see supplementary material).

Gain and split importance

Age, M3, and P3 accounted for 96•2% of LGB gain and 90•4% of XGB gain with all variables (Figure 3; supplementary Table S7). Their mean LGB gain shares were 44•69%, 36•58%, and 14•91%, respectively. Age contributed the largest share in seven years; M3 ranked first in 2020. P3 ranked third in every annual LGB gain summary. Deprivation accounted for 15•98% of LGB splits and 24•15% of XGB splits, compared with gain shares of 1•45% and 3•74%. FV accounted for 1•12% of LGB gain and 3•14% of splits.

Next year performance and SHAP profiles

Next-year AUROC and APS averaged 0•8429 and 0•1267 for LGB and 0•8423 and 0•1277 for XGB. Aligned to the same evaluation years, mean next-year minus cross-validation differences were -0•0007 for AUROC and -0•0009 for APS for LGB, and +0•0003 and -0•0001 for XGB (Figure 4). Improvements from morbidity and FV remained apparent in next-year evaluations (supplementary Tables S3-S6).

Across the beeswarm plots, age, P3, and M3 occupied the first three displayed positions. Both young and older ages were among the positive contributions, as expected. Higher morbidity indices and deprivation generally contributed positively, whereas recorded vaccination generally contributed negatively. Figure 5 compares nextyear and same-year explanations for 2018 and 2024 (see supplementary material, Figure S1, for profiles derived from other years). Broad patterns were similar, although the ordering and contributions of some variables varied.

Paired vaccination SHAP analysis

Setting FV to one produced a negative median change in the FV contribution across all displayed age groups (Figure 6). Among all vaccinated individuals within each group, median changes ranged from -0•327 to -0•418 in the four younger groups and reached -0•656 in the 65+ group. Median age-contribution changes were close to zero in younger groups (-0•001 to 0•029), compared with -0•171 in the 65+ group. Within that oldest group, changes were more negative when either morbidity index was nonzero than when both were zero: median FV changes were -0•675 versus -0•527, and age changes were -0•177 versus -0•145. All differences are in modeloutput units, not in model-estimated probability.

Discussion

Using linked New Zealand population records, we compared classification algorithms and quantified the extent to which historical morbidity and influenza vaccination information improved the ability to distinguish people with and without respiratory hospitalisation. Integrating hospital-and pharmaceutical-based morbidity indices improves models. Furthermore, our results show that these indices are complementary, with the highest APS achieved when models incorporate both. Additionally, recorded influenza vaccination provided a further improvement, with the greatest effect when both morbidity indices were included.

With all variables included, Tree (xgb) achieved a higher mean APS than RF (0•1054 vs 0•0720) despite a similar mean AUROC (0•8244 vs 0•8240). While the YDF random forest always uses a random subset of variables at each split, potentially omitting the important ones, the single XGB tree could consider all variables at every split. This difference may partly explain the single tree's higher APS, while successive trees in the XGB and LGB ensembles could further refine the ranking of people at elevated risk.

The paired vaccination analysis showed how gradient boosting captured clinically plausible joint patterns in our data. For each vaccinated person in the 2024 analysis, we changed only the vaccination input. Recording vaccination as present produced the largest median downward shifts in both the vaccination and age contributions among people aged 65 years and older, particularly those with a nonzero morbidity index (at least one medical condition was present). Even though a person's age is not changed in this analysis, the model assigns different contributions to the age variable based on vaccination status. This age-morbidity pattern is consistent with the priority given to older adults and people with chronic conditions in influenza vaccination recommendations. 12 Studies of vaccine effectiveness also document variation by age and, among older adults, by condition. 21, 22 The SHAP changes quantify how the fitted model used vaccination information. However, estimating vaccine effectiveness within these groups requires a causal analysis.

Age and morbidity helped identify groups vulnerable to severe respiratory illness, but exposure to infection also matters. Many people with increased estimated risk had no admissions in any of the datasets. As people with admissions made up only a small fraction of the population, even a low false-positive rate could produce many false positives relative to true positives. This reduces precision and helps explain why a model can achieve good AUROC alongside modest APS. 19 Accordingly, in 2020, APS fell as the prevalence of qualifying admissions dropped to 1•01%. Lockdowns, border restrictions and physical distancing curtailed transmission, 23 and surveillance documented changes in the circulation of influenza and RSV during 2020 and 2021. 24, 25 People vulnerable because of age or chronic illness may have had fewer opportunities to become infected. Lower prevalence itself also reduces precision at a given sensitivity and false-positive rate. The proportion with a qualifying admission rose from 1•19% in 2021 to 1•51% in 2022. During New Zealand's Omicron waves in 2022, older adults had higher COVID-19 hospitalisation rates. 26 Our study may have included some of these admissions through a qualifying respiratory diagnosis such as viral pneumonia. This suggests some overlap in vulnerability across infections, although the combined outcome prevents us from attributing the rebound to a particular virus.

Compared to the pre-pandemic era, the mean APS achieved by gradient boosting was slightly lower in 2022-2024, despite similar hospitalisation prevalence. Auckland surveillance documented changes in influenza admission rates and the age profile of admitted adults after restrictions eased, with a different pattern for human metapneumovirus. 2 Changes in circulating pathogens and affected populations may therefore have influenced classification, even when models were refitted each year. Similar average performance in next-year evaluations supports annual model updates, accompanied by regular assessment as respiratory disease patterns change.

Further development could use linked survey and census records to assess whether information on smoking, household crowding and employment improves classification. This is worth testing because APS remained well below its maximum of 1•0, which indicates that many variables are missing that could help a model to capture the high-risk group more precisely.

To make the models easier to use during consultations, we could replace M3 and P3 with a short list of conditions that clinicians can readily identify. Evaluating models based on individual medical conditions rather than the morbidity indices would show whether this can be done without decreasing performance. However, before the model can support individual health advice, calibration should establish how closely its scores agree with observed hospitalisation rates, with recalibration where needed. This is particularly relevant given the low prevalence rates. 27 Calibration, sensitivity and positive predictive value at clinically relevant thresholds should be assessed across age, ethnicity and deprivation groups, including Māori and Pacific peoples. Comparisons with models trained within these groups could show whether more specialised models add value.

Clinicians and patients could then assess whether individual explanations support informed discussions about respiratory risk and prevention.
