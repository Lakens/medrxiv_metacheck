What we found

We found 10 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Abstract

Although inherited variation is fixed at birth, its effects on health change with age and differ between common and rare variants. Here, we introduce Mendelnoulli, a longitudinal model that combines the age-dependent effects of polygenic and monogenic variation on health records, separating how much disease each individual accumulates overall from diseases to which they are prone. Mendelnoulli further allows polygenic and monogenic components to exert different influences on latent disease trajectories. We apply the model to 348 diseases, 36 polygenic scores, and 34 monogenic conditions across three biobanks and provide an atlas of the age-dependent effects of inherited variation on common human diseases. We show that polygenic effects change significantly with age, with Mendelnoulli improving held-out one-year discrimination for all 28 diseases tested compared with a model whose polygenic effects are constant over age (median AUC 0.95 vs 0.92). For individuals with no prior diagnoses, Mendelnoulli identified 8.6 more of every 100 new cases than that model, when each flagged the 5% at highest risk. For genes with an established disease, Mendelnoulli selected that disease for 27 of 30 genes and ranked it first for 17 (7 expected by chance), and recovered known differences in timing, such as earlier breast cancer effects for BRCA1 than BRCA2 (peak 48 vs 66 years). Mendelnoulli provides a unified model of common and rare genetic influences on disease trajectories across the life course.

[div-01]

Many common human diseases are partly determined by inherited genetic factors [1, 2], yet a genetic susceptibility present from birth need not have a constant effect across the life course: the per-standard-deviation effect of a polygenic score is often largest early in life and declines with age [3, 4, 5]. Prevention, therefore, requires more than identifying who is at inherited risk. It requires understanding when that risk becomes clinically important, what phenotype it produces, and how it is modified by other events over the life course.

Two observations motivate our work. First, when polygenic effects attenuate with age (for example, due to the accumulation of environmental factors), they violate the proportional-hazards assumption used by many risk models. A clinically important consequence is that a polygenic score may carry substantial predictive information for rapidly progressing individuals before related diagnoses accumulate [3, 4]; we test this below. Second, pathogenic variants typically generate syndromic phenotypes, distinctive sets of diseases [6, 7], that are not reproduced by simply moving farther into the tail of a polygenic score for any one of the constituent diseases.

Rare variants have been studied across many diseases [8, 9] and within disease areas [10], and common and low-frequency variants in genome-wide studies [11]. Multiple lines of evidence suggest that combining them can improve risk prediction. Penetrance estimates from unselected biobanks are lower than the estimates from clinically ascertained families [12, 13], reflecting ascertainment; the same rare variant may confer different risk in different genetic backgrounds [14, 15]. Within carriers of tier-1 genomic conditions [16], polygenic background can shift the risk of the syndrome's index disease across a wide range [17]. Likewise, a sufficiently extreme polygenic score can confer risk of that same disease comparable to a pathogenic variant [18]. Models that combine the two sources of risk exist for single diseases [19, 20], but to our knowledge, none has combined them across many diseases and organ systems at once. Several approaches model many diseases jointly. Our previous model, Aladynoulli [21], represents longitudinal disease histories as mixtures of latent signatures of related diseases, with individual loadings that change with age. Age-dependent topic models of comorbidity in the UK Biobank identified disease subtypes with different genetic associations [22], and generative transformers trained on health records predict the timing of more than a thousand disease billing codes [23]; one also uses polygenic scores [24]. Life-course analyses of polygenic scores have mostly considered one trait at a time, such as weight from birth to adulthood [25] or coronary artery disease by age [26], and have used common variants only. Modeling many diseases jointly has two advantages for genetic questions. A genetic effect on one disease is estimated concomitantly with all the other diseases an individual develops, accounting for comorbidities; and the syndromic effects of a pathogenic variant, which span several diseases, can be estimated directly rather than one disease at a time.

Here we develop a model for improved disease prediction and for characterizing how inherited risk is expressed temporally across many conditions. We show that when germline information is modeled across the life course and across the spectrum of human disease, it improves prediction.

We provide both an atlas of polygenic and monogenic effects on disease and methods for clinically 2 relevant stratification. Compared with these models, ours adds three elements. First, polygenic effects on disease signatures change with age and are estimated jointly across 348 diseases. Second, the effects of pathogenic variants are sparse [27] and age-varying, so the model selects which diseases each variant affects. Third, a reference state for health, which follows an age curve, and a perperson rate separate how much disease an individual accrues from which diseases. We validate our conclusions against model-free carrier-PRS contrasts, held-out prediction, penetrance in the linked national cancer registry, and replication in two independent cohorts.

Results

The model: two genetic mechanisms

Our approach begins from the Aladynoulli model [21], whose objective is to use clinical data to understand latent patterns of disease, which we call disease signatures, and their contributions to an individual's disease course over time. Each signature represents a group of diseases with a characteristic pattern of onset. An individual's loading on a signature varies with age.

Starting from this framework, we now model two distinct ways for inherited variation to act.

First, polygenic variation can change an individual's loading on a signature, making every disease in the signature either more or less likely together; we allow this genetic effect to increase or decrease with age. Second, a pathogenic variant can change the composition of a signature for its carriers, making one disease more likely relative to the other diseases in the same signature. For brevity, we use rescaling and reshaping for these two mechanisms. Two individuals with the same loading on the same signature at the same age can be prone to different diseases, depending on carrier status. This is in contrast to [21], where polygenic scores entered only as a constant level effect on the signature loading and thus could influence neither how an individual's loading changed with age nor which diseases within the signature they developed.

The model describes each individual's record as a set of first diagnoses by age. The outcome, for each individual, disease, and year of age, is whether that disease is first recorded at that age; follow-up for a disease ends at its first record or at censoring. The probability of a first diagnosis is a mixture over signatures. Each signature assigns every disease an age-specific probability, and the individual's loadings weight these probabilities. One further component represents health. In it, every disease is less probable than in the population as a whole, and its weight is highest at young ages and declines along the population's disease-free curve. A per-person rate, or frailty, scales how much disease an individual accrues overall, without changing which diseases they are prone to.

Formally, let Y idt = 1 denote the event where disease d is first recorded for individual i at age t, and 0 otherwise. Let E id represent the last age at which i is at risk for d. For signature k, ϕ kdt = µ dt + ψ kd + ϵ kdt is the log-odds of a first diagnosis of d at age t for a non-carrier with all weight on that signature, where µ dt is the population log-odds, ψ kd is the signature's enrichment for disease d, and ϵ kdt is a 3 smooth age-varying deviation with a Gaussian-process prior. Individual i's loadings are with weights θ i•t = softmax(λ i•t ), where r k (t) is a population-level age trajectory, G i the individual's polygenic scores and sex, γ 0k their effect on the loading, γ 1k the change in that effect with age, and δ ikt a smooth per-person deviation learned from the individual's own record.

With g im = 1 if individual i carries a pathogenic variant in gene m, the probability of a first diagnosis is with where σ is the logistic function, η mkd (t) is the effect of carrying a variant in gene m on the log-odds of disease d in signature k at age t, and f i is the individual's frailty.

We impose sparsity on the carrier effect with a 'spike-and-slab' prior, η mkd (t) = scope mk z mkd v mkd (t).

The indicator scope mk assigns each gene to a signature, usually the one in which the gene's primary disease is most enriched (Methods). Within that signature, a binary selection indicator z mkd records whether disease d has a non-zero carrier effect; a penalty on the number of selected diseases keeps the selected set small. The slab v mkd (t) is the size of the effect, with a Gaussian-process prior over age, to flexibly estimate the age at which a carrier effect is more pronounced.

The two genetic terms enter the model in different equations, changing disease risk in different ways (Fig. 1a). A polygenic score alters an individual's loadings, which in turn multiplies the probability of every disease in a signature by the same factor; therefore, the ratio of the probabilities of any two diseases within the signature remains unchanged. We refer to this as rescaling. A polygenic score, however extreme, moves weight between signatures but leaves each signature's composition fixed (Fig. 3a,b). In contrast, the carrier effect changes the probability of one disease within a signature, and therefore its probability relative to the other diseases in that signature. We refer to this as reshaping. Only the carrier term can perform reshaping within the signature.

We evaluate three ablations of the model as comparators, each obtained by removing one term from the full model above: the no-interaction model (γ 1 ≡ 0), in which each polygenic score has a constant effect on the loadings over age; the model without the carrier term (η ≡ 0); and the age-constant model, in which the carrier effect does not vary with age.

We fit the model to the electronic health records of 400,000 UK Biobank participants [28]: D = 348 diseases defined by phecodes, first diagnoses in single years of age from 30 to 81 (T = 52), K = 20 signatures and 36 polygenic scores [29] and sex, the scores adjusted for the first ten genetic principal components. Carrier analyses use ClinVar [30] pathogenic and likely pathogenic variants in the 200,000 exome-sequenced participants [31]. Every term, with its dimension and whether it is data, fixed before fitting or learned, is listed in Table S1; estimation is described in Methods.

Polygenic susceptibilities change with age

Polygenic scores are often modeled as having a constant effect at every age. We asked whether this assumption holds and whether an age interaction in the model captures departures from it. For ten illustrative disease-score pairs, we compared the model with two empirical measures: the hazard ratio per standard deviation of the score, estimated in five-year age windows, and the ratio of event rates between the top and bottom quintiles.

As expected [3, 4], the empirical hazard ratio declined with age for most pairs, and the full model reproduced the direction of the empirical trend in all ten pairs (binomial p = 0.002; Fig. 1b,c). For type 1 diabetes, for example, the ratio of event rates between the top and bottom score quintiles fell from 9.0 at age 45 to 2.0 at age 70, and the full model captures this decline (9.1 to 2.0).

Each score acts through signatures that jointly model 348 diseases, so the model's age effect is adjusted for comorbidities. The decline supports the observation that a polygenic score conveys most information early in life. In the fitted model, this decline arises from an age effect that reduces a positive overall level effect, most clearly for scores such as atrial fibrillation and type 1 diabetes (Fig. 1d).

Age-varying polygenic effects improve prediction, most for individuals with little recorded history

We first asked whether modeling the age interaction improves the prediction of new diagnoses. We measured discrimination as the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) for a first diagnosis of each of 28 diseases in the year after enrollment, in held-out participants, with both models fit so that they differ only in the age interaction.

The interaction model had a higher AUC for all 28 diseases (binomial p < 10 -8 ; median 0.945 against 0.922), by 0.023 on average (95% CI across diseases 0.018-0.028; Fig. 2c). Improvements in prediction accuracy were observed irrespective of prior history: when participants were grouped by the number of diseases recorded before enrollment (0, 1-2, 3-5, 6 or more), the AUC increased in 32 of 36 disease-group combinations (binomial p < 10 -5 ), with no decline as the number of prior diagnoses increased (Fig. 2d).

We next asked whether the age interaction can explain part of the difference in future trajectories between individuals who look the same at enrollment. Using the fitted coefficients for the cardiac signature, which includes atrial fibrillation, we constructed three hypothetical genetic profiles with the same loading on that signature at age 50, differing only in their genetic age term (the 10th percentile, zero, or the 90th percentile of its distribution in the cohort). Ten-year risk of atrial fibrillation reached 10% for the 90th-percentile profile and 6% for the 10th (Fig. 2a).

For individuals with no recorded history at enrollment, the interaction model provides better stratification based on polygenic scores. Among the 1,610 participants with no prior diagnoses who developed atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) within two years, the mean predicted risk at enrollment was 8.9% per year with the interaction and 7.5% without it (Wilcoxon signed-5 rank p < 10 -80 ), whereas in participants with equally empty records who remained free of ASCVD it changed by only 0.03 percentage points (Mann-Whitney p < 10 -100 for the difference; Fig. 2b).

We then asked whether the gain differs among the people each model would flag for screening.

For each disease, we let each model select the 5% of participants it considered at highest risk, and counted how many of the people diagnosed in the following year had been within that set. Among those diagnosed who had no prior diagnoses, the interaction model identified 8.6 more per 100 cases than the no-interaction model (1,746 found only by the interaction model, against 408 found only by the other; McNemar p < 10 -16 ). Among those with six or more prior diagnoses, it found only 1.9 more per 100.

Finally, we asked whether the predicted risks are accurate in absolute terms. On held-out data, the model predicted about 12% more events than were observed (observed-to-expected ratio 0.89), with the largest relative excess at the lowest predicted risks, compared with a ratio of 1.62 (too few events) without the health component, ancestry adjustment, and frailty described above (Fig. 2e).

A single monotone recalibration, estimated on half of the participants and evaluated on the other half, brought the ratio to 1.00 without changing the AUC.

Effects of pathogenic variants

First, without the model, we compared the carriers of each gene with the top fraction of the polygenic score for the gene's primary disease whose excess incidence of that disease, relative to everyone else, was closest to the carriers' (Table 2). BRCA1, BRCA2 and PALB2 carriers had a 21.6 percentage-point excess of breast cancer by age 70, similar to the top 0.5% of the breast cancer polygenic score (19.7 points); yet carriers had a 6.5-point excess of ovarian cancer (95% CI 4.2-8.6) and the high-score group had none (0.0; -0.4-0.5).

TTN carriers and the top 10% of the atrial fibrillation score had similar excesses of atrial fibrillation (5.5 and 6.1 points), but cardiomyopathy excesses of 3.8 and 0.3 points. No polygenic group reached the colon cancer excess of MLH1 and MSH6 carriers, so we compared secondary excess per point of primary excess: 0.71 (0.38-1.26) for uterine cancer in carriers, against at most 0.03 for any high-score group. At matched risk of the primary disease, carriers and high-score individuals differ in the other diseases they may develop.

In the model, the carrier term is not bound by this proportion: it selects which diseases of the gene's signature change, from the carriers' records (Fig. 3b).

Next, we asked whether the carrier effects identify the known disease associated with each gene as a positive control. We fitted the model to carriers of ClinVar pathogenic and likely pathogenic variants among 200,000 exome-sequenced participants, specifying only the signature each gene acts on and allowing the model to select which diseases change. Before fitting, we assigned each gene a target disease family from its established clinical phenotype (for example, hypercholesterolemia for LDLR, colon cancer for MLH1, and cardiomyopathy or heart failure for TTN ; Methods). The target family was among the selected diseases for 27 of 30 genes (Table S7). Because many syndromes include several diseases, such as breast and ovarian cancer for BRCA1 and BRCA2, the strongest selected effect need not fall on the target. It did for 17 of 30 genes, against 7.2 expected if the 6 selected diseases were ordered at random (Poisson-binomial p = 10 -5 ).

We also sought to understand how allowing carrier effects to vary with age could elucidate the peak age of each effect. BRCA1 and BRCA2 illustrate these age differences. Both genes increase the risk of breast cancer, and when allowed to act on the signature that encompasses most gynecological diagnoses, both also increase the risk of ovarian cancer. For both cancers, the effect peaked earlier in BRCA1 carriers, at age 48 against 66 for breast cancer and 54 against 65 for ovarian cancer (Fig. 3b,c). The recorded diagnoses showed the same order: among UK Biobank women, breast cancer rates at ages 40-49 were 3.6 times higher in BRCA1 than in BRCA2 carriers (exact binomial p = 0.003), and excess ovarian cancer was highest at ages 50-59 in BRCA1 carriers and at 60-79 in BRCA2 carriers, although the BRCA1 estimate rests on nine cases (rate ratio at ages 40-59, 2.4; p = 0.08; Supp. Fig. S1). The model recovers the established difference in timing between the two genes [32, 6].

We then asked whether the model provides a good fit to absolute carrier penetrance. For the cancers, we estimated the carrier effect from diagnoses in the national cancer registry linked to the UK Biobank and compared the model's penetrance with the registry's Kaplan-Meier estimate.

By age 70, the model indicated that BRCA2 carriers have a 34.1% cumulative incidence of breast cancer, compared to 31.5% in the registry, and every model carrier curve fell within the registry's 95% confidence interval at age 70 (Fig. 3d).

Finally, the model represents a carrier's risk in two places: the carrier effect on specific diseases and the carrier's loading on the signature. We asked whether these two agree. Across genes, the carrier-control difference in penetrance was correlated over age with the carrier-control difference in signature loading (median Pearson r = 0.68; Fig. 3e).

Selected diseases are specific to genes, stable, and match observed rates

Genes defining monogenic conditions can affect different diseases across signatures. Conversely, two genes can act on different diseases within the same signature. We next asked whether the model makes use of this feature. Five cardiac genes share the same assigned signature (S0, fixed before fitting; Methods), so any differences among them come from the diseases the model selects. The selections differed and matched each gene's known phenotype: cardiomyopathy for DSP (carriercontrol difference 11.1 percentage points), ventricular tachycardia for MYBPC3 and PKP2, heart failure for TTN, and atrial fibrillation for TTN, PKP2 and MYH7 (Fig. 4a). When effects were allowed to vary with age, every gene still selected diseases (TTN 15, MYBPC3 11, PKP2 9, DSP 7, MYH7 5), and all five kept heart failure (Fig. 4b).

We next asked whether these selections are stable when refitting the model with ten different random seeds. Every gene-disease pair with a large effect (mean magnitude above 0.69) was selected in all ten fits, and its effect size varied by at most 4% (Fig. 4c). Selection varied only for pairs with smaller effects (|v| ≤ 0.54). Finally, we compared these selections with empirical rates.

In carriers of Lynch syndrome genes, matched to non-carriers on sex and age, observed rates of colorectal cancer followed the established order of severity, MLH1 (21.7%) above MSH6 (11.6%) 7 above PMS2 (0%) [7] (Fig. 4d).

Some carriers develop diseases that are primarily enriched in other signatures. We asked whether the model can capture these. Of the 307 gene-disease pairs selected across 34 genes, 36 involve such diseases, and 24 of these raise penetrance (Fig. 4e). Selected pairs are candidates, so we compared each with carriers' observed diagnoses. Of the 24, 5 showed a significant excess over matched noncarriers (FDR q < 0.05): lymph-node metastasis for ATM, BRCA1 and BRCA2, and hypertension for APOB and LMF1 ; across all 307 pairs, 35 did. COL4A3 and COL4A4 contributed 74 of the 307 pairs and 11 of the 24, with no significant excess, so we do not interpret their selections individually.

Carrier status, polygenic scores and the health record provide complementary information

Because our model combines carrier status and polygenic score multiplicatively on the underlying scale, we asked whether their empirical joint effect supports this and how much complementary information each provides.

First, for polygenic scores to add information in carriers, the carriers must not already be concentrated at high scores. We compared the distribution of all 36 scores between carriers and noncarriers. For 35 of the 36 scores, the standardized mean difference was within ±0.1 (Supp. Fig. S10), as expected for unlinked variants. Polygenic scores therefore do not duplicate carrier status.

For genes of moderate effect, the polygenic gradient was similar among carriers and non-carriers (interaction odds ratio for TTN, 1.08; p = 0.59), whereas for high-penetrance cancer genes, the gradient was flatter in carriers (BRCA2, 0.72, 95% CI 0.57-0.92; Lynch genes, 0.69, 0.49-0.98; not adjusted for multiple testing; Fig. 5a,b). One explanation is that when a variant already confers high risk, each unit of polygenic score raises relative risk less; however, it may still raise absolute risk more because carriers start from a higher baseline. For TTN the data are consistent with the model's multiplicative form; for high-penetrance cancer genes the polygenic score adds less on the relative scale than this form implies.

We then asked whether the model's predictions, from polygenic score and health record, separate carriers of the same gene by risk. We ranked LDLR carriers by the model's predicted risk within five-year age bands and divided them into thirds. The observed ten-year ASCVD risk was 5%, 11% and 19% in the three groups (log-rank test for trend, p = 0.002), close to the model's predictions of 7%, 12% and 22% (Fig. 5c).

Finally, because polygenic scores do not stand in for carrier status, a model without the carrier term should underestimate carriers' risk. Across the 34 genes of the atlas, the model without the carrier term under-predicted carriers' risk of the gene's main disease (median observed/expected 2.2; Supp. Table S9). Without the carrier term, the model predicted a ten-year ASCVD risk of 7.7% for familial hypercholesterolemia carriers, against an observed 8.8%; scaling this prediction by the carriers' observed rate ratio (1.23) gave 9.4% (Fig. 5d). This moved 52 additional carriers above the 7.5% threshold for statin therapy, and their observed ten-year ASCVD risk was 13%.

The disease selection replicates in two independent cohorts

Replication requires that carriers can be identified comparably across cohorts. We applied the same carrier definition to All of Us [33, 34] (170,825 participants with genome sequences) and the Mass General Brigham Biobank (MGB; 53,111 with exome sequences; Table 1). Carrier rates agreed within two-fold for 39 of 61 genes (median ratio 1.19); the largest discrepancies came from a sequence-quality filter in the UK Biobank exome release that removes recurrent pathogenic alleles in PMS2, CHEK2 and BRCA1 (Fig. S13a).

We then asked whether the diseases selected for each gene in UK Biobank are also selected when the model is fitted separately in All of Us. For 20 of 26 genes, the two selections overlapped more than expected by chance. All of Us recovered a median of two of each gene's three strongest UK Biobank selections (67%, against 13% expected for a random selection of the same size; Fig. S13c). In MGB, the diseases selected in both cohorts distinguished carriers from matched non-carriers in the observed data (Fig. S14). The diseases linked to each gene, therefore, replicate in independent health systems.

Finally, we asked whether carriers develop these diseases earlier than non-carriers, using only observed diagnoses. For each selected disease, we compared the median age at first diagnosis in carriers with that in matched non-carriers. Carriers were diagnosed a median of 3.0 years earlier across 35 gene-disease pairs (earlier for 27, later for 5; sign test p = 10 -4 ); for example, BRCA1 carriers were diagnosed with breast cancer 8.5 years earlier (Fig. 6b). The order in which carriers of each gene are diagnosed with these diseases is shown in Fig. 6a. Because follow-up differs between cohorts, we compare ages within each cohort only. Some later diagnoses plausibly reflect treatment, such as osteoporosis after endocrine therapy, so we interpret the carrier-non-carrier difference rather than absolute timing.

Discussion

Common and rare variants are often summarized by their relative risk for a single disease. Our work shows that they shape disease over the life course and across diseases in different ways. A polygenic score quantifies the extent to which an individual exhibits traits associated with multiple diseases, scaling their relative probabilities collectively and varying over time. By contrast, a pathogenic variant alters the likelihood of specific diseases within a signature and the timing of their occurrence. Both effects are learned from the health record across 348 diseases, without prespecifying which diseases a gene affects or how a score's effect changes with age.

The clearest evidence comes from the recorded diagnoses, without the model. Polygenic scores can identify people whose risk of a disease equals that of monogenic carriers [18], but equal risk of the primary disease does not mean the same disease course: at matched breast cancer risk, carriers of pathogenic variants developed ovarian cancer, while high-score individuals did not. The model represents this difference with two terms: one that scales a signature and one that modifies its composition. These describe patterns of disease rather than molecular mechanisms.

Estimating carrier effects by disease and age reproduces established biology without being told which diseases each gene affects. For most genes, the largest inferred effect aligned with the gene's known disease; cardiac genes sharing the same signature nonetheless selected distinct cardiac conditions; and these selected diseases were consistent when the model was fit independently in All of Us and MGB. Letting effects change with age also captures when they occur: the impact of BRCA1 on breast cancer peaks almost two decades earlier than that of BRCA2, matching the observed diagnosis patterns, and carriers received diagnoses of their selected diseases a median of three years earlier than matched non-carriers.

Disease trajectories have been described from population registries and health records [35, 22, 23], and health record phenotypes can reveal unrecognized Mendelian disease [36]; here, the trajectory model itself separates the effects of common and rare variation and estimates when each acts. Evidence that polygenic effects weaken with age has been reported for several common diseases [3, 4, 5]. Here, the age dependence is estimated jointly across diseases through the signatures that link them, rather than being assumed. These effects declined with age for most scores but increased for some, and modeling it improved held-out discrimination for all 28 diseases tested at all levels of prior history, with the largest effect on the identification of future cases among people with no prior diagnoses. Separating how much disease a person accrues from which diseases, through a health component, per-person frailty, and ancestry adjustment of the scores improved the calibration of absolute risk.

These results bear on how genetic information is used in prevention. Because a polygenic score carries most of its information early, it is most useful before diagnoses accumulate. Because carrier effects have gene-specific timing, surveillance ages could follow the age at which each variant's effect rises, rather than a single lifetime risk. Critically, genotype alone does not determine who will develop the disease: among LDLR carriers of the same age, the model combining the health record with polygenic scores separated carriers with observed ten-year ASCVD risks of 5% and 19%, while a model without the carrier term underestimated their risk and would have left 52 carriers below the statin threshold. Carrier status and polygenic score combined roughly multiplicatively for moderate-effect genes; however, for high-penetrance cancer genes, each unit of polygenic score raised the relative risk less in carriers, as reported for breast cancer in BRCA carriers [37], although it may still raise their absolute risk more [17].

This study has limitations. Electronic health records capture diagnoses when they are recorded, not when the disease begins. Some diseases in carrier trajectories reflect treatment rather than the variant itself. Absolute penetrance is calibrated to population registries; calibration to family-based estimates would require explicit correction for ascertainment. Penetrance estimated in population cohorts is generally lower and more variable than in clinically ascertained families [12, 14, 13], and depends on polygenic background [17]. Future work on joint modeling of laboratory measurements could link these trajectories to intermediate phenotypes and mechanisms.

A genetic result is more informative when read alongside age and the clinical record. A polygenic score is most informative early in life; a pathogenic variant indicates which diseases to expect and 10 when; and the record shows which individuals are already progressing toward them.

Frailty. We add one additional source of flexibility: a per-person frailty, which allows individuals to differ in their overall rate of disease without changing which diseases they have. Because θ i•t is a composition, nothing in λ i•t can express how much disease an individual has, only which diseases: two people with the same mix of diagnoses, one with twice as many, receive the same θ.

Without a separate rate term, a difference in overall event rate can be represented only by moving weight onto whichever components happen to carry higher (or lower) disease probabilities, so that composition absorbs rate. We therefore give each individual a single frailty f i , applied to the mixture probability after the sum over components: with π idt as in eq. ( 4).

Equivalently, an additive intercept on the complementary log-log scale and exp(f i ) multiplies the individual's cumulative hazard for every disease at every age; for small π idt , πidt ≈ e f i π idt .

The complementary form keeps πidt ∈ [0, 1] without truncation. Because f i enters after the mixture, ∂θ i•t /∂f i = 0: it changes an individual's overall rate but not their signature composition, and it carries no dependence on age or disease. The two terms are complementary rather than redundant: θ describes the pattern of an individual's disease and its evolution with age, f i its overall level.

A common shift f i → f i + c for all i is indistinguishable from raising the baseline µ dt for every disease and age, so only differences between individuals are identified. We remove the common shift by centering, fi = f i -N -1 j f j , in the forward pass, and place a Gaussian prior on the centered values. The objective is the negative log-likelihood of eq. ( 6) plus scaled by 1/N exactly as the data term is.

The frailties are initialized at zero and are not warmstarted from any earlier fit. In the model with the age interaction they are learned throughout backbone training, including the phase in which δ is held fixed (otherwise an individual's overall rate would be pushed into the genetic age interactions γ 1k that this phase exists to estimate), and their learning rate is reduced tenfold once δ is released, so that δ, with N × (K+1) × T parameters, cannot absorb the rate that f i carries. The level-only model has no δ-frozen phase and learns f i at a single rate throughout. The frailties are then held fixed while the carrier effects are estimated.

The fitted spread is sd( f ) = 0.44 for the age-interaction model and 0.53 for the level-only model, 14 against the prior σ f = 0.5.

Each gene is assigned one canonical disease in advance (the disease the gene is established to cause) by a fixed gene-to-disease map applied before any fit and matched to the phecode vocabulary by name (LDLR → hypercholesterolemia, TTN → heart failure, KCNQ1 → atrial fibrillation, BRCA1/2 → malignant neoplasm of female breast); the full map is Table S7. Of the 64 panel genes, 61 have at least one P/LP carrier in the UK Biobank callset (STK11, APOC3 and PROS1 have none), 42 carry a canonical-disease assignment and were attempted, and those with at least 30 carriers were fit.

Two atlases, two gene sets. The age-varying η(t) atlas covers 34 genes (Table S8) and the age-constant η atlas 32 (Table S7); 30 are common to both. They are not nested, because the two were assembled separately: KCNQ1, LMF1, LPL and SCN5A appear only in the age-varying atlas, while PKD1 and HFE appear only in the age-constant one, having been fit by a separate script under a gene-specific ascertainment: PKD1 assigned to the renal/urologic signature S12 with functional-mask rather than strict-ClinVar carriers, and HFE restricted to the 1,202 C282Y homozygotes and assigned to several signatures covering the hemochromatosis manifestations, since the broad 28,260-carrier P/LP set is heterozygote-diluted. Where the two atlases are compared, the comparison is restricted to the 30 shared genes.

For LDLR, BRCA1/2, MLH1, TTN and DSP the canonical disease is selected. Two commonvariant genes are included for contrast rather than as monogenic hypotheses: SERPINA1 (N = 25,827 P/LP carriers) and HFE (as above). Genes that drop out do so for lack of strict-ClinVar carriers, not for lack of effect (PCSK9, 3 carriers; UMOD, 1).

We used 36 polygenic scores from the UK Biobank 15 Polygenic Risk Score Release [29]. G i holds the 36 polygenic scores of ref. Because the scores are residualised on the principal components, the fitted loadings are adjusted for ancestry, whereas the empirical per-SD hazard ratios below are not (Computing the ratio, below).

N ≈ 400,000 for the polygenic analyses; the carrier analyses use the 200K exome subset.

Carriers. We used a list of 64 genes fixed before any fitting: monogenic disease genes, together with the common-variant negative controls named under Disease outcomes above. Carriers were identified in the UK Biobank [28] 200K whole-exome sequence release [31]. A variant counts only if ClinVar [30] records it as pathogenic or likely pathogenic (P/LP) at that exact coordinate, so a variant is never counted on its predicted consequence or its rarity alone, as it would be in a gene-level rare-variant burden test [40]. We took all ClinVar P/LP variants in the panel genes (57,765 positions for UK Biobank; MGB was queried against the 2026-08-04 release, 58,197 positions across the same 64 genes; Table 1) and matched exome variants to them by chromosome, position, reference and alternate allele (Supplementary Note 1). Anyone with at least one P/LP allele at a matched site is a carrier. This gives carrier lists for 61 of the 64 genes; the other three (STK11, APOC3 and PROS1 ) have no carrier at a matched site in the UK Biobank exome callset, which the cross-cohort comparison attributes to a sequence-quality filter rather than to absence in the population (Fig. S13a). For two genes the P/LP set is narrowed to the variant class or genotype established as pathogenic. For TTN, truncating variants are restricted to exons highly expressed in heart (hiPSI); this recovers the heart-failure and atrial-fibrillation signal, with the carrier-control difference in cumulative incidence at age 70 rising from -0.4 (heart failure) and -0.6 (atrial fibrillation) percentage points under an allele-frequency mask (MAF < 10 -4 , n = 1,673), to +4.3 and +4.9 under ClinVar P/LP (n = 593), and to +5.8 and +7.0 with the hiPSI restriction (n = 369). For HFE, we used C282Y homozygotes, the penetrant genotype. Carrier counts agreed with an independent functional mask (BRCA2 : 664 carriers called from the exome sequences against 649 under the mask).

Model fitting

Overview. All learned parameters (Table S1) are point estimates, obtained by gradient-based optimization of the log likelihood plus the log prior; estimation in 40 subsets of the data allows for confidence intervals around the parameter estimates. Fitting has two steps. First, the population model (signatures, polygenic effects, the health component and each individual's frailty) is fitted to all 400,000 UK Biobank participants. Second, carrier effects are fitted to carriers and matched non-carriers with the population model held fixed, so that the small carrier samples cannot alter 16 the signatures shared by the whole cohort. This is a modular, or 'cut', analysis [41, 42]: the carrier estimates do not carry the uncertainty of the population fit (Supplementary Note 1). Starting from a model without the age interaction, the full model is fitted in two phases. In the first, the per-person deviations δ are held at zero, so that age-dependent genetic signal is captured by the polygenic age effects γ 1 rather than by individual deviations. In the second, δ is estimated as well, starting from zero, while the signature parameters are fine-tuned. Participants are fitted in 40 batches of 10,000. For prediction, population parameters are averaged over the other 39 batches and held fixed, and each held-out individual's δ and frailty are refit from zero on that individual's records up to the prediction age (200 optimization steps).

Selecting diseases.

Because the inclusion indicator z mkd is binary, its contribution to the objective is not differentiable. We use the hard-concrete relaxation of Louizos et al. [43], which allows gradientbased fitting while still letting a gene-disease pair be switched off exactly; selection parameters start with almost every pair included (ω mkd = 0.96), and the penalty ν 0 removes those the data do not support (details in Supplementary Note 1).

Order of fitting for carrier effects.

A carrier effect raises one disease within a signature, whereas a per-person deviation δ raises every disease in that signature; the two are distinguished by this contrast across diseases. For age-varying carrier effects we fit the carrier effects first, with δ held at zero, and then estimate both, because δ can change freely with age and could otherwise absorb part of an age-varying carrier effect. For age-constant carrier effects, which δ cannot mimic in this way, δ is estimated first. The same reasoning motivates holding δ at zero while the polygenic age effects are fitted. Optimization settings are given in Supplementary Note 1.

Empirical PRS calibration.

A recalibration mapping is a transformation applied to the predicted probability after fitting, leaving every model parameter unchanged. The paper uses four;

To compare observed and predicted hazards within PRS strata, we 17 write π = σ(φ) with φ the model logit. Events are rare, so log π ≈ φ and log obs pred ≈ φ obs -φ pred ≡ ∆φ.

A discrepancy that is multiplicative on the probability scale is therefore additive on the logit. The c j are fit to calibration alone: observed against predicted annual hazard across 20 PRSpercentile bins. The top-versus-bottom PRS-quintile event-rate ratio Q 5 /Q 1 and the per-SD hazard ratio are held out of the fit. Because the two summaries average the data differently, Q 5 /Q 1 over pooled person-years and the per-SD ratio over per-person at-risk hazards, the bin means are formed the same way as the summary they are compared with; fit on the mismatched basis, the T1D per-SD ratio falls to about half its empirical value at age 45 while Q 5 /Q 1 is unaffected. The quadratic term is needed only for T1D, where ∆φ rises faster than linearly in z; for CAD and AF it is close to linear. Before that term is applied, z is clipped to the range spanned by the fitting data (any z outside that range is replaced by the nearer endpoint), so the correction is never extrapolated.

Per-window application.

Every window therefore has its own coefficient triple c j (a), j ∈ {0, 1, 2}.

The correction used in Fig. 1b applies each window's coefficients unsmoothed, which matches the within-stratum hazards more closely than the smoothed variant; a variant that smooths each coefficient across age with a low-order polynomial in a (degree ≤ 2) and evaluates it at each year removes the step at window boundaries and serves as a continuity check. Fitting and application use the same per-year at-risk hazards. The correction is applied to the predictions after the fact: no parameter of the model is re-estimated, the personal residual δ included.

Held-out refit of the calibration mapping. π is leave-one-out, so the predictions entering the recalibration are out-of-sample for each patient, and Fig. 1b shows the full-cohort recalibration.

Calibration of carrier penetrance to registries

Fitting to registry diagnoses. Hospital records capture cancer diagnoses incompletely, so for breast, colorectal and prostate cancer we estimated the carrier effect from diagnoses in the national 18 cancer registry linked to UK Biobank (ICD-10 C50, C18-C20 and C61). For carriers and matched non-carriers, the cancer outcome was replaced by the registry diagnosis, and the age-varying carrier effect η mkd (t) was fitted to these events with the population parameters held fixed (Order of fitting for carrier effects). Carrier penetrance by age a is averaged over the carrier set C, and is compared with the registry Kaplan-Meier estimate for the same carriers, with Greenwood 95% confidence intervals (Fig. 3d).

Because the carrier effect is estimated from these diagnoses, the comparison shows that the model can represent the registry penetrance curve and its shape with age. A carrier effect held constant in age cannot: tuned to match the registry at age 70, it over-predicts at age 60 (Supp. Fig. S6).

Interpreting penetrance.

The carrier effect η is on the log-odds scale within the assigned signature, so the same η can mean very different changes in probability depending on the baseline: where a disease is rare in the assigned signature, a large η is a small change in risk.

To compare genes we therefore report the carrier-control difference in cumulative incidence at age 70 (∆pen, percentage points), which is on the probability scale. How large an effect can be attained also depends on the assigned signature, because a carrier effect raises disease risk only in proportion to the carriers' loading on that signature. Interaction odds ratios are reported with 95% confidence intervals, not adjusted for multiple testing.

Before fitting, each gene was assigned the disease used to choose its signature and a clinical keyword describing its known phenotype (for example, hypercholesterolemia for LDLR, colon cancer for MLH1, cardiomyopathy for TTN ).

The target family for a gene was this disease, the diseases sharing its phecode to one decimal place, and any disease whose name contains the keyword. For the 30 ClinVar genes assigned to a single signature, we asked whether the model selected a target disease and whether a target was the strongest selected effect (largest carrier-control difference in cumulative incidence at age 70).

Because each gene's signature was chosen using its target disease, the main test is restricted to the diseases whose strongest signature is the gene's own: we compared the number of genes whose strongest selected effect was a target disease with the number expected if the selected diseases were ordered at random (Poisson-binomial test).

Carriers and polygenic risk matched on the primary disease. For each gene, we compared carriers with groups at high polygenic risk for the gene's primary disease (BRCA1, BRCA2 and PALB2 : breast cancer score; Lynch genes: colorectal cancer score; TTN : atrial fibrillation score), defined as the top 10%, 5%, 2%, 1% and 0.5% of the score. Each group was compared with everyone else in the same sex-restricted population, as carriers were with non-carriers, and the excess was the difference in the proportion diagnosed by age 70. We chose the polygenic group whose excess of the primary disease was closest to the carriers' and compared the two groups' excess of the gene's secondary disease (ovarian cancer, uterine cancer and cardiomyopathy, respectively). Where no polygenic group matched, we compared the secondary excess per percentage point of primary excess. Confidence intervals are from 300 bootstrap resamples within each group.

Observed check of selected pairs. For each of the 307 selected gene-disease pairs, we compared the proportion of carriers ever diagnosed with the disease with that of non-carriers matched 1:4 on 20 sex and five-year age group (two-sided Fisher exact test, Benjamini-Hochberg FDR across the 307 pairs). The check uses the same carriers as the selection, so an excess is a necessary condition rather than independent confirmation, and with few carriers a true effect may not reach significance.

Replication in All of Us and MGB

Replication of selected diseases. Cross-cohort agreement is reported as recall, the fraction of the reference cohort's selected gene-disease pairs the second cohort also selects, because All of Us selects more diseases per gene for reasons unrelated to agreement: partly greater power (3-5× the carriers), partly a spike component that is not κ-scaled. Jaccard would penalize the larger union even when every reference gene-disease pair was recovered. Chance is the recall a random selection of the same size would achieve, so selecting more diseases raises the baseline it is compared against, and we report the median enrichment, recall divided by chance, as the replication measure.

Because recall also falls as the reference selection grows, we additionally report recall of each gene's k strongest reference gene-disease pairs, ranked by the carrier-control penetrance difference |∆pen|, for k = 3 (the minimum reference selection size analysed) and k = 5; this fixes the reference size and so does not depend on how many gene-disease pairs either cohort selects. The carrier atlases were fit separately in each cohort.

Order of fitting for carrier effects. δ and η are told apart by how they act across diseases, and only by that: raising δ ik ⋆ t raises π idt for every disease in signature k ⋆ , raising η mk ⋆ d raises it for disease d alone, so at any one disease the two are interchangeable and what identifies η is the contrast between the selected disease and the other diseases in its signature. For an age-constant carrier effect we estimate δ first: Phase A estimates δ alone, with η and the selection indicator held contrast identifies η under either order, so this is a property of the optimization path and not of the model. The reversal for an age-varying carrier effect is on the grounds that an η(t) that varies with age is more easily mimicked by δ than a constant η is; the argument for that asymmetry, given that δ is estimated for carriers and controls alike in the age-constant Phase A too, is that δ ikt is free in age, so it can reproduce a smooth η(t) almost exactly, whereas a constant η has no age structure for δ to absorb and is pinned by the across-disease contrast alone. The asymmetry is in what δ can mimic, not in whom it is estimated for. Phase 1 of stage 2 holds δ at zero on the same argument: δ is held fixed whenever an age-varying genetic effect is being estimated, polygenic (γ 1 ) or monogenic (η(t)).

Gene-signature-disease combinations.

Gene m is scoped to a set of signatures, those with scope mk = 1, on which its carrier effect is allowed to act; within that set a per-disease selection indicator z mkd decides which of those diseases the carrier effect applies to. Both are defined in full below. A triple (m, k, d): one gene, one signature, one disease, with scope mk = 1 is a cell : the unit the selection indicator switches on or off, and the unit every count in the atlas refers to; triples outside a gene's scope have η mkd ≡ 0 and are not cells. A cell is selected (or open) when z mkd > 0.

Because a gene is usually scoped to one signature, its cells share a k and differ in d; a gene fit under more than one scope contributes cells at each.

Which signature a gene-disease pair is read on. Two different signatures are in play for every selected cell, and a model curve for the cell can be computed on either. The gene is scoped to k ⋆ , the signature its carriers load, and the carrier effect η mk ⋆ d (t) is indexed by k ⋆ . The disease d has its own home signature, home(d) = arg max k ψ kd , the signature that expresses it. For an off-signature cell the two differ. We always compute on home(d), and because home(d) depends on the disease 37 and not on the gene, the choice is made separately for each cell: one gene's selection indicators can open diseases homing to several signatures (BRCA2 scoped to S8 opens cells homing to S6, S16, S13, S3, S4 and S2), so this is a per-cell convention. It matters, and we keep home(d) even where the other signature agrees better with the observed curve. Where a disease is barely expressed in a signature (ψ kd strongly negative, so the predicted baseline there is near zero, σ(ϕ) ≈ 0.001), almost any η(t) is consistent with the carrier data and the fitted curve can follow the observed hazard closely; a close fit obtained that way reflects the absence of a constraint rather than agreement.

LDLR→hypercholesterolemia is the worked case: computed on S16 (ψ = -2.08) the fitted peak is 54 against an observed 49; computed on its home signature S14 (ψ = +0.28) it is 75. We report 75, the value that falls outside the bootstrap interval and requires the explanation below (Multi-modal curves and the peak-age summary). Close agreement on an off-home signature is therefore not evidence.

Multi-modal curves and the peak-age summary.

LDLR→hypercholesterolemia has its fitted onset peak at age 75 against an observed peak at 49, outside the bootstrap interval. Both curves are multi-modal, and they agree on where the modes are (model at ages 36, 54 and 75; data at 37, 49-56 and 71); they differ only in which mode is tallest, and a small change in relative amplitude flips the arg max by decades while the curve barely moves. Its r s of +0.67 is the highest of any LDLR configuration because the structure does correspond. Agreement between a fitted and an observed onset curve is therefore reported as r s , the Spearman rank correlation over ages 35-75, and not as agreement between peak ages; r s is stable across 50 independent matched-control draws.

Observed ovarian comparison and the breast signature.

For the observed ovarian comparison we used women in the model cohort (80 BRCA1 carriers, 313 BRCA2 carriers and 217,065 non-carriers) and computed Kaplan-Meier cumulative incidence and hazards in 10-year age bands. Risk-reducing surgery is not recorded, so carriers who had it remain in the risk set. With the agevarying healthy reference, women's baseline is no longer absorbed by the female cancer signature S18 (the ref. [21] solution), so the carrier excess acts through S19 directly. Sex-limited diseases. A disease is sex-limited when only one sex can develop it. Prevalence µ prev dt is computed once in preprocessing as the incidence among those still at risk (E id ≥ t), smoothed on the logit scale, with both sexes in the denominator. For a sex-limited disease that denominator therefore includes people who cannot develop the disease, so µ prev dt falls below the incidence among the sex at risk: women remain in the prostate cancer denominator. Training compensates through ψ kd and ϵ kdt , and sex is a component of G i , so it enters λ and hence θ: women have θ S13 ≈ 0 on the male signature while men load it at 0.88. The trained base model is therefore calibrated populationwide: predicted men's prostate cancer cumulative incidence is 6.3% against an observed 6.2% at age 70, so neither µ dt nor ϕ kdt needs any correction. Carrier penetrance is then reproduced by η(t) acting on these fixed, calibrated population parameters with δ estimated: for prostate cancer the control cumulative incidence at age 70 is 5.6% (6.8% in the cancer registry) with the carrier effect set to zero, and an estimated η(t) reproduces both the 2.3-fold higher cumulative incidence observed in carriers and its earlier onset.

Breast cancer is assigned the same way, to S19, the signature that expresses it, with µ d computed among women. With the age-varying healthy reference, women's baseline is no longer held in a female cancer signature, so carriers' excess acts through S19 directly and the observed carrier penetrance is within reach of η (Fig. 3).

Per-SD hazard ratios by age.

In each five-year window we regressed the window outcome (a first diagnosis during the window) on the standardized score z by ordinary least squares, giving slope β and mean outcome p, and converted the slope to a per-SD odds ratio, exp β/[p(1 -p)] , using the logistic derivative at p; for rare outcomes this approximates the per-SD hazard ratio. For the model curves the outcome is replaced by the participant's mean predicted yearly probability over the window, with the same windows, at-risk sets and score standardization. Restricting each window to participants still at risk removes most of the selection that comes from following people of different enrollment ages over different lengths of time; the mean score of the at-risk set changes by less than 0.1 standard deviation by age 75, so selection explains little of the decline.

Quintile event rates. As a check that needs neither the regression nor its conversion, we divided participants into score quintiles and computed, in each window, the fraction of those at risk with a 40 first diagnosis, for the observed data and for the model's predicted probabilities. The ratio between the top and bottom quintiles falls with age in both (Fig. 1c).

LDLR risk groups.

Ranking within five-year groups of enrollment age gives thirds with the same mean age (56.3-56.7 years). For each third we report the mean predicted and observed ten-year risk and a summary of the group (mean age, polygenic score percentile, number of prior diagnoses, and the prior diagnoses that differ most between the highest and lowest thirds). Supplementary Note 1: Fitting and implementation details

This note collects technical details of model fitting and interpretation that support the Methods.

Implementation. All fits use Adam (PyTorch 2.5.1, Python 3.9.21) at a constant learning rate of 0.1. The age-constant carrier effect is fit δ-first: 80 epochs on δ alone, then 150 epochs on {δ, v, ℓ} jointly. The age-varying carrier effect reverses the order, η-first: 150 epochs on {v, ℓ} with δ held at zero, then 300 epochs with δ free. Carriers are compared with non-carriers matched on sex and 5-year age bin at 2:1 throughout (Figs. 3, 6); the model-free Lynch panel (Fig. 4d) uses 1:4. The population fit of eq. ( 5) runs 100 epochs with δ held at zero followed by 200 with δ free. Learning rates are 0.1 for γ 0 throughout and for δ once it is freed; 0.1 for γ 1 in the first phase and 0.01 in the second; and 0.01 (one tenth) for ψ and ϵ in both. The curvature weight is ρ = 30, fixed a priori rather than tuned. The stability refits (Fig. 4c) are ten runs identical to the deployed fit in every respect except the random seed.

Modular (cut) estimation.

In a cut (or modularized) analysis the parameters of one module are estimated from part of the data and conditioned on as if known, so that information cannot flow back from the second module into the first [41]. Here the population module (ψ, ϵ, γ 0 , γ 1 , the health component's age curve and genetic effect, and each individual's frailty f i ) is estimated on the full cohort, and the carrier module (the selection indicator and the magnitude of η, with the per-person deviation δ re-estimated) is estimated with it fixed. The population module therefore reaches stage 3 only as a point value: nothing in the carrier sample returns to the population parameters or to f i , which is what makes this a cut, and nothing about them beyond the fitted values (their uncertainty, and their dependence on the cohort the folds were drawn from) reaches η (refs. [41, 42] discuss estimators of this kind). The stage-3 estimates are therefore not the mode of a posterior for any single joint model. Equations ( 4) and (5) remain a complete generative specification, and every quantity we report (penetrance curves, carrier-control contrasts, predicted hazards) is obtained by evaluating them forward at the fitted values. What the fit does not deliver is a posterior for those values to summarize.

Fitting the population model in two phases. Phase 1 holds δ at zero while γ 1 is learned, so the age interaction is what fits the age-dependent genetic signal; Phase 2 then estimates δ as well, starting it at zero. We write δ 0 for any fit in which δ starts at zero rather than from the nointeraction fit. Starting δ instead from the no-interaction fit (mean |δ| ≈ 0.175) lets δ fit the same age-varying signal that γ 1 is there to estimate, and the identifiability simulation that validated the two-phase approach used a δ ≈ 0 start. The signature log-odds shifts (ψ), the Gaussian-process residual (ϵ) and the level effect (γ 0 ) carry over from stage 1 (Table S2); they are fine-tuned rather than held fixed, so that the signature structure stays close to the stage-1 solution while γ 1 is learned.

The two-phase γ 1 is more stable across folds and is the one used for interpretation. For prediction, the population parameters are averaged over the other 39 batches and held fixed, and δ and f i are refit from zero for 200 Adam steps (learning rate 0.1), f i under its prior: a leave-one-out prediction for an individual uses only that individual's records up to the prediction age.
