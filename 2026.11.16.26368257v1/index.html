Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Effect Sizes in t-tests and F-tests works

The Effect Size module checks if effect sizes are correctly reported in t-tests and F-tests.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Preterm and low-birth-weight newborns account for most neonatal deaths in sub-Saharan Africa, and survival depends on the quality and timeliness of care delivered after admission as well as on access to a facility. Quality is usually measured at facility level, and patientlevel measures that combine early and later care processes are vulnerable to bias because newborns must survive in order to receive later care. We assessed whether better documented early care was associated with lower in-hospital mortality among preterm and low-birthweight newborns in eight facilities in Western Kenya. In this multicentre retrospective record review, 291 source newborn records yielded 287 unique records, of which 275 met eligibility criteria and 268 had a calculable early-care patient-level quality score. This study-defined score covered thermal protection, skilled attendance and clinical review, assessment and monitoring, and medicines and fluids, each item scored as not done, partially done or fully done, with not-applicable items excluded from denominators. In-hospital survival status was known for 224 newborns, of whom 57 (25.4%) died before discharge; newborns referred out or still admitted were not counted as survivors. Modified Poisson regression with robust variance estimated risk ratios. Documented early care was high (median 93.5%, interquartile range 83.3-100), while kangaroo mother care was the weakest domain (median 87.5%).

Early-care quality was not associated with in-hospital mortality after adjustment for gestational age, multiple pregnancy, birth location, facility and admission severity (adjusted risk ratio 1.03 per 10 percentage points, 95% confidence interval 0.82-1.29), and this null result persisted across nine sensitivity analyses. In contrast, continuing-care quality appeared strongly protective (0.81, 0.74-0.89), a pattern consistent with newborns surviving longer accruing more documented care rather than with a benefit of care itself.

Introduction

Preterm birth is the leading cause of death in children under five years of age. An estimated 13.4 million babies were born preterm in 2020, and preterm and small newborns account for the majority of the world's 2.3 million annual neonatal deaths, with sub-Saharan Africa and southern Asia bearing most of that burden [1, 2]. Kenya's neonatal mortality rate stands at 21 deaths per 1,000 live births, and complications of prematurity remain a leading contributor [3].

Survival for these newborns depends on more than reaching a health facility. The interventions that determine outcome -thermal protection from the moment of birth, skilled assessment, respiratory support, structured monitoring, and appropriate fluids and antimicrobials -must be delivered correctly and within a narrow time window after admission [4, 5]. Where they are delayed or omitted, admission to a facility confers less benefit than its availability implies. This is the reasoning behind the shift in global newborn policy from measuring access to measuring the quality of care actually received [6, 7].

Most quality measurement in low-and middle-income settings nevertheless describes facilities rather than newborns. Health facility assessments quantify whether equipment, medicines, guidelines and staff are present, and such assessments have been conducted at scale across East Africa [8, 9]. Structural readiness is necessary but not sufficient: a functional warmer does not establish that a given newborn was kept warm, and a facility scoring highly on oxygen availability may still fail to monitor saturation in an individual newborn. Patientlevel process measures, derived from what was documented in each newborn's own record, sit closer to the care that newborn actually received, and are the level at which Donabedian's process dimension of quality was originally defined [10].

Kenya has an unusually strong platform for such measurement. The KEMRI-coordinated Clinical Information Network links structured newborn admission records across hospitals to audit and feedback, and has generated large multisite analyses of newborn care and outcomes [11, 12, 13]. Yet published patient-level analyses have concentrated on individual processesadmission temperature, oxygen use, antibiotic prescribing -rather than on composite measures spanning the domains of early newborn care, and the relationship between documented composite process quality and in-hospital mortality among preterm and lowbirth-weight newborns has been examined less often [14].

There is a methodological reason for caution in such analyses that has received insufficient attention. A composite quality score computed over an entire admission necessarily includes processes that can only occur if the newborn survives long enough to receive them: daily weight monitoring, sustained kangaroo mother care, feeding progression, discharge counselling and a discharge summary. A newborn who dies six hours after admission cannot accrue these items and will score poorly, not because care was deficient but because the opportunity to deliver and document it did not arise. Any association between such a score and survival is therefore partly structural. This is the same class of problem as immortal time bias in pharmacoepidemiology, in which exposure classification depends on surviving a period during which the outcome could occur [15, 16]. Restricting the exposure to care processes that should occur during initial assessment and stabilisation is a direct, prespecified way of reducing it.

We therefore conducted a multicentre retrospective review of newborn clinical records from health facilities in Western Kenya to determine whether higher documented early patientlevel quality of care was associated with lower in-hospital mortality among preterm and lowbirth-weight newborns. We hypothesised that higher early-care quality would be associated with lower mortality. Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This was a multicentre retrospective review of individual newborn clinical records, conducted in health facilities in Bungoma County, Western Kenya. Bungoma County operates a tiered public health system in which health centres and sub-county hospitals provide maternity services and stabilise and refer newborns requiring more than basic care, while the county referral hospital provides inpatient newborn care and receives referrals from across the county. Newborn records were abstracted from nine facilities; eligible preterm or low-birthweight newborns were admitted at eight of them: one level 5 county referral hospital, six level 4 hospitals and one level 3 health centre. Reporting follows the STROBE statement for observational studies [17]; the completed checklist is provided as S1 Checklist.

Data source and study population

Data were abstracted between 6 May and 26 June 2026 from newborn clinical records, admission registers, monitoring charts, treatment sheets and discharge or death summaries, using a structured instrument administered on tablets by trained research assistants. The instrument recorded maternal and obstetric history, newborn characteristics at birth and admission, condition at admission, care processes during admission, complications, and outcome. Eligibility criteria

Newborns were eligible if they met at least one of the following: recorded gestational age below 37 completed weeks; a recorded preterm classification; birth weight below 2,500 g; or a recorded low-birth-weight classification. Records were excluded if they were confirmed duplicates not selected as the primary record, or if they contained neither usable gestationalage or preterm information nor usable birth-weight or low-birth-weight information, so that eligibility could not be determined. Records were not excluded solely because covariates were incomplete, provided the primary exposure and outcome were valid.

Data management and quality assurance

Before analysis the dataset underwent independent cleaning, deduplication and validation.

Each raw worksheet was reconciled cell-by-cell against the original electronic export; facility names, which were recorded as free text, were standardised using a crosswalk mapping every observed alias to a standard facility name and level; potential duplicates were identified using facility, record identifier, dates and newborn characteristics and classified as confirmed duplicates, documented multiple births or distinct records. Within confirmed duplicate groups the record with the highest weighted completeness was retained and singular non-conflicting values were recovered from complementary records; contradictory values were never merged. Documented multiple births were retained as separate newborns.

Patient-level quality-of-care assessment

Care processes were assessed using a study-defined patient-level quality score (PLQS) based on prespecified clinical-care indicators drawn from national and World Health Organization 8. http://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-nc-nd/4.0/ It is made available under a perpetuity. is the author/funder, who has granted medRxiv a license to display the preprint in (which was not certified by peer review) preprint The copyright holder for this this version posted November 19, 2026. ; https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.11.16.26368257 doi: medRxiv preprint guidance for the care of small and sick newborns.

The score comprises 31 indicators grouped into eight domains: thermal protection; skilled attendance and clinical review; assessment and monitoring; medicines and fluids; feeding and nutrition; kangaroo mother care; infection prevention and control; and referral, escalation and continuity of care. Each indicator was scored 0 (not done or not documented), 1 (partially done, delayed or inconsistently documented) or 2 (fully and appropriately done and documented), with an explicit notapplicable option for indicators that did not apply to a given newborn. Domain and composite scores were calculated as the sum of observed applicable item scores divided by the maximum achievable score for those applicable items, multiplied by 100. Notapplicable responses were excluded from denominators and were never recoded to zero. No PLQS item score was statistically completed at any stage. The PLQS is a study-defined measure constructed for this study; it has not undergone formal external psychometric validation and is not described as validated.

Early-care PLQS: the primary exposure

The primary exposure was the early-care PLQS, calculated over the four domains comprising care processes that should reasonably occur during initial assessment and stabilisation: thermal protection, skilled attendance and clinical review, assessment and monitoring, and medicines and fluids. Items dependent on surviving long enough to receive later care were excluded by design, specifically daily weight and feeding monitoring, sustained kangaroo mother care documentation, discharge planning, discharge summaries and feeding at discharge. We verified that no discharge-dependent or transition-dependent indicator contributed to the primary exposure. The early-care PLQS was analysed as a continuous score from 0 to 100, modelled per 10-percentage-point increment, and secondarily in categories defined by the observed distribution.

Outcome definitions

The primary outcome was in-hospital death, defined as death before discharge (coded 1) compared with discharge alive (coded 0). Newborns referred out of the facility, still admitted at the time of abstraction, or with no recorded disposition were not classified as survivors and were excluded from the primary mortality analysis, because their final survival status could not be verified.

Secondary outcomes were: any complication documented during admission; sepsis during admission; hypothermia documented at admission; discharge alive; length of stay in days; weight change in grams between the baseline weight and discharge weight; average daily weight gain; human-milk feeding at discharge, defined as exclusive breastfeeding or expressed breast milk against other feeding modes; and referral out of the facility. All outcome definitions were taken from the audited dataset and no clinical outcome was inferred from absence of documentation.

Gestational age was used in the primary adjustment set; birth-weight category replaced it in a prespecified sensitivity model, and the two were not entered together.

Analysis populations

Four populations were prespecified. The quality-of-care cohort comprised eligible newborns with a calculable early-care PLQS. The mortality cohort comprised those with a definitively known in-hospital survival status. The discharged-survivor cohort comprised newborns discharged alive, and was used for length of stay, weight change, average daily weight gain and feeding at discharge. The 24-hour survivor cohort excluded newborns who died within 24 hours of admission where valid admission and outcome times permitted this determination, and was used for sensitivity analysis.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are summarised as mean with standard deviation where approximately symmetric and as median with interquartile range where skewed; categorical variables as counts and percentages. Missing data

Missingness in outcomes and PLQS item scores was never statistically completed. Covariates not recorded in the source records were completed once during the independent data audit using regression-based completion models; in the mortality cohort these were maternal age (49 of 224 records), antenatal visits (69), multiple pregnancy (13), 5-minute Apgar score (25), hypothermia at admission (14), hypoglycaemia at admission (24), respiratory distress at admission (8) and suspected sepsis at admission (8). The primary analysis used these completed covariate values. Gestational age, which the audit did not complete, was missing in 3 of 224 records (1.3%); it was imputed 20 times by chained equations, drawing regression parameters from their approximate posterior distribution and including the exposure, all model covariates and the outcome, and estimates were pooled using Rubin's rules [19]. A complete-case analysis for gestational age is reported alongside. Two further analyses examined the influence of covariate completion: a complete-case analysis restricted to newborns with every Model 3 covariate recorded in the source records, and pooling across the 20 completed covariate datasets supplied with the audited data package. Source-record missingness for every analytical variable is given in S1 Table.

Sensitivity and bias-control analyses

Five prespecified bias-control analyses were conducted. First, the early-care PLQS rather than the overall PLQS served as the primary mortality exposure. Second, we confirmed that no discharge-dependent or transition item contributed to that exposure. Third, the primary model was repeated among 24-hour survivors. Fourth, the analysis was restricted to newborns 13 only for duplicate resolution and the derivation of time intervals, and were removed from the analytical and shared datasets. Data were stored on password-protected devices accessible only to the study team, results are reported in aggregate, and the record-level dataset shared with this article is de-identified (see Data availability). MMUST-ISERC waived the requirement for individual informed consent for this retrospective review of routinely collected records; abstraction was conducted with the permission of the facility in-charges.

Cohort derivation

Interpreting the null association between early care and mortality

A null finding here should not be read as evidence that quality of care does not matter for preterm survival. Three explanations are more plausible and are not mutually exclusive. First, the exposure had little room to vary. The early-care score was strongly left-skewed, with 37.9% of newborns scoring exactly 100% and an interquartile range of 83.3-100. Where 30 . a process measure approaches its ceiling in most newborns, the contrast available to detect an association is small and largely confined to the tail of the distribution. Documented adherence to the early-care processes captured here was, in these facilities, close to complete for most newborns; the variation that remained may reflect documentation practice as much as care delivery.

Second, the score measures whether prespecified processes were documented, not whether they were effective in the individual newborn; the general limitations of routine facility data for newborn indicators are well established [14, 31]. An indicator scored 2 for appropriate CPAP management records that CPAP was initiated and managed according to protocol, not that the newborn's respiratory failure was reversed. The interventions most likely to alter survival for a newborn of 27 weeks -surfactant and mechanical ventilation -were not available in these facilities and therefore could not be scored at all. A process score constructed from the care that is available cannot capture the effect of the care that is absent.

Third, confounding by indication runs in the opposite direction to the hypothesis. Sicker newborns attract more intensive documented care: they are more likely to receive CPAP, oxygen, antibiotics and frequent monitoring, and each of these generates a scoreable item.

Newborns with the highest documented early care therefore include a disproportionate number of the most compromised. The non-monotonic category pattern we observed, in which adjusted risk was highest in the second-highest score category, is consistent with this. Adjustment for gestational age, Apgar score, hypothermia and suspected sepsis reduces but almost certainly does not eliminate this confounding, because the intensity of illness within a given gestational-age and admission-condition stratum is not fully captured by these variables.

Opportunity-to-receive-care bias and the choice of exposure

The contrast between the null early-care result and the apparently strong continuing-care result deserves emphasis because it has direct implications for how newborn quality-of-care research is conducted. Sustained kangaroo mother care, daily weight monitoring, feed tolerance documentation and a completed discharge summary can only accrue to a newborn who survives long enough. Where such items enter a composite exposure, the score partly measures survival time, and its association with mortality is partly tautological. Our data quantify this: an exposure that included these items produced an adjusted risk ratio of 0.81 per 10 points, while an exposure restricted to stabilisation-phase processes produced 1.03 in the same 224 newborns with identical adjustment. This is structurally the same problem as immortal time bias, in which exposure classification depends on surviving a period during which the outcome could occur, and which has been shown to generate substantial spurious protective associations in observational pharmacoepidemiology [15, 16]. To our knowledge it has been less often addressed in facility-based newborn quality research, where composite adherence scores spanning the whole admission are common. Restricting the exposure to early-care processes, as we prespecified, is a simple and transparent partial remedy; excluding deaths in the first 24 hours, which we also did, addresses a related but distinct component. Studies reporting protective associations between whole-admission quality scores and neonatal survival should state explicitly which items could only be delivered to survivors.

Two early-care indicators also stood out. Placing the newborn on an individual cot, warmer or incubator was fully documented in 68.7% of records, with 11.5% scoring zero, indicating that equipment sharing remains routine; this matters because hypothermia at admission was present in 31.7% of our cohort and was independently associated with an 80% higher risk of death, consistent with the association between admission hypothermia and mortality reported across the Kenyan Clinical Information Network [24]. Appropriate CPAP initiation and management was fully documented in 66.3% of applicable records, the weakest early-care indicator, in a setting where regional assessments have found CPAP readiness at around 46% of the achievable maximum in Kenyan neonatal units [8]; trial evidence indicates that investment in oxygen systems rather than devices alone improves outcomes for children and neonates in comparable settings [29]. Vitamin K administration was not documented at all in 19.2% of records, a low-cost omission with a straightforward remedy.

Secondary outcomes and their interpretation

Three inverse secondary associations require careful reading rather than acceptance. The association between higher early-care PLQS and lower documented sepsis (adjusted risk ratio 0.59 per 10 points) is not readily interpretable as a benefit of care, because both the exposure 33 . and the outcome derive from the same clinical record: a newborn with sparse documentation will tend to have both a lower score and fewer documented complications. The association with lower referral out of the facility (0.68) is more plausibly explained by capabilityfacilities documenting more complete early care are those able to retain and manage newborns rather than transfer them. The small inverse association with human-milk feeding at discharge (0.96) is of uncertain significance among ten exploratory outcomes and, with 91.4% of survivors receiving human milk, has little room to be clinically meaningful. None of these should be presented as evidence that better care produces worse feeding or that care prevents sepsis.

Comparison with existing evidence

In-hospital mortality of 25.4% among preterm and low-birth-weight newborns in our cohort is high but within the range reported for Kenyan newborn units caring for comparable populations. A multisite retrospective cohort across the Kenyan Clinical Information Network reported substantial between-hospital variation in neonatal inpatient mortality after case-mix adjustment [11], and earlier assessments of neonatal care in Kenyan clinical training facilities documented widespread deficits in essential newborn processes [12]. Our cohort was more severely selected than most: 32.1% were born before 32 weeks and 26.8% weighed under 1,500 g. Kenya's national progress in reducing under-five mortality has been driven substantially by factors outside inpatient newborn care [36], and facility-based perinatal death review remains inconsistently translated into change across the region [37].

The direction of our principal finding differs from analyses of survey-based newborn care indicators.

An analysis of the 2022 Kenya Demographic and Health Survey found that only 32.7% of mothers reported their newborn receiving all components of quality newborn care, and interpreted low coverage as a driver of neonatal mortality [25]. Health-system and clinical implications

Three implications follow. First, quality-improvement effort in these facilities should target the indicators that were genuinely deficient -sustained kangaroo mother care, individual thermal support, CPAP management and vitamin K -rather than the composite score, which is already high and would move little. Second, admission hypothermia deserves priority as a modifiable admission-state exposure independently associated with mortality in our data and in larger Kenyan series [24]; it is addressable through thermal capability at the point of admission and during transfer. Newborns transferred between facilities are a particular concern, since neonatal transport across sub-Saharan Africa is poorly resourced and newborns frequently arrive compromised [35].

For research, the implication is that composite quality exposures should be constructed with explicit attention to when each process can occur relative to the outcome. Reporting both an early-care and a whole-admission version, as we have done, makes the magnitude of the bias visible to readers rather than leaving it embedded in the estimate. Structured newborn record platforms of the kind operating in Kenya make such analyses feasible at scale [30, 32, 33], and standardised measurement definitions for newborn indicators would make them comparable across settings [34].

Strengths

The study has several strengths. It used an independently audited multicentre newborn dataset in which every raw worksheet was reconciled cell-by-cell against the source export before analysis. It measured quality at the level of the individual newborn across eight care domains and 31 indicators rather than relying on facility structural measures. The primary exposure was prespecified to exclude survival-dependent items, and this decision was tested rather than assumed, by fitting the alternative exposures and reporting the contrast. Relative risks were estimated directly by modified Poisson regression rather than approximated by odds ratios.

Nine sensitivity and bias-control analyses, including complete-case, 24-hour survivor and stratified analyses, were prespecified and all are reported. Newborns whose final outcome could not be verified were excluded from the mortality analysis rather than being counted as survivors.

Limitations

Thirteen limitations qualify these findings. First, the design is a retrospective record review; the exposure and covariates were recorded for clinical rather than research purposes. Second, the PLQS measures documented care, so care delivered but not recorded is scored as not delivered; documentation quality and care quality cannot be separated in these data. Third, and conversely, missing documentation for some indicators may be misclassified as incomplete care. Fourth, residual confounding by disease severity is likely: gestational age, Apgar score, hypothermia and suspected sepsis do not capture the full spectrum of illness intensity within strata. Fifth, confounding by indication operates specifically against the hypothesis, since sicker newborns attract more documented intervention. Sixth, the sample of 268 newborns with 57 deaths limits precision, and the confidence interval around the primary estimate remains compatible with a modest association in either direction. Seventh, newborn sex was not recorded in the abstraction instrument and could not be adjusted for. Eighth, newborns were clustered within eight facilities, and sparse strata required facility to be modelled as a binary term rather than as individual fixed effects. Ninth, gestational age was derived predominantly from last menstrual period rather than early ultrasound and is subject to measurement error. Tenth, 44 newborns referred out or still admitted had no verifiable final outcome and were excluded, which would bias the mortality estimate if their outcomes differed systematically. Eleventh, the continuing-care components of the PLQS remain vulnerable to survivorship and opportunity bias, which is precisely why the early-care score was prespecified as primary and why the continuing-care result is not interpreted causally.

Twelfth, the PLQS is study-defined and has not undergone external psychometric validation.

Thirteenth, this is an observational analysis and the associations reported do not establish causation in either direction.

Conclusion

Among preterm and low-birth-weight newborns admitted to eight facilities in Western Kenya, documented early patient-level quality of care was high, with a median early-care score of 93.5%, but was not associated with in-hospital mortality after adjustment for maturity, birth location, facility and condition at admission, and this null result was robust across nine sensitivity and bias-control analyses. Mortality was instead dominated by gestational age and by condition at presentation, particularly respiratory distress and hypothermia at admission. Specific indicators nonetheless require strengthening: sustained kangaroo mother care, individual thermal support rather than shared equipment, appropriate CPAP management and vitamin K administration were the weakest processes in these records. The marked difference between the null early-care result and the apparently protective association obtained when survival-dependent items were included in the same models indicates that composite quality scores spanning an entire admission can generate substantial spurious protective associations, and that quality-of-care research in this field should prespecify exposures that could reasonably have been delivered before the outcome occurred. These findings describe eight purposively assessed facilities and should not be generalised to newborn care in Kenya as a whole.
