Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Full text

Abstract

Endometrial cancer exhibits substantial molecular and immune heterogeneity that is incompletely captured by established genomic and histopathological descriptors. We investigated whether paired tumor-blood T-cell receptor β (TCRβ) profiling could provide a complementary view of adaptive immune organization by jointly resolving intratumoral repertoire structure, abundance-dependent peripheral detectability, and genomic correlates.

TCRβ repertoires were profiled in paired tumor biopsies and peripheral-blood mononuclear cells from 13 patients with molecularly characterized endometrial cancer. Clonotypes were defined by exact CDR3 amino-acid identity, and repertoire differences were evaluated at observed sequencing depth and under multiple depth-and coverage-standardization procedures. Tumor-blood sharing was assessed after 500 pair-specific matched-depth resamplings.

As expected, tumor biopsies contained fewer clonotypes than matched blood (13/13 patients); this difference was preserved across depth-and coverage-standardization procedures, indicating that this compartmental difference was not explained by sequencing depth alone. Peripheral detectability increased with intratumoral abundance, with the odds of blood detection rising 2.44-fold per tenfold increase in tumor frequency. Among shared clonotypes, 66.7% were at least twofold more frequent in tumor versus 8.7% in blood, consistent with preferential intratumoral enrichment. In a prespecified 16-test immune-genomic analysis, higher tumor mutational burden was strongly associated with lower intratumoral Hill D1 (Spearman ρ=-0.833; BH q=0.0115; max-|ρ| FWER P=0.0088), with consistent leave-one-subject-out direction and persistence after excluding hypermutated tumors. No histopathological endpoint showed a multiplicity-corrected association.

Together, these findings define a quantitative tumor-blood TCRβ architecture in endometrial cancer and support repertoire profiling as a complementary functional layer for future immunogenomic stratification and validation.

Paired tumor and blood TCRβ profiling revealed that clonotypes abundant in endometrial tumors were more often detectable in blood and that shared clonotypes were preferentially enriched in tumor tissue. Intratumoral effective diversity was lower with increasing tumor mutational burden, supporting TCR repertoire profiling as a complementary layer for immunogenomic characterization.

Introduction

Endometrial cancer is one of the most common gynaecologic malignancies worldwide, with more than 69,000 new cases annually in the United States alone 1 , and is characterized by substantial molecular and immunological heterogeneity. The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) classification identified four biologically distinct groups, POLymerase Epsilon (POLE) ultramutated, microsatellite instability (MSI)/hypermutated, copy-number low, and copy-number high, which differ in genomic architecture, prognosis, and immune contexture 2 . POLE-mutated and mismatchrepair-deficient (MMRd) tumors typically show high tumor mutational burden (TMB) 3 , increased predicted neoantigen load, and prominent lymphocytic infiltration 4, 5 , linking tumor genomics to adaptive immunity. Genomic features alone, however, do not fully account for the organization of the antitumor immune response: immune phenotype and response to immune-checkpoint blockade vary substantially among tumors of the same molecular class 5, 6 , T-cell recognition also depends on neoantigen clonality, intratumoral heterogeneity, and antigen presentation 7, 8 , and tumors within one molecular subgroup can differ markedly in the extent and spatial pattern of immune infiltration [9] [10] [11].

T-cell receptor (TCR) repertoire profiling offers a complementary view. The composition and abundance distribution of TCR clonotypes reflect the population structure of the T-cell response, and comparison of tumor and peripheral blood identifies clonotypes represented in both compartments 12 . Such measurements are not direct evidence of tumor-antigen specificity, but across cancer types they have been linked to mutational and neoantigen landscapes, tumor immune states, and response to immune-checkpoint blockade 12, 13.

In endometrial cancer, direct evidence remains limited. An intratumoral TCRβ clonality index was associated with immune gene expression and longer progression-free survival in 32 endometrioid tumors, but blood was not examined 14 . In paired tumor and blood repertoires from 39 patients with gynecologic cancer, eight of them endometrial, clonotypes found at several tumor sites were preferentially represented in blood 15 ; tumor-matched clonotypes have also been followed in the blood of patients with endometrial cancer during pembrolizumab treatment 16 and years after curative treatment of POLE-mutant disease 17 , and single-cell studies across cancer types show that clonotypes expanded in tumors can typically be detected in blood 18 . None of these studies quantified how peripheral detectability varies with a clonotype's abundance within a single tumor or controlled for sequencing depth and sampling coverage. Whether intratumoral abundance itself governs peripheral detectability in endometrial cancer, and in which direction shared clonotypes are enriched, therefore remains open.

Here, using paired tumor and peripheral-blood TCRβ repertoires from an endometrial-only cohort, we verified that compartmental differences in repertoire breadth were robust to sampling standardization, quantified peripheral detectability as a continuous function of intratumoral clonotype abundance, assessed the direction of the tumor-to-blood abundance asymmetry among shared clonotypes, and tested the association of prespecified repertoire features with tumor genomic and histopathological characteristics.

Results

Tumor-biopsy repertoires show reduced observed breadth with preserved organization of dominant clonotypes

We analyzed paired tumor-biopsy and peripheral-blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) TCRβ repertoires from 13 patients with endometrial cancer. Median age was 65 years (IQR, 61-70; range, 54-73). Eleven tumors were classified as endometrioid carcinoma (84.6%), including one reported as endometrioid carcinoma not otherwise specified 19 , and two (15.4%) were clear-cell carcinomas.

Among the 11 endometrioid carcinomas, seven were histological grade 1 (G1), three grade 2 (G2), and one grade 3 (G3), according to the International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) grading system 20 . Myometrial invasion exceeded 50% of the myometrial thickness in eight of 12 evaluable tumors (66.7%), whereas lymphovascular space invasion (LVSI) was identified in two of 11 evaluable cases (18.2%). Molecular classification assigned two tumors to the POLEmutant class (POLE p.Pro286Arg and p.Ser459Phe), two to the mismatch-repair-deficient class (MSI-High), two to the p53-abnormal class (TP53 p.Ser241Phe and p.Ala159Asp) and seven to the class with no specific molecular profile (NSMP); one POLE-mutant tumor also carried a subclonal TP53 nonsense mutation and was classified as POLE-mutant according to the hierarchy. The clinicopathological and molecular characteristics of the cohort are summarized in Table 1 and Supplementary Data 1.

Across the 13 matched tumor-blood pairs, we characterized 26 TCRβ repertoires. Clonotypes were defined by exact amino acid sequence identity in the complementarity-determining region 3 (CDR3), and their abundance was quantified using read counts. Repertoire architecture was summarized by observed clonotype richness, Hill diversity of order 1 (D 1 ; exponential of Shannon entropy) and order 2 (D 2 , inverse Simpson index), and the cumulative relative abundance of the ten most abundant clonotypes (Top-10) 12, 21 . Sequencing depth and Good's coverage were reported as read-based sampling descriptors. Paired compartments were compared with the two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test across the 13 pairs. Repertoire-level measurements and sampling diagnostics are provided in Supplementary Data 2.

Biopsy repertoires contained fewer observed clonotypes than their matched PBMC repertoires in all 13 patients (P = 0.0002). Median observed richness was 4,282 clonotypes (IQR, 2,803-5,234) in biopsies, compared with 19,424 (IQR, 13,061-55,500) in PBMC (Figure 1A). Sequencing depth varied by more than two orders of magnitude across biopsies (median 494,200 reads; IQR, 235,971-657,278; range, 6,194-722,423) and 6.6-fold across PBMC samples (median 548,518; IQR, 352,123-707,074; range, 194,746-1,283,973) but did not differ systematically between compartments (P = 0.13). Good's coverage was nominally higher in biopsies (median 0.9998 versus 0.9979 in PBMC), reflecting the smaller number of observed clonotypes and the correspondingly lower singleton fraction rather than more complete recovery of the underlying repertoire. Depth and coverage are shown in Supplementary Figure S1A-B.

The compartmental difference in repertoire breadth persisted after controlling for differences in sequencing depth and sampling coverage. Biopsy richness was lower in all five pairs in which the biopsy yielded more reads than the matched PBMC sample (Supplementary Figure S1C). When the deeper repertoire of each pair was subsampled without replacement to the depth of the shallower member, biopsy richness remained lower in all 13 pairs (P = 0.0002). Rarefying the cohort to a common depth of 63,199 reads (n = 11 pairs, after excluding the two biopsies of lowest depth) and, separately, standardizing all 26 repertoires to a common Good's coverage of 0.838 gave the same result, with biopsy richness lower in 11 of 11 and 13 of 13 pairs, respectively (P = 0.001 and P = 0.0002). Chao1 richness was likewise lower in biopsies in all 13 pairs (P = 0.0002). Rarefaction curves and depth-standardized paired comparisons are shown in Supplementary Figure S1D-E, and all standardized analyses are tabulated in Supplementary Data 3. Within biopsies, observed richness was positively associated with sequencing depth (Spearman ρ = 0.75, P = 0.003), whereas no such association was present in PBMC (ρ = -0.08, P = 0.80). This association persisted after rarefaction to a common depth (ρ = 0.73, P = 0.011), indicating that it does not reflect differential sampling effort. It is instead consistent with a shared dependence of both read yield and clonotype number on the amount of T-cell material recovered from the tissue.

The compartmental difference extended to Hill D 1 , the effective number of clonotypes corresponding to Shannon diversity. Median D 1 was 1,012 (IQR, 275-1,847) in biopsies and 3,002 (IQR, 1,171-6,045) in PBMC, with lower biopsy D 1 in 12 of 13 patients (P = 0.0007; Figure 1B); the single exception was the pair with the lowest PBMC richness in the cohort (6,566 clonotypes).

Lower biopsy D 1 was recovered in 11 of 13 pairs after per-pair rarefaction (P = 0.002), in 10 of 11 pairs at the common depth of 63,199 reads (P = 0.005), and in 12 of 13 pairs after coverage standardization (P = 0.001). The reduction in observed richness was therefore accompanied by lower Shannon-order effective diversity in most patients, and this held under every standardization applied.

In contrast, measures emphasizing the most abundant clonotypes showed no consistent paired direction and, if anything, pointed towards lower rather than higher dominance in biopsies. Median Hill D 2 was 124.2 (IQR, 47.1-288.5) in biopsies and 111.3 (IQR, 42.5-265.9) in PBMC, with lower biopsy D 2 in six pairs and higher D 2 in seven (P = 0.95; Figure 1C). The ten most abundant clonotypes accounted for a median of 20.3% of biopsy reads (IQR, 13.0-30.2%) and 22.0% of PBMC reads (IQR, 14.5-35.2%), with a higher biopsy Top-10 frequency in six of 13 pairs (P = 0.79; Figure 1D). Neither the Gini coefficient over observed clonotype frequencies (P = 0.69) nor the fraction of clonotypes required to reach 50% of reads (P = 1.00) differed between compartments, and both D 2 and Top-10 remained without consistent direction under every depthand coverage-standardized analysis (P ≥ 0.57).

Top-100 cumulative frequency showed a nominal paired difference between compartments, being higher in biopsies in 10 of 13 pairs (median 38.2% versus 30.6%; P = 0.040). Together, these observations locate the compartmental difference in the breadth of the rarer fraction of the repertoire rather than in the organization of its dominant clonotypes.

Peripheral detection of intratumoral clonotypes scales with their abundance, and shared clonotypes show intratumoral enrichment

Having established differences in repertoire architecture between compartments, we next examined which components of the tumor-biopsy repertoire were also detectable in matched peripheral blood.

Sharing was defined by exact TCRβ CDR3 amino-acid identity and evaluated both in the observed repertoires and after 500 pair-specific matched-depth resamplings. Standardized cohort summaries were calculated across patient-specific resampling medians.

After depth matching, a median of 9.2% of biopsy clonotypes were also detected in the corresponding PBMC repertoire (IQR, 6.8-24.5%). These shared clonotypes accounted for a larger fraction of biopsy read abundance, with a median of 20.0% (IQR, 11.3-37.0%; Figure 2A). In the reverse comparison, a median of 1.4% of PBMC clonotypes were detected in the biopsy (IQR, 0.7-3.1%), representing 24.9% of PBMC read abundance (IQR, 6.0-36.5%). These directional fractions describe sharing relative to the repertoire of each compartment and do not imply a direction of cellular trafficking.

Despite the contribution of shared clonotypes to read abundance, overall repertoire similarity remained limited. The median matched-depth Jaccard index was 0.0123 (IQR, 0.0068-0.0242) and the median Morisita-Horn index was 0.0257(IQR, 0.0196-0.0493). Raw and standardized cohort medians were closely aligned, with biopsy clonotype sharing changing from 9.1% to 9.2% and shared biopsy read abundance remaining approximately 20.0%. This cohort-level agreement did not imply that individual estimates were unaffected by downsampling; patient-specific raw-versusstandardized comparisons are shown in Supplementary Figure S2, with the corresponding numerical summaries provided in Supplementary Data 4.

The greater contribution of shared clonotypes to biopsy read abundance prompted an examination of peripheral-blood detection across the intratumoral abundance distribution. Biopsy clonotypes were ranked by read abundance and grouped into non-overlapping percentile strata. After matcheddepth standardization, median PBMC detection declined across these strata, from 35.9% among the most abundant 1% of biopsy clonotypes to 18.2% in the 1-5% stratum, 15.7% in the 5-10% stratum, 11.9% in the 10-25% stratum, 8.4% in the 25-50% stratum, and 7.0% in the leastabundant half of the repertoire (Figure 2B). Percentile boundaries refer to the number of distinct clonotypes ranked by biopsy abundance, rather than to cumulative fractions of reads. The gradient was directionally consistent within patients, with a negative rank correlation between stratum and PBMC detection in 12 of 13 patients (median Spearman ρ = -0.83), although a strictly monotonic decline across all six strata was observed in five. This contrast was also evident within patients: the PBMC detection fraction among the most abundant 1% of biopsy clonotypes exceeded that among the least-abundant 50% in 12 of 13 pairs, both before and after depth matching (two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank P = 0.0005 in both cases; Figure 2C). Thus, the cohort-level abundance gradient was accompanied by a consistent withinpatient difference between the two ends of the biopsy abundance distribution. Its persistence after pair-specific depth matching indicates that the pattern was retained under balanced read sampling within pairs, although this procedure does not eliminate differences in sampling completeness or other technical sources of variation between patients.

Because this gradient is a property of individual clonotypes rather than of patient-level summaries, we modelled peripheral detection directly at the clonotype level. Across 62,170 biopsy clonotypes, the odds that a clonotype was also detected in matched blood increased 2.44-fold for each tenfold increase in its intratumoral frequency (95% CI, 2.12-2.80), estimated within patients using patient fixed effects with a cluster-robust variance across the 13 patients. Fitted separately in each patient, the slope was positive in 12 of 13, with a median odds ratio of 2.33 per tenfold increase (Wilcoxon signed-rank P = 0.0007); the single exception was the patient with the lowest-depth biopsy.

Peripheral detectability of an intratumoral clonotype is therefore a graded function of its local abundance rather than a property of an arbitrarily defined dominant fraction (Figure 2D). Shared clonotypes were not merely present in both compartments but were systematically more abundant in the tumor. Among clonotypes detected in both, a median of 66.7% were at least twofold more frequent in the biopsy than in matched blood (range, 21.2-93.5%), compared with a median of 8.7% that were at least twofold more frequent in blood (range, 0-41.0%); the tumorenriched fraction exceeded the blood-enriched fraction in 12 of 13 patients (Wilcoxon signed-rank P = 0.0007). The median within-patient log2 tumor-to-blood frequency ratio among shared clonotypes was 1.83, corresponding to an approximately 3.6-fold enrichment in the tumor. Shared clonotypes showed a marked tumor-to-blood relative-frequency asymmetry, consistent with intratumoral enrichment. Local expansion is one plausible mechanism, although selective recruitment or retention and compositional effects cannot be excluded. (Figure 2E).

In a separate comparison across patients, exact sequence sharing between biopsies was limited. Of 60,916 distinct biopsy CDR3 amino-acid sequences observed across the cohort, 59,852 (98.3%) were detected in only one biopsy and 1,064 (1.7%) in at least two. Because cohort-wide recurrence is strongly depth-dependent, we repeated this analysis with all biopsies downsampled to the cohortminimum biopsy depth of 6,194 reads: a median of 22,916 distinct sequences remained, of which 262 (1.1%) were detected in at least two biopsies, so the predominance of patient-private sequences was preserved under this extreme common-depth standardization. Pairwise overlap between biopsies from different patients was correspondingly low across the 78 biopsy pairs, with a median Jaccard index of 0.0015 and a median Morisita-Horn index of 0.0001. These data are summarized in Supplementary Data 5.

Taken together, these observations describe largely individual biopsy repertoires whose overlap with matched blood involves a minority of distinct clonotypes. Within that minority, peripheral detectability showed a graded positive association with intratumoral abundance, and the shared clonotypes themselves are preferentially expanded in the tumor.

TMB is inversely associated with intratumoral TCRβ effective diversity across the nonhypermutated range

Before testing immune-genomic associations, we assembled an audited subject-level dataset linking paired TCRβ repertoire measurements with OCA Plus molecular data. All 13 curated patients had complete paired PBMC and tumor-biopsy TCR data and complete OCA measurements. All sampleidentity, sequencing-depth and clonotype-count consistency checks were passed (Supplementary Data 6).

To define a compact immune panel independently of genomic or clinicopathological associations, TCR measurements were organized into four prespecified biological domains: intratumoral effective diversity, clonal dominance, abundance-conditioned tumor-to-blood clonotype recovery, and abundance-weighted tumor-blood similarity (Table 2). Candidate measurements within competitive domains were evaluated using TCR-only properties, including completeness, value resolution, sequencing-depth association, within-domain representativeness, matched-depth resampling precision, and clonotype denominator support where applicable. Leave-one-subject-out (LOSO) re-selection was used to assess the stability of representative choice. The resulting panel comprised Hill D1, Top-10 cumulative read frequency, PBMC detection of the top 5% of biopsy clonotypes ranked by abundance, and Morisita-Horn similarity. Each representative was recovered in all 13 LOSO analyses. The Top-5% recovery measure retained a low-denominator-support review flag because its smallest subject-specific denominator comprised three clonotypes, but its selection remained stable in 13/13 LOSO runs. No pair of frozen immune representatives reached the prespecified high-redundancy threshold |ρ| ≥ 0.80 (Supplementary Figure S3; Supplementary Data 7).

In parallel, four source-defined continuous OCA Plus measures, TMB, MSI score, genomic instability metric (GIM), and loss of heterozygosity (LOH), were frozen without reference to immune associations (Table 2). All four were complete in the 13-patient cohort and satisfied the prespecified technical eligibility criteria. GIM showed a high zero mass, with 9 of 13 tumors having a value of zero and only five distinct values across the cohort; it satisfied the prespecified technical eligibility criteria and was retained, but its limited rank resolution constrains the interpretation of any GIM association. Correlations among the four genomic measures were audited rather than used for data-driven pruning; the largest was between GIM and LOH ( 0.769, below the prespecified high-correlation threshold of 0.80, although high enough that GIM and LOH should not be interpreted as independent genomic dimensions (Supplementary Figure S4; Supplementary Data 8). The frozen four-feature immune and four-feature genomic panels therefore defined 16 prespecified primary comparisons. Across this primary family, higher TMB was associated with lower intratumoral Hill D1 (Spearman ρ = -0.833; two-sided permutation P = 0.00072; Figure 3A-B). This was the only association supported by both prespecified multiplicity procedures, with BH q = 0.0115 across the 16 primary tests and max-ρ permutation FWER P = 0.00884. The subject-level bootstrap 95% interval was -0.962 to -0.496. The inverse association was preserved in all 13 LOSO analyses, with ρ ranging from -0.875 to -0.787 (Figure 3D). Complete primary effect sizes, uncertainty intervals, permutation results, and LOSO summaries are provided in Supplementary Figure S5A and Supplementary Data 9. The association was not attributable to the two hypermutated tumors, both of which were POLE-mutant: it was preserved after excluding them (ρ = -0.874), after excluding all four tumors carrying the assay POLE flag (ρ = -0.919), and after excluding the two biopsies of lowest sequencing depth (ρ = -0.878). It was also independent of biopsy sequencing depth, which was itself unrelated to TMB (ρ = -0.207, P = 0.50) and which left the association essentially unchanged in a depth-adjusted partial rank analysis (ρ = -0.831). The relationship was graded rather than driven by hypermutation: among the 11 tumors with TMB below 30 mutations per megabase it was at least as strong as in the full cohort (ρ = -0.874, P = 0.0004), whereas a contrast between the two hypermutated tumors and the remainder was not significant (Mann-Whitney P = 0.31). The four tumors with the lowest TMB values had the four highest Hill D1 values. These TMB measurements were tied at the assay's lower reporting limit, reducing rank resolution at the low end and warranting cautious interpretation.

TMB also showed a positive association with Top-10 cumulative read frequency (ρ = 0.716; permutation P = 0.00714; Figure 3C), with a bootstrap 95% interval of 0.319-0.881 and the same direction in all 13 LOSO analyses. However, this association did not meet the prespecified multipletesting thresholds (BH q = 0.0571; max-ρ FWER P = 0.101). Hill D1 was also positively associated with LOH (ρ = 0.630) and negatively associated with MSI score (ρ = -0.621), but neither remained supported after multiplicity correction (BH q = 0.106 for both). The complete 4 × 4 association structure is shown in Figure 3A and Supplementary Data 9.

The two tumor-blood connectivity descriptors in the primary panel showed no multiplicitycorrected genomic associations. For example, Morisita-Horn similarity showed an inverse association with TMB (r = -0.462), but this was not supported after correction (permutation P=0.112, BH q=0.256, max-ρ FWER P=0.750); abundance-conditioned Top-5% PBMC recovery likewise showed no corrected association with TMB, MSI score, GIM, or LOH. A separate 12-test sensitivity family using global biopsy clonotype recovery, Top-10% PBMC recovery, and shared biopsy read abundance yielded no corrected associations (Supplementary Figure S5B; Supplementary Data 10). Exploratory analyses of the four primary immune descriptors according to the OCA POLE source flag also yielded no within-family significant association; the smallest BH-adjusted value was q = 0.198 (Supplementary Figure S5C; Supplementary Data 11).

Because only two of the four flag-positive tumors carried a pathogenic POLE exonuclease-domain mutation, this flag-based comparison does not correspond to the POLE-mutant molecular class.

Together, these analyses identify an inverse association between TMB and effective intratumoral TCRβ diversity that is graded across the ordinary range of mutational burden rather than confined to hypermutated tumors. A concordant increase in Top-10 clonal concentration was observed with increasing TMB but did not meet the prespecified multiple-testing thresholds, whereas the tested dimensions of tumor-blood repertoire connectivity showed no comparable multiplicity-corrected genomic association.

Histopathological features show no multiplicity-corrected associations with the frozen immune and genomic panels

We next examined whether the frozen immune and genomic descriptors were associated with conventional histopathological features, without further feature selection. The four immune descriptors and four genomic measures were evaluated against endometrioid grade, myometrial invasion and LVSI, yielding eight prespecified comparisons per endpoint and 24 tests overall.

Grade was analyzed ordinally in 11 tumors (7 G1, 3 G2, 1 G3), myometrial invasion in 12 evaluable tumors (8 >50%,4 ≤50%) and LVSI in 11 (2 positive, 9 negative). Multiplicity was controlled within each eight-test family by Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) correction and by permutation-based max-|effect| family-wise correction, with an additional global BH correction across all 24 comparisons (Supplementary Figure S6; Supplementary Data 12 and 13).

No association remained supported after correction. The two strongest nominal effects were higher LOH in tumors with >50% myometrial invasion (rank-biserial effect = 0.750, exact permutation P = 0.0485) and lower Morisita-Horn tumor-blood similarity with increasing grade (Spearman ρ = -0.576, exact permutation P = 0.0697); neither survived within-family or global correction (minimum BH q = 0.388, minimum max-|effect| FWER P = 0.244, global q = 0.836). For LVSI the smallest nominal P was 0.436. That endpoint had no capacity to yield a corrected association in the first place: with two LVSI-positive tumors the exact label-permutation space contains only 55 assignments, so the smallest attainable two-sided P is 0.036 and the smallest attainable withinfamily BH q is 0.29, and the negative result therefore reflects the limits of the design rather than evidence against an association. Histotype and p53 pattern were summarized descriptively because of sparse subgroup sizes. Full results for all 24 comparisons are given in Supplementary Data 12-14. None of them showed multiplicity-corrected evidence of association, indicating that the TMB-Hill D1 relationship identified in the immune-genomic analysis was not mirrored by the conventional histopathological features available in this cohort.

Discussion

This study characterizes the TCRβ repertoire in paired tumor and blood samples from patients with endometrial cancer. Tumor repertoires were narrower than matched peripheral repertoires, as expected for a tissue-infiltrating population, and this difference persisted under all applied depthand coverage-standardization procedures. However, Hill D2 and Top-10 concentration showed no consistent compartmental shift, indicating that reduced tumor breadth was not equivalent to a generalized increase in clonal dominance. Among biopsy clonotypes, peripheral detectability increased with intratumoral abundance, whereas clonotypes shared between compartments were usually enriched in the tumor. Higher TMB was associated with lower intratumoral Hill D1, whereas tumor-blood connectivity and the evaluated histopathological features showed no multiplicity-corrected associations.

Only a minority of distinct biopsy clonotypes was detectable in matched blood, but their detection followed a graded abundance relationship rather than being restricted to an arbitrarily defined dominant subset. In the clonotype-level model of the observed repertoires, the odds of peripheral detection increased approximately 2.4-fold per tenfold increase in intratumoral frequency.

Consistently, the separate matched-depth stratum analysis retained an overall abundance gradient, and patient-specific slopes were positive in 12 of 13 patients. Shared clonotypes were enriched in the tumor by a median of approximately 3.6-fold. Exact CDR3 sharing cannot establish trafficking direction or antigen specificity, and the cross-sectional design cannot determine where proliferation occurred; these findings therefore support intratumoral relative-frequency enrichment consistent with, but not demonstrative of, local expansion. These findings extend previous TCRβ studies in endometrial cancer. The first linked intratumoral clonality to immune gene expression and progression-free survival but examined tumor tissue only 14 . Subsequent paired tumor-blood studies showed that clonotypes present at multiple tumor sites are over-represented in blood 15 , that circulating tumor-matched clonotypes increase in a subset of pembrolizumab responders 16 , and that such clonotypes may persist for years after treatment of POLE-mutant tumors 17 . Pan-cancer data similarly indicate that tumor-expanded clonotypes can be detected in the circulation 18 . The present study adds a quantitative within-patient analysis showing that peripheral representation tracks intratumoral abundance in a single biopsy. It also shows that, among shared clonotypes, tumor-enriched sequences outnumbered blood-enriched sequences in 12 of 13 patients. This pattern is not readily explained by passive blood carryover alone, although selective recruitment, retention, compositional effects, and local proliferation cannot be distinguished. Technical differences from the study by Kim et al., including RNA rather than genomic DNA, no replicate-concordance filter, retention of all productive clonotypes, and explicit depth resampling, should also be considered when comparing results 15.

Bulk TCRβ sequencing provides no phenotypic or specificity information. Single-cell studies in other cancers suggest that exhausted intratumoral clones, which are often enriched for tumor reactivity, are less frequently detected in blood than memory-phenotype clones 22 . The shared, tumor-enriched clonotypes observed here may therefore include memory-like tumor-specific cells, bystander clones of unrelated specificity, or both. Any future blood-based application will require paired phenotyping or direct specificity testing to distinguish these possibilities.

The TMB-Hill D1 relationship was the only immune-genomic association supported by both falsediscovery-rate and family-wise error correction. Its direction was preserved in every LOSO analysis and remained at least as strong after excluding the two hypermutated POLE-mutant tumors or all tumors carrying the assay POLE flag. This supports a graded relationship that was not driven solely by hypermutated cases. The association is biologically plausible because hypermutated endometrial cancers can generate more candidate neoantigens and often show prominent lymphocytic infiltration 4 , potentially promoting expansion of selected T-cell populations and reducing effective diversity. TMB is not, however, a direct measure of immune recognition, which also depends on neoantigen quality, clonality, expression, and presentation 8, 9 . Repertoire architecture may therefore capture a downstream functional dimension of the immune response that is not represented by mutation count alone.

Tumor-blood connectivity and conventional histopathology behaved differently. Neither abundance-conditioned peripheral detectability nor Morisita-Horn similarity showed a genomic association that survived correction, suggesting that the observed genomic signal concerned intratumoral repertoire organization rather than the extent of peripheral representation. Likewise, grade, myometrial invasion, and LVSI showed no corrected association with the selected immune and genomic descriptors. These negative findings do not establish the absence of an association, particularly given the small and imbalanced subgroups, but they are compatible with immune heterogeneity extending beyond the histopathological variables evaluated in this cohort 10 . These observations may be relevant to immunotherapy, although treatment response was not evaluated. Immune checkpoint inhibitors are now central to the management of advanced and recurrent endometrial cancer [23] [24] [25] [26] , yet response heterogeneity remains substantial. Because integrated genomic-immune models have outperformed TMB alone in pan-cancer analyses 13 , repertoire-derived measures could provide complementary information by directly quantifying the organization of the adaptive immune response.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The cohort was small, limiting statistical precision and representation of molecular subgroups, and the association analyses were exploratory. With only two LVSI-positive tumors, the exact permutation space could not yield a multiplicity-corrected result, so that negative finding primarily reflects the study design. Molecular classes were assigned from sequencing and MSI testing rather than mismatch-repair immunohistochemistry, and subgroup sizes precluded formal comparisons among molecular classes. The cross-sectional design does not address temporal evolution, prognosis, treatment response, trafficking direction, or the site of clonal proliferation. Depth and coverage standardization cannot eliminate differences in tissue cellularity, RNA recovery, or spatial heterogeneity. Clonotypes were defined by CDR3 amino-acid sequence without V or J gene information, potentially merging distinct rearrangements, and abundance was measured using sequencing reads rather than unique molecular identifiers. Finally, resampling, permutation inference, and outcome-blind feature selection improve internal robustness but cannot replace validation in larger independent cohorts.

In conclusion, paired TCRβ profiling revealed a consistent relationship between intratumoral abundance and peripheral detection, together with relative tumor enrichment of shared clonotypes.

Higher TMB was associated with lower effective intratumoral diversity, whereas tumor-blood connectivity and the evaluated histopathological features showed no comparable corrected associations. These findings are hypothesis-generating and warrant validation in larger longitudinal studies before any biomarker application.

METHODS

Study design and patient selection

This retrospective, observational, cross-sectional exploratory study included 13 women with histologically confirmed endometrial cancer who underwent surgical treatment at the Unit of Obstetrics and Gynaecology of the "Renato Dulbecco" University Hospital in Catanzaro, Italy, between November 2023 and November 2025. The study was non-interventional and involved no investigational drug or medical device.

Patients were eligible for inclusion if they had a histologically confirmed diagnosis of endometrial carcinoma, underwent surgery with availability of tumor tissue suitable for molecular analyses, and had a paired peripheral-blood sample available for TCRβ repertoire analysis. Peripheral blood was collected on the day of surgery as part of routine clinical care, and residual material from samples submitted to the clinical laboratory was used for research analyses. Thus, tumor and peripheralblood samples represented contemporaneous sampling of the two compartments.

The final study population comprised 13 patients, with a median age of 65 years (interquartile range, 61-70 years). Histopathological characteristics, including histological subtype, tumor grade, depth of myometrial invasion, and lymphovascular space invasion (LVSI), were retrieved retrospectively from pathology records when available. p53 immunohistochemical pattern and regional nodal findings were available only for a subset of patients. Missingness reflected the availability of clinical records and examinations rather than patient selection: the definitive surgical pathology report could not be retrieved for one patient, for whom grade, myometrial invasion, LVSI, and p53 immunohistochemical pattern were left missing rather than inferred. p53 immunohistochemistry had been performed in 7 of 13 patients, and regional nodal findings were documented in 8 of 13. Patient-level clinicopathological data are provided in Supplementary Data 1.

The study was approved by the Regional Ethical Review Board of Regione Calabria (Ethics Committee approval no. 84/2026) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and Regulation (EU) 2016/679 (General Data Protection Regulation, GDPR). Data and biological samples used for research were pseudonymized using internal laboratory codes. The key linking these codes to patient identities was retained exclusively at the clinical center and was neither shared outside the institution nor deposited in public repositories. All participants provided written informed consent authorizing the use of their biological samples and associated clinical data for research purposes.

Sample processing and TCRβ sequencing

Peripheral venous blood was collected in EDTA tubes, and peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated by density-gradient centrifugation using Ficoll-Paque (Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Total RNA was extracted from isolated PBMCs and used for TCRβ repertoire analysis. Fresh tumor specimens were obtained at the time of surgery, and DNA and RNA were extracted from endometrial cancer tissue samples following standardized protocols for downstream molecular analyses 29.

TCRβ libraries were generated using the Oncomine™ TCR Beta-LR Assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) as previously described. Libraries were barcoded and processed on the Ion Chef™ System (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for template preparation, enrichment, and chip loading onto Ion 530™ chips, followed by sequencing on the Ion GeneStudio™ S5 System (Thermo Fisher Scientific), as previously described [29] [30] [31].

Raw sequencing data were analyzed using MiXCR (version 4.1.2) 32 with the manufacturer-specific preset optimized for the Oncomine™ TCR Beta-LR Assay. Downstream analyses were conducted on productive TCRβ clonotypes and included estimation of repertoire diversity, clonality, and tumor-blood repertoire overlap metrics 29.

Comprehensive genomic profiling

Comprehensive genomic profiling of endometrial tumor samples was performed using the Oncomine™ Comprehensive Assay Plus (Thermo Fisher Scientific), an amplicon-based nextgeneration sequencing panel designed for simultaneous analysis of DNA and RNA targets 33, 34 . The assay interrogates 517 cancer-related genes and supports the detection of single-nucleotide variants (SNVs), insertions/deletions (indels), copy number variations (CNVs), gene fusions, and splice variants, together with the assessment of TMB, MSI, and GIM 33, 35 . Libraries were prepared according to the manufacturer's instructions and sequenced on the Ion GeneStudio™ S5 platform. Sequencing data were processed using the Oncomine™ Comprehensive Assay Plus analysis workflow. TMB was expressed as mutations per megabase, and MSI was assessed using targeted microsatellite loci and summarized by the assay-derived MSI score 33 . Genomic instability was quantified using the GIM, a proprietary score ranging from 0 to 100 that summarizes unbalanced autosomal copy-number changes identified through genomic segmentation 33, 35 . The resulting genomic variables, including TMB, MSI score, GIM and LOH, were integrated with TCR repertoire features for downstream immunogenomic analyses.

Molecular classification

Tumors were assigned to surrogate molecular classes with the hierarchical assay-based algorithm of the WHO classification of female genital tumours: POLE-mutant (POLEmut), then mismatchrepair-deficient (MMRd), then p53-abnormal (p53abn), then no specific molecular profile (NSMP) 36, 37 . POLEmut status was defined by a pathogenic POLE exonuclease-domain mutation 38 ; the categorical POLE flag reported by the assay, which also captures variants outside the exonuclease domain and copy-number losses, was not used for classification. Because mismatchrepair immunohistochemistry was not available, MMRd status was defined by an MSI-High call of the assay MSI algorithm. p53abn status was defined by a pathogenic or likely pathogenic TP53 variant. POLE and TP53 variants were taken from the unfiltered variant calls of the assay workflow (hg19) and verified independently from the aligned reads of each tumor, by counting alleles at every covered position of the POLE exonuclease domain and the TP53 coding sequence (base quality ≥10, mapping quality ≥4, at least 20 variant reads on both strands, variant allele fraction ≥5%); read-level verification was possible for 12 of 13 tumors, and all variant calls were concordant.

Tumors with more than one classifying alteration were assigned according to the hierarchy.

TCRβ repertoire characterization

Read-based sampling descriptors and repertoire-architecture measures were calculated using custom Python scripts, available on GitHub (https://github.com/cpalmieri71/endometrial-TCRb-repertoire).

Within each sample, identical CDR3 amino acid sequences were collapsed by summing read counts, and clonotype relative abundances were calculated by normalizing these counts to the total retained reads. Sampling descriptors included sequencing depth, singleton and doubleton counts, Good's coverage, and Chao1-estimated richness. Repertoire architecture was characterized by observed clonotype richness (Hill diversity of order 0), Hill diversity of orders 1 and 2 (the exponential of Shannon entropy and inverse Simpson concentration, respectively), the Gini coefficient, the cumulative relative abundance of the 10 and 100 most abundant clonotypes, and the minimum fraction of observed clonotypes required to account for at least 50% of reads. All measures were calculated at the observed sequencing depth, without rarefaction, using read counts rather than cell counts as the abundance measure. The GitHub repository provides detailed documentation of input processing, implemented calculations, mathematical formulas, and output files.

Clonotypes were identified by CDR3 amino acid sequence alone, without reference to V or J gene assignment; sequences sharing a CDR3 across different V-J rearrangements were therefore merged. Abundances were quantified as sequencing read counts rather than unique molecular identifiers, so residual amplification bias is expected to affect measures weighted towards abundant clonotypes (D 2 , Top-10 and Top-100 frequencies) more than observed richness or D 1 . All retained sequences consisted exclusively of the 20 standard amino acids. A minority departed from the expected CDR3 termini or length, taken as a cysteine at the first position, a phenylalanine or tryptophan at the last, and a length of 8-30 amino acids; these accounted for a median of 1.6% of biopsy and 0.7% of PBMC clonotypes and for less than 0.2% of reads in both compartments, and were retained.

Complete definitions, formulas and implementation details for every repertoire measure are given in Supplementary Methods.

Exact clonotype sharing and sequencing-depth standardization

Exact TCRβ clonotype sharing was analyzed using custom Python scripts, available on GitHub (https://github.com/cpalmieri71/endometrial-TCRb-repertoire). For each matched tumor-biopsy-PBMC pair, we calculated the number of shared clonotypes, the compartment-specific fractions of clonotypes and reads attributable to shared sequences, the Jaccard index, the overlap coefficient, and Morisita-Horn similarity 39 . PBMC detection of biopsy clonotypes was evaluated across six non-overlapping abundance-rank strata (top 1%, 1-5%, 5-10%, 10-25%, 25-50%, and 50-100%) and cumulative top fractions (1%, 5%, 10%, 25%, and 50%). These percentages refer to the number of distinct clonotypes ranked by decreasing biopsy read abundance, rather than their cumulative read contribution. Relative enrichment among shared clonotypes was described using log biopsyto-PBMC relative-frequency ratios. Sensitivity to sequencing depth was assessed using 500 repeated downsampling iterations without replacement, implemented by multivariate hypergeometric sampling to the lower read depth of each pair. Overlap and detection measures were recalculated at each iteration, including biopsy abundance ranks and stratum membership, and summarized by resampling medians and 2.5th-97.5th percentile ranges. These ranges describe conditional read-sampling variability, not biological confidence intervals.

Pairwise overlap was also calculated between biopsies from different patients, with pair-specific depth matching. Cross-patient clonotype recurrence (publicness) was described by the number of biopsies containing each sequence and the fractions of clonotypes and reads attributable to sequences detected in at least two or three patients. Observed publicness measures were retained as the primary descriptors; downsampling all biopsies to the cohort-wide minimum depth provided an additional sensitivity analysis. These analyses were descriptive. The script and detailed documentation of input processing, mathematical formulas, resampling procedures, and output files are available on GitHub (https://github.com/cpalmieri71/endometrial-TCRb-repertoire).

Complete definitions and formulas for the overlap measures, the matched-depth resampling procedure, the abundance-rank strata and the between-patient recurrence analysis are given in Supplementary Methods.

Clonotype-level modelling of peripheral detection

Peripheral detectability was additionally modelled at the level of individual clonotypes. For each biopsy clonotype, detection in the matched PBMC repertoire was treated as a binary outcome and modelled by logistic regression on the base-10 logarithm of its intratumoral frequency, with patient fixed effects so that the coefficient is identified within patients, and a cluster-robust sandwich variance estimator over the 13 patients. The same model was fitted separately within each patient, and the resulting slopes were compared with zero using the two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test; this patient-level test, rather than the pooled model, provides the inference that respects the number of independent subjects. Relative abundance between compartments was summarized for clonotypes detected in both, as the fraction at least twofold more frequent in each compartment and as the median within-patient log 2 tumor-to-blood frequency ratio.

Outcome-blind construction of the immune and genomic panels

Candidate immune descriptors were grouped into four prespecified biological domains, intratumoral effective diversity, intratumoral clonal dominance, abundance-conditioned tumor-to-blood recovery, and tumor-blood abundance similarity, and a single representative measurement was selected within each domain. Selection used repertoire and overlap data only: genomic, clinical and histopathological variables were excluded programmatically, and the selection script terminates with an error if any such variable enters candidate scoring. Candidates were screened for completeness, variability, residual association with sequencing depth and within-domain redundancy, and the stability of each selection was assessed by repeating the entire procedure with each subject omitted in turn. Where a domain offered several abundance thresholds, the competing thresholds were compared on the same outcome-blind criteria.

For downstream primary immunogenomic analyses, the frozen immune panel comprised intratumoral Hill , intratumoral top-10 cumulative read frequency, the depth-standardized PBMC detection fraction of the most abundant 5% of biopsy clonotypes, and depth-standardized Morisita-Horn similarity. These descriptors represented effective diversity, clonal dominance, abundanceconditioned tumor-to-blood recovery, and abundance-weighted tumor-blood similarity, respectively. They were retained as individual variables rather than combined into composite focusing or sharing scores, and were evaluated against four separately defined continuous genomic measures: TMB, MSI score, genomic instability metric, and loss of heterozygosity. Feature selection was performed independently of immune-genomic association results. The primary TMB association was additionally examined in post hoc sensitivity analyses that excluded the two hypermutated tumors, all tumors carrying the assay POLE flag, and the two biopsies of lowest sequencing depth, and in a partial rank analysis adjusting for biopsy sequencing depth.

Four continuous genomic measures, TMB, MSI score, genomic instability metric and loss of heterozygosity, were frozen in parallel from the Oncomine Comprehensive Assay Plus output without reference to immune data. Pairwise correlations among the selected immune representatives and among the genomic measures were audited against a prespecified redundancy threshold of |r| ≥ 0.80 but were not used for further data-driven pruning.

Statistical analysis

Paired comparisons between compartments used the two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test across the 13 matched pairs. Monotonic associations between continuous variables were quantified by the Spearman rank correlation coefficient, and associations with binary endpoints by the rank-biserial correlation.

Because observed richness and D 1 are not depth-corrected, all compartmental comparisons were repeated under three standardizations. First, within each pair the deeper repertoire was randomly subsampled without replacement to the depth of the shallower member, with 100 subsamples per repertoire and metrics averaged across subsamples. Second, all repertoires were rarefied to a cohortwide common depth of 63,199 reads, retaining the 11 pairs in which both members reached that depth; an intermediate depth of 12,792 reads was also evaluated, retaining 12 pairs. Third, all 26 repertoires were standardized to a common Good's coverage of 0.838, the highest value attainable in every repertoire, following coverage-based standardization as described by Chao and Jost 40.

Rarefaction curves were computed as exact hypergeometric expectations rather than by simulation.

Paired compartments were compared using the two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test; P values are reported as descriptive, without correction for multiple comparisons. Results of all standardized analyses are provided in Supplementary Data 3.

Immune-genomic associations were tested as a prespecified family of 16 comparisons between the four frozen immune descriptors and the four frozen genomic measures. Inference was by permutation: subject labels of the genomic block were permuted 100,000 times, with the same permutation applied to all four genomic measures so that their mutual correlation was preserved, and two-sided P values were computed as (k + 1)/(B + 1), where B is the number of permutations and k the number yielding an absolute correlation at least as large as the observed one. Multiplicity was controlled in two complementary ways: Benjamini-Hochberg false-discovery-rate correction within the family, and a permutation-based family-wise error rate derived from the null distribution of the maximum absolute correlation across all 16 comparisons. Uncertainty around each coefficient was summarized by subject-level bootstrap resampling with percentile intervals, and stability by recomputing each coefficient with one subject omitted in turn. A secondary family of 12 recovery-sensitivity comparisons and an exploratory family of four comparisons according to the OCA POLE source flag were analysed separately, each with its own within-family correction.

Associations with histopathological endpoints were analysed as three endpoint families of eight comparisons each: endometrioid grade, treated as ordinal; myometrial invasion exceeding 50% of the myometrial thickness; and LVSI. Because the label spaces were small enough to enumerate exhaustively, permutation P values were obtained from the complete set of distinct label assignments rather than by Monte Carlo sampling. Multiplicity was controlled within each family by Benjamini-Hochberg correction and by a permutation-based maximum-effect family-wise procedure, with an additional global Benjamini-Hochberg correction across all 24 comparisons.

The resolution attainable by these tests is bounded by the size of the permutation space: with two LVSI-positive tumors only 55 distinct assignments exist, so the smallest attainable two-sided P value is 0.036 and the smallest attainable within-family q value is 0.29. P values are reported as descriptive measures of evidence. Multiplicity was controlled within each prespecified family, and additionally across the three histopathological endpoint families; no correction was applied between the immune-genomic and histopathological blocks, nor between the primary, secondary and exploratory association families, and the study was not powered for formal hypothesis testing.
