How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Abstract

Subtle declines in mobility may emerge during midlife and can be detected using smartphone-based motion measures. Instrumented assessment of balance and functional tasks commonly uses lower-back placement, which requires device fixation and may limit convenient real-world monitoring. This study compared mobility outcomes obtained from a practical front-pocket/thigh placement with those from a standardized lower-back placement during static balance, sit-to-stand (STS), and Timed Up and Go (TUG) tests. Forty-six adults aged 40-64 years completed static balance, 30-second STS, and TUG assessments during simultaneous smartphone recording at the lower back and pocket/thigh. Outcomes included two-and three-dimensional sway, STS repetition count, average repetition duration, lower limb power, and maximal vertical velocity, as well as TUG total duration and peak yaw angular velocity during both turns. Associations, intraclass correlation coefficients, and Bland-Altman analyses were used to characterize the relationship and agreement between the two placements. Balance outcomes showed strong correlations between placements (r s =0.83-0.93), with moderate-to-excellent consistency for most conditions, although pocket/thigh sway values were systematically higher. TUG total duration showed strong correlation and good absolute agreement (r s =0.895; ICC=0.881), and turning velocity showed good consistency during the first and the second turns. STS repetition count and average repetition duration demonstrated strong correlations between placements (r=0.996 and 0.985, respectively), with excellent absolute agreement for repetition count and excellent consistency for average repetition duration (ICC=0.993 and 0.982, respectively). In contrast, STS power and maximal vertical velocity showed weaker correlations and consistency (r=0.709 and r s =0.382; ICC=0.600 and 0.417, respectively). Pocket/thigh smartphone placement supports practical mobility assessment across balance, STS and TUG. Although some movement-derived measures showed placement-dependent differences in absolute values, relative performance patterns were generally preserved across placements.

[div-01]

These findings support the development of pocket-based smartphone assessment for simplified and potentially unsupervised mobility monitoring in midlife, while placement-sensitive outcomes require further validation against external reference measures.

Author Summary

Wearable technologies, including smartphones, offer promising ways to assess mobility during midlife, when subtle changes in physical performance may begin to emerge but go undetected by conventional clinical tests. However, most research using these technologies has been conducted under controlled conditions, with devices often attached to the lower back. Although this approach provides useful measurements, it requires fixation, making it less practical for everyday use.

In this study, we examined whether smartphones carried in a front pocket could provide mobility measurements comparable to those obtained from the lower back. Forty-six middle-aged adults completed balance, chair-standing, and walking and turning tasks while smartphones recorded their movements at both locations.

We found that measures of mobility, such as chair-standing repetitions and walking task completion time, were highly comparable between smartphone locations. Balance measurements, such as body sway while standing on one leg, showed similar patterns, although their absolute values differed. In contrast, measures of muscle power and maximum upward movement speed during chair-standing were more sensitive to smartphone placement.

Our findings support the potential of pocket-based smartphone assessment as a practical approach to measuring mobility beginning in midlife, while further research is needed to establish its suitability for everyday use.

Background

Midlife represents a critical stage in the aging trajectory, during which subtle and often undetected declines in mobility may begin to emerge, even when overall health is largely preserved [1]. Changes in key mobility-related domains, including gait, balance, and lowerlimb strength and power, may represent early manifestations of functional decline [1] [2] [3] [4] [5].

Accordingly, there is a growing need for accessible, sensitive, and convenient assessment methods capable of detecting early declines in locomotion performance during midlife.

Traditional assessments, typically based on simple clinical outcomes such as walking speed or sit-to-stand (STS) duration, may lack the sensitivity required to detect early mobility deterioration in midlife [2] [3] [4]. Recent studies have shown that instrumented assessments using wearable inertial sensors can overcome some of the ceiling limitations of conventional measures and detect subtle differences among community-dwelling middle-aged adults [2] [3] [4] 6]. These sensor-derived measures may provide added sensitivity by capturing specific components of postural control and movement quality that are not reflected in conventional outcomes. For example, smartphone-derived balance measures based on postural sway differentiated middle-aged from younger adults [2]; Timed-Up-and-Go (TUG) derived turning velocity was associated with early functional decline despite limited sensitivity of total TUG duration [6]; and smartphone-derived STS power distinguished middle-aged from younger adults when conventional STS duration did not [4].

Although wearable technologies, including smartphones, have shown promising results, the existing evidence is largely based on studies conducted under controlled research conditions, using standardized sensor configurations typically placed at the lower back to ensure signal consistency [3, 4, [7] [8] [9] [10] [11] [12]. While this placement provides a stable representation of whole-body movement, it requires dedicated fixation during measurement, which may limit real-world implementation and ecological validity. A front-pocket configuration better reflects everyday smartphone carrying habits and may facilitate independent use by eliminating the need for dedicated mounting accessories or assistance with device placement, thereby supporting future unsupervised assessment. However, because the pocket-mounted device captures motion farther from the body's center of mass and from a different body segment, sensor-derived outcomes may differ from those obtained at the lower back [13], highlighting the need to evaluate the relationship between measurements obtained from the two configurations. Existing validation studies that examined smartphone-based mobility assessment have primarily involved younger or older adults [7, 13, 14], with limited investigation in middle-aged populations, despite the variability in functional performance characteristics in this age group [1, 2]. Moreover, these studies have typically assessed single mobility domains rather than a comprehensive set of outcomes [8, 11, 13, 14].

OneStep is a smartphone-based platform that uses embedded inertial sensors and machine-learning algorithms to assess mobility without requiring dedicated external sensors [14] [15] [16] [17]. Previous validation studies have demonstrated its ability to measure spatiotemporal gait parameters using a smartphone carried in the pocket, with generally good agreement with reference gait-analysis systems [14, 15]. Additional studies have supported its application for gait assessment under clinical and real-world recording conditions [16, 17]. However, evidence regarding pocket-based assessment has focused primarily on gait, with limited investigation in middle-aged adults and little evidence regarding other mobility domains, including balance, STS, and TUG, which may help detect early mobility deterioration in midlife.

This study aimed to compare mobility metrics obtained from practical pocket/thigh and standardized lower-back smartphone placements and to quantify placement-related differences across static balance, TUG, and STS tasks in community-dwelling middle-aged adults. We hypothesized that pocket/thigh-derived measurements would show moderate-tostrong associations and good-to-excellent consistency with lower-back-derived measurements. For conventional clinical outcomes, specifically STS repetition count and TUG total duration, we expected good-to-excellent absolute agreement between placements, as these outcomes are less dependent on sensor location.

Methods

Participants

An a priori sample size calculation for the planned ICC analysis indicated that 45 participants were required, assuming a minimum acceptable ICC of 0.50, an expected ICC of 0.75, two measurements per participant, α = 0.05, and 80% power [18]. Accordingly, 46 community-dwelling middle-aged adults were recruited.

Participants were included if they were 40-64 years old, lived independently in the community, and were able to walk outdoors without assistance. Exclusion criteria included neurological, orthopedic, vestibular, or visual conditions that could affect mobility (e.g., stroke, Parkinson's disease, recent fracture or orthopedic surgery, age-related macular degeneration, glaucoma, or diabetic retinopathy).

The study was approved by the Ariel University Ethics Committee (AU-HEA-SS-20230806), All research was conducted in accordance with the pre-approved study protocol, applicable institutional guidelines and regulations, and the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent prior to participation.

Experimental Setup

Data were collected simultaneously from two primary smartphone placements, a standardized lower-back placement [3, 4, 13], with the smartphone secured in a dedicated pouch over the lumbar region, and a dominant-side front-pocket/thigh placement, with the smartphone carried in the front pants pocket when available, or in a snug pouch over the anterolateral thigh on the front-pocket region, when a suitable pocket was unavailable. For the TUG task only, an additional smartphone was placed at the non-dominant frontpocket/thigh location so that data from the pivot-leg side could be analyzed regardless of the participant's self-selected turning direction, as schematically illustrated in Fig 1. To reduce potential device-specific bias, smartphone device assignment to body location was randomized across participants. The devices used were two Xiaomi Mi A3 smartphones (Android 11; approximate dimensions 153.5 × 71.9 × 8.5 mm; weight 173 g) and one Samsung Galaxy S23 smartphone (Android 15; approximate dimensions 146.3 × 70.9 × 7.6 mm; weight 168 g).

All smartphones recorded inertial data simultaneously using OneStep's smartphone gait and mobility analysis system (software versions 4.0. #; Celloscope Ltd., Tel Aviv, Israel), which captured tri-axial linear acceleration and angular velocity from the embedded accelerometer and gyroscope, along with device timestamps [14]. Recordings from the relevant device locations were uploaded to the OneStep platform and processed using a standardized analysis pipeline. Before each trial, participants performed three steps in place, generating a predefined acceleration marker that was used to temporally align recordings from the two smartphone placements using signal-correlation-based matching. Participants were identified in the OneStep application and platform only by their unique study identification number.

Procedure and Outcome Measures

All assessments were conducted in a single supervised session following a standardized protocol. Tasks were performed in the following order: static balance, TUG, and 30-second STS. The STS was performed last to minimize the potential influence of fatigue on the preceding assessments. Task initiation and termination were guided by standardized audio cues ("GO" and "recording stopped").

In cases of technical recording issues (e.g., incorrect recording initiation or inadequate device fixation), or incorrect task execution due to misunderstanding of the instructions or delayed response to the start cue, participants were permitted a second attempt. For static balance tasks, a second attempt was also permitted when the task was not completed because of loss of balance. If neither attempt was completed successfully, the better performance was retained for analysis. For the TUG assessment, participants began seated on a standard chair, stood without using their hands, walked three meters at their fastest safe speed without running, turned around a predefined marker, returned to the chair, and sat down. TUG outcomes included total task duration (s), and peak yaw angular velocity during the first and second turns (°/s) [6, 19]. In the 30-second STS task, participants began seated on a standard chair and completed as many repeated sit-to-stand cycles as possible. Arms were crossed over the shoulders throughout the test. Each repetition required full knee and hip extension during standing and complete contact with the chair while sitting, ensuring standardized movement execution. STS outcome measures included repetition count (n), average repetition duration (s), average lower-limb power (W), and maximal vertical velocity during the rising phase (m/s).

Signal Processing

Inertial signals were sampled at 100 Hz and processed using the standardized OneStep analysis pipeline. Signals from both smartphone placements were processed using the same general analytical approach, with placement-specific adjustments made where required to account for differences in signal characteristics. Linear acceleration signals, with the gravitational component removed by the smartphone's sensor-fusion algorithm, were used for subsequent processing. For balance assessments, 2D and 3D sway dispersion were calculated from the covariance matrices of the mediolateral and anteroposterior acceleration components and the full three-dimensional acceleration signal, respectively [2]. Eigenvalues of the respective covariance matrices were scaled according to the 95% χ² confidence region and used to calculate the area of the 95% confidence ellipse and volume of the 95% confidence ellipsoid.

For TUG, pocket/thigh sub-phases were identified from yaw and pitch signals using windowed angular-displacement thresholds, with minimum cumulative angular changes of 105° for turns and 60° for sit-to-stand and stand-to-sit transitions. Turn boundaries were refined using a fourth-order zero-phase Butterworth low-pass filter with a 1.5-Hz cutoff. Peak yaw angular velocity was derived using a Savitzky-Golay filter (101-sample window, fourthorder polynomial, first derivative) and calculated within each segmented turn. For lower-back recordings, the previously validated TUG algorithm was applied [6], with turns identified from filtered yaw angular velocity and postural transitions from pitch angular velocity. Total TUG duration was calculated from the onset of standing to completion of the final sitting phase.

For STS, repetitions were identified from cyclic changes in the smoothed pitch-angle signal using prominence-based peak detection. For pocket/thigh recordings, gravity-free acceleration was rotated to the earth frame to obtain vertical acceleration, detrended, and numerically integrated to derive vertical velocity. Rising and sitting phases were identified from positive and negative vertical velocities, respectively. Maximal vertical velocity was defined as the peak velocity during the rising phase, and lower-limb power was calculated as the product of body mass, vertical acceleration including gravity, and vertical velocity, averaged across repetitions. For lower-back recordings, the previously established processing approach was applied [4], with vertical acceleration and pitch angular velocity filtered using a fourth-order Butterworth filter with a 1.5-Hz cutoff. Vertical acceleration was integrated with polynomial detrending to obtain vertical velocity and rising and sitting phases were identified from the corresponding velocity peaks.

Recordings with incomplete data, unclear synchronization markers, non-informative signals, or unreliable task segmentation were excluded from the relevant task-specific analysis.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and median with interquartile range (IQR) for demographic and outcome measures. Distributional characteristics of continuous variables were assessed by visual inspection of histograms and Q-Q plots.

Associations between lower-back and pocket/thigh measurements were assessed separately for each task-specific outcome using Pearson or Spearman correlation coefficients, according to the distributional characteristics of each outcome. Correlation coefficients were interpreted using commonly applied thresholds, with values <0.30 considered weak, 0.30-0.50 moderate, and >0.50 strong [20]. The lower-back placement was compared with the dominant-side pocket/thigh placement for balance and STS outcomes and with the pivot-leg pocket/thigh placement for TUG outcomes.

Agreement and consistency between placements were quantified using single-measure intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) based on a two-way mixed-effects model. Absolute agreement ICCs were used for TUG total duration and STS repetition count because these outcomes were expected to yield equivalent values across placements. Consistency ICCs were used for each signal-derived outcome to assess whether relative between-participant differences were preserved across placements despite potential differences in absolute magnitude. ICC values were interpreted as poor (<0.50), moderate (0.50-0.75), good (0.75-0.90), and excellent (>0.90) [21, 22].

Bland-Altman analyses were used to quantify systematic bias and variability between placements. Bias was calculated as the lower-back measurement minus the pocket/thigh measurement; therefore, positive values indicate higher measurements at the lower-back placement, whereas negative values indicate higher measurements at the pocket/thigh placement. The limits of agreement (LoA) were calculated as the mean bias ± 1.96 × the standard deviation of the differences.

Due to technical recording failures, one participant had missing data for all balance outcomes and STS power, and a different participant had missing pocket/thigh data for all TUG outcomes. Discussion

The present study examined whether mobility outcomes derived from a practical front-pocket/thigh smartphone placement showed comparable measurement patterns to those obtained from a standardized lower-back placement in community-dwelling middle-aged adults. As hypothesized, all static balance outcomes showed strong associations between the two smartphone placements, although consistency varied across conditions and was lower for tandem stance with eyes open. STS repetition count and TUG total duration also supported our hypothesis for conventional clinical outcomes, demonstrating excellent and good absolute agreement between placements, respectively. In contrast, consistency between placements varied across the other outcomes. STS power and maximal vertical velocity showed lower consistency between placements, whereas turning velocity during the TUG showed good consistency across the two turns. These findings indicate that values obtained from the two placements may not be interchangeable for all outcomes.

For static balance, participants who showed greater sway at the lower-back placement generally also showed greater sway at the pocket/thigh placement, despite differences in absolute sway values. However, consistency varied across conditions, and it was lower during tandem stance with eyes open. The availability of visual input may have reduced the postural challenge [23], and consequently, the variability in performance between participants. Because ICC values are influenced by between-participant variability [24], this may help explain the lower consistency observed in this condition. The pocket/thigh recordings also produced higher sway values. This may reflect greater movement of the thigh relative to the lower back during postural adjustments, resulting in larger accelerations recorded at the thigh. Sensor location along a body segment has previously been shown to influence acceleration magnitude, supporting the possibility that the higher sway values reflect the biomechanical characteristics of the measurement location rather than poorer measurement performance [25]. Thus, pocket/thigh placement may be useful for practical balance assessment and monitoring, but absolute sway values obtained from the two placements should not be used interchangeably.

TUG outcomes showed good agreement and consistency between smartphone placements. Total duration demonstrated good absolute agreement, while turning velocity showed good consistency across both turns, indicating that the practical pocket/thigh placement captured TUG performance in a manner broadly comparable with the lower-back placement. The good agreement observed for total duration was consistent with our hypothesis, as this outcome reflects completion time of the entire task rather than the magnitude of segment-specific angular motion. The wide confidence interval for TUG total duration may reflect limited between-participant variability, which can affect ICC precision [26], as well as small placement-related differences in detecting task completion due to variations in turn-to-sit strategy and sensor location [27, 28]. Turning velocity demonstrated good consistency across both turns, despite the potential influence of sensor placement and variation in segmental motion during turning. Previous research has shown that sensorderived measurements can be influenced by sensor placement and that this effect may depend on the specific functional movement being analyzed [29]. In the present study, bilateral pocket/thigh recordings allowed turning velocity to be derived from the pivot-leg side, based on evidence that the inner and outer limbs have different biomechanical roles during turning [30, 31]. However, practical assessment with a single smartphone would require consideration of both carrying side and turning direction. Future studies should determine whether pocketderived turning measures remain valid when the pivot-leg side cannot be identified.

The STS findings further illustrate that the influence of smartphone placement depends on the specific outcome being measured. Repetition count and average repetition duration were highly consistent between placements, whereas power and maximal vertical velocity showed substantially weaker consistency. This difference likely reflects the nature of these measures. Repetition count and duration primarily capture the timing and completion of the movement, whereas power and maximal vertical velocity are more directly influenced by the movement of the body segment on which the smartphone is located. During the rising phase, the lower-back smartphone primarily captures trunk motion closer to the body's center of mass, whereas the pocket/thigh smartphone captures thigh motion and hip flexionextension dynamics. Consequently, power and maximal vertical velocity derived from the two placements may represent different aspects of the STS movement and should not be used interchangeably.

Beyond the comparison between smartphone placements, an important finding of the present study is the feasibility of obtaining smartphone-derived measures across multiple mobility domains using a practical pocket/thigh configuration in community-dwelling middle-aged adults. This extends the potential role of smartphone-based assessment from controlled testing toward more accessible monitoring of mobility beginning in midlife. The good agreement and consistency observed between smartphone placements across several balance, STS, and TUG outcomes support the potential use of practical front-pocket placement for mobility assessment without dedicated mounting accessories. However, this potential appears to be outcome-specific, as STS power and maximal vertical velocity were more sensitive to measurement location and should not be used interchangeably across placements. Given that mobility measures in midlife have been associated with future disability [32, 33], the ability to obtain instrumented mobility measures from a smartphone carried in the pocket may provide an important basis for developing accessible approaches to earlier mobility monitoring. Future studies should examine its application in field-based and unsupervised settings, as the ecological validity of pocket-based smartphone mobility assessment outside supervised testing remains to be established.

The results of this study should be interpreted in light of several limitations. First, the pocket/thigh configuration was not completely uniform. Participants used their own front pockets when suitable, whereas a snug pouch positioned over the front-pocket region of the thigh was used when a suitable pocket was unavailable. This pragmatic approach introduced variability in device positioning and pocket characteristics, but also better reflected the intended practical use of the assessment. Outcomes that remained highly consistent across placements despite this variability may therefore be more robust to differences in everyday smartphone carrying conditions. Similarly, the use of different smartphone models, with devices randomized across measurement locations, was intended to reduce dependence on a specific device configuration and increase the practical relevance of the findings. Second, all assessments were conducted during a single supervised session. Thus, although the findings support the feasibility of pocket-based smartphone assessment, they do not establish its performance during independent or repeated assessment in real-world settings. Finally, the relatively modest sample consisted of community-dwelling middle-aged adults able to walk independently, which may limit generalizability to individuals with greater functional limitations.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates the feasibility of obtaining smartphone-derived measures across multiple mobility domains using a practical pocket/thigh configuration in communitydwelling middle-aged adults. Pocket/thigh measurements demonstrated good agreement or consistency with lower-back measurements for most balance, STS, and TUG outcomes.

These findings support the further development of pocket-based smartphone assessment as an accessible approach for mobility assessment and monitoring during midlife, with future studies needed to establish its performance during independent and real-world use.

Balance was assessed under four standing conditions: (a) tandem stance with eyes open, performed for 30 seconds with the dominant foot behind the non-dominant foot and hands on hips; (b) tandem stance with eyes closed, performed in the same position for 10 seconds; (c) single-leg stance, performed for 30 seconds on the dominant leg with hands on hips and without contact between the legs; and (d) standing on a foam surface with eyes closed for 30 seconds in a narrow stance. For each balance condition, two-dimensional (2D) sway dispersion, quantified using the 95% confidence ellipse (m/s²)², and three-dimensional (3D) sway dispersion, quantified using the 95% confidence ellipsoid (m/s 2 ) 3 were extracted as outcome measures.

Fig 2. Agreement and consistency between smartphone placements. Intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) estimates with 95% confidence intervals for agreement or consistency between lower-back and pocket/thigh smartphone placements across balance, Timed Up and Go (TUG), and sit-to-stand (STS) outcomes. Circle markers indicate consistency ICCs, whereas square markers indicate absolute-agreement ICCs. Vertical reference lines denote conventional interpretation thresholds for ICC magnitude: poor (<0.50), moderate (0.50-0.75), good (0.75-0.90), and excellent (>0.90). Values above each marker indicate the ICC point estimate. EC: eyes closed; EO: eyes open.
