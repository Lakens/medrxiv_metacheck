Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Abstract

When benzodiazepines fail in catatonia, clinicians know what to escalate to but not what to stabilize first. We asked a narrower question that clinical records can address: does recovery follow a fixed order? We defined five capacities in plain clinical terms and mapped them onto precision domains of hierarchical inference. The domains are theoryderived clinical abstractions, not latent variables estimated from these data. In 25 consecutive inpatients managed without electroconvulsive therapy, an order fixed before coding held without inversion in every patient, and held again when a hypothesis-blind co-author re-rated all milestones according to a pre-registered protocol. In five patients the sequence was completed while hallucinations and delusions persisted, so the order does not follow the resolution of psychotic symptoms. Bush-Francis scores fell from 26.3 to 2.0. This retrospective study shows that the order held, not why. Prospective studies, preferably with blinded daily rating, are needed to corroborate the order.

Introduction

Catatonia is a distinctive neuropsychiatric syndrome characterized by abnormalities of motor behaviour, volition, speech, and autonomic regulation. 1, 2 Contemporary diagnostic systems, including the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition, Text Revision (DSM-5-TR) and the International Classification of Diseases, 11th Revision (ICD-11), formalize it through observable criteria, improving diagnostic reliability. 3, 4 Yet despite this phenomenological definition, catatonia remains mechanistically unresolved, and its marked heterogeneity is still managed largely through empirical rather than principled approaches, a clinically consequential limitation, given its substantial morbidity and potential mortality if not promptly recognized and treated. 5 Benzodiazepines and electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) are the cornerstones of treatment and are often effective, 1,2 yet a substantial minority of patients respond incompletely to first-line benzodiazepines, 6, 7 and some show paradoxical worsening or excessive behavioural suppression. 8 Reported response to ECT is high, between 59% and 100% across studies, including after benzodiazepine non-response, 9 so the difficulty is rarely the absence of an effective escalation but the absence of a rule for which capacity to stabilize first, particularly where ECT is unavailable, contraindicated or delayed. Escalation can attenuate overt motor manifestations without restoring coherent agency or functional reintegration, and these outcomes are often regarded as idiosyncratic. 9, 7 A central limitation is that catatonia is defined almost exclusively at the level of surface phenomena; DSM-5-TR phenomenology, though clinically useful, offers no account of the latent processes governing action selection, learning, or recovery, leaving clinicians without a principled basis for sequencing interventions when first-line treatments fail.

Recent developments in computational psychiatry offer a framework for this gap.

Bayesian models of brain function and the Free-Energy Principle conceptualize perception, action, and learning as hierarchical inference regulated by precision, the confidence assigned to sensory evidence, action policies, motivational priors, and beliefs about environmental volatility. 10, 11 Within this view, psychopathology arises not from isolated deficits but from misallocation of precision across hierarchical levels, producing unstable inference, impaired agency, or maladaptive belief updating. 11, 12 We therefore hypothesized that the heterogeneity of catatonia reflects distinct expressions of failure within a limited set of precision-control domains rather than multiple unrelated mechanisms. We define five domains, each stated in computational terms and then as something a clinician can observe. Sensory precision (πs) is the weight given to incoming sensory evidence: clinically, whether ordinary light, sound and touch arrive as usable information or as noise that provokes withdrawal or agitation.

Policy precision (β) is confidence in selecting and executing an action: clinically, whether the patient can carry out an action at all, including one prompted by another person, such as opening the mouth when a spoon is brought. Motivational precision (πm) is the weight on prior preferences that makes an outcome worth pursuing: clinically, whether the patient initiates action without prompting. Fast contextual volatility precision (πv_fast) is the estimate of how quickly the present situation is changing: clinically, whether a minor event is treated as such rather than as a catastrophe. Slow structural volatility precision (πv_slow) is the same estimate over long timescales: clinically, restored sleep-wake structure and a stable account of oneself across contexts and caregivers (Fig. 1).

A road analogy may help a clinical reader hold the five together; it describes the different roles and does not by itself establish the recovery order or any treatment effect.

Sensory precision is the information coming up from the road surface; policy precision the steering, by which an intended action is carried out; motivational precision the engine, the drive to set off without being pushed; fast contextual volatility precision the satellite navigation (Global Positioning System, GPS), which re-plans the route as the situation changes; and slow structural volatility precision the driver, who keeps the car on track across changing roads and hours. Table 1 maps all DSM-5-TR catatonia criteria onto these domains, with policy precision conceptualized as a cross-cutting gate on behavioural expression. 12, 13 The status of these five quantities determines how the study can be read. The domains are clinically interpretable abstractions informed by active inference and hierarchical precision models. They were not estimated as latent variables from behavioural data; the π and β notation denotes the theoretical construct being indexed, not a fitted parameter.

The order follows from a prerequisite argument: where a lower capacity is absent, the record carries no information about the capacity above it, so the lower capacity must be documented as returning first. This is a prediction about the order in which capacities can be observed to return in a clinical record, conditional on the dependency structure the companion model assumes; it does not by itself show that the underlying function above cannot recover earlier. Of the 120 possible orderings of five domains, those in which a capacity is documented before another capacity that its definition presupposes are excluded by construction; the framework licenses one order of recovery, πs → β → πm → πv_fast → πv_slow. Because the ordering was specified a priori and then used to operationalize the milestones, the analysis that follows demonstrates internal consistency of that scheme with the records; it does not independently establish the hierarchy. The domain definitions, the ordering and the classification of transitions as separable or nested were fixed in writing before any milestone date was assigned, but after the admissions had taken place and with clinical familiarity with the cohort. The study is therefore hypothesis-generating, and the order it reports was formed with knowledge of the clinical course rather than predicted independently of it.

We examined the records of twenty-five consecutive inpatients with catatonia treated during routine care (Supplementary Note 1), and then had every milestone date re-rated by a co-author blind to the hypothesis.

Methods

Study design and cohort

This study was conducted as a retrospective, hypothesis-generating clinical investigation based on anonymized medical records. We reviewed the charts of twentyfive inpatients who met DSM-5-TR criteria for catatonia and were treated at Onda-daini Hospital, a psychiatric facility without ECT capability. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of the twenty-five patients, including primary diagnosis and catatonia subtype, are summarized in Supplementary Table 1. Five of these twenty-five cases were previously reported in a preliminary case series describing the clinical resolution of catatonia with guanfacine 14 ; however, the present study does not extend or reanalyse pharmacological outcomes but instead re-examines all cases within a unified computational framework.

Between April 2021 and March 2026, these twenty-five patients constituted all consecutive catatonia cases managed at the institution, with no exclusions; all reached sustained functional remission and none were transferred elsewhere for ECT. Cases were identified by review of the hospital's complete inpatient records rather than by diagnostic-code search. Because the hospital has no ECT capability and no external facility accepted transfer for ECT during the study period, treatment-resistant patients were necessarily managed on site to remission rather than transferred out, so the cohort cannot have been depleted of refractory cases through transfer. The corollary is that the cohort contains no non-remitting or relapsing course, and therefore cannot test the framework's predicted signatures of failed recovery (see Discussion). No experimental intervention, protocolized treatment, or prospective assignment was performed. Clinical management was determined solely by treating physicians according to standard practice. The present analysis represents a secondary interpretation of previously collected clinical data.

Diagnostic ascertainment and exclusion of medical causes

Catatonia was diagnosed by DSM-5-TR criteria on longitudinal clinical assessment, and psychiatric diagnoses and clinical features, including malignant risk, were established by chart review. Medical causes were investigated in all twenty-five patients by cranial computed tomography, electroencephalography and blood testing, and in no patient did these identify a condition that explained the clinical picture. The hospital has no facility for cerebrospinal fluid analysis or magnetic resonance imaging, and formal neuropsychological assessment and urine drug screening were not performed, so secondary catatonia from an occult cause cannot be excluded with certainty in these patients. The aetiological scope of the framework, and where a secondary cause would be expected to depart from the presentations studied here, are set out in Supplementary Note 2.

Pharmacological interventions

All treatment was chosen by the treating physicians on clinical grounds during routine care and not by protocol. Every patient received a benzodiazepine (lorazepam in 23, diazepam in one and ethyl loflazepate in one). Pregabalin, for which improvement of catatonia has been reported in a single case, 15 was added in four patients, and memantine, used adjunctively in refractory catatonia, 7 in two. Guanfacine, an alpha2Aadrenoceptor agonist, was used in 16 of the 25 patients. Its indication was catatonia itself, after incomplete response to benzodiazepines, including the autonomic instability of malignant catatonia, and not attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder or agitation as such. The rationale was that noradrenergic modulation of prefrontal signal-to-noise might stabilize action selection where sedation alone had not, following a case series from this institution. 14 The experimental basis is that α2A-adrenoceptor stimulation strengthens prefrontal network activity by inhibiting cyclic AMP-HCN channel signalling. 16 This rationale is distinct from increasing sedation and does not establish an effect on policy precision, nor efficacy in catatonia. Guanfacine was started at a low dose and titrated in relation to response and blood pressure over a range of 1 to 6 mg daily. An antipsychotic was used during contextual stabilization in every patient, most commonly olanzapine (11 patients), brexpiprazole (5), quetiapine (4), aripiprazole (4), lurasidone (4) or paliperidone (3); valproate was used in three and an antidepressant in four patients. Agents by patient and by milestone are given in Supplementary Table 2, and the change in concurrent agents between baseline and remission is reported in the Discussion.

Computational framework and inferential strategy

We employed a qualitative, theory-informed analytic framework grounded in Bayesian inference and the Free-Energy Principle. Precision was treated as a latent construct inferred retrospectively from observable clinical signs, behaviours, and functional capacities documented in the medical records, consistent with Bayesian formulations of individual learning under uncertainty. 10, 17, 18 No precision parameter was estimated numerically. Three things are therefore kept apart: the theoretical construct (the precision term), its clinical operationalization (the milestone definition, an observable event) and an empirically estimated latent variable, which was not obtained; the five domains are clinical abstractions, not latent variables identified from these data. This concerns what can be estimated from the present records; whether the five parameters are identifiable in principle within a specified model is a separate question, addressed in a companion theoretical work. 19 DSM-5-TR criteria served as clinical anchors: each was assigned a priori to one or more domains according to the level of inference most directly implicated by the observable sign (Table 1), 12, 13 and the mapping was then used to operationalize the milestones.

Coding procedure, ambiguity and blinding Primary coding was performed by one investigator (H.S.), who knew the hypothesized order, and every milestone statistic in the Results that is not labelled as from the blinded re-rating derives from this coding. A second rater, blind to the hypothesis, re-rated all milestone dates after the primary analysis had been completed, under a pre-registered plan (Blinded re-rating of milestone dates, below), and agreement between the two ratings is reported. Assignment required judgement in two places. Some signs are compatible with more than one domain. Agitation, for example, can reflect heightened sensory reactivity, weakened control of action or rapid contextual instability. Such a sign was not assigned to a mechanism: an entry was used only where it met a specific milestone definition, and the alternative reading was recorded. Where the record was ambiguous as to whether a behaviour was prompted or spontaneous, the more conservative assignment was taken, that is, β rather than πm. Worked examples of source entries and their resulting assignments are given in Supplementary Note 3. Slow structural volatility precision (πv_slow) corresponds to no single DSM-5-TR criterion; it was ascertained from documented cross-context stability during remission, as defined below, and not from the criterion set.

Ascertainment of resolution

Routine records document the presence of a sign more reliably than its resolution. Two rules were therefore applied. A milestone was dated from the first record entry that positively documented the relevant capacity, that is, an entry describing the behaviour itself, rather than from an entry in which the earlier abnormality was merely absent from the note. Where the record did not establish that a domain's baseline disturbance had been present, the domain was coded as not applicable rather than as resolved; this is why fast contextual volatility precision is missing in three patients of the primary coding. Both rules err towards dating milestones late rather than early. Medical record entries are also more frequent at the acute peak of catatonia than during recovery, so a capacity may have returned some time before the entry that first documents it; neither rule can correct for this. Functional remission was defined as near-complete resolution of catatonic features accompanied by restoration of goal-directed behaviour, without escalation of antipsychotic load or use of ECT.

Sequence analysis of recovery milestones

Five recovery milestones, one per precision domain, were each defined as the first documented, sustained resolution of a domain-specific disturbance present at baseline, rather than as a fixed behaviour, so that heterogeneous presentations could instantiate the same milestone through different observable events. Reduction of sensory precision disturbance (πs) was the first sustained attenuation of sensory-driven reactivity documented at baseline, including autonomic instability, unprovoked agitation, feardriven withdrawal, or sensory-driven defensive behaviour. Stabilization of policy precision (β) was the first sustained tolerance and execution of structured care, feeding or ambulation without behavioural collapse under external guidance. Restoration of motivational precision (πm) was the first sustained recovery of goal-directed drive, evidenced either by spontaneous, unprompted initiation or by self-sustained continuation and generalization of goal-directed behaviour beyond a prompt without step-by-step re-prompting; engagement requiring a fresh external prompt for each step was classified as β rather than πm. Containment of fast contextual volatility (πv_fast) was the first sustained interval, within a day or across repeated interactions within a day, free of short-timescale (within-interaction, second-to-minute) instability documented at baseline, including echo phenomena, context-inappropriate behaviour, abrupt behavioural branching, or non-psychotic fluctuation in responsiveness and communication, irrespective of the presence of psychotic symptoms or antipsychotic treatment. The milestone indexes the contextual coherence of speech and conduct, not the presence or absence of psychotic content: a patient who retains delusional beliefs but whose speech and behaviour are coherent across an interaction reaches it, and residual psychotic symptoms were not rated with a standardized instrument (their documented course is reported separately). Where no such short-timescale instability was documented at baseline, the domain was coded as not applicable. Slow structural reintegration (πv_slow) was the first sustained period (≥24-72 h) of cross-context stability, defined as a consistent level of functioning maintained across day and night and across at least two distinct contexts or caregivers, including restored sleep-wake structure and non-disrupted eating, conversation, mobility and interpersonal responsiveness.

For each patient, a domain was coded only if its baseline disturbance was documented; otherwise it was recorded as not applicable. The day (counted from the onset of catatonic symptoms, the day of onset being day 1) of each applicable milestone was extracted from the medical record, with the supporting record entry retained for audit; every applicable milestone was reached before discharge in every patient, so no censoring was required. Each milestone was scored in the same way, as a single calendar day identified retrospectively from the chart record. Sustained resolution was judged from subsequent documentation rather than from a uniform assessment schedule, so the stored date is the entry at which the capacity was first positively documented; the interval to its confirmation was not recorded separately, and observation density can therefore affect the dates. This applies to πv_fast as it does to πs, β and πm: each was dated to the first day on which its definition was met, and no domain required the rest of the admission to be observed before it could be dated, so all four were dated on the same footing. Domain milestone dates were coded independently of one another, and the hypothesized order (πs → β → πm → πv_fast → πv_slow) was specified a priori. A companion theoretical paper derives this order formally: where a prerequisite is absent, the likelihood is flat in the dependent parameter, so the prerequisite relations form a chain whose topological ordering is unique and identical to the order tested here. That derivation takes only the five domains as input and does not use the milestone timings reported in this paper. 19 For each adjacent domain pair, we computed the proportion of patients in whom both milestones were applicable and the observed order matched the prediction; same-day milestones were treated as ties and the rate of same-day cooccurrence was reported. As a global index, we computed Kendall's coefficient of concordance (W) across patients, with its χ² statistic testing the observed ordering against chance. Ties were resolved by mean ranks and W was corrected for ties.

Because same-day co-occurrence is predicted by the ordering hypothesis rather than being a nuisance, deflating the coefficient for ties would penalize the hypothesis for a prediction it makes; the tie-corrected value is therefore reported as the primary index, with the uncorrected value (W = 0.82, χ² = 72.2) given for comparison. We pre-specified that adjacent-pair concordance not exceeding chance would be interpreted as failure to support the hypothesized sequence. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 32.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA); the descriptive statistics, the coefficient of concordance and Figs. 2 and 3 were independently reproduced in Python with the archived analysis script (see Code availability).

Adjacent-pair separability was classified a priori from the milestone definitions: a pair was labelled separable if neither definition referenced the capacity indexed by the other (πs → β; πm → πv_fast), and nested otherwise (β → πm, in which motivational precision presupposes executable action; πv_fast → πv_slow, in which the slowstructural window presupposes short-timescale stability). This classification was fixed before ordering analysis. For separable pairs, the observed order is not entailed by the definitions, though this does not establish that the domains are mechanistically independent; for nested pairs, we additionally report inter-milestone latency, which the definitions do not constrain.

Blinded re-rating of milestone dates

After the primary analysis had been completed, the five milestone dates were re-rated for all 25 patients from the medical records by a psychiatrist at the same hospital (I.M.) who had previously taken no part in the study, had not seen the manuscript, the preprint or the companion theoretical papers, and was not told the hypothesized order or the first rater's dates. The rater received the five milestone definitions given above, labelled A to E in an order unrelated to the hypothesized sequence, and a rating manual, and entered for each label the first day on which the definition was met, or a code (NA or ND) where it could not be dated. The manual's rules for doubtful cases err towards dating milestones late, as in the primary coding, and the sheet was locked on return (procedure, codes and label correspondence in Supplementary Note 4). The rater returned each milestone as a day count from the onset date recorded for the primary analysis (day of onset = day 1), so the returned sheet carries no calendar information.

The analysis plan, the rating manual and the label correspondence were deposited at Zenodo 21 (doi:10.5281/zenodo.23076104) on 1 October 2026, before any rating had been returned, and the deposit was disclosed to M.T. on the same day; it was held under embargo while the ratings were collected and was made public on 16 October 2026. The plan fixed the primary criterion as the number of inversions of the two separable transitions (πs → β; πm → πv_fast) in the blind ratings: a single inversion refutes the pre-specified order, and no threshold for partial agreement was set. The nested pairs (β → πm; πv_fast → πv_slow) are reported descriptively. Agreement between the two raters is summarized by the intraclass correlation coefficient ICC(A,1) with its 95% confidence interval and Bland-Altman limits of agreement for the milestone day, by Cohen's κ for whether a milestone could be dated at all, and by Kendall's τ-b for the order of the five milestones within each patient; Kendall's W computed on the blind ratings is reported beside the primary value as a reproducibility index, without a threshold. Analyses were run in IBM SPSS Statistics v32 and reproduced with a script archived with the figure code (Code availability). The rater was unblinded, and joined the author list, only after the ratings had been locked.

Course of the underlying disorder

As an exploratory check on whether the milestone order tracks the resolution of the underlying disorder, H.S. extracted from the same records, for each patient, whether the symptoms of that disorder were still documented at discharge. For the 22 patients with schizophrenia or schizoaffective disorder these symptoms were hallucinations and delusions; for the three with a mood disorder they were mood symptoms, with any psychotic features. Where the symptoms resolved, the last entry documenting them was placed relative to the five milestone dates. This extraction was made after the primary analysis, by the unblinded investigator, without a standardized instrument, and is reported descriptively.

Statistics

All tests were two-sided, with α = 0.05. Peak and remission BFCRS scores were compared within patients using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test; because the scores are bounded ordinal sums containing ties, p values are reported from the tie-corrected normal approximation, and the effect size is r = |z|/√n. Descriptive statistics are given as mean ± standard deviation with median and interquartile range. The χ² statistic accompanying Kendall's W was evaluated on k -1 = 4 degrees of freedom in the 22 patients in whom all five milestones were applicable. For each adjacent pair, the direction of the observed order was tested with an exact two-sided sign test restricted to the patients whose two milestones fell on different days and therefore carry order information. No covariates were tested and no adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied: the ordering was specified a priori as a single directional prediction, and the concordance and sign-test statistics are reported as summary indices of that one prediction rather than as independent hypotheses. No Bayesian analysis was performed and no hierarchical or multilevel model was fitted; the design is a within-patient ordinal comparison in which each patient serves as their own reference. Statistics for the blinded re-rating follow the pre-registered plan and are specified under Blinded rerating of milestone dates.

Results

Cohort characteristics

The 25 patients comprised 13 men and 12 women, aged 55.9 ± 13.9 years (median 57, IQR 46-63, range 26-81; individual ages are given by decade in Supplementary Table 1, to limit re-identification). Primary diagnoses were schizophrenia in 18, schizoaffective disorder in four, bipolar disorder in two and major depressive disorder in one. Catatonia was excited in 11 patients, retarded in 10 and malignant in four. Peak Bush-Francis Catatonia Rating Scale (BFCRS) total was 26.3 ± 5.6 (median 26, interquartile range (IQR) 23-30, range 14-34). Per-patient characteristics are given in Supplementary

Table 1 and per-patient BFCRS totals in Supplementary Table 3, and the prevalence of each BFCRS item at peak in Supplementary Table 4.

Treatment context Initial management documented in the records was empirically selected and guidelineconcordant, and reduction of visible agitation or sedation alone did not reliably coincide with functional reintegration: when aroused, many patients remained incoherent, excited, manneristic or frozen, and BFCRS totals frequently stayed elevated. Agents of distinct classes were in use around the milestones (Supplementary Table 2). The same milestone order was documented irrespective of which agents were in use, and narrative coherence and cross-context consistency recovered last.

Summary of outcomes

Across all twenty-five cases, mean BFCRS score decreased from 26.3 ± 5.6 at peak to 2.0 ± 2.4 (median 0, interquartile range (IQR) 0-4) after remission (Wilcoxon signedrank test, z = 4.38, p = 1.2 × 10 ; Supplementary Table 3); all improved, with 13 reaching 0 and 17 scoring 3 or below, and all achieved sustained functional remission. A sensitivity analysis confined to observationally scorable signs left this reduction essentially unchanged (Methods; note to Supplementary Table 3), so it was not an artefact of untested elicited items. Benzodiazepine doses were reduced in many cases after remission. This convergence across heterogeneous presentations supports a shared temporal structure of recovery from catatonia.

Ordered recovery across precision domains

The predicted order (πs → β → πm → πv_fast → πv_slow) held without a single inversion in every patient carrying order information for a given pair, and, when sameday milestones are counted as consistent, in every patient in whom both milestones of a pair were applicable. Because two adjacent transitions are partly definitional (see below), the primary test rests on the two operationally separable transitions (πs → β; πm → πv_fast), and the global concordance across all five milestones (tie-corrected Kendall's W = 0.91, uncorrected 0.82; χ²(4) = 80.4, p = 1.5 × 10 ¹ ; n = 22) is reported as a summary index rather than the principal evidence. Median time from symptom onset increased monotonically across domains: πs, 3 days (IQR 2-7); β, 4 (3-11); πm, 8 (3-14); πv_fast, 14.5 (6.8-23.2); πv_slow, 15 (8-27) (Fig. 2; Supplementary Table 5).

Pairwise, the number of patients carrying order information (no same-day tie) was 15 for πs → β, 12 for β → πm, 18 for πm → πv_fast and 22 for πv_fast → πv_slow, and in each of these the direction was the predicted one (exact sign test, p = 6.1 × 10 , 4.9 × 10 , 7.6 × 10 and 4.8 × 10 , respectively); counting same-day ties as consistent, agreement was 25/25, 25/25, 22/22 and 22/22, respectively (Supplementary Table 6). πv_fast was coded not assessable in three patients. All three had received an antipsychotic from the start of treatment, and the baseline record was ambiguous as to whether short-timescale instability had been present.

For the two separable pairs (πs → β; πm → πv_fast), the order is not entailed by the milestone definitions, and both held without inversion. For the two nested pairs (β → πm; πv_fast → πv_slow), whose definitions share a rank-order dependency, the informative quantity is instead the inter-milestone latency, which the definitions do not constrain and which was non-zero in 12/25 and 22/22 patients, respectively.

Blinded re-rating

In the blinded re-rating (I.M.), neither separable transition was inverted in any patient carrying order information (πs → β, 0 of 16; πm → πv_fast, 0 of 17), so the prespecified order was not refuted. For the nested pairs, inversions were 0 of 11 for β → πm and 0 of 24 for πv_fast → πv_slow (Fig. 3; Supplementary Table 7). Agreement between the blind and primary milestone days, estimated with ICC (A,1), was 0.71 (95% CI 0.46 to 0.86) for πs, 0.72 (0.42 to 0.87) for β, 0.75 (0.50 to 0.88) for πm, 0.90 (0.76 to 0.96) for πv_fast and 0.84 (0.66 to 0.92) for πv_slow; Bland-Altman mean differences and limits of agreement are given in Supplementary Table 7. On applicability, the blind ratings contained no NA or ND entry, whereas the primary coding had three (all πv_fast); in four domains, therefore, both raters dated every patient.

For πv_fast the blind rater dated all 25 patients, including the three coded not assessable in the primary analysis; κ was 0 because, with every patient in one category for the blind rater, the observed agreement (22 of 25) equaled the agreement expected by chance (Supplementary Table 7). The median within-patient Kendall's τ-b between the two raters' orders was 0.95 (range 0.67 to 1.00; n = 25). Tie-corrected Kendall's W in the blind ratings was 0.89 (n = 25) against 0.91 in the primary coding. Both raters' days for every patient and domain are listed in Supplementary Table 8.

Relation to the course of the underlying disorder In five patients (cases 6, 7, 11, 12 and 13; four with schizophrenia and one with schizoaffective disorder), hallucinations and delusions remained documented throughout the admission and at discharge, while all five milestones were attained in the predicted order of recovery from catatonia. In the three patients with a mood disorder (cases 23 to 25), the underlying disorder remitted after πv_fast. In the remaining 17 patients the last documentation of psychotic symptoms fell on the day coded as πv_slow.

In no patient did the underlying disorder remit before πv_fast, and in 22 of 25 its symptoms were documented up to the day of πv_slow or beyond. The coincidence between the last documentation of psychotic symptoms and πv_slow in 17 patients was recorded by the same unblinded rater.

Discussion

We reinterpret catatonia as a structured disturbance of hierarchical precision control underlying its heterogeneous motor and behavioural manifestations and constraining recovery in a state-dependent manner. Across cases, phenomenology and recovery could be parsimoniously organized around five latent precision domains: sensory (πs), policy (β), motivational (πm), and fast and slow volatility precision (πv_fast, πv_slow).

Fig. 1 summarizes this organization. A key feature is the temporal asymmetry between fast contextual and slow structural volatility, which explains why attenuation of acute symptoms frequently failed to produce functional reintegration and why recovery, when it occurred, unfolded in a delayed yet reproducible sequence.

The ordering is a prediction stated so as to fail. Five domains admit 120 possible orderings; the framework licenses one, fixed before the milestones were coded and derived independently of them. 19 More generally, a dependency graph fixes the admissible recovery orders exactly, as the set of its linear extensions, so that a chain such as this one admits a single order; the result holds for any acyclic dependency structure. 22 Within the applicability conditions established there, namely the specified model class, recovery law and gating-dominated rate regime, a single patient in whom a separable transition inverts (organized action stabilizing before sensory-driven instability abates, or contextual instability resolving before goal-directed initiation returns) would falsify the proposed ordering; outside those conditions such an inversion is permitted rather than disconfirming. None occurred here. Because the cohort remitted uniformly, however, it could not test the framework's predictions for failed recovery, which remains the sharper test. Companion theoretical work derives them: the same dependency structure, coupled to evidence-dependent bistability, yields self-sustaining recovery, maintained remission after support is withdrawn, or stable partial recovery and abrupt collapse in one construction. 23 Retrospective review showed that stabilization of organized action repeatedly coincided with clinical turning points. Experience-based dose adjustments, both increases and reductions, often attenuated motor activity or autonomic arousal while patients remained fragmented, frozen or incoherent. By contrast, stabilized action execution was associated with tolerance of structured activity, rest and care without behavioural collapse. As organized action stabilized (β), motivational initiation re-emerged (πm). To assess whether recovery was driven by escalating antipsychotic exposure, we compared the regimen at remission with baseline: concurrent agents had decreased in 9 patients, were unchanged in 14, and increased in only 2, consistent with recovery accompanying stabilization of organized action (β) without requiring escalation of dopaminergic blockade. Counts of concurrent agents do not measure cumulative exposure, dose, or receptor occupancy, 24 so this comparison constrains escalation rather than quantifying dopaminergic blockade. Containment of fast contextual volatility (πv_fast) followed, with slow structural reintegration (πv_slow) consolidating last as cross-context coherence stabilized within a sustained behavioural envelope, not pharmacologically accelerated.

Guanfacine has been reported as a treatment option, 14 and it was in use in most of this cohort around the time organized action stabilized. In the nine patients who were not treated with guanfacine the order held without inversion, so the sequence is not dependent on alpha 2 -adrenoceptor agonism; the claim concerns the gating role of organized action, not pharmacology. Some presentations remit rapidly with benzodiazepine monotherapy, plausibly lower-complexity states in which attenuating excessive sensory precision suffices; the cases here instead required restoration of organized action.

Two existing accounts of catatonia bear directly on the domain assignments used here.

The first is motor: several catatonic signs are better explained by disturbed motor control than by sensory gain, and stereotypies in particular are usually attributed to basal ganglia circuits that store overlearned movement patterns, while waxy flexibility has been linked, though not established, to proprioceptive processing. 25, 26 Reduced benzodiazepine-receptor binding in the left sensorimotor cortex in akinetic catatonia, and delayed late movement-related cortical potentials with an abnormal response to lorazepam, 27, 28 locate the disturbance in the motor circuits that the policy and sensory domains are taken to index; these findings are consistent with the assignments used here but do not test them. The second account is longitudinal: symptom change under benzodiazepines and under ECT has been characterized in previous cohorts, including differential response across symptom clusters. 29, 30 Those studies describe how much and how fast catatonic signs improve, whereas the present question is which capacity returns before which. The two lines of research are complementary rather than competing. A direct comparison would require item-level longitudinal ratings at a sampling density that these records do not have.

All 25 patients remitted without ECT, but the cohort cannot show that following this order improves outcome relative to timely ECT, nor that delaying ECT in favour of the order is safe for a patient in whom ECT is indicated. The framework is not an argument for withholding ECT. The comparison of order-guided sequencing against timely escalation requires a prospective design in a setting where ECT is available.

After the primary analysis, a co-author blind to the hypothesis re-rated every milestone date from the medical records according to a pre-registered plan that fixed a single inversion of a separable transition as the refutation criterion. No inversion was found, and agreement on dates was moderate to good, with ICC (A,1) from 0.71 to 0.90 (Results). This shows that the sequence can be recovered from the record by an independent rater, not that it holds outside this cohort; and both raters read the same documentation, so a bias in what was recorded, as distinct from how it was read, is not excluded. Structured psychopathological information can now be extracted from clinical narratives by natural language processing; an independently specified extraction rule applied to the same records would test whether the domain assignments depend on a human coder at all, which has not been done.

The order did not track the resolution of the underlying disorder. In five patients hallucinations and delusions were still documented at discharge after all five milestones had been passed in order, and in none of the 25 did the underlying disorder remit before πv_fast. Standardized ratings of psychotic symptoms were not available, and a scale such as the PANSS 31 would have been difficult to apply for this purpose: several of its items overlap with the phenomena the BFCRS records, and most presuppose an interview that the acutely catatonic patient cannot give. The record-based course reported here is descriptive and unblinded; what it supports directly is that remission of hallucinations and delusions was not required for any of the five milestones to be attained.

Recovery trajectories were not linear but recurrently reinforcing, formalized in Fig. 4 as the Saito Loop. The term denotes recurrence in the parameters that govern recovery, not a cycle in the recovery trajectory itself: the state does not return to πs, and the sequence is not reiterated once πv_slow is reached. Each domain's stabilization supplied the evidence the next required, from learnable sensory gain through executable action and sustained initiation to contextual and then structural coherence. Disruption at any point would predict non-response, premature escalation, or relapse. However, although causal inference is precluded by the retrospective design, convergence toward a shared phase structure across treatments with distinct modes of action (GABAergic, noradrenergic, dopaminergic/serotonergic, and glutamatergic) is more consistent with a state-dependent constraint on hierarchical inference than with a drug-specific effect. It is a computational hypothesis grounded in hierarchical active inference, 32 whereby higherlevel structural beliefs, once stabilized, exert recurrent top-down constraints on lower-level precision allocation through ongoing message passing between levels. 33, 34 Neurophysiologically, precision-weighted prediction errors at distinct hierarchical levels are encoded by dissociable midbrain and basal-forebrain substrates during sensory learning, 35 which is consistent with the general principle of level-specific precision on which the loop rests, though it does not test the loop's particular allocation. Formally, the recurrence in the loop is threshold-level rather than state-level: accumulated slowtimescale evidence enters only through the threshold at which each lower domain stabilizes, so that the trajectory converges to the recovered configuration rather than to a cycle, and that configuration becomes self-sustaining. The companion theoretical work derives this feedback rather than assuming it. 23 The ordering also runs along the sensorimotor-to-association axis of cortical maturation, primary sensory and motor regions maturing before association cortex. 36 We note the correspondence without claiming that either explains the other: the present account fixes recovery order from dependency structure and is silent about anatomical arrangement, so whether the two share a common pathway remains open.

Fast contextual volatility appeared as moment-to-moment disorganization, echo phenomena, and context-inappropriate branching over short timescales, whereas slow structural volatility appeared as narrative coherence and cross-context consistency, recovering last. The separation between the two was small in this cohort (median 14.5 versus 15 days, with overlapping interquartile ranges), so these data support the ordering rather than a large temporal gap; stronger claims await prospective data. Slow structural reintegration emerged only after sustained containment of fast volatility (Fig. 1).

Integrating these observations yields a unified model, synthesized in Fig. 5 as "Computational titration": the state-contingent alignment of hierarchical precision domains across a single traversal of the recurrent loop (Fig. 4).

Symptom severity alone did not capture the temporal structure of recovery. The ability to taper benzodiazepines after remission is interpretable as a self-sustaining reconfiguration of hierarchical precision control rather than continued dependence on global sensory suppression. It offers a model for apparent treatment failures, paradoxical responses and delayed recoveries. The framework further generates a directly testable prediction for prospective study: once a stable recovery dynamic is established, further pharmacological escalation may be unnecessary or even counterproductive, and controlled reduction of residual agents once organized action has stabilized may itself facilitate recovery.

Whether the framework is general or specific to psychosis-associated catatonia turns on its applicability conditions, which do not reference aetiology. Medical, epileptic, hypoxic, and autoimmune causes account for a substantial share of hospital catatonia, and first-episode psychosis cohorts have been screened for the neuronal autoantibodies that underlie the autoimmune form. 37, 38 Its two dynamical conditions, a common impaired onset and a deficit-closing drive, are where secondary catatonia should depart: a focal or receptor-selective insult need not impair the five domains uniformly, and an active insult opposes closure of the deficit. Non-uniform onset weakens rather than abolishes the prediction, permitting a restricted set of inversions. 19, 22 The ordering should hold after the cause is removed, not during its removal. These findings should be read against three limitations. First, the analysis is retrospective, single-centre, based on a small (n = 25) and uniformly remitting cohort (precluding any test of the framework's failure predictions). The primary milestone coding was drawn from records not generated for this purpose and performed by a single investigator with knowledge of the hypothesis, and the course of the underlying disorder was extracted by the same investigator without a standardized instrument. The blinded re-rating addresses rater bias but not the dependence of both ratings on the same records. Causal inference is precluded, and replication in independent cohorts will be required. The cohort reflects what a psychiatric hospital without ECT admits, so generalization to medical, epileptic and autoimmune catatonia is untested. Second, the framework is partly confirmatory by construction: because the ordering was specified first and then used to define the milestones, the nested pairs can only show internal consistency, and the test of the order rests on the two separable pairs; early milestones often fell on the same day, which leaves fewer informative observations, although strict temporal separation was nonetheless observed in a substantial proportion of patients.

Third, the order in which capacities are first documented need not be the order in which they recover. A capacity cannot be seen until the capacity below it has returned (goaldirected behaviour, for example, can only be identified once organized action is observable), so it may have recovered earlier without leaving a trace in the record; likewise, a capacity that is easier to document may appear to return earlier without having done so. Separating true constraints from these observational dependencies will require prospective, pre-registered validation with predefined criteria, blinded raters, and independent multi-site cohorts. Ordered timing also does not by itself establish mechanistic independence: separable milestones may co-vary through a shared clinical driver (for example, reduced reactivity permitting tolerance of care), so common-cause confounding cannot be excluded.

Conclusion

Catatonia may be interpreted as a disorder of hierarchical precision control in which disruption of organized action is a key constraint during non-recovery. Recovery coincided not with symptom suppression but with stabilization of organized action preceding motivational initiation, followed by contextual and structural reintegration, across heterogeneous presentations and different treatments. The framework defines testable constraints on sequenced recovery, best understood not as a linear cascade but as a recurrent, self-stabilizing loop (Fig. 4); its principal contribution is a falsifiable recovery ordering, carried primarily by the two operationally separable transitions, that prospective, blinded studies can test. The order held again when every milestone was rerated by a co-author blind to the hypothesis. Across criteria, sustained catatonic expression is hypothesized to require collapse or instability of β, which governs confidence in selecting and executing action policies.

The mapping of DSM-5-TR catatonia criteria to precision domains was based on the clinical level at which each sign becomes observable within a hierarchical active inference architecture, drawing on established models rather than introducing de novo constructs. 10, 11, 17 In this framework, DSM criteria were not treated as independent symptoms with one-to-one mechanisms, but as behavioural readouts of disturbances in precision weighting across sensory, policy, motivational and volatility-related levels of inference. Signs dominated by autonomic arousal, grimacing, defensive withdrawal or unprovoked agitation were assigned primarily to sensory precision (πs), because they indicate excessive gain on exteroceptive or interoceptive prediction errors. Signs involving failure to initiate, sustain or flexibly select actions, including stupor, negativism and stereotyped behavioural narrowing, were assigned to policy precision (β), reflecting reduced confidence in action policies and impaired psychomotor control.

Mutism and loss of spontaneous goal-directed engagement were mapped to motivational precision (πm), as these signs reflect impaired precision over prior preferences sufficient to initiate action. Echo phenomena, mannerisms and contextinappropriate behaviours were mapped to fast contextual volatility (πv_fast), because they indicate unstable moment-to-moment contextual inference and rapid behavioural switching. Slow structural volatility (πv_slow) was not mapped to a single DSM criterion, but to the delayed recovery of cross-context coherence, narrative continuity and stable interpersonal engagement during remission. Thus, DSM-5-TR criteria served as observable anchors for retrospective coding, whereas precision domains provided the latent computational structure used to interpret recovery trajectories. Several criteria load onto more than one domain, and these assignments are interpretive rather than unique: agitation, for example, is attributed jointly to elevated sensory precision, reduced policy precision, and elevated fast contextual volatility (πs↑ + β↓ and/or πv_fast↑), and manneristic or context-inappropriate behaviours to elevated fast volatility interacting with unstable policy selection, so plausible alternative assignments exist for such multi-determined signs. The ordering hypothesis is nonetheless robust to this ambiguity, because it is defined over the recovery of each domain rather than over the domain attribution of any single criterion: reassigning an individual sign changes which milestone a given behaviour helps index, not the predicted sequence of domain stabilization. Criteria whose attribution is least ambiguous (e.g., autonomic instability → πs; stupor → β/πm) anchor the milestones most heavily, whereas multi-determined signs contribute corroboratively rather than decisively.
