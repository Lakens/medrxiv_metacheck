Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Text Sentence
p = 0.5575 A likelihood-ratio test compared models with and without this interaction term, yielding p = 0.5575.

For metascientific articles demonstrating the rate of misinterpretations of non-significant results is high, see:

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Abstract

Postmarketing pharmacovigilance relies on spontaneous adverse event reporting and laborintensive observational studies, which suffer from underreporting and delayed risk detection. Large language model (LLM) agents could automate real-world evidence generation, but generalist agents make ad hoc analytical decisions that compromise epidemiological validity and reproducibility. Here we present MedCheckpoint, a multi-agent LLM system for automated postmarketing pharmacovigilance. MedCheckpoint grounds each analytical stage in a curated, source-traceable medical knowledge base, locks core study-design parameters before execution, performs patient-level analyses with human-written deterministic statistical tools, and enforces checkpoint rules that abort infeasible analyses, with optional human-inthe-loop review. We evaluated MedCheckpoint using a 54-question benchmark on a dualpurpose simulated electronic health record (EHR) testbed of 2.7 million individuals, designed with reliable clinical signals to evaluate routine analytical accuracy alongside spurious signals to stress-test system rigor. Across the benchmark, MedCheckpoint outperformed two coding-agent baselines in report quality across five dimensions rated by three independent LLM judges (overall means 4.81-4.88 versus 4.25-4.55 and 4.02-4.29) and yielded higher run-to-run consistency (mean within-question standard deviation 0.116-0.186 versus up to 0.440). Crucially, while baselines failed on up to 2 of 21 runs when handling infeasible comparisons or unmeasurable outcomes, MedCheckpoint produced no misleading failures across all seven stress-test trap questions with known data limitations. MedCheckpoint enables reliable, auditable, and scalable pharmacovigilance analysis.

Introduction

Preapproval randomized controlled trials are the gold standard for establishing drug efficacy, but their limited sample sizes, short follow-up, and restrictive eligibility criteria leave rare, delayed, and subgroup-specific adverse effects undetected 1, 2 . Postmarketing pharmacovigilance is therefore essential for identifying risks that emerge only in routine clinical use 1 . Current practice relies mainly on spontaneous adverse event reporting and manually conducted observational studies 2, 3 . Spontaneous reporting suffers from severe underreporting and reporting bias 2, 4 , whereas observational studies are typically reactive and hypothesis-driven, requiring custom cohort construction, statistical analysis, and expert interpretation for each safety question 3, 5 . This case-by-case paradigm consumes substantial time and specialized expertise, fundamentally limiting the scale and timeliness of drug safety surveillance, particularly for long-term treatment effects that may take years to manifest 1, 3, 6.

LLM agents offer a route to breaking this scalability bottleneck by automating complex analytical workflows. In related clinical domains, LLM agents have been shown to perform clinical calculations with curated toolkits 7 , to assist the design of randomized controlled trials 8 , and to complete multi-step clinical tasks in controlled evaluations 9 . However, automating postmarketing pharmacovigilance poses a distinct methodological challenge. Proactive surveillance tests one drug exposure against many clinical outcomes, producing long multi-task workflows that rapidly exhaust the model's context 10 . Under this load, generalist agents improvise analytical decisions mid-workflow 11 , and prompt-based instructions alone cannot enforce epidemiological rules across extended execution chains. Such improvisation compounds into silent protocol departures, omitted statistical tests, and discrepancies between planned and executed analyses-failure modes that are unacceptable in clinical and regulatory settings, where an irreproducible decision can directly alter a safety conclusion.

Here we present MedCheckpoint, a multi-agent LLM system designed to automate postmarketing pharmacovigilance under explicit epidemiological constraints. MedCheckpoint organizes a curated medical knowledge base by analytic stage-query interpretation, cohort specification, analysis execution, and report generation-so that each agent decision is grounded in source-traceable, human-verified evidence rather than improvised. A protocol lock prospectively fixes core study-design parameters before analysis begins. Patient-level computations are delegated to human-written deterministic statistical tools instead of code generated at runtime, and a checkpoint engine records execution status and aborts analyses that fail predefined feasibility rules, with optional human-in-the-loop review at critical decision points. In this architecture, the LLM serves as an interface and coordinator, while the decisions that determine validity are governed by verified knowledge and prespecified rules.

We evaluated MedCheckpoint on a benchmark of 54 natural-language drug safety questions using a dual-purpose simulated EHR from 2.7 million individuals, which is designed with reliable clinical signals to evaluate routine analytical accuracy alongside spurious signals to stress-test system rigor. MedCheckpoint is evaluated against two baselines sharing the same backbone LLM: a general-purpose coding agent, and the same agent supplied with detailed methodological guidance. We used LLM judges to support scalable, automated assessment of report quality. Across five quality dimensions rated by three independent LLM judges, MedCheckpoint achieved the highest report quality and the most consistent outputs across repeated runs. On seven questions with known data limitations, it produced no misleading failure, correctly aborting infeasible comparisons while baselines occasionally substituted general medical knowledge for data analysis. A case study on effect modification by preexisting diabetes illustrates how the protocol lock and deterministic tools prevent the midanalysis revisions that compromised baseline analyses. These results demonstrate that constraining LLM agents with verified knowledge and deterministic workflows delivers reliable and auditable pharmacovigilance analysis at scale.

Results

Overview of MedCheckpoint workflow

MedCheckpoint structures exploratory EHR-based pharmacovigilance into three stages: protocol generation, analysis, and reporting (Fig. 1). In the first stage, the system converts a natural language drug safety question into a structured study protocol. The protocol defines the drug exposure, comparator when applicable, target population, safety outcomes, covariates, and statistical analysis plan.

MedCheckpoint uses a curated medical knowledge base to support this step. The knowledge base provides standardized drug and outcome definitions, as well as source-traceable medical evidence. These resources help the system identify both core safety outcomes, which are directly relevant to the question, and exploratory safety outcomes, which could reveal additional potential safety signals (Fig. 2).

After the protocol is generated, MedCheckpoint applies a protocol lock. This lock prevents the system from changing key study design elements during analysis. These locked elements include the exposure definition, outcome definitions, cohort criteria, and main statistical plan. The purpose of the lock is to reduce post hoc changes that could bias the analysis.

During the analysis stage, MedCheckpoint does not write new statistical code from scratch. Instead, it coordinates a set of human-written deterministic tools. These tools perform cohort construction, confounding control, multi-outcome analysis, and statistical testing. This design makes the analytical workflow more reproducible and easier to audit.

MedCheckpoint also uses checkpoints during execution. These checkpoints summarize whether the study is feasible and whether each analytical step has completed successfully. For example, a checkpoint can report whether the exposed cohort is large enough, whether outcomes are observed frequently enough, or whether statistical models have converged. In fully automated mode, predefined rules stop analyses that fail critical feasibility checks. In human-in-the-loop mode, researchers can review checkpoint diagnostics, decide whether the analysis should continue, and provide feedback if needed.

In the reporting stage, MedCheckpoint first summarizes the results from the EHR analysis. It then compares these findings with evidence from the medical literature. The final outputs include the study protocol, analysis results, checkpoint records, and the rationale for key decisions. These outputs are designed to support researcher review and auditability.

Benchmark construction

We evaluated MedCheckpoint using a purpose-built pharmacovigilance benchmark of 54 natural language drug safety questions. The complete set of benchmark questions is provided in Supplementary Table 1. The benchmark was designed to test two capabilities. First, we assessed whether each system could produce appropriate epidemiological analyses. Second, we assessed whether each system avoided unsupported conclusions when the available data were insufficient.

The benchmark used a simulation-based longitudinal EHR dataset generated by Synthea. The dataset contains drug exposure and health outcome records for 2,709,803 individuals. Associations in these records can reflect assumptions of the data-generating model rather than true clinical drug effects 12.

We deliberately used simulated longitudinal EHRs to evaluate analytical rigor without treating reproduction of established drug effects as the criterion for success. This setting allowed us to examine whether agents remained faithful to the analyzed data when findings diverged from clinical expectations. This design was also intended to test whether agents could recognize potentially misleading signals and avoid treating apparent associations as established drug effects. Both capabilities are essential for drawing defensible conclusions in pharmacovigilance.

The 54 questions covered three types of pharmacovigilance tasks. Twenty-one questions examined potential adverse drug reactions or off-target effects. Fourteen questions compared safety outcomes between drugs. Nineteen questions evaluated dose-specific or patient subgroup-specific safety patterns.

Baseline systems

We compared MedCheckpoint with two baseline systems. Baseline A was a general-purpose autonomous coding agent. It could write and execute code to answer the safety questions, but it did not have access to MedCheckpoint's knowledge base, protocol lock, checkpoint rules, or specialized analysis tools.

Baseline B used the same execution framework as Baseline A, but it received additional methodological guidance derived from MedCheckpoint. This guidance was provided through prompts. The full study-specific prompts for both baselines are provided in Supplementary Note 1. Baseline B therefore tested whether prompting alone could reproduce the benefits of the full MedCheckpoint system.

All three systems used the same underlying LLM and the same EHR dataset. Each system was run three times for each question. The comparison with Baseline B was intended to isolate the added value of MedCheckpoint's system design, including its curated knowledge base, deterministic tools, protocol lock, and checkpoint enforcement mechanisms. A qualitative comparison of system capabilities is provided in Supplementary Fig. 1. at checkpoints. Outputs include intermediate statistics, audit trails documenting analytical steps and decisions, and analysis reports.

Overall quality and consistency of pharmacovigilance reports

The overall quality of analytical outputs and the methodological rigor of the analytical process are central to the value of autonomous EHR-based pharmacovigilance. Broader exploration of drugs and outcomes is useful only if the findings provide a sound basis for assessing potential safety signals and planning further investigation.

We therefore evaluated analytical quality using the completed reports and structured results from all three systems. The evaluation covered five dimensions of study design, analysis, and interpretation. Kimi-K3 (Kimi), DeepSeek-V4-Flash (DeepSeek), and MiniMax-M3 (MiniMax) served as LLM judges. Each scored every report on a 1-5 scale for each dimension. The scoring rubric described poor, intermediate, and strong performance in each dimension (Supplementary Note 2).

Design Alignment assessed whether the exposure definition, patient population, comparison group, and planned analysis addressed the research question. It also assessed whether reports justified these choices and the feasibility of the analysis.

Cohort

Validity assessed whether reports accurately described the study groups and design, including how well matching made the groups comparable. It also assessed whether reports clarified the population to which findings applied and addressed residual confounding-bias from factors not fully controlled in the analysis.

Statistical

Rigor assessed whether methods were appropriate and special cases, such as analyses with few outcome events, were handled correctly. It also assessed whether conclusions were supported by formal tests, estimates of uncertainty, and correction for multiple comparisons to limit false-positive findings.

Outcome Validity assessed whether outcome identification and interpretation were supported by the available data. This included handling outcomes that could not be measured reliably, assessing the validity of proxy measures used as indirect indicators, and comparing findings with prior evidence.

Interpretive Integrity assesses whether reports distinguish association from causation. It also assesses whether key claims are traceable to supporting evidence and whether residual confounding and uncertainty are explained for specific findings.

MedCheckpoint received the highest mean scores across all five dimensions from each of the three judges (Fig. 3a; Supplementary Table 2). The quality evaluation included 130 completed reports from MedCheckpoint, 158 from Baseline B, and 154 from Baseline A. Although MedCheckpoint completed fewer analyses, stopping before completion is warranted when the available data or study design cannot support a valid analysis. The following sections examine when analyses should proceed or stop and whether greater completion is accompanied by higher report quality.

In the order Kimi, DeepSeek, and MiniMax, MedCheckpoint's overall mean scores across the five dimensions were 4.88, 4.81, and 4.86. The corresponding scores were 4.55, 4.25, and 4.32 for Baseline B and 4.29, 4.02, and 4.15 for Baseline A. With the underlying LLM held constant, Baseline B scored above Baseline A but below MedCheckpoint. This pattern supports the added value of integrating knowledge support, analysis tools, and process constraints beyond methodological guidance alone. During development, MedCheckpoint generated reports of broadly similar quality when using either DeepSeek or MiniMax as its backbone LLM. This observation suggested that the framework's methodological safeguards were not specific to a single backbone LLM.

Design Alignment.

In the same judge order, MedCheckpoint's mean scores were 4.80, 4.77, and 4.64. These scores are consistent with MedCheckpoint's use of a study protocol to guide autonomous analysis. The protocol links the research question to key constraints on exposure, population, and comparison.

Cohort Validity.

MedCheckpoint's mean scores were 4.98, 4.87, and 4.90, all higher than those of both baselines. For example, in a report comparing clopidogrel with warfarin, MedCheckpoint specified that its estimates applied to treated patients within the region of propensity score overlap. The report also discussed channeling bias, or confounding when clinical characteristics influence treatment selection, and unmeasured clinical differences. In contrast, a separate Baseline A report on calcium channel blockers labeled a comparison with never-users as an active-comparator design. Its comparison group had no recorded use of calcium channel blockers, rather than being defined by use of an alternative treatment.

Statistical Rigor.

This dimension showed the largest gap between MedCheckpoint and both baselines. MedCheckpoint's mean scores were 4.85, 4.77, and 4.88. The corresponding scores were 3.78, 3.23, and 3.25 for Baseline B and 3.53, 2.99, and 3.17 for Baseline A.

MedCheckpoint's human-written deterministic tools correct for multiple comparisons and perform formal interaction tests, which assess whether associations differ across patient subgroups. The system requires the corresponding outputs before report generation, reducing reliance on agents to implement these steps during execution.

For example, MedCheckpoint's exploratory statin report identified an association with proteinuria, or protein in the urine, that was statistically significant before correction. The report noted that the association did not remain significant after false discovery rate correction. Report safety under data limitations

In addition to quality and precision, safety and reliability are important aspects of pharmacovigilance reports. When data are limited, reports should state the limitations and explain how they affect the conclusions. They should not present overly confident findings.

We used a targeted challenge set of seven prespecified trap questions to assess whether systems could recognize and appropriately handle known data limitations (Fig. 4; Supplementary Note 3). Three questions involved use of both study drugs, which prevented the intended comparison between groups receiving one drug but not the other. Four involved key outcomes that could not be reliably identified from the available records. Each system was run three times per question, yielding 21 runs per system. Kimi, DeepSeek, and MiniMax evaluated the resulting reports or abort records using a dedicated prompt and reference information on the known data limitations provided only to the judges. Using a prespecified rubric, they assigned one of three handling categories based on evidence in the system outputs.

Handled correctly required identifying the specific limitation and responding appropriately. For infeasible comparisons, this meant recognizing that the intended groups could not be formed. Systems then had to stop for that reason or explicitly state that the comparison was infeasible. For unmeasurable outcomes, systems had to identify the affected outcomes based on data coverage, avoid unsupported effect claims, and analyze measurable components.

Partly handled included noting confounding or group imbalance without recognizing that the comparison was infeasible. It also included recognizing that some outcomes were unmeasurable but incorrectly extending this limitation to measurable outcomes or downplaying its importance. Interpreting zero recorded events as evidence of no clinical effect also qualified. Vague or unrelated reasons for stopping also precluded a rating of Handled correctly.

Handled incorrectly included presenting infeasible comparisons as valid, reporting unsupported findings for unmeasurable outcomes, or omitting key limitations. It also included using clinically inappropriate proxies, meaning recorded outcomes that did not validly represent the outcomes of interest.

We separately counted Misleading failures using five predefined error types. These included unsupported findings or effect claims for unmeasurable outcomes and clinically inappropriate proxies. They also included presenting infeasible comparisons as valid and omitting key unmeasurable outcomes or their measurement limitations. The fifth type was interpreting inadequate data coverage as evidence of no clinical association or risk. For each judge, runs were counted by their primary error type, separately from the handling categories.

None of the three judges identified misleading failures in MedCheckpoint's 21 evaluated runs. In the order Kimi, DeepSeek, and MiniMax, Baseline B had 0, 1, and 2 such runs. Baseline A had 1, 2, and 1, respectively (Fig. 4c).

Two examples illustrate the distinction between answering from clinical knowledge and drawing research conclusions from the specified data. Baseline A answered a question about sex differences in amlodipine-associated peripheral edema, or swelling caused by fluid accumulation in the limbs. It reported incidences of 5.6% in men and 14.6% in women, describing these as approximate figures from prescribing information. It did not inspect the data or disclose that peripheral edema was not reliably measurable in the dataset. All three judges counted this response as a misleading failure.

Baseline B answered a metformin question involving patients with chronic kidney disease without inspecting the data. It discussed lactic acidosis, in which lactate accumulation is accompanied by increased blood acidity. It also described kidney function thresholds for metformin use and vitamin B12 deficiency. The response did not disclose that lactic acidosis and B12 deficiency were not reliably measurable in the dataset. All three judges classified this run as Handled incorrectly, but only MiniMax included it in Misleading failures. The original execution records likewise showed that both responses omitted these data-coverage limitations.

MedCheckpoint also had the most runs classified as Handled correctly. In the order Kimi, DeepSeek, and MiniMax, the counts were 19, 20, and 20 of 21 runs. The corresponding counts were 16, 16, and 17 for Baseline B and 13, 13, and 15 for Baseline A. All remaining MedCheckpoint runs were Partly handled; none were Handled incorrectly (Fig. 4b).

To summarize handling performance, we assigned scores of 1 to Handled correctly, 0.5 to Partly handled, and 0 to Handled incorrectly. The Safe handling score was the mean across runs, with higher values indicating better handling of data limitations. MedCheckpoint had the highest scores: 0.952, 0.976, and 0.976. The corresponding scores were 0.857, 0.833, and 0.881 for Baseline B and 0.786, 0.786, and 0.833 for Baseline A (Fig. 4a; Supplementary Table 10).

The largest differences in correct handling occurred when the intended comparison was infeasible. In all 9 runs of this type, MedCheckpoint identified the limitation and terminated the analysis, and all three judges rated them as Handled correctly. The corresponding counts were 6, 5, and 6 for Baseline B and 2, 3, and 4 for Baseline A. In runs not rated Handled correctly, both baselines often noted confounding or group imbalance without recognizing that the intended comparison was infeasible. For the 12 runs involving key unmeasurable outcomes, MedCheckpoint instead generated reports in every run, with 10, 11, and 11 classified as Handled correctly. The remaining runs were Partly handled because judges considered some outcomes labeled unmeasurable to be measurable in these data. MedCheckpoint thus aborted infeasible comparisons while continuing analysis and reporting when measurable components remained. Analysis completion varied across systems and repeated runs of the same question. We examined these differences and whether completing more analyses was accompanied by higher report quality.

The resulting proportions describe system behavior within this selected sample, rather than completion rates across the full benchmark.

We used LLM assistance to organize the original reports, analysis outputs, and execution logs. Final judgments were made through human review. We classified execution outcomes into three categories, separately from report quality. Completed with available data denotes conducting a data analysis and reporting findings and limitations based on its outputs. Properly stopped (stopped at cohort or matching checks) denotes stopping when cohort or matching checks indicate that analysis cannot proceed under the current protocol. A case of researcher-guided protocol refinement

Human expertise remains a cornerstone of clinical research. MedCheckpoint enables researchers to guide study design and intervene at analytical checkpoints. These review points allow timely input of domain knowledge to guide the analysis and correct its course when needed.

We present a demonstration of researcher-guided protocol refinement after automated runs encountered design-related barriers. The selected question compared amlodipine and metoprolol in adults starting treatment for hypertension, or high blood pressure. It covered cardiovascular and adverse outcomes. We compared two conditions, without and with guidance. The unguided condition used only the original research question. In the guided condition, a researcher note informed by cohort construction and abort records was added to the question before a new protocol was generated.

Both conditions used the same system version, underlying LLM, and study dataset. We conducted five independent runs per condition. Each run generated a new protocol and then proceeded in fully automated mode. Completion required both an analysis report and a final results table. We reviewed locked protocols, cohort and matching outputs, and abort records to assess how guidance informed study design and execution.

In all five unguided runs, the Case Generator required hypertension for inclusion. Four protocols added exclusions for varying combinations of other conditions. These included heart failure, in which the heart cannot fill or pump blood adequately, and prior myocardial infarction (heart attack). Two runs stopped because the metoprolol comparison group was empty. Two others stopped because the amlodipine group was empty. None of these four runs reached statistical analysis.

The only completed run omitted these additional exclusions. It included 48,025 amlodipine users and 35,118 metoprolol users. Matching yielded 3,454 pairs, each containing one patient from each drug group. This run showed that an exploratory comparison remained feasible when the population with hypertension was retained without these additional exclusions.

Based on the cohort construction and abort records, the researcher provided the following researcher note: Keep the study focused on its intended population -adults with hypertension who are new users of amlodipine or metoprolol. Within that hypertensive population, do not narrow the cohort further by excluding patients with common cardiovascular comorbidities such as heart failure, prior myocardial infarction, atrial fibrillation, or stroke: because metoprolol is frequently prescribed to such patients, excluding these conditions removes most of the comparator arm and makes the comparison infeasible. Retain hypertension as the core eligibility requirement, and keep an analyzable population representative of real-world users of both drugs, unless a specific exclusion is essential to the research question or patient safety.

The note addressed which patients to include or exclude. It did not specify an expected effect direction or magnitude. The same note was used in all five guided runs.

With guidance, all five runs retained hypertension as an inclusion criterion and omitted the additional cardiovascular exclusions. Completion increased from 1/5 without guidance to 5/5 with guidance. Cohort sizes were identical to those of the completed unguided run. Matching yielded 3,448-3,454 pairs per run.

The reports nevertheless noted that retaining patients with coexisting cardiovascular conditions could leave residual confounding despite propensity score adjustment. Patients receiving the two drugs could still differ in their reasons for treatment and disease severity. The matched groups also differed in the year patients entered the study and the frequency of visits and tests. These differences could affect whether outcomes were recorded or detected. The reports therefore treated the findings as exploratory associations requiring further validation, not as a basis for clinical prescribing recommendations. This demonstration illustrates how saved protocols and abort records can support researcherguided protocol refinement. The researcher used these records to identify restrictive eligibility criteria and provide natural language guidance for subsequent protocols. MedCheckpoint then executed the new protocols, performed statistical analyses, and reported results, allowing exploratory analyses to proceed.

A case study of stratified pharmacovigilance analysis

To help readers understand MedCheckpoint's automated workflow, we present a case study of its application to stratified pharmacovigilance analysis. The case addresses the question: "Is the hyperglycemic effect of systemic corticosteroids modified by pre-existing diabetes?" Systemic corticosteroids act throughout the body. Hyperglycemia means abnormally high blood glucose.

Answering this question requires retaining patients with and without diabetes before treatment. Stratified analyses estimate the drug-hyperglycemia association separately in these two groups. A formal interaction test assesses whether these associations differ. All three systems attempted stratified analyses but differed in how they revised analyses, handled few outcome events, and interpreted evidence (Fig. 6). The simulation-based setting also allowed us to examine how systems responded when observed associations conflicted with established clinical expectations.

Baseline A compared new users of oral prednisone with never-users. Initial propensity score matching yielded 4,968 pairs, but absolute standardized mean differences (SMDs) for age were 0.764 without pre-existing diabetes and 0.894 with diabetes. This indicates substantial imbalance. Baseline A discarded these matches and analyzed the full cohort of 214,366 individuals. It used regression adjustment to account for measured patient characteristics in the statistical model.

Baseline A's early subgroup model omitted the exposure variable identifying prednisone use. It misinterpreted the estimated age association as the treatment association, then corrected this error. After correction, the odds ratio (OR) for hyperglycemia in the subgroup without pre-existing diabetes was 0.112, with a 95% confidence interval (CI) of 0.073-0.172.

Pooled logistic models combined both diabetes subgroups to test interaction. For hyperglycemia diagnoses recorded at any time after cohort entry, the interaction p value was 4.02 × 10¹. The composite outcome counted either hyperglycemia or newly initiated diabetes medication within 365 days. Its interaction p value was 7.34 × 10. Baseline A's execution log then questioned the protective association: "Prednisone should INCREASE hyperglycemia. Why does it show DECREASED?" Baseline A cited selection bias and population differences as reasons for reanalysis. It argued that the groups differed in ways that could distort the comparison. It required at least one visit in the year before cohort entry in both groups and redesigned matching.

When individual matching proved slow, Baseline A switched to matching within groups defined by diabetes status, sex, and age. Each treated patient could have up to five controls. After refitting, interaction p values were 0.27 for hyperglycemia diagnoses and 0.10 for the 365-day composite outcome. A subsequent Cox model, which analyzes time to an event, yielded an interaction p value of 0.34. Two of the five measured patient characteristics (covariates) still had absolute SMDs above 0.1, with a maximum of 0.54.

The final report concluded that effect modification was unsupported. It did not present the corrected early results or explain the changed interaction evidence after revising the population and matching. Observed results and their conflict with clinical expectations thus influenced Baseline A's subsequent analytical choices. However, the report did not adequately describe how these choices affected its conclusions.

Baseline B also analyzed oral prednisone, with hyperglycemia within 90 days as the primary outcome. However, its definition of pre-existing diabetes included impaired glucose tolerance and metabolic syndrome. Impaired glucose tolerance is an intermediate state of abnormal glucose regulation, not a diabetes diagnosis. Metabolic syndrome is a cluster of conditions associated with increased diabetes and cardiovascular risk. The matched cohorts of Baseline B and MedCheckpoint shared 4,687 treated individuals with identical treatment initiation dates. Among them, 881 received Baseline B's diabetes label solely because of prior impaired glucose tolerance records. Its diabetes subgroup therefore extended beyond diagnosed diabetes.

Baseline B tried different propensity score models and matching calipers. Calipers limit the permitted difference between propensity scores for a match. Final matching produced 4,990 pair records from 4,990 treated individuals and 2,421 distinct controls, with controls reused. It reported both matched estimates and regression-adjusted estimates from the unmatched cohort. Six of 31 covariates remained imbalanced after matching, with absolute SMDs above 0.1 and a maximum of 0.36.

Under Baseline B's subgroup definitions, the primary outcome occurred in 0 treated patients and 1 control without diabetes. The corresponding counts in the diabetes subgroup were 1 and 16. Baseline B considered Firth penalized likelihood, which reduces small-sample bias in logistic regression, or other approaches. However, the final script's "Firth-like regularization" label did not describe its implementation. It used ordinary, unpenalized logistic regression with the Broyden-Fletcher-Goldfarb-Shanno optimizer. This algorithm fits the model but does not itself apply the Firth correction. Recognizing the problem and proposing an appropriate method did not ensure its implementation.

In Baseline B's diabetes subgroup, the OR was 0.063 with a 95% CI of 0.008-0.474. Interaction p values were 0.91 in the unmatched adjusted analysis and 0.98 in the matched analysis. The report acknowledged that the subgroup without diabetes provided insufficient information for a reliable answer. Yet it attributed apparent subgroup differences entirely to sparse-data bias and described the interaction as "precisely estimated". However, the matched interaction coefficient had a standard error of 4.71 on the logarithmic odds scale used by logistic regression. This large standard error indicated substantial uncertainty, contradicting the claim of precision.

The report also interpreted the protective association in the diabetes subgroup as a likely artifact of the data-generating process. However, failure to detect interaction with sparse data did not by itself establish that bias or artifacts caused the subgroup differences. Despite acknowledging limitations, Baseline B had not fully translated methodological guidance into reliable execution and interpretation.

MedCheckpoint followed the Case Generator's protocol, with core study-design parameters fixed by protocol lock. The protocol specified pre-existing type 2 diabetes as the effect modifier and required retaining both subgroups. It compared adult new users of systemic corticosteroids with never-users over a two-year outcome window. Execution records confirmed that all patients in the exposed group started prednisone.

Matching was performed separately within the two diabetes subgroups. It yielded 6,715 oneto-one pairs without patient reuse: 6,354 without and 361 with pre-existing type 2 diabetes. After matching, 9 of 13 covariates had absolute SMDs below 0.1. Emergency care utilization remained imbalanced, with an absolute SMD of 0.357. After excluding pairs with unresolved diagnosis information, 6,674 pairs entered the hyperglycemia analysis. Matching diagnostics were retained for inspection.

MedCheckpoint's Analyst uses human-written deterministic statistical tools to handle sparse data, perform formal interaction tests, and correct for multiple comparisons. In patients without pre-existing type 2 diabetes, there were 2 hyperglycemia events in the exposed group and 4 in controls. The tool used Firth logistic regression, yielding an OR of 0.528 with a 95% CI of 0.123-2.260.

In the subgroup with pre-existing type 2 diabetes, it used ordinary logistic regression with standard errors clustered by matched pair. This accounts for dependence between patients in the same pair. The OR was 0.901 with a 95% CI of 0.471-1.724, and the overall OR was 0.725 with a 95% CI of 0.411-1.278.

The pooled logistic model tested the interaction between corticosteroid exposure and diabetes status. A likelihood-ratio test compared models with and without this interaction term, yielding p = 0.5575. Benjamini-Hochberg correction for multiple comparisons gave a q value of 0.9309.

The hazard ratio (HR) interaction model failed to converge to a stable numerical solution. The tool flagged this failure and returned no interaction p value. Structured outputs recorded the estimation methods, correction results, and failure reasons. These records allowed the actual statistical execution to be checked, rather than inferred from method names.

MedCheckpoint's Report Writer stated that the OR test did not detect statistically significant effect modification. This did not establish equal associations across subgroups. The failed HR model provided no interaction test result, rather than evidence against effect modification.

The report also compared the findings with knowledge base evidence. The data did not reproduce the established increase in hyperglycemia risk with corticosteroids. The report described this as insufficient evidence in these data, not evidence of no association. Clinical expectations therefore informed interpretation without replacing the findings from the current analysis. This case illustrates how MedCheckpoint uses deterministic tools to execute statistical methods and make their implementation verifiable. Recorded estimates, corrections, and failures support interpretation consistent with the available evidence.

Discussion

In this study, we present MedCheckpoint, a domain-grounded multi-agent framework for automated postmarketing pharmacovigilance. Our results show that LLM agents, when constrained by curated biomedical knowledge, prespecified epidemiologic rules, deterministic statistical workflows, and human-in-the-loop checkpoints, can generate more reliable and reproducible pharmacovigilance analyses than generalist coding-agent baselines. These findings suggest that LLM-based systems have the potential to help shift pharmacovigilance from a predominantly reactive, case-by-case paradigm toward a more systematic, scalable, and proactive model of postmarketing drug safety surveillance.

The potential public health value of such systems is particularly high for adverse effects that are delayed, uncommon, population-specific, or difficult to detect in preapproval trials. Randomized controlled trials are often underpowered to identify rare harms and can exclude older adults, patients with multimorbidity, pregnant individuals, or people receiving polypharmacy 1, 13, 14 . Large-scale electronic health record and claims data can complement trials by capturing medication use across broader and more clinically heterogeneous populations 15 . However, this opportunity has historically been constrained by the expertise and time required to translate each safety question into a valid observational study 16 . MedCheckpoint addresses this bottleneck by automating major components of the pharmacovigilance pipeline while preserving key safeguards required for epidemiologic validity. Although automated screening cannot by itself establish causality, it can prioritize signals for confirmatory evaluation and thereby reduce preventable harm from adverse drug reactions.

Our findings also highlight an important distinction between automation and rigor. Generalpurpose LLM agents are increasingly capable of writing code, querying databases, and producing analytical reports. However, in clinical epidemiology, plausible-looking outputs are insufficient. A system can generate syntactically correct code and fluent explanations while silently violating core design principles, such as incorrect cohort entry dates, immortal time bias, inappropriate comparator selection, outcome misclassification, omitted confounding control, or inconsistent reporting of statistical results. These failures are not merely technical errors; they can directly alter safety conclusions 17, 18 . Therefore, in medical applications, the primary challenge is not only whether an LLM can automate a task, but whether it can do so under methodological constraints that are transparent, auditable, and reproducible.

MedCheckpoint was designed around this principle. Rather than relying solely on prompt instructions or unconstrained agent reasoning, the system grounds each analytic stage in a curated knowledge base and locks critical analytical decisions before execution. This design reflects the reality that different stages of pharmacovigilance require different forms of expertise. Query interpretation requires clinical and pharmacologic knowledge; cohort construction requires epidemiologic design rules; analysis execution requires deterministic and validated statistical procedures; and report generation requires conservative interpretation of uncertainty. By organizing the knowledge base around these stages, MedCheckpoint reduces the need for the LLM to improvise under uncertainty. The LLM is used primarily as an interface and coordinator, while high-risk analytical steps are governed by prespecified rules and human-verified computational tools.

This architecture provides a practical approach to making LLM agents both useful and conservative. In many biomedical settings, excessive model autonomy can be hazardous because the model can fill gaps with unsupported assumptions. Conversely, overly rigid systems can fail to generalize across diverse pharmacovigilance questions. MedCheckpoint attempts to balance these competing needs by allowing flexible natural-language interaction and task decomposition while constraining decisions that determine validity. The system can interpret a broad range of drug safety questions, but it must map them to standardized study designs, verified code modules, and predefined reporting structures. This separation between flexible reasoning and locked execution is critical for safety-critical artificial intelligence. It allows the system to benefit from LLM capabilities without allowing the model to freely invent methodology during analysis.

The quality and scope of the curated knowledge base are central to system performance. A knowledge base that is too narrow can fail to support diverse pharmacovigilance questions, whereas an overly broad or weakly curated knowledge base can introduce conflicting recommendations, irrelevant context, or outdated information. This raises an important design question for future LLM-based medical systems: whether knowledge should be curated prospectively, retrieved dynamically at query time, or combined through a hybrid approach. Prospective curation offers stability, auditability, and methodological consistency, which are particularly important for regulatory-grade evidence generation. Dynamic retrieval could improve coverage and currency, especially for newly approved drugs or emerging adverse event literature, but it also introduces risks of retrieving low-quality or inconsistent information.

Thus, this work still has some directions for future exploration. Future work should examine the optimal size, structure, updating frequency, and governance process for pharmacovigilance knowledge bases. A tiered model could be preferable, in which core epidemiologic principles and statistical workflows are fixed and version-controlled, while drug-specific clinical evidence is periodically updated through transparent review. Besides, with additional resources, independent assessments by pharmacovigilance experts could also be integrated into the evaluation framework. A key next step is to deploy MedCheckpoint in real-world research settings and evaluate its performance using routinely collected clinical EHRs.

Methods

Ethics statement

This study involved no human participants or protected health information. Review by an institutional review board was therefore not required.

Data source and OMOP CDM preprocessing

We evaluated the systems using a simulation-based longitudinal EHR dataset generated by Synthea 12 . It contains records for 2,709,803 simulated individuals, describing their health and care over time. The data follow the Observational Medical Outcomes Partnership Common Data Model (OMOP CDM) 19 . This model organizes healthcare data into standardized tables for conditions, drug exposures, visits, measurements, and observation periods. Conditions are primarily coded using SNOMED CT, a standardized clinical terminology. Medications are primarily coded using RxNorm, a standardized drug terminology.

During preprocessing, we used the OMOP vocabulary to replace numeric concept identifiers with their corresponding names. Each table was stored in Parquet, a file format that organizes data by column. During analysis, MedCheckpoint loads data only when needed to reduce memory use.

We used these records to construct study cohorts, describe patients' characteristics at study entry, and identify subsequent outcomes. The evaluation covered exploratory pharmacovigilance workflows from natural language questions to analyses of multiple outcomes. It assessed the quality of study design, analytical execution, and interpretation. We also examined whether systems recognized data limitations and avoided unsupported conclusions when comparisons or outcomes could not be evaluated.

The drugs and outcomes available for analysis depend on what Synthea simulates and which codes appear in the dataset. Some research questions therefore cannot be addressed. All individuals have an observation-period record specifying the time span covered by their data. Cohort construction checks whether this period meets study-specific duration requirements. Associations observed in the study data were not interpreted as evidence of drug safety in real-world populations.

MedCheckpoint system overview

MedCheckpoint comprises three agents: the Case Generator, Analyst, and Report Writer (Fig. 1a). Together, they produce an observational analysis report from a natural language drug safety question.

The Case Generator translates the question into a structured protocol specifying the population, drug exposure, comparison group, and outcomes. It uses source-traceable medical evidence and standardized drug and outcome definitions from the knowledge base. Core outcomes include those targeted by the question, known drug effects, and predefined serious safety outcomes. Exploratory outcomes extend screening to other medically plausible outcomes.

The Analyst carries out the protocol by selecting and calling human-written deterministic tools. These tools execute predefined calculations. A protocol lock restricts changes to core study-design parameters during execution. The checkpoint engine governs analytical execution and records key decisions. In human-in-the-loop mode, researchers can review intermediate results and intervene at these checkpoints.

The Report Writer prepares the report from the protocol and analysis outputs, including statistical estimates and diagnostic summaries. It is instructed to present the current analysis results before comparing them with evidence from the knowledge base. The report separates data-derived findings from literature-informed interpretation and describes uncertainty and study limitations.

Tool calling for all three agents is implemented with LangChain (version 1.3.9), and LLMs are accessed through a unified LiteLLM (version 1.89.0) interface. Backbone LLMs for knowledge-base construction and agent execution can be configured independently. A fixed random seed makes randomized steps in control sampling and matching repeatable for the same inputs. LLM decisions can still vary between runs. We therefore assess consistency in report quality scores through independent repeated runs.

Knowledge base construction and concept grounding

MedCheckpoint organizes its medical knowledge base around a drug-class ontology (Fig. 2). This structured representation defines drug classes and their members using the Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) classification. Each class is specified by a set of standard RxNorm concepts that identify its active ingredients. Candidate drug-class labels are matched to classes in the ontology. Entries meeting the acceptance criteria are added to the main knowledge base. Entries with low-confidence or unresolved class assignments, or labels spanning multiple classes, are held in a separate staging area for review. Medical terms rejected during validation against standard vocabularies are logged separately. Concept grounding links exposure and outcome definitions to standardized medical concepts in the OMOP vocabularies. For diagnosis-based outcomes, the system retrieves candidates from the Condition domain, which contains concepts for diseases and other clinical conditions. An LLM selects concepts to include in or exclude from each outcome definition. Concept identifiers outside the retrieved candidate set are rejected.

During analysis, a concept resolver maps names recorded in the data back to standard concepts. Drug exposure is identified using the study-specific set of active ingredients. Outcomes are identified from records matching the inclusion concepts or their descendants. Descendant relationships are determined from the concept hierarchies in the OMOP vocabularies. Records matching exclusion concepts or their descendants are not counted toward that outcome. These constraints restrict selection to existing vocabulary entries and make exposure and outcome identification traceable. They are intended to reduce incorrect inclusion caused by matching free-text terms.

The system supports three operating modes. It can expand the knowledge base alone or expand it before protocol generation and analysis. Alternatively, it can analyze data using an existing knowledge base without literature-based expansion. In analysis-only mode, a frozen snapshot, or previously saved version of the knowledge base, can be loaded. This supports repeated evaluations with a consistent knowledge base.

Evidence-grounded protocol generation and locking

The Case Generator uses design elements from target trial emulation to structure observational study protocols. This approach uses a hypothetical randomized trial to guide observational study design 5 . Each protocol specifies the study drug, comparator, target population, outcomes, and follow-up window. Exposure is defined at the drug-class or activeingredient level. The system supports adverse-effect exploration, drug comparisons, and comparisons between different product strengths of the same drug. It also assesses whether drug-outcome associations differ across patient subgroups.

An active-comparator group receives an alternative treatment. A never-user group has no recorded exposure to the study drug. The choice depends on the research question and the clinical suitability of candidate comparator drugs. Comparator feasibility is assessed during cohort construction. Inclusion and exclusion criteria are tailored to the research question and draw on literature-derived candidates stored in the knowledge base. The protocol also specifies requirements for a new-user design, which focuses on patients initiating treatment. These include a washout period with no recorded use of the relevant drug before treatment initiation.

Outcome selection combines a core set with an exploratory extension. The core set includes primary outcomes specified in the research question. It also includes outcomes linked to existing drug-class evidence and serious outcomes from the safety panel. The Case Generator then identifies organ systems that the drug could plausibly affect based on its mechanism of action. It selects exploratory outcomes from the corresponding knowledge-base candidate pools. Selections outside these pools are excluded. Each outcome's selection source is recorded in the protocol, and duplicates are merged. This combines question-driven analysis, prespecified safety surveillance, and mechanism-guided exploration to support proactive pharmacovigilance across multiple outcomes.

Covariate extraction uses a predefined set of patient characteristics informed by phenomewide association studies (PheWAS). These studies examine associations across many health outcomes. The set includes demographics, healthcare use before study entry, comorbidity burden, and the length of medical history available before study entry. Comorbidity burden is summarized using an Elixhauser score, which combines pre-existing conditions using van Walraven weights 20 . These covariates form a common pool for propensity score estimation. A propensity score estimates the probability of receiving the study treatment given recorded patient characteristics. Using a common pool supports consistent basic adjustment across outcomes and reduces discretion in choosing adjustment variables for individual questions. The covariates used and their availability are recorded during execution.

The protocol lock constrains analysis execution according to the generated study protocol. Before the Analyst begins patient-level analysis, the system saves the complete structured protocol. It also adds a timestamped record of key design fields to the audit log. These records support later checks of whether the analysis followed the protocol. The Case Generator is instructed to omit expected effect sizes and predictions about effect direction from the protocol. This is intended to reduce the influence of anticipated results on analytical decisions.

The locked protocol is made available to the analysis tools through a shared analysis context. Parameter inheritance and consistency checks take place when the relevant tools are called. When called, cohort tools inherit the protocol's drug-class or ingredient-level exposure definition and its specified ingredient set. They also inherit protocol-specified inclusion and exclusion criteria, age limits, and washout requirements. The Analyst does not need to reenter these settings.

The relevant tools also check the study drug class, comparator, outcome set, and follow-up window against the locked protocol. They reject conflicting calls, including attempts to add or remove protocol-specified outcomes. An error is returned to the Analyst so that it can correct the call. Main and subgroup analyses use the same protocol-specified follow-up window. These constraints govern key design decisions while retaining the Analyst's discretion over execution details, including the sequence of tool calls, covariate selection for the propensity score model, and adjustment of the matching caliper.

When the comparator is infeasible, predefined feasibility rules within the tools govern fallback or termination. The comparator actually used and the reason for any fallback are recorded separately. The original protocol, analysis outputs, and checkpoint decisions are retained. Together, these records allow the executed analysis to be checked against the planned design.

Tool-augmented analysis and checkpoint control

The Analyst is an autonomous tool-calling agent equipped with a library of pharmacovigilance analysis tools. It uses the locked protocol and outputs from preceding tools to select subsequent calls. Within the permitted scope, it can adjust analytical parameters or correct invalid calls. Human-written deterministic tools perform patient-level numerical analyses. They return structured results, diagnostic information, and execution status. The Analyst coordinates the workflow within protocol constraints and the input requirements of each tool. New analytical methods can be incorporated by adding tools.

The cohort tools construct new-user cohorts according to the protocol's exposure definitions and eligibility criteria. They support active-comparator and never-user designs, as well as comparisons of different product strengths of the same drug. Baseline covariate tools provide the predefined PheWAS-informed covariate set for propensity score estimation.

The matching tool performs 1:1 nearest-neighbor matching on the logit, or log-odds, scale of the propensity score. Each treated patient is paired with the closest available comparison patient. Matching is without replacement, so comparison patients are not reused. For patientsubgroup questions, initial matching is restricted to patients in the same subgroup, using each patient's own subgroup label. Covariate balance is assessed using SMDs. The tools report patient losses during cohort construction and matching, together with any remaining covariate imbalance. These diagnostics help the Analyst assess feasibility and interpret the results.

Outcome ascertainment tools identify qualifying events during follow-up using standardized concept sets. Some outcome definitions require excluding prevalent cases, meaning patients with that outcome recorded at or before study entry. For these outcomes, the tools define an outcome-specific risk set, the patients eligible for that outcome analysis. Only complete original matched pairs in which both patients are eligible are retained. Covariate balance is reassessed within each risk set, without rematching.

Event ascertainment and censoring use the same follow-up boundaries. The cutoff is the earlier of the protocol-defined follow-up limit and the patient's recorded observation-period end. Patients without a recorded event are censored at this cutoff, marking the end of their observation. The tools separately record outcomes that cannot be assessed, assessed outcomes with no observed events, and failed statistical estimates.

Association estimation tools use logistic regression to estimate ORs and Cox proportional hazards regression to estimate HRs, with CIs. Outcome models adjust for age and sex, while matching provides the primary control for confounding. When events are few or conventional fitting is unstable, logistic regression uses Firth penalized likelihood 21 . This method reduces small-sample estimation bias. Cox regression uses cluster-robust standard errors at the matched-pair level to account for dependence within pairs. Multiple testing across outcomes is addressed separately for OR and HR analyses using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure 22 . This procedure is used to control the expected proportion of false-positive findings among findings declared significant. Stratified analysis tools estimate associations within patient subgroups. Formal interaction tests assess whether these associations differ across subgroups. The tools also check model fits and record the status and reason whenever estimation or testing is not completed.

The checkpoint engine oversees the Analyst's tool calls during execution. It receives results from tools for cohort construction, matching, balance assessment, and statistical analysis. Alongside their results, these tools return quality checks with measured values, status indicators, descriptions of issues, and recommended actions. Checks cover patient numbers in each study arm, the proportion retained after matching, and covariate balance. They also cover outcome event counts and whether statistical estimation succeeded. The tools assign quality statuses using predefined rules. The checkpoint engine uses these statuses to determine whether execution can continue.

In fully automated mode, the checkpoint engine allows steps that pass the checks to proceed. Warnings are recorded without stopping execution. Critical gate failures trigger an abort. For example, a tool can trigger an abort if a comparator cohort or matched sample fails predefined feasibility checks.

The tool wrapper links an analysis tool to the checkpoint engine. It records the abort decision in the shared analysis state. Subsequent analysis calls are then rejected. Termination therefore does not depend solely on the Analyst following a stop instruction. When continuation is permitted, tool diagnostics guide the Analyst's subsequent calls and adjustments within the allowed scope. Skipped outcomes and estimation failures remain documented in the results and do not automatically terminate the entire study.

In human-in-the-loop mode, checkpoints present the current step's metrics, issues, and recommendations to the researcher. The researcher decides whether to continue and can provide feedback. This feedback is returned to the Analyst with the tool output. It allows the researcher to guide subsequent execution while the analysis is in progress.

Each checkpoint record includes the step, status, decision, researcher feedback, and a timestamp. Together with the original protocol and analysis outputs, these records support review of key analytical decisions and the reasons for termination.

Two-stage reporting and evidence comparison

MedCheckpoint uses two-stage reporting, with statistical results presented before comparison with existing evidence. In the first stage, the Report Writer is instructed to use the locked study protocol and structured analysis outputs. It writes results sections on the study population, covariate balance, and outcome analyses. Effect estimates, CIs, and result counts are taken from the analysis tool outputs. The agent adds each section to the draft using section writing tools. The writing instructions place literature retrieval and comparison after completion of these results sections.

In the second stage, the Report Writer queries the knowledge base for prior evidence and its sources. It compares the reported associations with prior findings in terms of direction and magnitude. The comparison considers the original study designs, effect measures, and certainty of the evidence. Evidence certainty describes confidence in the reliability of the prior findings. The agent discusses agreement, discrepancies, and possible biological mechanisms.

Interpretations supported by the literature are presented in a separate evidence comparison section. Citations are linked to the claims they support, and gaps in the evidence are explicitly identified. This ordering is intended to reduce anchoring, in which known effects unduly influence the selection and presentation of current results. Medical knowledge can then inform the interpretation of exploratory findings. Current findings and literature-based claims can be reviewed separately.

The Report Writer is instructed to report all outcome analysis results and their uncertainty. It is also instructed to describe the main data and design limitations and interpret findings as observational associations. Agreement with the literature can support plausibility but does not establish causality. When saving the report, the system directly appends the locked structured study protocol. This allows the reported design to be checked against the original protocol.

Benchmark and comparison systems

We constructed a purpose-built pharmacovigilance benchmark of 54 natural language questions across three task categories (Supplementary Table 1). The benchmark included 21 questions on adverse drug reactions and off-target effects, and 14 comparisons between drugs. Another 19 questions examined how associations varied by dose or patient subgroup. Here, off-target questions explore effects beyond a drug's intended therapeutic action. The questions were developed with reference to drug, population, and outcome records in the study dataset. They covered both open-ended exploration and analyses of outcomes specified in the questions.

The benchmark included a targeted challenge set of seven prespecified trap questions. These were deliberately designed around known data limitations that constrained analytical feasibility. Some involved cases in which a valid comparator cohort was difficult to construct. Others involved target outcomes without usable records in the data. These questions tested whether systems recognized analytical limitations and restricted their conclusions accordingly. The first comparison system, Baseline A, is a general-purpose autonomous coding agent. It has access to the same EHR data as MedCheckpoint. It receives the research question, general pharmacoepidemiological guidance, and output requirements (Supplementary Note 1). It selects analytical methods and writes and executes its own code. This code performs cohort construction, confounding adjustment, outcome analysis, and report generation. Baseline A does not have access to MedCheckpoint's knowledge base or specialized analysis tools. This comparison evaluates the analytical performance of a general-purpose agent working directly with the study data. The second comparison system, Baseline B, uses the same execution framework as Baseline A but receives more detailed methodological guidance. This guidance is derived from the prompts for MedCheckpoint's Case Generator, Analyst, and Report Writer (Supplementary Note 1). It covers target trial design, new-user cohorts, eligibility criteria, and confounding control. It also covers association estimation, stratified testing, and interpretation of results.

Baseline B does not have access to MedCheckpoint's knowledge base, specialized tools, protocol lock, or checkpoint execution mechanisms. It still performs analyses using code written by the agent. Comparing MedCheckpoint with Baseline B assesses the full system's overall benefit over methodological guidance supplied through prompts alone. Comparing Baseline B with Baseline A assesses the contribution of more detailed methodological guidance.

Claude Code (version 2.1.198) provides the execution framework for both baselines. Their backbone LLM is specified in the run configuration. All three systems use the same Parquet data containing concept names. The baselines also have read-only access to the original OMOP vocabularies. They cannot access MedCheckpoint's curated knowledge base, concept sets, or source code. Reports and structured analysis results from each system are used for subsequent evaluation.

Experimental setup and evaluation procedures

MedCheckpoint, Baseline A, and Baseline B all used MiniMax-M3 as the backbone LLM. Temperature, which controls randomness in LLM responses, was set to 0.1. Each system was run once per question in each of three independent batches. The natural language question served as the task input.

MedCheckpoint operated in fully automated mode using a prebuilt knowledge base snapshot. The knowledge base was not expanded during execution, and no human checkpoint decisions were made. Each baseline run took place in a separate container, an isolated software environment with read-only access to the study data. The baselines were prohibited from retrieving external information online. Their tool-call and execution logs were retained and audited for compliance. We summarized observed run times. Summaries covered all retained runs and question-batch combinations completed by all three systems (Supplementary Table 15).

Reports and structured analysis outputs from all systems underwent the same preprocessing before evaluation. A normalization program converted their different output formats into a common data structure. Original values and additional information were retained for verification. The preprocessing pipeline computed report-level metrics, including outcome counts, missing CIs, and covariate counts. It also classified each run's completion status. Only runs classified as having completed the analysis were included in report-quality assessment.

For report-quality evaluation, system identity fields were removed from the structured results. Systems were assigned random anonymous labels within each question and batch. The labelto-system mappings were stored separately. Each set of evaluation materials contained the research question, report text, normalized results, and precomputed report metrics. Kimi-K3 (16 July 2026 release), DeepSeek-V4-Flash (31 July 2026 release), and MiniMax-M3 (1 June 2026 release) served as LLM judges. Their base temperatures were 1.0, 0.0, and 0.0, respectively. For each evaluation task, all three judges received the same materials and scoring instructions (Supplementary Note 2). Their judgments were reported separately.

Pointwise scoring assessed each report individually. The five dimensions were Design Alignment, Cohort Validity, Statistical Rigor, Outcome Validity, and Interpretive Integrity. Each dimension was rated on a 1-5 scale. Judges were required to justify their scores using evidence from the report or analysis results. The scoring rubric instructed judges not to infer analytical correctness solely from agreement or disagreement with published effect directions. The rubric also penalized unsupported causal claims and interpretations of unmeasurable outcomes as evidence of no risk. The judges returned scores and explanations in a structured format. Before saving the results, the program checked required fields, permitted score values, and the presence of explanations.

Run-to-run stability was assessed using report quality scores from the three batches, separately for each judge. Only questions with valid scores in all three runs were included for each system. For each question and system, we averaged the five dimension scores within each run. We then calculated the sample SD of these averages across the three runs. These question-level SDs were averaged to summarize each system's stability. We also calculated and averaged question-level SDs separately for each quality dimension. Lower SDs indicated more consistent report quality scores.

Pairwise comparisons assessed two reports at a time. They were restricted to question-batch combinations with valid evaluation materials from all three systems. Each judge evaluated every anonymous pair twice, with the presentation order reversed for the second evaluation.

Consistent (system 1) and Consistent (system 2) indicated that both judgments favored the corresponding system. Weak (system 1) and Weak (system 2) indicated one judgment favoring the corresponding system and one tie. Winner flipped by order indicated that the two judgments favored different systems. No preference indicated a tie in both orders. These six categories were counted and reported separately. In Fig. 3c, the two Weak categories, Winner flipped by order, and No preference were combined as No consistent preference.

We assessed interjudge agreement using pointwise scores for 442 completed reports. We calculated two-way mixed-effects, single-rating ICCs. ICC(A,1) measured absolute agreement, whereas ICC(C,1) allowed for systematic differences in judge severity 23 . For individual dimensions, Krippendorff's ordinal α assessed three-judge agreement, and linearly weighted Cohen's κ assessed judge pairs 24, 25 . Gwet's AC2 provided a prespecified complement when scores were concentrated. It used the same linear weights as κ but a different chance-agreement model 26 . Both κ and AC2 retained the full 1-5 scale. We also reported exact agreement and the proportion of reports with an interjudge score range ≤1. Percentile 95% CIs were obtained from 5,000 question-level bootstrap samples. Each question's reports and ratings were resampled together, with judges treated as fixed. Additional measures and sensitivity analyses are described in Supplementary Tables 6 7 8 9.

For the prespecified trap questions in this challenge set, judges received additional reference materials describing known data limitations (Supplementary Note 3). These explained whether the intended comparison groups could be constructed and which outcomes were not expected to be reliably identified. They also provided the rationale for these expectations.

Judges evaluated reports or abort records against these materials using a common rubric. Each assessment was saved in a structured format containing the judgment, its justification, and the type of handling error, if any. Justifications had to cite specific evidence from the report or abort record.

We retrospectively audited analysis completion and termination across systems. Questions from the data-limitation tests were excluded. A question was selected if at least one system had a run initially classified as not completed during output preprocessing. For every selected question, we included all three runs from each system. The audit comprised 14 questions and 42 runs per system (Supplementary Table 11).

We reviewed original reports, analysis outputs, and execution logs, checking source data when needed. LLMs assisted with organizing the evidence, and human review determined the final judgments. Execution outcomes were classified separately from report quality. The three categories were Completed with available data, Properly stopped (stopped at cohort or matching checks), and Fabricated answer without analyzing.
