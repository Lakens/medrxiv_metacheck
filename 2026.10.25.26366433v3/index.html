Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How StatCheck works

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Abstract

Objective. It remains unclear which architecture best sorts 12-lead electrocardiograms into diagnostic superclasses, and whether added model complexity pays off. Methods. Using 21,375 PTB-XL records under one patient-level split, we compared a Mamba-style state space model, a residual convolutional network, a bidirectional long short-term memory network, a convolutionalrecurrent network with attention, soft-voting ensembles of these networks, and MiniRocket features with LightGBM. Each record took the superclass of its highest-likelihood diagnostic code, and we scored saved predictions with paired patient-level bootstrap intervals. Results. No model clearly outperformed MiniRocket with LightGBM, which needs no graphics processor. The ensemble of the three deep models with the highest validation macro F1 score reached 0.656, against 0.645 for MiniRocket with LightGBM (difference +0.011, 95% confidence interval -0.007 to +0.031), and an ensemble of all four deep models reached 0.657. An exploratory ensemble, whose members we chose after seeing test results, reached 0.665 and was the only ensemble whose interval excluded zero (+0.021, 0.002 to 0.040); we regard that margin as optimistic. Hypertrophy was the hardest class (F1 0.33-0.41), and differences in its recall mostly reflected threshold placement. Conclusions. Under one shared split, deep ensembles added little over a classical pipeline. Significance. These results give biomedical informatics researchers a statistically grounded reference for judging whether complex models justify their cost on this benchmark. With one dataset, one split, one training seed per model, and no external validation, they do not show readiness for clinical use.

I. INTRODUCTION

Cardiovascular disease is still the leading cause of death worldwide [1], and the 12-lead ECG is one of the most widely available ways to detect it. Software that reads ECGs already exists, but assigning a recording to a diagnostic category is still hard. The abnormalities can be subtle, several conditions often appear in the same patient, and the categories are heavily imbalanced. Deep learning has even found signals that clinicians cannot read from the tracing, such as atrial fibrillation in ECGs recorded during sinus rhythm [2]. Systematic reviews cover deep learning for ECG data in much more depth [3], [4].

In this paper we compare deep learning and classical approaches on one task. The task is to assign each 12-lead recording in the public PTB-XL dataset to one of five diagnostic superclasses. These are conduction disturbance (CD), hypertrophy (HYP, which in PTB-XL covers left and right ventricular hypertrophy, atrial overload or enlargement, and septal hypertrophy), myocardial infarction (MI), normal ECG (NORM), and ST/T change (STTC). Convolutional networks have generally done well on this kind of task, and recurrent models suit the sequential nature of the signal. Selective state space models (SSMs) are a newer option for long sequences, and MiniRocket, a random convolutional feature transform, offers an alternative that does not use deep learning.

The six approaches are a Mamba-style SSM (M1), a 1D residual CNN (M2), a BiLSTM (M3), a CRNN with attention (M4), a soft-voting ensemble of M1, M3, and M4 (M5), and a MiniRocket plus LightGBM pipeline (M6). M5 combines the three deep models with the highest validation macro F1. We also report two other ensembles as extra checks. One averages all four deep models (M5A). The other is an exploratory ensemble of M2, M3, and M4 (M5T), whose members we chose after seeing test results. Every model with learnable parameters was trained from scratch, and all models were tested on the same 3,182 records, which share no patients with the training or validation data. Besides argmax macro F1, we report AUROC, AUPRC, per-class results, and calibration, and we compare pairs of models with paired, patient-level statistics. Our main finding is that no model was clearly better than MiniRocket+LightGBM. The ensemble chosen on validation was ahead of the Mamba-style SSM and the CRNN, but it was not detectably different from the ResNet, the BiLSTM, or MiniRocket+LightGBM. Only the test-selected ensemble M5T had an interval over MiniRocket+LightGBM that excluded zero, and that margin is probably optimistic. II. LITERATURE REVIEW Wagner et al. released PTB-XL as a large, free, public 12-lead ECG dataset for reproducible machine learning work, with SCP-ECG diagnostic codes mapped to broader superclasses [5]. It is now a common benchmark for deep architectures in this area. Nonaka and Seita compared many deep architectures on PTB-XL and two other 12-lead datasets and found that a standard ResNet-18 performed consistently well [6]. The PhysioNet/Computing in Cardiology Challenge 2020 made multi-source 12-lead classification a shared task [7]. Convolutional networks, including residual ones [8], are standard benchmarks for this task [9], and their local filters suit features such as the QRS complex. Recurrent models built on LSTM units [10], and convolutional-recurrent hybrids, instead capture features that spread across the full 10-second, 12-lead recording.

State space models are a fairly recent alternative to conventional sequence models. Mamba makes the state-space parameters depend on the input and adds a hardware-aware implementation. This gives linear-time modeling of long sequences [11], which suits ECGs with thousands of samples. SSMs are not new to ECG work, though. Mehari and Strodthoff applied S4 models [12] to PTB-XL and reported a macro AUC of 0.942 on the 71-label task, above their previous best [13]. A Mambabased classifier has also been tested on PTB-XL [14]. Our contribution is narrower. We compare a pure-PyTorch Mambastyle model with CNN, recurrent, and classical baselines on one split, without the original fused CUDA kernel.

Classical methods also stay competitive in many settings. ROCKET and its faster, nearly deterministic variant MiniRocket use random convolutional kernels to extract fixed features that do not depend on labels and can feed any downstream classifier [15], [16]. LightGBM, a gradient-boosted tree framework, handles such features efficiently [17]. Soft-voting ensembles tend to help when the errors of the members are not strongly correlated [18].

Published PTB-XL numbers are hard to compare directly with ours for several reasons. Strodthoff et al. [9] treat the superdiagnostic task as multi-label and report macro AUC (about 0.93) rather than macro F1. Ribeiro et al. [19] address a different 12-lead abnormality task at much larger scale, and Hannun et al. [20] study single-lead ambulatory arrhythmia detection. The Results section returns to these comparisons.

III. METHODOLOGY A. Data and Preprocessing

We use PTB-XL v1.0.3 [5], which holds 21,799 clinical 12-lead recordings sampled at 500 Hz, each 10 seconds long, giving tensors of shape (12, 5000). The SCP-ECG diagnostic codes on each recording map to five superclasses (CD, HYP, MI, NORM, STTC). Records whose codes mapped to no superclass were dropped, which left 21,375 recordings from 18,610 patients. NORM makes up about 43% of these records, and HYP about 6%. NORM is the normal-ECG superclass in PTB-XL, not a rhythm category. Sinus rhythm alone does not make an ECG NORM, and a record with both NORM and an abnormal code takes whichever has the higher likelihood under the rule below. HYP labels come from ECG criteria, not echocardiography, so HYP is not limited to left ventricular hypertrophy. Rhythm is a separate statement category in PTB-XL and was not used here, so HYP records were not restricted to non-sinus rhythm. No IRB approval was needed because the dataset is public, de-identified, and released under a Creative Commons license. PTB-XL was curated by the Physikalisch-Technische Bundesanstalt in Germany from recordings made with Schiller devices between 1989 and 1996, and it covers about 18,900 patients (roughly half women, ages 0 to 95, median about 62) [5]. Ethnicity is not recorded. Voltage criteria for hypertrophy differ by age, sex, and ethnic origin, and our labels come from the dataset's annotations, so our results may not carry over to other populations.

Label assignment. About a quarter of the records (23.7%) carry more than one superclass, so we had to reduce each of them to a single label. We used the superclass of the highestlikelihood SCP code among codes with likelihood above zero, and we broke ties in favor of the first-listed code. For 3,130 records (14.6%) the label would change if ties went to the lastlisted code. The ground truth therefore depends partly on this rule, and the per-class results carry that caveat. A single label is also a clinical limitation, because several cardiac conditions often occur together in real patients. Under our tie-breaking rule, a model is penalized in evaluation even when it correctly detects a secondary condition. This probably understates the multi-label diagnostic ability of both the deep ensembles and the classical pipeline.

No record was excluded for carrying several superclasses, and only records with no superclass code were dropped. Our F1 scores therefore measure agreement with the dominantcode label, not full ECG interpretation, and we did not measure how performance differs between records with one superclass and records with several. We did not keep the original label array, so we re-derived the labels from the released metadata. The test class counts (497, 208, 597, 1,386, and 494 for CD, HYP, MI, NORM, and STTC) fall within two records per class of those from our initial analysis, and each deep model's test macro F1 reproduced to within 0.0011.

Each recording went through three preprocessing steps in order. A zero-phase IIR notch filter at 50 Hz (Q = 30) removed powerline interference, a reasonable choice for PTB-XL's European recording setting. A zero-phase fourthorder Butterworth bandpass filter (0.5-40 Hz) removed baseline wander and high-frequency noise. Both filters used sosfiltfilt, so they add no phase distortion. Finally, we z-scored each lead in each recording separately, using its own mean and standard deviation within the recording, with a floor of 10 -6 on the standard deviation to avoid division by zero on flat or disconnected leads.

Z-scoring each lead removes absolute voltage and the am-plitude relationships between leads that the voltage criteria for hypertrophy depend on. The 0.5-40 Hz band also reduces QRS amplitude compared with the 150 Hz diagnostic bandwidth.

Weak HYP performance may therefore reflect our preprocessing as well as class imbalance. We did not test other normalization or bandwidth settings. The pipeline ran during signal extraction, so the stored array X of shape (21, 375, 12, 5000) holds only preprocessed float32 signals.

We split by patient, roughly 70/15/15 into training, validation, and test sets, because a record-level split could put one patient's several recordings on both sides. We shuffled the 18,610 patient IDs with seed 42 and divided them into disjoint groups of 13,028 training, 2,791 validation, and 2,791 test patients, giving 14,978, 3,215, and 3,182 records. The split is not stratified by class, and it differs from the recommended PTB-XL strat_fold partition (which also respects patient boundaries). Our absolute numbers are therefore not directly comparable with published benchmarks. The comparisons in this paper are relative, among models trained and tested under one shared split. All models share this split.

B. Class Imbalance and Augmentation

For the deep models M1 to M4 we used two mechanisms. A WeightedRandomSampler weighted each training sample by the inverse of its class frequency, which oversamples minority classes within batches. We also replaced cross-entropy with focal loss [21] (γ = 1.0, no per-class α weights) and label smoothing of ϵ = 0.1. The focal term (1-p t ) γ shifts the gradient toward hard and rare examples, and label smoothing softens the one-hot targets, which suits PTB-XL's likelihoodbased labels. Stacking all three mechanisms (the sampler, inverse-frequency α weighting, and focal loss with γ = 2.0) overcorrected in early development experiments and gave up too much NORM performance for HYP recall. So we dropped the α weighting and lowered γ to 1.0. Those runs were not kept.

We trained M6 without SMOTE [22], a choice made on validation macro F1 (0.649 without, 0.645 with), and used class_weight="balanced" in LightGBM. The SMOTE variant, which expands the training set from 14,978 to 32,190 samples, is reported as an ablation.

We augmented the training data for M1 to M4 with six transforms, each applied independently with a fixed probability. Gaussian noise had a standard deviation of 1% of the signal's own (probability 0.5). Amplitude scaling ranged from 0.9 to 1.1 (0.3), and a circular time shift reached up to ±50 samples (0.3). One random lead was zeroed (0.15) so the model could not rely on any single lead. Simulated baseline wander, a sinusoidal drift with frequency between 0.05 and 0.5 Hz and amplitude between 0.05 and 0.15 (0.4), mimicked breathing and motion artifacts. Finally, the six limb leads (I, II, III, aVR, aVL, aVF) were randomly permuted with probability 0.2 to reduce sensitivity to lead order.

The limb-lead permutation is a problem for some labels. It changes the frontal-plane axis and moves findings such as inferior Q waves into other leads, which can change the meaning of an MI or axis-dependent conduction label. We did not run an ablation, so it may have hurt M1 to M4 on those classes. C. Model Architectures M1 (Mamba-style SSM, pure PyTorch). This is a selective state space model inspired by Mamba [11], written entirely in PyTorch with no custom CUDA kernels. A two-stage convolutional stem (strides 4 and 4) cuts the 5,000-sample input to 313 time steps with d model = 128 channels. Four MambaBlock modules follow the stem. Each block applies layer normalization, an input projection to 2 × d inner = 512 channels, a depthwise Conv1d with kernel size 4, a SiLU activation, and a selective scan with d state = 16. We compute the scan in parallel, not as a sequential recurrence, using a prefix sum over log-space cumulative products of the discretized state transition matrices. Mean and max pooling over the 313 output steps give a 256-dimensional vector, which feeds a two-layer head with GELU activations and dropout of 0.2. The model has about 1.1 million parameters and was trained with a learning rate of 2 × 10 -4 and batch size 32.

M2 (multi-lead 1D residual CNN).

This ResNet-style network [8] works directly on the raw 12-lead signal of shape (12, 5000). A stem with kernel size 15, stride 2, and max pooling feeds four residual stages with 64, 128, 256, and 512 channels, each made of two ResidualBlock1D modules (kernel size 7, batch normalization, ReLU, dropout 0.2). Adaptive average pooling then reduces the time axis to a single step. A twolayer head with dropout 0.3 produces the five outputs. It has about 8.9 million parameters and was trained with a learning rate of 3 × 10 -4 and batch size 64.

M3 (multi-lead BiLSTM).

A bidirectional LSTM [10] runs on a convolutional summary of the raw signal. A three-stage stem with strides 4, 2, and 2 shortens the sequence from 5,000 to about 313 steps and projects to 96 channels. A two-layer bidirectional LSTM (hidden size 192, dropout 0.3) follows. Mean and max pooling over time give a 768-dimensional vector for a two-layer head. The model has about 1.5 million parameters; training used a learning rate of 5×10 -4 and batch size 64.

M4 (CRNN with attention).

A three-stage 1D convolutional extractor (kernel sizes 11, 7, and 5; strides 2, 2, and 2) produces 128 channels. A two-layer bidirectional LSTM (hidden size 256, dropout 0.3) and temporal attention come next. Attention weights come from a learned linear projection of the 512-dimensional LSTM output to a scalar, normalized by a softmax over time. The weighted sum is the final sequence representation. The model has about 2.6 million parameters; training used a learning rate of 4 × 10 -4 and batch size 64.

M5 (soft-voting ensemble of M1, M3, and M4, selected on validation). M5 averages the softmax outputs of M1, M3, and M4 and needed no further training. Its members are the three deep models with the highest validation macro F1 (M4, M1, M3), so the selection rule uses no test information. The order of events matters here. Our first ensemble was M2+M3+M4, whose members we picked using test macro F1. We adopted the validation-based rule as the main definition only after seeing that result. We therefore report two more ensembles next to M5. M5A averages all four deep models and involves no selection. M5T is the original M2+M3+M4 ensemble. Because test results guided its choice, we treat it as exploratory and expect its advantage to be optimistic. M6 is not a neural network and serves as a separate classical baseline.

M6 (MiniRocket + LightGBM).

We applied the MiniRocketMultivariate transform from sktime [16] with 10,000 random convolutional kernels, which produces a 9,996-dimensional vector of proportions of positive values per recording. We fit the transform on a random 5,000-record subset of the training set only. The kernel weights do not depend on labels, but the bias values are set from the data, so fitting on training records alone keeps validation and test signals out of the transform. We then extracted features for all records in chunks of 1,000 and standardized them with a scaler fit on the training set. LightGBM [17] used at most 400 trees, num_leaves = 31, learning_rate = 0.05, colsample_bytree = 0.3, subsample = 0.8, and class_weight="balanced". Early stopping with patience 50 on the validation set chose the number of trees (221 without SMOTE, 196 with). The remaining hyperparameters were fixed in advance and not tuned.

D. Training and Evaluation

We trained M1 to M4 with AdamW [23] (weight decay 10 -4 ) and a cosine annealing schedule over at most 70 epochs, with early stopping (patience 12) on validation macro F1 and gradient clipping at a maximum norm of 1.0. After every epoch we saved the weights, optimizer state, best validation F1, and early-stopping counter to Google Drive so training could resume after a runtime disconnect. All deep models ran on an NVIDIA Tesla T4 in Google Colaboratory. The M6 pipeline ran on the Colab CPU runtime. The MiniRocket transform of all 21,375 records took 60.5 min (0.17 s per record), and LightGBM took 22.1 min (30.3 min with SMOTE).

Each model was trained once with seed 42, so the confidence intervals reflect sampling of the test set only, not run-torun training variation, which we did not measure. Small gaps, such as M2 versus M3 (0.001) and M4 versus M1 (0.013), should be read with this in mind. The learning rate and batch size of each deep model were set by hand and not tuned. Training stopped through early stopping on validation macro F1 (patience 12, maximum 70 epochs), and the best-validation checkpoint was used. We did not record the epoch at which each run stopped.

Inference was rerun from each deep model's best-validation checkpoint, with per-record class probabilities saved, and macro F1 matched the values from our initial runs to within 0.0011. All comparisons use the same 3,182 test records. Confidence intervals come from a patient-level paired bootstrap, in which we resampled test patients with replacement 10,000 times and scored every model on each resample. We also ran exact McNemar tests on per-record correctness, with Holm adjustment across all 21 model pairs among M1 to M4, M5T, M6, and M6 S . We call two models equivalent in macro F1 only if the 90% CI of their difference lies within ±0.02, and we report the 90% interval with every equivalence claim. We set this margin before looking at results, and it is close to the half-width of a single model's 95% CI on this test set. When the criterion is not met, we say only that no significant difference was found. Besides macro F1, we report one-vsrest AUROC and AUPRC (per class and macro-averaged), sensitivity, specificity, and PPV at the argmax operating point, and calibration (expected calibration error with 15 bins, Brier score, and log loss). We also report patient-level bootstrap intervals (2,000 resamples) for macro and HYP AUROC and AUPRC, including paired M6 -M5T differences. The other per-class AUROC and AUPRC values come without intervals, and we made no correction for multiple comparisons.

C. Calibration and Cost

Calibration varied more across models than macro F1 did (Table II, Fig. 3). M4 had the lowest ECE (0.020) and M1 the highest (0.054), with M1 overconfident at high confidence. The exploratory ensemble M5T had the best Brier score (0.370) and log loss (0.709), narrowly ahead of M6 (0.371 and 0.715), but its ECE was 0.046. The primary ensemble M5 had an ECE of 0.030, a Brier score of 0.383, and a log loss of 0.732. These are comparable to the ResNet (0.026, 0.378, and 0.735) and better than M1, M3, and M4 on Brier score and log loss, although its ECE is worse than that of M2, M3, and M4. Both ensembles are underconfident on average (mean confidence 0.698 for M5 and 0.694 for M5T, against accuracies of 0.728 and 0.741), which is common when probabilities are averaged across models. No model was calibrated after training. Macro F1 at argmax depends on the threshold and on class prevalence, so it says little about whether a model is ready for deployment.

D. Comparison with Published Literature

Strodthoff et al. [9] frame the superdiagnostic task as a multi-label problem and report a macro AUC of about 0.93 for their best models, whereas we use single-label macro F1 on a custom split (our one-vs-rest macro AUROC is 0.89-0.92). Ribeiro et al. [19] and Hannun et al. [20] study different tasks (12-lead abnormality detection and single-lead ambulatory arrhythmia detection) on much larger datasets. Because our split is patient-disjoint but not the recommended strat_fold partition, we cannot attribute any gap with published numbers to stricter evaluation.

V. CONCLUSION AND DISCUSSION

We compared six ECG classification approaches on 21,375 PTB-XL records using one patient-level split throughout. Our main finding is that no model clearly outperformed MiniRocket+LightGBM on macro F1. The ensemble selected on validation (M5, M1+M3+M4) scored 0.656, and the all-four ensemble scored 0.657. Both were ahead of the Mamba-style and CRNN models, but neither was detectably ahead of the ResNet, the BiLSTM, or MiniRocket+LightGBM (+0.011 and +0.012 macro F1 over M6, with 95% CIs including zero). The exploratory ensemble of M2, M3, and M4 reached 0.665 and was modestly ahead of MiniRocket+LightGBM (+0.021, 95% CI 0.002-0.040), but its members were chosen on test results, so we treat that margin as optimistic. MiniRocket+LightGBM came close on accuracy and threshold-free metrics and had a higher HYP AUROC than both ensembles (0.871 versus 0.839 for M5 and 0.849 for M5T), although it labeled more abnormal ECGs as NORM (15.2% versus 10.0% for M5 and 12.0% for M5T). Under our ±0.02 criterion it was also equivalent to the ResNet and BiLSTM on macro F1. It also trains without a GPU and did not benefit from SMOTE. The Mamba-style model scored below the CNN and BiLSTM. Differences in HYP recall mostly reflect threshold choice, and our preprocessing, which removes amplitude information, may also limit HYP performance.

The dataset is fairly small, and the conclusions could change with a larger N . We used only one dataset and one split. We had no external validation, subgroup analysis, or clinically defined operating points, and we did not evaluate on the recommended strat_fold partition, so our absolute numbers cannot be compared directly with published benchmarks. None of the models used self-supervised pretraining, which could change the ranking, and we did not test a 1D vision transformer. We adopted the validation-based ensemble rule after first examining the test-selected ensemble, which is why we report that ensemble as exploratory. The single-label ground truth depends on a tie-breaking rule that changes 14.6% of labels, and 23.7% of records carry more than one superclass. Z-score normalization, the 40 Hz low-pass filter, and limblead permutation may have hurt HYP, MI, and axis-dependent classes, and we did not ablate them. The equivalence margin was our own choice. Each model was trained once, so our intervals do not capture training variability. Intervals for AUROC and AUPRC cover only macro and HYP values, use 2,000 resamples, and are not corrected for multiple comparisons, and no model was calibrated.

Future work includes pretraining, evaluation on the official strat_fold partition (fold 9 for validation, fold 10 for testing) with repeated cross-validation and multiple training seeds, so that results can be compared with published benchmarks, multi-label evaluation, ablations of preprocessing and augmentation, and external clinical validation before any deployment.
