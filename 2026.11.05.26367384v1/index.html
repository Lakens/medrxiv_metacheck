Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 3 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Mapping genetic effects on distal genes and regulatory programs is essential for understanding how genetic variation influences complex diseases; yet trans-QTL discovery remains limited by weak effect sizes, extensive multiple testing and poor reproducibility. Here, we introduce TransMap, a generalizable computational framework that combines multi-scale trans-QTL mapping with multi-phenotype genetic colocalization to improve the discovery and interpretation of distal regulatory variants. Beyond individual trans-eGenes, TransMap constructs local co-regulatory program phenotypes that account for background expression variation to improve the detection of weak, coordinated distal genetic effects. Across a broad range of simulation settings, TransMap achieved 9.2-36.7% higher trans-QTL discovery power than existing methods while maintaining well-controlled type I error rates. We applied TransMap to bulk and single-nucleus transcriptomic profiles from the aging human brain in the ADSP Functional Genomics Consortium (FunGen-AD), further integrating proteomic, glycoproteomic and metabolomic biomarker measurements (average N=476 donors). Genome-wide fine-mapping of TransMap single-gene and program-level QTL yielded variant annotations with 2.75-2.88x heritability enrichment across 57 complex diseases and traits (average N=307K), with complementary heritability information captured across regulatory scales. Multi-phenotype colocalization identified 49,532 trans-regulatory events supported by shared genetic signals with cis-eQTL or biomarker-QTL, including 1,825 candidate cis-trans-biomarker regulatory configurations. TransMap further revealed an asymmetric cross-cell-type regulatory architecture in which neuronal cis-regulatory signals preferentially associate with coordinated glial trans-regulatory responses, supported by co-culture experiments and spatial transcriptomics. Alzheimer's disease (AD) GWAS-prioritized colocalization identified trans-regulatory associations in 115 AD-associated loci, including a KNOP1 cis-eQTL associated in trans with a microglial regulatory program containing MS4A4E, with additional support from statistical mediation analysis. TransMap provides a multi-scale, cell-type-resolved atlas of trans-regulatory variation and a generalizable framework for elucidating the regulatory architecture of complex diseases.

Introduction

Most genetic variants associated with complex diseases lie in noncoding regions of the genome, yet the molecular mechanisms through which their effects propagate beyond proximal or cis-regulated genes to downstream cellular pathways remain incompletely understood [1] [2] [3] . Trans-eQTL mapping, which identifies genetic variants associated with the expression of distant genes, provides a means to uncover regulatory networks and downstream molecular processes through which disease risk may propagate [4] [5] [6] . However, trans-eQTL discovery remains fundamentally challenging because individual distal effects are typically weak and the genome-wide variant-gene search space imposes a substantial multiple-testing burden 4, 7, 8 . Large-scale studies such as eQTLGen 4, 9 and MetaBrain 10 have advanced trans-eQTL discovery through extensive sample aggregation, while more recent efforts including OneK1K 11 , SingleBrain 12 and PsychAD 13 have extended trans-eQTL mapping to individual cell types in immune cells and the human brain. Nevertheless, detecting weak distal genetic effects remains particularly difficult at cell-type resolution, where molecular QTL cohorts typically have smaller effective sample sizes and greater measurement noise than large bulk-tissue studies 9 . These challenges are particularly pronounced across many disease-relevant tissues where molecular QTL datasets remain comparatively limited in sample size, including GTEx tissues 7 , pancreatic islets 14 , and kidney cohorts 15 . Recent methods, such as TransPCO 16 and ARCHIE 17 , have sought to improve sensitivity by aggregating coordinated distal genetic effects across multiple genes, but distinguishing subtle genetically coordinated regulatory programs from background expression variation remains challenging. In parallel, the growing availability of molecular QTL maps across additional modalities, including large-scale proteomic and metabolomic resources, presents opportunities to leverage shared genetic signals across molecular phenotypes to strengthen transregulatory inference [18] [19] [20] [21] [22] . However, existing trans-QTL mapping approaches have not systematically combined gene-and program-level mapping with multi-phenotype genetic colocalization across these molecular modalities.

Here, we introduce TransMap, a generalizable computational framework that combines multi-scale trans-QTL mapping with multi-phenotype genetic colocalization to improve the discovery and interpretation of distal regulatory variants. TransMap captures genetic regulation in a cell type at three complementary scales: individual distal genes, local co-regulatory programs and global genetically coordinated programs. For local programs, TransMap uses background-whitened principal component analysis to capture coordinated regulatory variation while accounting for background expression covariance 23 , improving sensitivity to weak distal genetic effects. These complementary gene-and program-level representations are converted into quantitative molecular phenotypes for genome-wide QTL fine-mapping using SuSiE 24 . To further strengthen discovery, TransMap integrates cell-type resolved trans-eGene and trans-eProgram QTL with cell-type-resolved cis-eQTL and transacting biomarker-QTL through multi-phenotype colocalization using ColocBoost 25 . While program-level phenotypes improve the detection of coordinated distal genetic effects, multi-phenotype colocalization leverages shared genetic signals across molecular layers to support weaker trans-QTL associations that may not reach conventional single-phenotype significance thresholds. Matching cis and trans signals across cell types can further nominate candidate cross-cell-type regulatory configurations and potential intercellular relationships for downstream investigation.

We applied TransMap to whole-genome genetic variation, bulk and single-nucleus transcriptomic profiles, and proteomic, glycoproteomic and metabolomic measurements from the aging human brain in ROSMAP, as part of the ADSP Functional Genomics Consortium (FunGen-AD) (N=361-784 donors; Supplementary Table 1), encompassing individual-gene expression phenotypes and transcriptomic program-level phenotypes across 6 cell types and 3 bulk regions [25] [26] [27] . TransMap was deployed in both disease-agnostic (xQTL-only) and Alzheimer's Disease (AD) GWAS-prioritized setting (GWAS-xQTL). Gene programs from TransMap improved the detection of coordinated distal genetic effects relative to existing program-level approaches. Across 57 relatively independent complex diseases and traits (average N=307K, genetic correlation 𝑟 ! < 0.7) 25 , TransMap eGene and eProgram QTL exhibited stronger disease heritability enrichment than existing methods and captured complementary genetic information across regulatory scales. Multi-phenotype colocalization further identified trans-regulatory associations supported by shared genetic signals with proximal cis-eQTL and downstream biomarker-QTL, with program-level associations showing stronger concordance with biomarker QTL and functional gene networks. These analyses also revealed an asymmetric cross-cell-type regulatory architecture, particularly linking neuronal cis-regulatory signals to glial trans-regulatory responses, supported by orthogonal co-culture experiments and spatial transcriptomic analyses. Finally, AD-centered colocalization identified candidate trans-regulatory associations at 115 AD-associated loci, connecting disease risk to distal genes, programs and molecular biomarkers. These results establish TransMap as a generalizable framework and multiscale, cell-type-resolved resource for investigating the regulatory architecture of complex diseases.

Results

Overview of the TransMap framework

We present TransMap as an end-to-end framework for defining, genetically mapping and interpreting multi-scale trans-acting molecular phenotypes across diverse tissues and cell types (Figure 1a). TransMap extends existing trans-QTL approaches along two major methodological dimensions: (i) from individual distal genes to quantitative phenotypes characterizing coordinated molecular responses within local and global gene programs, and (ii) from single-phenotype association testing to genome-wide multi-phenotype genetic colocalization across molecular modalities, leveraging additional molecular QTL maps, including cis-eQTL and biomarker-QTL when available, to strengthen the discovery and interpretation of distal genetic effects. TransMap can be applied either in a disease-agnostic manner, with downstream applications across a broad range of complex diseases and traits, or in a disease-specific, GWAS-prioritized manner.

TransMap integrates whole-genome sequencing with donor-level, cell-type-resolved pseudobulk expression profiles to represent trans regulation at three complementary molecular scales. At the individual-gene level, TransMap uses normalized expression of distal genes as the conventional unit of trans-eQTL analysis (Methods). Additionally, TransMap defines compact local co-expression programs and broader global transregulatory programs. At the local-program level, which we refer to as TransMap-local, TransMap identifies coexpression modules using Hotspot 28 and derives donor-level co-regulatory activity scores for each module using background-whitened principal component analysis (WPC) 23 (Methods). Unlike conventional principal component analysis, which identifies dominant axes of expression variation, WPC prioritizes program-level variation relative to a background covariance structure, thereby reducing the influence of shared expression variation unrelated to the program. For each gene program 𝒢, we first create 22 leave-one-chromosome-out (LOCO) programs, 𝒢 "# , by leaving out all genes from chromosome 𝑐. WPC identifies a set of directions 𝑘 for the program 𝒢 "# by maximizing the signal-to-noise ratio of observed program-level variance to a size-matched background residual variance: where 𝚺 𝒢 "+ represents the expression covariance matrix corresponding to genes in 𝒢 "# , and 𝚺 * "+ represents the estimated background residual covariance matrix (Methods).

At the global-program level, which we refer to as TransMap-global, TransMap captures genome-wide patterns of genetically coordinated expression by learning sparse variant and gene embeddings from inter-chromosomal variant-gene trans-associations using sparse canonical correlation analysis (sCCA) 29 (Methods). The resulting gene embeddings are projected onto donor-level expression profiles in a LOCO manner to derive global program activity phenotypes.

TransMap also moves beyond trans-eQTL mapping of preselected variants to generate genome-wide, finemapped annotations of trans-regulatory potential. For each individual-gene or program-level trans-acting phenotype (denoted by trans-eGene and trans-eProgram respectively), TransMap performs genome-wide finemapping using the SuSiE (Sum of Single Effects) 24 model across 1,361 approximately independent LD blocks 26 , yielding 95% credible sets (CS) and variant-level posterior inclusion probabilities (PIP) (Methods). Unlike previous large-scale trans-eQTL studies, such as eQTLGen Phase II, which restricted fine-mapping to loci with significant associations 9 , TransMap performs genome-wide fine-mapping across individual-gene and programlevel phenotypes, retaining posterior evidence at loci that do not reach conventional association significance thresholds. This strategy is motivated by recent studies demonstrating the utility of genome-wide fine-mapping for characterizing polygenic genetic architectures and informing downstream genetic analyses 30, 31 . For each trans-acting phenotype, we define significant trans-QTL signals as 95% fine-mapping credible sets containing at least one variant passing an FDR < 5% threshold based on the univariate association p-values computed for SuSiE fine-mapping, with FDR correction performed separately for individual-gene and program-level phenotypes (Methods). Additionally, we define genome-wide variant annotations using the maximum PIP (trans-MaxPIP) 32 across the corresponding trans phenotypes, constructing both cell-type-specific and cross-cell-type annotations. We evaluate the disease relevance of these annotations across 57 complex diseases and traits using stratified LD score regression (S-LDSC) 33, 34 (average N=307K, genetic correlation 𝑟 ! < 0.7), estimating heritability enrichment and standardized effect sizes after conditioning on 53 baseline annotations comprising coding, conserved and tissue-aggregated epigenomic annotations 25, 34 (Methods). Following previous analyses of cell-type-specific functional annotations 35, 36 , we did not additionally condition on the LD-related annotations included in the baseline-LD model 37 which capture aspects of negative selection and may attenuate cell-typespecific heritability signals 38 . We further perform joint heritability analyses of individual-gene and program-level trans-MaxPIP annotations, along with cis-eQTL-derived MaxPIP annotations, to assess whether distinct scales of distal regulation capture complementary disease-relevant genetic information beyond proximal cis-regulatory annotations.

Finally, TransMap leverages multi-phenotype genetic colocalization using ColocBoost 25 to integrate cell-typeresolved trans-eGene and trans-eProgram QTL with proximal cis-eQTL and additional biomarker-QTL, thereby improving both trans-QTL discovery and functional interpretation (Methods, Figure 1a). Because proximal cis-eQTL and distal trans-QTL can differ substantially in effect size and statistical power, we perform separate genome-wide colocalization analyses: (i) gene-level cis-eQTL colocalization across cell types, and (ii) LD-blocklevel colocalization of trans-eGene, trans-eProgram and biomarker QTL across molecular phenotypes and cell types. We subsequently integrate events from these analyses based on shared genetic signals. Each colocalization event is characterized by this integrated colocalization confidence set (CoS) and an associated configuration of molecular phenotypes sharing the genetic signal. ColocBoost also provides a variant-level colocalization probability (VCP) in alignment with the posterior inclusion probability (PIP) in standard finemapping (Methods). This approach provides convergent genetic evidence for distal regulatory associations, including weak trans-QTL associations that may not achieve conventional single-phenotype significance thresholds. In the FunGen-AD application, we integrate cell-type-resolved trans-QTL and cis-eQTL across 6 cell types and 3 bulk regions with molecular QTL for proteins, glycoproteins and metabolites measured in the aging human brain cortex 26, 27 . Using shared distal genetic signals through genome-wide multi-phenotype colocalization, we nominate candidate cis-trans and cis-trans-biomarker regulatory configurations. We define the cell types associated with proximal cis-regulated genes as the putative "initiating cell types" and those associated with distal trans-eGenes or trans-ePrograms as the putative "recipient cell types" (Figure 1a). These designations describe the positions of cell types within candidate regulatory configurations rather than experimentally established directions of signaling. This framework therefore enables the characterization of shared genetic associations across molecular layers and cellular contexts, extending conventional bulk cis-trans regulatory mapping. However, colocalization does not establish that a cis-regulated gene causally mediates a distal effect or that associations across cell types reflect direct intercellular signaling. Subsequent pathway-level, spatial and co-culture experimental analyses provide orthogonal evidence for the biological coherence, anatomical organization and cellular interaction signatures of the nominated configurations. The AD GWASprioritized ColocBoost analysis further assesses whether disease-associated genetic signals are shared with proximal cis-eQTL, distal trans-QTL or biomarker-QTL, thereby nominating candidate genes, programs, molecular biomarkers and cellular contexts for downstream functional investigation of AD susceptibility loci.

The TransMap framework is implemented as a command-line interface with modular components for transphenotype construction, QTL fine-mapping, and multi-phenotype colocalization (see Code Availability). Summary statistics from all program-level QTL maps and colocalization results are made publicly available to facilitate downstream analyses and broader scientific use (see Data Availability).

Local and global trans-regulatory programs capture reproducible genetic effects

Having defined local and global program-level phenotypes, we first evaluated whether these representations capture coordinated distal genetic effects, using independent trans-eQTL resources and simulation benchmarks. For local programs, we first asked whether Hotspot-derived co-expression modules grouped genes exhibiting shared trans-eQTL associations. We quantified "trans-eQTL coherence" for each significant variant-program pair as the proportion of constituent genes exhibiting suggestive association with the same distal variant (individual-gene association p-value< 10 "0 ) (Methods). Higher coherence therefore indicates that a programlevel genetic association is supported by a larger fraction of its constituent genes, reflecting tighter coordination of distal genetic associations within the program. Hotspot programs derived from GTEx brain cortex and whole blood expression data showed 1.6-fold and 1.8-fold higher mean trans-eQTL coherence compared to WGCNA programs when evaluated against independent MetaBrain and eQTLGen bulk trans-eQTL associations respectively (Figure 1b). Consistent improvements were observed in the ROSMAP cohort, where co-expression programs and trans-eQTL maps were derived from the same single-cell transcriptomic resource. Hotspot programs showed 1.3-2.9-fold higher mean coherence across cell types than WGCNA (Figure 1b; Supplementary Figures 1a-b). These differences were not explained by program size, as Hotspot and WGCNA programs had comparable mean sizes and the higher trans-eQTL coherence of Hotspot persisted after matching programs by size (Figure 1b).

We next benchmarked the WPC-derived local program activity phenotypes (TransMap-local) across a range of simulated gene program architectures varying (i) program size, (ii) sample size, (iii) the proportion of causal trans-genes, (iv) AR(1) residual expression covariance structure with varying correlation parameter 𝜌, (v) per-SNP trans heritability based on causal effect sizes, and (vi) the sign heterogeneity of trans-eQTL effect sizes for genes in the program (Supplementary Figure 1c; Methods). In each simulation, genetic effects propagated through a proximal cis-gene to a prespecified subset of distal genes within a program, with effect sizes calibrated to span low-, intermediate-and high trans heritability regimes 39 (Methods). We benchmarked TransMap-local against TransPCO 16 , Eigengene 40 and conventional single gene trans-eQTL approach (trans-eGene). We evaluated statistical power using Bonferroni-corrected, method-specific significance thresholds, and assessed type I error (T1E) under the 0%-causal null across 1,000 simulation replicates (Methods). Across many tested simulation scenarios, TransMap-local achieved the highest power while exhibiting comparable or lower T1E than competing methods (Figure 1c, Supplementary Figure 1d-e). Figure 1c illustrates a simulation setting with a 20-gene program containing 50% causal trans-genes, 1,000 samples, varying degrees of AR(1) residual expression correlation, and either homogeneous genetic effects (all causal genes affected in the same direction) or heterogeneous effects (equal proportions of positive and negative effects). Under sign heterogeneity, TransMap-local at low per-SNP trans heritability (0.0025) achieved 81.6% power, representing a 9.2%-36.7% improvement over competing approaches (Figure 1c). Under the sign homogeneous setting, with high per-SNP trans heritability and low residual correlation, TransMap-local performed similarly to Eigengene; however, its advantage increased when genetic effects were weakly homogeneous or sign heterogeneous. This pattern is consistent with the ability of background whitening and multiple retained WPCs to improve sensitivity to weak trans-regulatory effects and capture heterogeneous regulatory architectures, respectively, that may not be adequately represented by the first conventional principal component used in Eigengene. We further examined the effects of residual expression correlation and genetic-effect heterogeneity in direct comparisons with TransPCO, the strongest competing program-level method, which integrates association evidence across multiple conventional principal components but does not explicitly perform background covariance whitening (Figure 1d). For 68 out of 72 different simulation settings involving varying causal trans gene proportion, per-SNP trans heritability, residual correlation and sign heterogeneity, TransMap-local exhibited higher power compared to TransPCO. The largest power gains were observed under lower per-SNP trans heritability, consistent with improved sensitivity to weak distal genetic effects. Under the 0%-causal null, TransMap-local maintained well-calibrated T1E across the evaluated simulation settings, whereas TransPCO showed modest inflation under specific architectures (Supplementary Figure 1e).

Multi-scale trans-regulatory mapping reveals complementary disease genetic architectures

We next applied TransMap to bulk and single-nucleus RNA-sequencing profiles across 6 brain cell types and 3 brain cortical regions from the aging brain cortex in the ROSMAP cohort (average N = 481, Figure 2a, Supplementary Table 1, see Supplementary Figure 3a and Supplementary Notes S1-S2 for technical choices). Across cell types and contexts, we identified 17,054 significant TransMap-eGene QTL associations across 16,901 genes, 21,315 significant TransMap-local QTL associations across 6,453 Hotspot co-expression programs and 4,507 significant TransMap-global QTL associations across 2,700 global programs (FDR < 5%, Methods, Figure 2b). Among significant TransMap-eGene QTL associations, 19.9% involved genetic signals associated with multiple distal genes, compared with 10.5% of cis-eQTL associations involving multiple proximal genes. Similarly, 26.4% of TransMap-local QTL associations involved multiple programs. Given the large number of correlated molecular phenotypes and genome-wide genetic associations evaluated in trans-QTL mapping, we independently assessed the empirical calibration of the nominal FDR-controlled discoveries using genotypelabel permutations. The empirical FDR ranged 4.8%-9.2% in bulk contexts and ranged 7.7% to 14.9% across six cell types (Supplementary Figure 3b), indicating that conventional significance thresholds did not uniformly achieve nominal FDR control for single-phenotype trans-QTL mapping. These findings motivate the integration of complementary molecular-QTL evidence through multi-phenotype colocalization to provide additional statistical support for weak distal genetic associations, as evaluated in subsequent sections.

We generated 3 genome-wide variant-level trans-MaxPIP annotations separately for individual-gene (TransMap-eGene-MaxPIP), local-program (TransMap-local-MaxPIP) and global-program (TransMap-global-MaxPIP) phenotypes by aggregating fine-mapping posterior inclusion probabilities across phenotypes and cell types (Methods). We evaluated their disease relevance using two S-LDSC metrics: heritability enrichment and standardized effect size (𝜏 ⋆ ), the latter quantifying annotation informativeness conditional on other annotations in the model. In the meta-analysis across 57 traits, 2 out of 3 annotations (TransMap-eGene-MaxPIP and TransMap-local-MaxPIP) showed high heritability enrichment (2.75x-2.88x) (p-value ranging from 2.9 × 10 "23 to 4.3 × 10 "24 ) and 𝜏 ⋆ (0.22-0.27) (p-value ranging from 6.5 × 10 "56 to 3.5 × 10 "55 ) conditional on the baseline annotations (Figure 2c). Both annotations showed significant heritability enrichment and 𝜏 ⋆ when meta-analyzed across 18 brain-related traits and 22 blood-related traits, and remained significant when modeled jointly and consistent results were observed in brain-and blood-related trait meta-analyses (Figure 2c). These results indicate that individual distal genes and compact local programs capture complementary components of complex-trait genetic architecture, whereas broad global programs contribute no detectable heritability beyond baseline annotations.

We next benchmarked the disease relevance of TransMap annotations against existing approaches. At the local program scale, we compared TransMap-local-MaxPIP annotation against analogous trans-MaxPIP annotations generated using Eigengene, TransPCO and AUCell approaches (Methods). Because TransPCO combines multiple principal components (PC)-level association statistics using an ACAT omnibus test, we adapted it for fine-mapping by separately fine-mapping retained PCs and aggregating their PIPs into a MaxPIP annotation (TransPCO*-MaxPIP; Methods). TransMap-local-MaxPIP consistently exhibited 1.6x-3.0x higher heritability enrichment than the competing annotations across all-trait, blood-related and brain-related trait meta-analyses (Figure 2d, Supplementary Figure 4a). Both TransMap-local-MaxPIP and TransPCO*-MaxPIP showed significant heritability information (𝜏 ⋆ ) in meta-analyses across these trait categories. Notably, TransMap-local-MaxPIP exhibited 1.5x-2.3x higher 𝜏 ⋆ compared to TransPCO*-MaxPIP, and was the only annotation retaining significant positive conditional 𝜏 ⋆ when the two annotations were jointly included in the heritability model (Figure 2d, Supplementary Figure 4a). Besides the fine-mapping based comparison, we generated native TransPCO variant-level associations for 43,090 xQTL-informed independent variants (𝑟 7 <0.1, Supplementary Note S3), and compared heritability information against TransMap-local-MaxPIP restricted to these variants. TransMaplocal-MaxPIP retained its improved performance in this comparison (Supplementary Figure 4b). When WGCNA programs were used instead of Hotspot, TransMap-local-MaxPIP showed 13.9% lower heritability enrichment and 42.1% lower marginal 𝜏 ⋆ across the meta-analyses (Figure 2d, Supplementary Figure 4a). Nevertheless, WPC-based TransMap-local annotations remained more disease-informative than competing program-level approaches when applied to the same WGCNA programs, indicating that the advantage of background-whitened program representations is not restricted to Hotspot-derived programs. At global program scale, we benchmarked TransMap-global against ARCHIE 17 . Neither TransMap-global-MaxPIP or ARCHIE-MaxPIP showed significant heritability signals across all three meta-analyses (Supplementary Figure 4c). Therefore, we restricted all downstream program analyses to local Hotspot programs. At individual gene scale, TransMap-eGene-MaxPIP annotation showed 1.95x higher 𝜏 ⋆ in all traits meta-analysis compared to fine-mapping centered around significant trans-eQTL at FDR<5% as performed in eQTLGen Phase II 9 , while marginally associated trans-eQTL (p-value< 10 "0 ) did not show a significant 𝜏 ⋆ (Supplementary Figure 4d). In a joint S-LDSC model, TransMap-eGene-MaxPIP remained the only annotation with a significant positive 𝜏 ⋆ (𝜏 ⋆ =0.35, p-value=6.6 × 10 "88 ) (Supplementary Figure 4d). Thus, retaining genome-wide fine-mapping posterior evidence captured distributed disease-relevant trans-regulatory information that was lost when downstream analyses were restricted to conventionally significant trans-eQTL; suggesting hidden heritability information.

We compared the proportion of trait heritability captured by cis-MaxPIP annotations, defined as the maximum fine-mapping PIP for each variant across cis-eGenes and cell types 25 , with that captured by individual-gene (TransMap-eGene-MaxPIP) and local-program (TransMap-local-MaxPIP) trans-QTL annotations. Across 28 brain-related traits 26, 41 (Supplementary Table 4), individual-gene trans-MaxPIP annotations captured a greater proportion of heritability than cis-MaxPIP annotations for 9 of 28 traits. When individual-gene and local-program trans annotations were combined, this increased to 26 of 28 traits (Figure 2e; Supplementary Figure 4e), highlighting the additional disease-heritability information captured when local-program annotations are combined with individual-gene trans-QTL annotations. This pattern was particularly pronounced for Autism and Schizophrenia, whereas AD and multiple sclerosis showed more comparable proportions of heritability captured by cis-and trans-MaxPIP annotations.

We performed two secondary analyses. First, we assessed whether TransMap yielded reproducible variant-level trans-MaxPIP annotations across independent datasets. For each expression profile, we randomly split donors 50:50, independently reconstructed the three scales of trans phenotypes, and repeated genome-wide finemapping in both donor subsets (Methods). The resulting split-level trans-MaxPIP annotations showed Pearson's correlations ranging from 0.45 to 0.79 across regulatory scales (Supplementary Figure 5a). Despite the reduced sample sizes, the individual-gene and local-program annotations showed consistent disease-heritability signals, with significant enrichment and positive marginal 𝜏 * estimates in both splits (Supplementary Figure 5b). Second, we evaluated whether the framework could extend beyond data-derived local co-expression programs to two prespecified functional classes: 50 curated MSigDB Hallmark gene sets 16, 42, 43 and cell-type-resolved SCENIC regulons 44 (Methods). WPC-derived TransMap annotations from both program classes showed stronger disease-heritability informativeness than competing program-scoring approaches (Supplementary Figures 5c-d). When jointly modeled with our primary individual-gene and local-program TransMap annotations, most of their disease-relevant information was explained away, although Hallmark annotations retained additional conditional informativeness for blood-related traits (Supplementary Figure 5e).

We conclude that genome-wide trans-QTL fine-mapping yields reproducible and disease-informative variant annotations, with individual distal genes and compact local programs capturing complementary components of complex-trait genetic architecture.

Trans-regulatory loci from TransMap colocalize with proximal gene regulation and downstream biomarker-QTL and exhibit distinct functional architectures

Having established the disease relevance of individual-gene and local-program trans-QTL annotations, we next investigated whether distal genetic associations could be linked to proximal gene regulation and downstream bulk-level biomarker molecular phenotypes (proteins, glycoproteins and metabolites) through shared genetic signals. We performed a genome-wide multi-phenotype colocalization using ColocBoost 25 (Methods), representing each shared causal variant by a colocalization confidence set (CoS) if it contained at least one variant at p-value<10 "0 and its associated configuration of molecular phenotypes sharing the genetic signal. Across the genome, we retained 49,532 colocalization events involving trans-eGenes or trans-ePrograms with support from at least one proximal cis-eQTL or additional biomarker-QTL (Figure 3a, Supplementary Figure 6a). To assess the empirical reliability of these shared-signal associations, we performed two complementary permutation analyses, yielding empirical FDR estimates of 0.4%-1.34% for genome-wide trans-phenotype colocalization and 0.52%-0.95% for matched cis-trans configurations (Methods, Supplementary Figure 3b). When evaluated against independent cell-type resolved trans-eQTL maps in PsychAD 13 (N=1,494 samples), Colocalization-supported trans-eQTL associations showed 11.1-to 21.9-fold higher recall across cell types for similar precision compared to fine-mapped trans-eQTL hits (Figure 3b). Colocalization-supported trans-eQTL associations aggregated across cell types also showed higher recall with respect to the MetaBrain bulk trans-eQTL 10 (N=2,683 samples) compared to fine-mapped trans-eQTL hits (Supplementary Figure 6b). The lower empirical FDR estimates and stronger external concordance support the increased reliability of colocalizationsupported distal genetic associations compared to standard single-phenotype trans-QTL maps. Among all cis-eQTL signals, 26.7% were linked to at least one distal trans-regulatory phenotype, including 17.3% linked to both individual-gene and program-level trans phenotypes (Supplementary Figure 6c), revealing shared genetic associations across distinct scales of distal expression regulation.

We designated colocalization events containing at least one cis-eGene and one trans-eGene or trans-eProgram as candidate cis-trans regulatory configurations (Methods). These included 2,198 cis-trans configurations and 1,825 cis-trans-biomarker configurations. We next examined whether program-level trans-QTL showed stronger genetic convergence with biomarker-QTL than individual-gene trans-QTL. Among trans-QTL loci with biomarker-QTL support, 86.4% were connected to individual trans-eGenes and 59.6% to trans-ePrograms, with substantial overlap of 46.2% across two trans-regulatory scales (Supplementary Figure 6d). Despite their lower representation among biomarker-supported loci, trans-eProgram QTL showed 1.35-1.40-fold enrichment for biomarker-QTL convergence compared to trans-eGene QTL (Figure 3c), indicating that program-level phenotypes preferentially identify trans-regulatory effects with measurable downstream molecular consequences. Trans-eGene and trans-eProgram QTL identified in glial cell types showed stronger enrichment (1.27x-1.41x, p-value < 10 "0 ), whereas excitatory-neuron trans-QTL showed no significant enrichment across the three biomarker modalities (p-value=0.15; Supplementary Figure 6e).

We next examined the functional properties of genes associated with these colocalization events. We classified the colocalization events as cis-only, cis-trans or trans-only based on the representation of cis and trans phenotypes among colocalized phenotypes (Methods). Pairs of distal trans-eGenes implicated by the same colocalization event showed 1.5-fold enrichment (p-value=0.009) for known protein-protein interactions in the STRING database 45 , whereas pairs connecting proximal cis-eGenes and distal trans-eGenes showed no detectable enrichment (Figure 3f). Importantly, pairs of highly weighted genes within the same program exhibited an even higher 1.86-fold enrichment in protein-protein interactions. These results indicate that program-level trans-QTL preferentially implicate groups of functionally connected genes, complementing their stronger convergence with biomarker-QTL. We further examined whether cis-only, cis-trans and trans-only colocalization events exhibited distinct genomic and evolutionary properties. Genes nearest to trans-only QTL were significantly enriched for a curated set of 1,639 human transcription factors 46 (1.2x-1.3x with p-value < 0.001) (Supplementary Figure 7a). Cis-only and cis-trans variants were located closer to transcription start sites and showed stronger evolutionary conservation 47, 48 than trans-only variants (Supplementary Figures 7b-c). However, genes nearest to trans-only loci showed greater enrichment for loss-of-function constraint, with 1.64fold and 1.58-fold enrichment for loci involving trans-eGenes and trans-ePrograms, respectively (p-value < 0.001; Supplementary Figure 7d). These findings reveal distinct patterns of evolutionary constraint at transonly loci, with weaker variant-level conservation but greater loss-of-function intolerance among nearby genes.

This observation is consistent with recent reports that trans-regulatory loci can occur near highly constrained genes 46 , although the functional relationship between these genes and the associated distal molecular phenotypes remains unresolved.

Overall, these analyses establish that multi-phenotype colocalization provides additional statistical support for distal genetic associations and reveals complementary molecular and functional properties of individual-gene and program-level trans-QTL. In particular, local-program associations show stronger convergence with biomarker variation and functionally connected gene networks, supporting their utility for interpreting distal regulatory effects across molecular layers.

Neuronal cis effects converge on coordinated glial trans responses

Having identified candidate cis-trans regulatory configurations, we next investigated whether shared genetic signals revealed systematic relationships between proximal and distal regulatory effects across brain cell types. Out of 4,023 cis-trans colocalization events, 32.3% involved distinct initiating and recipient cell types (Figure 4a). Trans-QTL represented in these colocalization events showed greater cell type specificity compared to cis-eQTL (Figure 4a). Cis-eQTL were distributed similarly between neuronal and glial populations, whereas trans-QTLs were enriched in glial cell types and relatively depleted in neurons (Figure 4b, Supplementary Figure 8a). Permutation tests revealed significant enrichment of neuronal-initiated, glial-recipient configurations among the initiating and recipient cell type pairs underlying the colocalization events (Methods, FDR<5%; Figure 4c, Supplementary Figure 8b). Conversely, excitatory neurons showed depleted representations among recipient cell types (0.34x-0.86x), with the strongest depletion observed for inhibitory-neuron-initiated configurations (Figure 4d, Supplementary Figure 8c). A similar pattern was observed when trans-eGene and trans-eProgram QTL were considered separately, supporting consistency across regulatory scales (Supplementary Figures 8d-e). We assessed whether these patterns could be explained by differences in cell-type representation as excitatory neurons comprised a large 39.8% of the 630K cells studied. We therefore performed donor-stratified subsampling of excitatory neurons, independently reconstructing their cis-and trans-eQTL maps while leaving other cell types unchanged (Methods). Enrichment of excitatory-neuron-initiated, glial-recipient configurations and the greater representation of excitatory neurons among initiating cell types persisted across the subsampled datasets (Supplementary Figures 8f-g). Fewer than 0.5% of the candidate cis-trans configurations overlapped detectable cell type fraction QTL, suggesting that the observed asymmetry was not predominantly explained by the cell-composition QTL identified in this cohort (Supplementary Figure 8h-i).

We next evaluated whether neuronal-initiated, glial-recipient configurations were associated with coherent biological pathways and molecular functions. Among colocalization events involving excitatory neuron cis effects, astrocyte recipient trans-eGenes were enriched for asparagine N-linked glycosylation and post-translational protein modification (FDR<5%; Figure 4e). We additionally identified 26 colocalization events involving 53 glycoproteins, with enrichment for Neurofascin-and L1CAM-related interaction pathways, involving genes such as NRCAM, CNTNAP1 and NFASC (FDR<5%, Supplementary Figure 9a). Of these glycoproteins, 52.5% showed STRING protein-protein interaction connections with colocalized astrocyte trans-eGenes (score>0.4 45 ; Supplementary Figure 9b). Microglial trans-eGenes linked to excitatory-neuron cis effects were strongly enriched for RHO-ROCK signaling and a SEMA4D-associated cell-migration pathway (FDR<5%, Figure 4e). These pathways shared genes including RHOB, ROCK2, LIMK1 and MYL12B, consistent with previously reported roles of Rho-family signaling and SEMA4D in microglial responses 49, 50 . These findings indicate that candidate cross-cell-type configurations implicate distinct, functionally coherent glial programs associated with proximal neuronal genetic signals.

We next sought orthogonal support for the inferred neuronal-glial configurations using independent neuronastrocyte co-culture transcriptomic data and spatial transcriptomic measurements from the ROSMAP cortex. First, in the co-culture experiment, human Ngn2-induced excitatory neurons were cultured with and without mouse cortical astrocytes and profiled by single-cell RNA sequencing at five time points 51 . Differentially expressed genes were pooled across time points for downstream enrichment analyses (Methods, Figure 4f). Excitatory-neuron trans-eGenes from astrocyte-initiated configurations were specifically 1.43-fold enriched among neuronal genes responsive to astrocyte co-culture (Figure 4f). Similarly, astrocyte trans-eGenes from excitatory-neuron-initiated configurations were 1.32-fold enriched among astrocytic genes responsive to neuronal co-culture. This directional correspondence was observed for both trans-eGenes and key driver genes of trans-ePrograms (Supplementary Figure 9c). Second, we examined the anatomical organization of neuronal-glial configurations using 10x Visium spatial transcriptomic data from 32 ROSMAP cortical tissue sections spanning different stages of AD pathology 52 (Methods, Figure 4g). Cis-regulated neuronal genes participating in neuronal-glial configurations showed preferential expression in deep cortical layers (L4-L6) proximal to the white matter compared to the outer layers (L1-L3), whereas their associated glial trans-regulated genes were enriched in adjacent white matter regions (Methods, Figure 4g, Supplementary Figure 9d). The pattern was preserved across pathological stages and across strata of amyloid-plaque burden (Supplementary Figure 9e). Although spot-level resolution precludes direct inference of cell-cell contact, these results place candidate initiating neuronal genes and recipient glial responses in anatomically adjacent cortical compartments. To illustrate how TransMap resolves candidate cross-cell-type regulatory configurations at individual genetic loci, we first examined an excitatory neuron-initiated (cis) and microglial-recipient (trans) colocalization event in the SEC61G gene locus (CoS spanning chr7:54723624-54780677). The SEC61G cis-eQTL in excitatory neurons colocalized with 23 microglial trans-eGene QTL, as well as 2 microglial trans-eProgram QTL (Program 31 and Program 100) (Figure 5a). To investigate whether variation in neuronal SEC61G expression statistically mediated the corresponding microglial trans associations, we applied structural equation model 53 (SEM) separately for each colocalized microglial trans-eGene and trans-eProgram (Methods). 10 gene-level and 2 program-level associations showed significant indirect effects (p-value < 0.05 based on donor bootstrap analysis) (Figures 5b). Both colocalized trans-ePrograms also contained multiple genes with marginal associations at this locus: 2 of 49 genes in Program 31 and 2 of 18 in Program 100 had p-value< 10 "0 . Pathway analysis linked Program 31 to the TYROBP causal network in microglia (FDR=0.006) and erythropoietin-activated PI3K signaling (FDR=0.01; Figure 5c), whereas Program 100 was enriched for nephrin-family interactions (FDR=0.0034) and RHO GTPase-related pathways (FDR=0.004; Supplementary Figure 10a). To investigate the anatomical distribution of the genes implicated by this candidate configuration, we examined SEC61G and the associated microglial trans-gene MAP4K4 in ROSMAP Visium spatial transcriptomic sections spanning different stages of AD pathology. SEC61G expression was more prominent across cortical layers, whereas MAP4K4 expression was stronger in white-matter regions (Figure 5d), consistent with the broader spatial organization observed for neuronal cis-and glial trans-associated genes (Figure 4g). We next examined a complementary configuration linking neuronal cis regulation to astrocytic transcriptional programs and glycoprotein variation. Consistent with the enrichment of astrocytic trans-eGenes for N-linked glycosylation pathways, an excitatory-neuron GDA cis-eQTL (CoS spanning chr9:72,219,468-72,236,972) colocalized with QTL for 9 glycoproteins, including NRCAM, CNTNAP1, NFASC and CNTN2, and a 97-gene astrocytic trans-eProgram (Supplementary Figure 10b). These 9 glycoproteins were consistently enriched for neurofascin interactions and L1CAM interactions with FDR < 0.01 (Supplementary Figure 10c). The astrocytic trans-eProgram was included heavily prioritized genes SLC3A2, ALG3, KDELR1 and SEC24C, that overlapped genes responsive to neuron-responsive astrocyte genes in the co-culture experiment (Supplementary Figure 10d). Although SEM found no significant indirect association through GDA excitatory neuron expression or astrocyte trans-eProgram to glycoproteins, the STRING protein-protein interaction network linked the glycoproteins to genes in astrocyte trans-eProgram (Supplementary Figure 10e).

These analyses reveal an asymmetric organization of cell-type-resolved cis-trans genetic associations, characterized by preferential convergence of proximal neuronal signals with functionally coherent glial distal responses. Concordance with co-culture transcriptional responses and spatial organization provides complementary biological support for these candidate cross-cell-type regulatory configurations.

Multiscale trans-regulatory maps resolve the functional architecture of Alzheimer's disease loci

We evaluated how TransMap trans-QTL resolve the regulatory architecture of Alzheimer's disease. We performed AD-centered multi-phenotype colocalization analysis integrating cis-eGene, trans-eGene, trans-eProgram and biomarker-QTL maps with a recent AD GWAS meta-analysis 54 (cases=128,681, controls=849,833) (Methods). Overall, we identified 118 colocalization events involving AD and at least one molecular-QTL (Figure 6a). For 51.7% of these events, the corresponding AD GWAS locus lacked a wellresolved 95% credible set from GWAS-only SuSiE fine-mapping, illustrating how molecular-QTL integration can nominate shared regulatory signals at loci that remain poorly resolved by GWAS fine-mapping alone. (Supplementary Figure 11a). 84 of these 118 colocalization events showed both cis-and trans-QTL evidence, 3 showed cis-only evidence and 31 showed trans-only evidence; 70.4% of the events involving trans-QTL had support across multiple trans-regulatory layers. Trans-eProgram QTL showed 1.43-fold enrichment among AD colocalization events relative to trans-eGene QTL (p-value= 5.4 × 10 "27 ; Figure 6b). Among AD colocalization events involving trans-eGene QTL and trans-eProgram QTL, 74.8% also showed biomarker-QTL evidence (Figure 6a), revealing frequent convergence of disease association signals with distal expression phenotypes and additional molecular traits. No AD-molecular-QTL colocalization events were recovered under the genotypepermutation null, providing additional empirical support for the observed associations (Methods). These results highlight the utility of trans regulatory information specifically in the context of understanding AD disease genetics. Bulk tissue cis-eQTL were more frequently represented among AD colocalization events than bulk tissue trans-QTL (20.7% versus 4.3%; Supplementary Figure 11b). AD colocalization events frequently involved distinct trans phenotypes across multiple cellular contexts; 59 loci implicated more than three recipient cell types (Figure 6b). This extends prior observations that a single AD locus may engage distinct distal genes or programs across several brain cell populations 55 . Microglia was the most frequently represented initiating cell types, and astrocytes and OPC were significantly enriched among recipient cell types (Supplementary Figures 11c-d).

We next investigated whether AD-colocalized variants exhibited distinct evolutionary properties according to their molecular-QTL configurations. For each AD-cis, AD-trans or combined AD-cis-trans colocalization event, we selected the variant with the highest variant colocalization probability (VCP) and compared its evolutionary conservation score 47, 48 (Methods). Variants supported by both cis-and trans-QTL colocalization showed the highest conservation scores, significantly exceeding those with trans-only support (p-value = 0.0007; Figure 6c). As a GWAS-only reference, we selected the highest-PIP variant from AD fine-mapping at loci without detectable cis-or trans-QTL colocalization. These AD-only variants also showed significantly greater conservation than ADtrans-only variants (p-value = 0.04; Figure 6c). To explore whether variant conservation differed across cellular configurations, we regressed conservation scores against indicators of candidate initiating cell types, recipient cell types and initiating-recipient pairs (Methods). No initiating cell type was significantly associated with greater conservation. Among recipient cell types, microglia and oligodendrocytes were associated with higher conservation (p-value = 0.01 for each), whereas astrocytes and inhibitory neurons were associated with lower conservation (p-value = 0.02 for each; Supplementary Figure 11e). Exploratory pair-level analyses also identified nominally higher conservation for selected excitatory-neuron-initiated configurations involving OPC, oligodendrocyte and microglial recipient phenotypes (Supplementary Figure 11e). These findings suggest that evolutionary conservation varies across AD-associated molecular QTL configurations and their implicated cell types, complementing the neuronal-glial asymmetry observed genome-wide.

We visualized the genome-wide distribution of AD-associated molecular-QTL evidence by annotating the AD GWAS Manhattan plot with colocalized cis-and trans-regulatory phenotypes (Figure 6d; Supplementary Figure 11f). This revealed substantial heterogeneity in the regulatory evidence associated with different AD loci. Some loci were predominantly supported by multiple distal regulatory phenotypes, including the APP region (highest VCP variant rs432766), whereas others showed convergence of proximal and distal associations, such as the BIN1 locus (rs6733839), linking BIN1 cis-eQTL with an AP4M1 trans-eQTL, and the APH1B locus (rs117618017), linking APH1B cis-eQTL with 11 trans-eGenes, including IKBKE, and two trans-ePrograms. Several loci with comparatively modest AD association statistics also showed colocalization with multiple distal or biomarker phenotypes, illustrating the additional molecular context provided by integrated genetic associations. To assess the reproducibility of these findings across AD genetic studies, we repeated AD-focused TransMap colocalization using a previous GWAS meta-analysis 56 with a smaller effective sample size (111,326 cases and 677,663 controls). This analysis replicated 69 out of the 118 colocalization events, including 44 with replicated cis-and trans-QTL evidence (Supplementary Figure 12).

These analyses demonstrate how multiscale, cell-type-resolved trans-QTL maps enhance the discovery and functional characterization of AD-associated regulatory signals by prioritizing candidate regulatory genes, coordinated expression programs, cellular contexts and biomarker-associated molecular phenotypes at AD susceptibility loci.

Multiscale cis-trans regulatory maps connect genes across independent AD susceptibility loci

We next examined two case studies illustrating how multiscale trans-regulatory mapping can connect molecular associations across independent AD susceptibility loci and nominate candidates for experimental follow-up.

We first examined an AD-associated locus near KNOP1 on chromosome 16 57, 58 , where an astrocytic KNOP1 cis-eQTL colocalized with a microglial trans-eProgram (Program 27) containing MS4A4E, MS4A6A, APOE and RIN3 (Figure 7a). Among the distal molecular phenotypes implicated at this locus, microglial Program 27 showed the strongest statistical support for an indirect association in SEM analysis (Supplementary Figure 13a). Astrocytic KNOP1 expression exhibited the largest estimated indirect effect among the evaluated proximal candidates (Figure 7b). The 87-gene Program 27 included MS4A4E, which ranked fourth among constituent genes by marginal trans-association significance, and STRING analysis placed MS4A4E in a functionally connected component with APOE, RIN3 and MS4A6A (Figure 7c). Interestingly, MS4A4E was also implicated as a proximal cis-regulated gene at the independent chromosome 11 MS4A-family AD locus 59 . AD-centered colocalization at this locus identified 2 distinct colocalization confidence sets with different molecular-QTL configurations (minimum between-set purity = 0.44; Figure 7d), consistent with distinct signals also identified by AD GWAS fine-mapping. One colocalization event included microglia-specific cis-effects at MS4A4A and MS4A6A, the other one included MS4A4E cis-eQTL in DLPFC and 9 trans-eGene-QTLs and 3 trans-eProgram-QTL in inhibitory neurons (52.2% of all colocalized trans phenotypes; Supplementary Figure 13b). STRING protein-protein interaction analysis revealed functional connections among the implicated inhibitory-neuron trans-eGenes and colocalized protein phenotypes (Supplementary Figure 13c). One of the colocalized trans-ePrograms (program 59) was enriched in MECP2-related transcription and protein-folding pathways, (FDR < 5%; Supplementary Figures 13d-e). These observations connect two independent AD susceptibility loci through MS4A4E, which appears as a distal member of a microglial program associated with the KNOP1 locus and as a proximal cis-regulated gene at the MS4A locus, where the same genetic signal also implicates an inhibitory-neuron program enriched for MECP2-related transcription 60, 61 . This cross-locus convergence implicated by TransMap nominates MS4A4E for AD susceptibility, but whether it play the key role of AD risk at MS4A-family locus remains to be established experimentally.

We next examined a complementary cross-locus configuration beginning with INO80E at the chromosome 16p11.2 BP4-BP5 region 62, 63 , a structurally complex genomic locus. At this locus, an AD-associated signal colocalized with cis-eQTL for ten genes across expression contexts and a DLPFC trans-eQTL for ABCA1, an established AD-associated gene at an independent chromosome 9 locus 64, 65 (Figure 7e; Supplementary Table 7). To prioritize candidate proximal regulators of the distal ABCA1 association, we fitted structural equation models with ABCA1 expression as the distal outcome (Methods). Among the ten proximal cis-eGenes, INO80E expression in excitatory neurons and oligodendrocytes showed nominally significant indirect associations with distal ABCA1 expression (p-value < 0.05; Figure 7f). INO80E encodes a subunit of the INO80 chromatin remodeling complex 66, 67 and belonged to a functionally connected group of these local cis-eGenes in the STRING network, while they did not connect with ABCA1 (Supplementary Figure 14a). We then examined indirect associations among the local cis-eGenes with INO80E by fitting additional mediations with INO80E alternately as mediator and outcome. These models yielded 14 and 15 indirect associations, respectively (p-value < 0.05; Supplementary Figures 14b-c). With INO80E modeled as the mediator, MAPK3 and KCTD13 showed the strongest indirect effects among the local genes. Although ERK1/2 and RhoA signaling have been implicated in ABCA1 regulation in other systems [68] [69] [70] [71] , whether INO80E contributes to the distal ABCA1 association through either gene remains to be tested experimentally. At the independent chromosome 9 ABCA1 locus, ABCA1 instead appeared as a proximal cis-regulated gene, with bulk-tissue ABCA1 cis-eQTL colocalizing with astrocytic and microglial trans-eProgram QTL (Figure 7g). These configurations also showed nominal support for indirect associations in SEM analyses. Within the 79-gene microglial program, FDFT1 ranked sixth by marginal transassociation significance and was functionally connected to ABCA1 in the STRING interaction network (Figure 7h). These results link independent AD-associated loci through ABCA1, which appears as a distal transregulated gene associated with the INO80E locus and as a proximal cis-regulated gene connected to glial regulatory programs at its own locus.

Discussion

TransMap provides a generalizable framework for performing (i) multiscale cell-type-resolved trans-QTL mapping, involving individual genes and gene programs, and (ii) multi-phenotype colocalization of these trans-QTL with cell-type-resolved cis-eQTL and biomarker-QTL. Applied to the aging human brain through the ADSP Functional Genomics Consortium (FunGen-AD), TransMap provides a cell-type-resolved atlas of transregulatory variation and their relationships with AD-associated genetic variation. The resulting maps reveal (i) complementary contributions of individual-gene and local program-level QTL annotations to complex trait heritability, (ii) stronger correspondence of program-level genetic signals with downstream biomarker-QTL and functional networks, (iii) candidate cis-trans regulatory configurations spanning distinct brain cell types, and (iv) expanded interpretation of AD susceptibility loci through shared genetic associations with distal genes, regulatory programs and molecular biomarkers.

A central insight from TransMap is that distal genetic regulation can be more sensitively detected and functionally characterized through coordinated molecular responses than through individual gene associations alone. Although a regulatory variant may exert only weak effects on individual distal genes, its effects can converge on groups of co-regulated genes, producing a more detectable program-level genetic signal. TransMap's whitened PCA approach improves the detection of coordinated trans effects while accounting for background expression covariance and capturing multiple axes of regulatory variation within a program. Notably, individual-gene and local-program trans-QTL annotations capture complementary components of complex-trait heritability despite being derived from the same transcriptomic measurements, highlighting the value of representing distal genetic regulation at multiple molecular scales. Complementing this program-based strategy, multi-phenotype colocalization leverages shared genetic signals across molecular phenotypes to support weak trans associations that may not achieve conventional significance thresholds individually. More broadly, the integration of multiple molecular modalities provides complementary views of the consequences of genetic variation, allowing evidence from proximal gene regulation, distal expression programs and molecular biomarkers to jointly inform transregulatory inference. By connecting these complementary molecular phenotypes through shared genetic signals, TransMap can pinpoint candidate regulatory relationships underlying an association, providing a more detailed basis for downstream functional investigation. The stronger convergence of program-level trans-QTL with biomarker-QTL and functional protein networks further suggests that coordinated expression programs provide informative representations of distal genetic effects across molecular layers. Moreover, the enrichment of neuronal cis-regulatory signals among loci associated with glial trans-regulatory responses, supported by coculture and spatial transcriptomic analyses, suggests that distal genetic regulation may involve relationships across cellular contexts rather than being restricted to the cell type containing the proximal genetic effect. This has broader implications for disease-variant interpretation, as the cellular context of proximal gene regulation may differ from those exhibiting associated distal molecular responses, as also highlighted in recent mouse models 72, 73 . These findings support a multiscale and multimodal view of trans regulation in which genetic effects are distributed across coordinated molecular programs, molecular layers and cellular contexts.

TransMap provides several reusable outputs and opportunities for downstream applications in human genetics. First, its modular end-to-end pipeline for trans-acting phenotype construction, genome-wide QTL fine-mapping, and multi-phenotype colocalization, can be deployed across diverse tissues, cell types and molecular QTL cohorts. This framework may be particularly useful in tissue-specific or cell-type-resolved QTL datasets with modest sample sizes, where conventional individual-gene trans-QTL discovery remains underpowered. Moreover, as molecular QTL resources expand across transcriptomic, proteomic, metabolomic and other molecular measurements, integrating these complementary genetic views offers opportunities to improve the detection and functional interpretation of distal regulatory effects. Second, although the gene programs examined in this study were primarily derived from the analyzed tissues, TransMap can also incorporate externally defined gene programs, including those identified from mouse models or genetic perturbation experiments, to investigate their regulation by human genetic variation. Third, genome-wide fine-mapped trans-eGene and trans-eProgram QTL provide variant-level annotations that can complement cis-eQTL annotations in functional annotation-based heritability analyses 32, 34 and potentially inform polygenic risk prediction 74, 75 and polygenic localization methods 30 across a broad range of complex diseases and traits. Fourth, cis-regulated genes and distal trans-eGenes or trans-ePrograms implicated through colocalization with disease GWAS provide specific molecular and cellular hypotheses for targeted functional validation. In particular, cell-type-resolved cis-trans regulatory configurations can guide the design of cell-type-specific CRISPR perturbation experiments in co-culture systems, where candidate cis-regulated genes are perturbed in putative initiating cell types and their predicted distal transcriptional responses are measured in recipient cell types 76 . Finally, while the present study primarily focuses on molecular assays with single readouts, we are currently extending TransMap to 10X scRNA+ATAC multiome datasets as part of the FunGen-AD effort to characterize distal genetic regulation across coupled molecular layers.

Our work has several limitations, representing important directions for future research. First, the substantial differences in effect sizes between proximal cis-eQTL and weaker distal trans-QTL create statistical challenges for performing untargeted multi-phenotype colocalization jointly across cis and trans phenotypes, motivating our strategy of performing separate cis-and trans-QTL colocalization analyses, followed by integration of colocalization events sharing genetic signals. Additionally, weak individual-gene cell-type-resolved trans-QTL associations exhibited elevated empirical false discovery rates despite nominal FDR control. In contrast, multiphenotype colocalization yielded trans-regulatory associations with lower empirical false discovery rates in the evaluated permutation analyses and stronger concordance with independent trans-QTL resources (Supplementary Figure 4c). Further methodological development is needed to accommodate differences in statistical power across molecular phenotypes and enable more comprehensive joint cis-trans colocalization. Second, although TransMap identifies candidate configurations through multi-phenotype colocalization, shared genetic associations do not establish that a cis-regulated gene causally mediates a downstream trans effect or that cross-cell-type associations reflect direct intercellular signaling. Although statistical mediation, co-culture and spatial transcriptomic analyses provide complementary support for selected configurations, targeted genetic perturbation experiments will be important for establishing the causal architecture of these regulatory relationships. Third, the performance of background-whitened PCA depends on accurately estimating the background expression covariance structure. Limited sample sizes, expression sparsity and heterogeneous cellular states may compromise this estimation and affect the sensitivity and interpretability of derived programs. Fourth, strong residual correlations among molecular phenotypes can result in loss of power for large-scale multiphenotype colocalization, as highlighted in the ColocBoost paper 25 . The residual correlations estimated in the present datasets fall within the range where statistical power and false discovery rates were generally well maintained 25 , and empirical permutation analyses further support favorable null calibration in the current application. Fifth, the present study does not systematically address cross-cohort harmonization of expression measurements, program definitions and genetic association signals, which will be necessary for integrating independently generated trans-QTL maps and improving portability across datasets and populations.

Methods

TransMap: an end-to-end framework for distal genetic regulation across cell types

TransMap is proposed as an end-to-end computational framework that represents trans regulation at three molecular resolutions from gene expression, including individual distal genes (trans-eGene) and gene programs (trans-eProgram). A gene program may be represented by a set of small number of genes (typically ranging from tens to hundreds of genes) or a continuous weighting of all genes genome-wide (Figure 1a). Each trans-acting gene or gene program within a cell type is encoded as a donor-level quantitative activity phenotype and carried through genome-wide LD-aware trans-QTL fine-mapping. These cell-type resolved trans-QTL are then analyzed together with cell-type-resolved cis-eQTL and downstream bulk tissue biomarker-QTL using multi-phenotype colocalization, which tests whether proximal cis signals, distal trans signals and downstream biomarker signals are consistent with a shared underlying genetic locus. Colocalization indicates compatibility with a shared causal variant across a set of molecular phenotypes but does not establish mediation or direct intercellular signaling. TransMap nominated candidate regulatory configurations represented by (cis-eGene, cis-effect cell type, trans-eGene/eProgram, trans-effect cell type, trans-biomarker). We term the cis-effect cell types as 'initiating cell types' and the distal trans-effect cell types as 'recipient cell types'. This design allows TransMap to compare regulatory evidence across scales and cell types for biological characterization.

Genome-wide trans-QTL characterization through statistical fine-mapping

Statistical fine-mapping analysis provides an LD-aware approach to pinpoint putative causal variants underlying regional association signals. Each independent signal is summarized by an 𝛼-level credible set (CS) and variantlevel posterior inclusion probabilities (PIP) 24 . TransMap defines an independent trans-QTL as a distal genetic signal represented by a 95% CS for a standardized, cell-type-resolved, donor-level trans molecular phenotype 𝒀 "# from statistical fine-mapping analysis. Each 𝒀 "# can represent an individual distal-gene expression trait (TransMap-eGene), a local WPC program score (TransMap-local), or a global sCCA program score (TransMapglobal). We summarized the resulting trans-QTL and PIP as genome-wide variant-level trans-MaxPIP annotations to evaluate their enrichment for complex-trait heritability (Methods).

Genome-wide fine-mapping is organized across 1,361 approximately independent autosomal LD blocks 26 . Each LD block is extended by 50 kb on both sides to define a fine-mapping region and reduce boundary effects for signals near block edges. For each region on chromosome 𝑐, we first assembled the corresponding trans phenotype set across three scales by excluding genes on chromosome 𝑐. We then performed fine-mapping using the SuSiE (Sum of Single Effects) model 24 with a maximum of 10 causal signals per locus and purity= 0.5. We defined a significant trans-QTL association as a 95% credible set (CS) from SuSIE containing at least one variant with a marginal association p-value below the method-specific threshold corresponding to FDR < 5%.

To assess the expected false discovery burden, we estimated the effective number of independent common variants (MAF > 5%) in the ROSMAP WGS reference panel as 619,380 (PLINK --indep-pairwise 50 1 0.2) 9 . Across ~108K individual trans-genes (average 12,444 genes, Supplementary Table 1) and ~9K trans-programs (average 1,017 programs, Supplementary Figure 1a) genome-wide scans spanning 3 cortical regions and 6 major brain cell types, we identified 91,019 and 42,030 independent 95% credible sets whose lead variant reached p-value < 5 × 10 "6 and p-value < 5 × 10 "K , respectively. Under a calibrated null, we would expect approximately 3,345 (619,380 × 108K × 5 × 10 "6 ) and 2,787 (619,380 × 9K × 5 × 10 "K ) false-positive credible sets at these thresholds, corresponding to approximate FDRs of 3.7% and 6.6% 9 . Because correlation among phenotypes does not alter the expected number of false positives, these estimates are approximately unbiased but do not account for residual p-value miscalibration, which we assess empirically using genotype-label permutations.

Genome-wide QTL discovery for curated and data-driven gene programs

To enable program-level QTL discovery beyond co-expression modules, we extended TransMap to analyze any prespecified functional gene set. For each gene set and expression context, TransMap applies the WPC procedure used for TransMap-local to derive donor-level co-regulatory activity scores. Each retained WPC is treated as a quantitative molecular phenotype and subjected to genome-wide fine-mapping across 1,361 LD blocks 26 . For trans-specific mapping of variants on chromosome 𝑐, genes located on this chromosome are excluded before phenotype construction; complementary network-wide mapping retain all member genes to capture genetic effects on the complete program. To evaluate curated biological programs, we analyzed the 50 Hallmark gene sets from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) previously examined using TransPCO 16 , comprising 32-200 genes and provide coherent and relatively nonredundant representation of biological states and processes. To evaluate data-driven regulatory programs, we inferred context-specific TF-target regulons separately for six major brain cell types and three bulk cortical regions using SCENIC 44 : GRNBoost2 78 was first used to estimate weighted regulatory association between human TFs and all expressed genes, after which the top highest-ranked targets per TF were partitioned according to their correlation direction and pruned using RcisTarge motif enrichment (normalized enrichment score > 3.0) 79 . The resulting motif-supported regulons and Hallmark gene sets were summarized using WPC and mapped using the same genome-wide framework. These applications establish TransMap as a general-purpose for converting curated or data-driven gene sets into quantitative phenotype for program-level QTL discovery.

Multi-phenotype colocalization of cross-cell-type cis-trans regulatory paths

A trans-QTL can represent a downstream molecular consequence of a proximal cis effect, in which local genetic regulation propagates to distal molecular phenotypes within or across cell types. These effects may further converge with downstream biomarker signals measured through proteomic, glycoproteomic and metabolomic assays. Proximal cis-regulatory genes are relatively few and typically have stronger cis-QTL effects, whereas distal trans-regulatory phenotypes are far more numerous and individually weaker 7 . This imbalance in dimensionality and association strength can substantially limit power for a single joint colocalization analysis. We therefore implemented a two-stage genome-wide multi-phenotype colocalization strategy using ColocBoost 25.

In the first stage, we identified colocalization among distal molecular phenotypes within the same 1,361 extended LD block regions. Candidate phenotypes comprised cell-type-resolved trans genes and trans programs together with trans biomarkers when available. Within each region, we retained phenotypes for which fine-mapping identified at least one 50% CS overlapping the region 26 . To reduce redundancy, highly correlated phenotypes were pruned within each phenotype class at an absolute pairwise correlation of | 𝑟 |≥ 0.7. ColocBoost was then applied jointly to the retained distal phenotypes. Each trans-only colocalization event was summarized by a 95% colocalization confidence set (CoS), variant-level variant colocalization probabilities (VCP), and a trait configuration specifying the subset of phenotypes compatible with the shared genetic signal. We retained 95% CoS as an independent locus for colocalization events when it contains at least one variant with marginal pvalue < 10 "0 for the traits in configurations.

In the second stage, we matched these distal colocalization events to events from a separate genome-wide gene-level colocalization analysis of cell-type-resolved cis-eQTL 26 . A distal trans-only colocalization event and a proximal cis-only colocalization event were integrated when their 95% CoS shared the same causal variants. Therefore, we define a `Colocalization Event` as this integrated colocalization confidence set and an associated configuration of molecular phenotypes sharing the genetic signal. Configurations that link one or more proximal cis-eGenes to one or more distal trans-eGenes or trans-ePrograms were defined as `Candidate Regulatory Configurations`, indicating the core cis-trans link and may further connect to a downstream trans-biomarker. The cis-effect cell types define 'initiating cell types', whereas the distal trans-effect cell types define 'recipient cell types' (Figure 1a). Because the two stages were connected through overlap between independently estimated colocalization events, these configurations do not represent formal joint cis-trans colocalization and cannot establish mediation or direct intercellular signaling. Nevertheless, this design allows TransMap to organize shared regulatory evidence across molecular scales and cell types for subsequent biological characterization.

AD-prioritized multi-phenotype colocalization of cis-and trans-regulatory signals

In contrast to LD-block-based genome-wide fine-mapping and colocalization analyses, AD-prioritized colocalization was organized around topologically associating domain (TAD)-based regions containing candidate AD loci. We extracted candidate AD loci curated by the FunGen-xQTL project 26 and mapped their constituent variants by genomic position to a genome-wide reference set of 1,328 TAD regions 27 . Regions containing at least one candidate AD variant were retained, and candidate variants mapping to the same TAD region were analyzed jointly. Because a TAD region may contain multiple proximal genes, each TAD region was therefore extended to encompass the cis windows of all candidate genes assigned to that region, with each window defined as ±1.5 Mb from the transcription start site 27.

For each tested TAD region on chromosome 𝑐, we performed targeted QTL mapping of all candidate molecular phenotypes at the AD variants assigned to this region. Candidate proximal phenotypes comprised cell-typesolved expression traits for genes within the TAD region; candidate distal phenotypes comprised cell-typeresolved TransMap phenotypes constructed from genes outside chromosome 𝑐. Proteomic, glycoproteomic and metabolomic phenotypes were additionally evaluated as potential downstream trans-biomarkers. A molecular phenotype was retained for colocalization analysis if at least one candidate AD variant in this TAD region showed a marginal QTL association at p-value< 10 "8 as suggested in ColocBoost 25 . We jointly analyzed all retained molecular phenotypes with an AD GWAS meta-analysis using the disease-prioritized mode of ColocBoost in each TAD region, with AD designed as the focal trait (Data resources and preprocessing below). This mode uses expedited single-effect coupling to prioritize colocalization signals involving AD while allowing multiple distinct signals within the region. Each colocalization event was summarized by a 95% CoS, where its corresponding trait configuration specified the molecular phenotypes sharing the AD-associated signal. We retained colocalization events linking AD to at least one molecular phenotype and containing at least one variant with marginal p-value < 10 "8 for AD colocalized molecular traits in configuration 25 . We then classified retained colocalization events as AD-cis, AD-trans or AD-cis-trans according to whether the configuration contained proximal cis-eGenes, distal trans phenotypes or both. AD-cis-trans configurations were interpreted as ADsupported candidate cis-trans regulatory paths. For comparison, we additionally fine-mapped the AD GWAS using SuSiE and classified an AD locus as AD-only when its 95% CS shared no variants with any AD-centered 95% CoS containing at least one molecular effect.

Empirical assessment of statistical calibration and reproducibility

To assess the calibration and robustness of TransMap, we performed two complementary empirical analyses: (i) 50:50 split-sample analyses to assess reproducibility of trans-MaxPIP annotation from genome-wide finemapping analysis, and (ii) genome-wide permutation analyses to evaluate the null calibration of fine-mapping and multi-phenotype colocalization for downstream trans-QTL discovery. These analyses evaluated two distinct properties of the framework: control of spurious discoveries under the null and generalizability of the inferred programs and genetic associations across donor subsets.

(i) Split-sample assessment of TransMap trans-MaxPIP annotation reproducibility. We randomly divided donors into two non-overlapping halves (50:50 donor-level split) for each expression profile. We ran the full workflow independently in each split, including trans phenotype construction and genome-wide fine-mapping at three trans-regulatory scales. Thus, Hotspot modules, WPC co-regulatory activity phenotypes and sCCA-derived global embeddings were estimated independently in the two splits, without transferring program definitions or model parameters between two splits. We evaluated reproducibility using two complementary metrics. First, we calculate the Pearson correlation between split-specific trans-MaxPIP annotations, separately for three transregulatory scales. Second, we evaluated whether the independently derived annotations captured consistent disease-relevance genetic information. We applied the same S-LDSC models to each split-specific trans-MaxPIP annotation and compared heritability enrichment and marginal 𝜏 * estimates (Methods below).

(ii) Permutation-based assessment of null calibration. We assessed the calibration of fine-mapping using SuSiE for individual trans genes and multi-phenotype colocalization using ColocBoost when jointly analyzing thousands of trans-eGene, trans-eProgram, and trans-biomarker phenotypes. We performed genome-wide permutations across 1,361 LD blocks used in the primary analyses. For statistical fine-mapping, we randomly shuffled donor labels in the genotype matrix while molecular phenotypes was unchanged; then we applied SuSiE to each permuted block and defined the genome-wide empirical false discovery rate (FDR) as the number of fine-mapped trans-eQTL under permutation divided by the number of trans-eQTL detected in the original data. For multiphenotype colocalization, two permutation schemes separated the effect of analyzing thousands of phenotypes from that of their observed correlation structure. First, to assess null colocalization events when cross-phenotype correlations were disrupted, we independently permuted donor labels for each trans-phenotype within each block while leaving the genotype matrix unchanged. This preserved LD among variants but disrupted genotypephenotype associations and the observed correlations among trans-phenotypes. Second, to assess null colocalization events under the phenotype covariance present in the primary analysis, we applied a single donorlabel permutation to the genotype matrix within each block while leaving all trans-phenotypes unchanged. This procedure preserved both LD among variants and covariance among trans-phenotypes while disrupting genotype-phenotype associations. We reran ColocBoost under each permutation scheme and estimated a genome-wide empirical FDR as the number of colocalization events detected under permutation divided by the number of events detected in the original data. To account for differences in the number of phenotypes tested across LD blocks, we also calculated a weighted FDR. Each detected event was weighted by the fraction of phenotype in its trait configuration among all phenotypes tested in that block, with identical weights applied to permuted and observed events. We then matched this permuted trans-only colocalization events to genome-wide cis-gene events identified from the unpermuted data. A false cis-trans match required at least one shared variant between their 95% CoS. The genome-wide empirical cis-trans FDR was calculated as the number of cistrans matches obtained under permutation divided by the number of cis-trans matches obtained in the original analysis.

xQTL-informed independent and background variants selection

We constructed an xQTL-informed set of independent variants to represent common variants with prior evidence for molecular regulatory activity. These variants were used for TransMap-global program learning and for benchmarking against competing methods, including evaluation of trans-eQTL coherence and trans-eQTL calling. Candidate variants were assembled from multiple resources, including eQTLGen cis-and trans-eQTL 4 , MetaBrain cis-and trans-eQTL 10 , cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) protein QTL 90, 91 , and FunGen-xQTL cis-QTL across four cohorts and 77 molecular datasets 26 . Candidate variants were harmonized to ROSMAP whole-genome sequencing genotypes. We then removed duplicate variants, retained common variants with MAF ≥ 0.05, and performed LD pruning using the ROSMAP genotype reference with a pairwise 𝑟 7 < 0.1 threshold. This procedure yielded 43,090 approximately independent xQTL-informed variants (Supplementary Figure S1b. Resourcespecific extraction rules and pipeline implementation details are provided in Supplementary Note S3.

For WPC background construction in TransMap-local, null variants were selected from genome-wide common variants, after excluding the xQTL-informed variants described above and any variants in LD with these variants (𝑟 7 > 0.1). Functional characterization of QTL variants and target genes

To characterize the genomic context and biological interpretation of cis and trans genetic signals identified by TransMap colocalization analysis, we classified colocalization events into three regulatory categories according to their trait configurations. A 'cis-only' colocalization event contained one or more proximal cis-eGenes without a distal trans-eGene or trans-eProgram. A 'cis-trans' colocalization event contained at least one proximal cis-eGene and at least one distal trans-eGene or trans-eProgram. A 'trans-only' colocalization event contained at least one distal trans-eGene or trans-eProgram without a proximal cis-eGene. We then tested whether these categories showed distinct genomic and functional architectures by characterizing variant-level conservation 48 , gene-level loss-of-function (LoF) constraint 92 , human transcription factor (TF) 93 , and protein-protein interaction (PPI) 46 . We used the same Enrichment across variant-, gene-and gene-gene interaction-level analyses after redefining the analysis unit and background universe. Depending on the analysis, a unit represented a colocalized variant in 95% CoS, the gene nearest to a QTL variant, a molecular target gene, or a gene-gene interaction. Let 𝒰 denote the background set of units, and let 𝒜, ℬ ⊆ 𝒰 denote two sets of interest. Enrichment is defined as Enrichment(𝒜, ℬ | 𝒰) = (|𝒜 ∩ ℬ|/|𝒜|)/(|ℬ|/|𝒰|).

(i) For variant-level characterization, distance to transcription start site (TSS) was calculated from the corresponding each CoS to the nearest protein-coding gene TSS. Variant conservation was evaluated using Zoonomia primate PhastCons scores 48 , with conserved variants defined as PhastCons > 0.9.

(ii) For gene-level characterization, we distinguished two target-gene definitions. First, nearest genes were defined as protein-coding genes closest to representative CoS and were used to characterize locus-proximal gene properties. Second, molecular target genes were defined from the phenotypes included in the trait configuration, including proximal cis-eGenes, distal trans-eGenes and genes contributing to trans-ePrograms. Loss-of-function-constrained genes were defined as genes with pLI > 0.9. Human transcription factors were defined using a curated 1,631 human transcription-factor catalogue 46.

(iii) For gene-gene interaction characterization, the unit of analysis was a gene connection. For cis-trans configurations, observed gene pairs were defined between proximal cis-eGenes and distal trans-eGenes or genes assigned to trans-ePrograms in the same colocalization event. For within-trans analyses, observed gene pairs were defined among distal genes linked to the same trans-regulatory event or program. Protein-protein interaction support was evaluated using STRINGdb 45 after mapping genes to protein-coding identifiers.

For AD-centered colocalization, we compared lead-variant conservation across AD-only, AD-cis, AD-trans and AD-cis-trans events using two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum tests 94 , where the lead variant was the highest-VCP variant in each 95% CoS (or the highest-PIP variant from AD fine-mapping for AD-only loci). To test whether conservation depended on cellular context, we fitted a linear model regressing the Zoonomia mammalian phyloP score of the lead variant on a binary indicator denoting the presence of a given cell type among the initiating (cis) cell types, the recipient (trans) cell types, or a specific initiating-recipient cell-type pair, adjusting for the leadvariant VCP and the number of distinct cell types in the colocalization event.

Cross-cell-type enrichment of neuron-initiated cis-trans regulatory configurations

We evaluated whether candidate cis-trans regulatory configurations preferentially connected specific initiating and recipient cell types. Each candidate path was represented by a proximal cis-eGene, its initiating cell type, a distal trans-eGene or trans-eProgram, and the recipient cell type in which the distal signal was observed. Celltype-pair enrichment was calculated for each initiating-recipient cell-type pair by comparing the observed pairs to a randomized background from the null distribution. To generate the null distribution, we randomly permuted the cell-type labels separately among cis-signals and among trans-signals, while preserving the colocalization event, the number of cis and trans signals within each event, and the marginal cell-type composition of each signal type. This preserves the overall colocalization-level signal structure but disrupts the observed pairing between initiating and recipient cell types. Statistical significance was calculated as the fraction of shuffled datasets with enrichment at least as large as the observed enrichment.

Co-culture validation of neuron-astrocyte regulatory configurations

To obtain orthogonal support for neuron-astrocyte-specific cis-trans regulatory configurations, we compared the genes implicated by TransMap candidate paths with a published neuron-astrocyte co-culture transcriptomic dataset 51 , in which human iPSC-derived Ngn2-induced glutamatergic neurons were cultured either alone or with primary mouse cortical astrocytes. This study defined 5,330 astrocyte-responsive neuronal genes as human genes differentially expressed between co-cultured and mono-cultured neurons, and 3,587 neuron-responsive astrocytic genes as mouse genes differentially expressed between co-cultured and mono-cultured astrocytes at an FDR level of 0.01. For validation of TransMap candidate regulatory configurations, recipient-astrocytic trans-eGenes or genes assigned to recipient-astrocytic trans-ePrograms from neuron-initiated configurations were compared with neuron-responsive astrocytic genes, whereas recipient-neuronal trans-eGenes or genes assigned to recipient-neuronal trans-ePrograms from astrocyte-initiated configurations were compared with astrocyte-responsive neuronal genes. Enrichment quantified whether TransMap recipient trans-eGenes or ePrograms were more concentrated in the corresponding co-culture-responsive gene set than expected for protein coding genes. Statistical significance for enrichment was evaluated using a leave-one-chromosome-out jackknife.

Spatial proximity validation of initiated-neuronal-recipient-glial regulatory configurations

To evaluate whether cis-neuronal and trans-glial genes from candidate cis-trans configurations showed compatible spatial proximity in human AD brain, we analyzed a published ROSMAP 10x Visium spatial transcriptomic dataset from DLPFC 52 . The dataset contains 32 spatial transcriptomic sections, including 4 control samples from 2 healthy donors and 28 samples from 15 donors with AD pathology. Spots were assigned to cortical layers L1-L6 or white matter using the layer annotations provided by the original study 95 . We grouped L1-L3 as outer cortical layer, L4-L6 as deep cortical layer and retained white matter as a separate anatomical compartment. Gene-level spatial differential expression (DE) analysis was performed using the zero-inflated hurdle model implemented as zlm function 96 . For initiated-neuronal-recipient-glial stratum, we compared spatial expression of initiated-neuronal genes in deep cortical layer against outer cortical layer, and compared expression of recipient-glial genes in white matter against all cortical-layer spots. Following the original study, AD samples were stratified into lower, middle and higher pathology groups, with 10, 9 and 9 samples, respectively. DE analysis was evaluated using the full dataset and within each control or AD-pathology group.

Donor-stratified subsampling of excitatory neurons

To assess whether the greater representation of excitatory neurons influenced the inferred cross-cell-type regulatory architecture, we applied donor-stratified subsampling to generate excitatory neuron subsets containing approximately 𝑀 = 100,000, 𝑀 = 200,000and 𝑀 = 300,000 nuclei. Genome-wide fine-mapping of cell-type composition QTL

To characterize genetic effects on brain cellular composition, we generated donor-level phenotypes for cell-type proportions in ROSMAP. For each donor, cell-type proportions were calculated as the fraction of annotated nuclei assigned to each of six major cell types. Each proportion phenotype was adjusted for age, sex, postmortem interval and the first 15 genotype PCs. For each phenotype, we performed genome-wide QTL fine-mapping across 1,361 LD blocks used in the primary TransMap analyses. Given the generally modest genetic effects on cell-type composition, candidate QTL were represented by 50% CS for downstream integration. All association and fine-mapping settings matched those used in corresponding primary analyses.

Mediation analysis of candidate cis-trans regulatory configurations

To assess candidate molecular mediation at colocalized loci, we fitted a structural equation model (SEM) for each variant-mediator-outcome triplet, specifying genotype dosage at the lead variant with highest VCP from the corresponding 95% colocalization confidence set as the exposure, proximal cis-eGene expression as the mediator, and a distal trans-eGene or trans-eProgram phenotype as the outcome. Molecular phenotypes were adjusted for age, sex, pmi and the top 15 genotype PC before model fitting. We estimated the indirect association as the product of the genotype-mediator and mediator-outcome coefficients. For assays with partially overlapping donor samples, SEM was fitted with ` lavaan` using full-information maximum likelihood under the missing-at-random assumption 53 . We assessed uncertainty in the indirect association using 1,000 donor-level bootstrap resamples and percentile 95% confidence intervals. Selected triplets were further examined using Bayesian SEM and missing-data sensitivity analyses.
