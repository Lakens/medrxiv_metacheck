Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

This study developed a data-driven decision-support framework to project annual hospital admissions and in-hospital mortality for ischemic heart disease (IHD) and cerebrovascular disease in Suriname from 2023 through 2050.Using retrospective discharge data from 2013-2022 across three major public hospitalsaccounting for approximately 71.8% of national admissions-historical trends were analyzed using log-linear regression across six age groups. Population projections relied on the UN World Population Prospects 2024 Revision. Future admissions and deaths were modelled across five policy pathways: status quo, current HEARTS, strengthened HEARTS, full HEARTS targets, and full HEARTS combined with improved hospital survival, with uncertainty assessed via 10,000 Monte Carlo simulations.

Results showed that admission rates increased for most age groups between 2013 and 2022. Under the status quo pathway, combined cardiovascular admissions are projected to reach 15,592 in 2030, 30,772 in 2040, and 62,643 in 2050. Compared to the status quo in 2050, projected admissions would drop to 58,073 under current HEARTS, 41,690 under strengthened HEARTS, and 27,469 under full HEARTS-representing median reductions of 6.0%, 33.5%, and 56.1%, respectively. Additionally, projected 2050 deaths would decrease from 2,644 under the status quo to 1,155 under full HEARTS, and further to 924 with improved hospital survival.

Cardiovascular hospital demand could rise substantially if historical trends continue, impacting health-system capacity. While not causal estimates or precise forecasts, these modelled pathways illustrate how routine health data can create a transparent framework to guide cardiovascular prevention, resource allocation, and health-system planning in Suriname.

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease remains one of the major public health challenges facing countries throughout the Region of the Americas(1) . Its consequences extend beyond premature mortality and morbidity to increasing demands on health services, including emergency care, inpatient services, diagnostic capacity, medicines, specialist care and rehabilitation (2) . These pressures are particularly important in health systems where financial; workforce and infrastructure constraints limit the ability to expand services in response to rapidly increasing demand (2).

Ischemic heart disease (IHD) and cerebrovascular disease are major contributors to cardiovascular morbidity and mortality. Both are strongly associated with modifiable risk factors, particularly hypertension, diabetes, tobacco use, obesity and physical inactivity. Hypertension is especially important because it contributes to both IHD and cerebrovascular disease and can be addressed through population-level prevention and strengthened primary care (3) . For health-system decision-makers, however, documenting current disease burden is only one part of the policy problem. Historical surveillance describes what has happened, but does not directly indicate how hospital demand may evolve under different future policy trajectories. A health system therefore needs evidence not only about the magnitude and direction of current disease trends, but also about the potential consequences of alternative choices regarding prevention, primary care and hospital services (4).

Suriname provides an important setting in which to examine this issue. Previous analyses of hospital discharge data from the country's major public hospitals demonstrated sustained increases in cardiovascular and renal disease burden between 2013 and 2022 (5) . The observed increase in cardiovascular hospital utilization occurred in parallel with persistent exposure to cardiovascular risk factors and continuing demands on a health system with limited specialist and hospital capacity (6).

At the same time, Suriname has begun strengthening cardiovascular prevention through the HEARTS initiative (2) . HEARTS in the Americas promotes standardized cardiovascular risk management, evidence-based hypertension treatment, access to essential medicines and technologies, team-based care, and systematic monitoring (7) (8) (9) . The Surinamese implementation program has expanded substantially, but early implementation data have also identified challenges related to continuity of care, workforce capacity, infrastructure, medicines and financing. The implementation study documented 56 participating facilities, 460 healthcare workers and 11,187 patients, while 52.6% of patients had at least one follow-up visit and bloodpressure control among patients with follow-up measurements reached 56.2% (10). These developments create a policy-relevant question: how might future cardiovascular hospital demand differ if current trends continue compared with alternative trajectories involving progressively stronger cardiovascular prevention and improved hospital care?

Scenario modelling can provide one approach to answering this question. By combining routinely collected hospital data, population projections and program implementation parameters, epidemiological evidence can be translated into alternative future trajectories that can be compared at relevant planning horizons.

The objective of this study was therefore to develop a data-driven decision-support framework to project annual IHD and cerebrovascular disease hospital admissions and in-hospital mortality in Suriname from 2023 through 2050. We additionally examined alternative policy pathways representing progressively stronger cardiovascular prevention through HEARTS and improved hospital survival. The projections for 2030, 2040 and 2050 were selected as decision horizons to illustrate the potential implications of alternative policy trajectories for long-term health-system planning and resource allocation.

Methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a retrospective hospital-based time-series study followed by model-based projections of IHD and cerebrovascular disease hospital admissions and in-hospital mortality. The historical analysis used annual hospital discharge data from three major public hospitals in Suriname covering 1 January 2013 through 31 December 2022. These hospitals collectively accounted for approximately 71.8% of all hospital admissions in Suriname during the study period. The 71.8% figure represents overall hospital activity and should not be interpreted as diseasespecific national coverage. Acute IHD is predominantly managed at the major hospitals receiving patients with acute cardiovascular presentations. Cerebrovascular disease admissions are concentrated at the largest referral hospital, although additional patients are admitted to other private hospitals. The participating hospitals therefore provide a substantial and clinically relevant source of hospital data for modelling cardiovascular hospital demand, but the dataset does not constitute a complete national registry of all IHD or cerebrovascular disease events.

Hospital admissions and disease definitions

Hospital admissions were identified from discharge diagnoses recorded in the hospital databases. IHD was defined using ICD-10 codes I20-I25. Cerebrovascular disease was defined using ICD-10 codes I60-I69, consistent with the disease definitions used in the preceding analyses of the research program. Because the I60-I69 category includes I69, which represents sequelae of cerebrovascular disease, the operational outcome is referred to as cerebrovascular disease rather than stroke throughout this manuscript. The analysis used six age groups: 0-29, 30-39, 40-49, 50-59, 60-69, and ≥70 years.

The unit of analysis was the hospital admission, rather than the individual patient. Patients with multiple admissions during the study period could therefore contribute more than one observation. Unique patients were not identified or deduplicated. Transfers between participating hospitals could also have been recorded as separate admissions and may therefore have resulted in double counting of a single clinical episode.

Discharge status was available for all but 28 admissions. These 28 admissions with unknown discharge diagnosis were excluded from further analysis. Accordingly, the analysis represents recorded hospital admissions with an available discharge status rather than unique patients or incident disease events.

Data-driven decision-support framework

The projection model was developed as a decision-support framework rather than as a conventional predictive forecasting model. The framework linked three components [1] observed historical hospital admission trends; [2] projected population change; and [3] alternative assumptions regarding cardiovascular prevention and hospital survival. The purpose was to translate routinely collected epidemiological data into alternative future trajectories that could be compared at clinically and operationally relevant planning horizons.

The primary outputs were annual hospital admissions and in-hospital deaths. Results were summarized for 2030, 2040 and 2050 because these years represent near-, medium-and longterm planning horizons. The framework was intended to support policy deliberation rather than determine a single optimal policy. It does not incorporate intervention costs, budget constraints, cost-effectiveness, workforce feasibility or competing health priorities.

Policy pathways

Five policy-relevant pathways were modelled.

Pathway 1: Status quo

Historical admission trends and projected population growth continued without an additional HEARTS-related effect. Age-and disease-specific CFRs remained at 2022 levels.

Pathway 2: Current HEARTS implementation

This pathway represented the current level of HEARTS implementation. It assumed [1] 80% program reach; [2] 52.6% treatment/follow-up retention; and [3] 56.2% blood-pressure control among patients retained in follow-up. These assumptions resulted in modelled reductions in admissions of 7.5% for IHD and 10.8% for cerebrovascular disease. The 80% program reach was a modelling assumption, not an observed estimate of national HEARTS coverage. The 52.6% followup and 56.2% control estimates were derived from available implementation data (10). Because reporting was incomplete across participating facilities, they were not interpreted as nationally representative population estimates.

Pathway 3: Strengthened HEARTS implementation

This pathway retained 80% program reach and 56.2% blood-pressure control while increasing treatment/follow-up retention to 80%. The resulting modelled reduction in admissions was 35.6% for both IHD and cerebrovascular disease.

Pathway 4: Achievement of predefined HEARTS targets

This pathway assumed [1] 80% program reach; [2] 80% treatment/follow-up retention; and [3] achievement of the predefined HEARTS target for blood-pressure control. The resulting modelled reduction in admissions was 58.9% for both IHD and cerebrovascular disease.

Pathway 5: Full HEARTS plus improved hospital survival

This pathway retained the admission reductions of Pathway 4 and additionally applied a 20% reduction in age-and disease-specific CFRs. The 20% CFR reduction was treated as an illustrative modelling assumption, not an empirically established effect of HEARTS or improved hospital care in Suriname. The pathways therefore represent counterfactual policy trajectories, not causal estimates of intervention effectiveness.

The relationship between population hypertension control and cardiovascular mortality was parameterized using the ecological analysis by Martinez and colleagues, which examined 36 countries in the Americas (12). The study reported associations between population hypertension control and mortality from IHD and stroke. Probabilistic sensitivity analysis

Parameter uncertainty was assessed using 10,000 Monte Carlo simulations. Uncertainty was propagated through the stochastic parameters of the final model, including the estimated ageand disease-specific temporal trends and mortality parameters according to the final model specification. The same parameter draws were applied across all five policy pathways within each simulation. This paired approach ensured that comparisons between pathways were based on the same underlying sampled epidemiological parameters. For each outcome and projection year, the median and 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles were calculated and reported as 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs). A fixed random seed of 42 was used to facilitate reproducibility. The population growth trajectory was fixed to the UN-projected annual rates in the final probabilistic analysis and therefore does not contribute stochastic uncertainty to the reported 95% UIs.

One-way sensitivity analysis

A deterministic one-way sensitivity analysis (OWSA) was conducted by varying individual model parameters across prespecified plausible ranges while holding all other parameters constant. The purpose was to identify parameters that exerted the greatest influence on projected admissions and deaths. The OWSA was interpreted as a deterministic parameter sensitivity analysis and was distinguished from the probabilistic uncertainty analysis.

Temporal holdout assessment

An internal temporal holdout assessment was performed by fitting the trend models using 2013-2019 data and estimating outcomes for 2020-2022. This assessment was intended to evaluate temporal correspondence between observed and model-estimated values. It was considered an internal temporal assessment, rather than external validation.

COVID-19 exclusion analysis

Because the COVID-19 pandemic may have disrupted hospital utilization patterns, a sensitivity analysis was performed in which the historical trend models were estimated using 2013-2019 data only. The resulting projections were compared with those from the primary model to assess the influence of the 2020-2022 period on long-term projections.

Statistical reporting and milestone years

The primary planning outputs were annual admissions and in-hospital deaths in 2030, 2040 and 2050. Scenario differences were calculated within each Monte Carlo simulation before summary statistics were generated. Consequently, uncertainty intervals for scenario reductions represent paired simulation-based comparisons rather than arithmetic subtraction of independently summarized medians. All analyses were conducted using the final reproducible model implementation.

Ethics

Ethical approval for the hospital-data analyses was obtained from the Commissie Mensgebonden Onderzoek (CMWO), Suriname, letter CMWO-14, dated 25 March 2026. The study used anonymized routinely collected health data, and informed consent was waived in accordance with the applicable national framework.

Results

Historical cardiovascular hospital trends

Admission rates for both IHD and cerebrovascular disease increased across most age groups during 2013-2022. For IHD, APCs ranged from 2.56% (95% CI -2.61 to 8.02) among people aged 0-29 years to 9.25% (95% CI 5.36-13.28) among those aged 30-39 years. For cerebrovascular disease, APCs ranged from 2.38% (95% CI -1.96 to 6.91) among people aged 40-49 years to 12.73% (95% CI 3.65-22.61) among those aged 30-39 years. The confidence interval included zero for IHD among people aged 0-29 years and for cerebrovascular disease among people aged 40-49 years. The remaining APC estimates were positive with confidence intervals excluding zero. The magnitude of the observed increases among younger adults indicates that the future cardiovascular hospital burden is not attributable solely to population ageing.

Alternative policy pathways and hospital demand

The divergence between policy pathways increased over time. In 2030, projected total annual admissions were 15,592 under status quo; 14,500 under current HEARTS; 10,352 under strengthened HEARTS; and 6,836 under full HEARTS. Probabilistic uncertainty

The PSA demonstrated increasing uncertainty with increasing projection horizon. For the status quo pathway, the 2050 estimate of 62,643 admissions had a 95% UI of 46,351-102,113. The widening interval reflects uncertainty in the estimated historical age-specific trends and the compounding effect of these trends over time. The PSA used paired parameter draws across pathways, allowing scenario differences to be evaluated under the same underlying epidemiological uncertainty. The uncertainty analysis therefore provides information about the range of outcomes compatible with the specified model parameters rather than a probability distribution over all possible future cardiovascular trajectories.

One-way sensitivity analysis

The OWSA identified several age-and disease-specific trends that strongly influenced projected outcomes. For admissions, the largest relative outcome range was associated with cerebrovascular disease among people aged 30-39 years (74.96%), followed by cerebrovascular disease among those aged 60-69 years (40.09%), IHD among those aged ≥70 years (27.85%), cerebrovascular disease among those aged ≥70 years (20.65%), and IHD among those aged 50-59 years (17.80%). For deaths, the largest relative ranges were observed for cerebrovascular disease among people aged 30-39 years (149.14%) and 60-69 years (64.19%), followed by cerebrovascular disease among people aged ≥70 years (52.53%), cerebrovascular disease among those aged 50-59 years (18.26%), and IHD among those aged ≥70 years (13.95%). The findings indicate that improvements in age-specific cardiovascular surveillance, particularly for cerebrovascular disease, could materially strengthen future decision-support models.

Temporal holdout assessment

In the internal temporal holdout assessment, models fitted to 2013-2019 and used to estimate 2020-2022 demonstrated strong correspondence between observed and model-estimated values. Across the 36 age-disease-year combinations, the correlation was r=0.964, with R²=0.889, MAE of 117.5, RMSE of 179.7, and MAPE of 20.18%. The MAPE was 20.43% for IHD and 19.92% for cerebrovascular disease. These results support the usefulness of the trend framework for scenario analysis while also demonstrating meaningful prediction error. The temporal assessment therefore does not establish precise long-term predictive accuracy.

COVID-19 exclusion analysis

Excluding the COVID-19 period from trend estimation had a limited effect on the long-term trajectory. The deterministic 2050 projection increased from approximately 56,959 admissions under the primary specification to approximately 57,643 admissions when the 2020-2022 period was excluded, corresponding to a difference of approximately 1.2%. Differences at earlier planning horizons were smaller. The findings suggest that the long-term status quo trajectory was not primarily driven by the inclusion of the pandemic years.

Discussion

Principal findings

This study demonstrates how routinely collected hospital data can be transformed into a quantitative framework for cardiovascular health-system planning. Three findings are particularly important. First, if historical admission trends continue, Suriname could experience a substantial increase in annual cardiovascular hospital demand. The status quo pathway projected 62,643 annual IHD and cerebrovascular disease admissions by 2050, with a 95% UI of 46,351-102,113. Second, alternative prevention pathways produced substantially different trajectories. By 2050, projected admissions were approximately 6% lower under current HEARTS, 34% lower under strengthened HEARTS and 56% lower under the full HEARTS pathway. Third, prevention and hospital care address different components of the health-system problem. Prevention primarily changes the number of cardiovascular events reaching hospital, whereas improved hospital survival changes the probability of death among patients who are hospitalized. The combined pathway therefore generated the lowest projected mortality (14) . The principal contribution of the study is consequently not a prediction of the exact number of cardiovascular admissions in 2050. Rather, it is the demonstration of how epidemiological surveillance can be translated into policy-relevant alternative futures.

From routine health data to actionable decision support

A central contribution of this study is to demonstrate how routinely collected hospital data can be transformed into actionable evidence for public-health policy and health-system planning(4) . In settings such as Suriname, where comprehensive population-based disease registries may be limited, routinely generated hospital information can nevertheless provide an important basis for identifying emerging patterns in cardiovascular disease and anticipating future service needs. By combining observed age-and disease-specific admission trends with population projections and alternative prevention and cardiovascular-care scenarios, this study moves beyond describing historical burden to examining how different policy pathways could shape future hospital demand (15) . This approach is particularly relevant to health-system strengthening in the Americas, where policymakers must allocate limited resources across prevention, primary care, chronic disease management, and hospital services (16) . The projections should not be interpreted as precise forecasts of future admissions or as estimates of the causal effect of HEARTS. Rather, they quantify alternative trajectories conditional on explicit assumptions and therefore provide a transparent framework for policy discussion and planning. The broader implication is that routine health information systems do not need to be perfect or fully population-based to contribute to strategic planning; when their coverage and limitations are clearly defined, they can be linked to modelling approaches that generate decision-relevant evidence for national and local health authorities (17).

Prevention as a health-system strategy

The results also highlight prevention as an integral component of health-system planning rather than a policy domain separate from hospital capacity (18) . Under the status quo scenario, admissions for ischemic heart disease and cerebrovascular disease increased substantially toward 2050, whereas progressively stronger prevention and cardiovascular-care pathways were associated with markedly lower projected demand. By 2050, the modelled scenarios corresponded to approximately 6%, 34%, and 56% fewer admissions than the status quo under current, strengthened, and full HEARTS implementation, respectively. These estimates are counterfactual scenario results rather than observed program effects. Nevertheless, they demonstrate the potential scale of the relationship between population-level cardiovascular prevention and downstream health-service demand (19) . For health systems with constrained resources, preventing avoidable cardiovascular events can therefore be viewed simultaneously as a public-health objective and a capacity-management strategy. The findings support a healthsystem approach in which investments in primary prevention, hypertension detection and control, treatment continuity, and cardiovascular risk management are considered alongside investments in emergency and inpatient care. Such integration is consistent with the broader objective of strengthening health systems by reducing preventable demand while ensuring that adequate capacity remains available for patients who develop acute cardiovascular disease (20).

HEARTS implementation as a modifiable health-system pathway

The scenario analysis provides a practical illustration of how implementation gaps within a population hypertension program can be translated into potential long-term health-system consequences. The Surinamese HEARTS program has established a national implementation platform involving primary-care facilities and health workers, but available program data indicate that important gaps remain in follow-up and blood-pressure control (10). In the present model, strengthening treatment and follow-up retention and achieving the predefined implementation targets resulted in progressively lower projected cardiovascular hospital demand. These findings should not be interpreted as an evaluation of HEARTS effectiveness, because the model combines observed program indicators with assumptions about future program reach, retention, and control. In particular, the assumed 80% reach represents a policy scenario rather than observed national coverage. The value of the analysis is instead that it makes implementation ambition measurable in terms of potential downstream demand. This creates a direct link between program monitoring and health-system planning: indicators such as program reach, retention, treatment continuity, and blood-pressure control can be followed not only as measures of primary-care performance but also as leading indicators of future cardiovascular burden (21) . For PAHO Member States pursuing integrated hypertension and cardiovascular disease strategies, this type of framework may help connect implementation monitoring with longer-term resource planning (22).

Hypertension control as the prevention-hospital interface

Hypertension provides a particularly important link between primary-care performance and hospital demand because inadequate blood-pressure control contributes substantially to both ischemic heart disease and cerebrovascular disease. The scenario framework incorporated published evidence from 36 countries in the Americas showing an association between population hypertension control and mortality from ischemic heart disease and stroke (12) . However, this ecological evidence does not directly estimate the effect of HEARTS implementation on hospital admissions in Suriname and cannot establish a causal pathway from program implementation to hospital utilization. It was therefore used as an external parameter within counterfactual scenarios rather than as a direct estimate of program impact. This distinction is important, but it does not diminish the policy relevance of the analysis. Instead, it identifies a measurable pathway through which improvements in hypertension management could potentially influence downstream cardiovascular burden (23) . For Suriname, and potentially other health systems in the Americas facing similar data constraints, strengthening routine measurement of hypertension treatment, retention, control, cardiovascular events, and hospital utilization would allow future analyses to replace externally derived associations with locally estimated relationships. In this way, the model can also serve as a starting point for strengthening the information systems required to evaluate and improve cardiovascular programs.

Younger adults and the need for a life-course prevention approach

The projected trends among younger adults provide an additional public-health signal that is particularly relevant for prevention policy. The highest estimated annual percentage increase in ischemic heart disease occurred among adults aged 30-39 years, while cerebrovascular disease also showed its largest estimated increase in this age group (24, 25) . Although older adults continue to account for a large proportion of absolute cardiovascular admissions, rapidly increasing rates among younger adults suggest that cardiovascular prevention should not focus exclusively on populations already approaching older age (26) . Earlier cardiovascular disease may also have wider social consequences because affected individuals are more likely to be economically active and may experience prolonged disability or loss of productivity. These findings therefore support a life-course approach to cardiovascular risk reduction, including earlier identification and management of hypertension and other modifiable risk factors (27) . At the same time, age-specific uncertainty needs to be considered: some estimated trends were not statistically distinguishable from no change. The policy implication is consequently not that every age-specific projection should be treated as certain, but that prevention strategies should include younger and middle-aged adults rather than relying primarily on interventions delivered after cardiovascular risk has already accumulated. This is particularly relevant to health-system strengthening because prevention initiated earlier in the life course may reduce future demand while simultaneously improving population health (25, 27, 28).

Implications for health-system capacity and service planning

The projected increase in cardiovascular admissions has implications across the continuum of care and should not be translated simply into a requirement for additional hospital beds. Increasing cardiovascular demand may place pressure on emergency departments, diagnostic imaging, cardiology and neurology services, interventional procedures, inpatient nursing, rehabilitation, medicines, and post-discharge follow-up (29) . The divergence between the modelled scenarios indicates that future service requirements will depend partly on the effectiveness and scale of prevention and chronic disease management. This supports a coordinated health-system planning approach in which primary-care strengthening and hospital preparedness are developed together (29) . Importantly, the findings do not imply that prevention can substitute for investment in acute cardiovascular services. Even under the most effective modelled prevention pathway, substantial cardiovascular demand remains. Rather, prevention can reduce avoidable demand while health systems maintain sufficient capacity for patients who continue to experience acute events (30) . The results may therefore assist health authorities in considering where additional investments are likely to have the greatest long-term system value, including hypertension control, continuity of chronic care, emergency cardiovascular pathways, diagnostic capacity, specialist workforce, and rehabilitation. Because the underlying data cover three major public hospitals and approximately 71.8% of overall hospital admissions during the study period, the projected absolute numbers should not be interpreted as a complete national estimate of service requirements. Their principal value for policy is to demonstrate the potential direction and relative scale of future demand and the extent to which alternative prevention pathways could modify it.

The importance of acting before demand peaks

The widening differences between scenarios over time have an important implication for publichealth policy: decisions made during the current decade may influence the health-system pressures experienced decades later. The divergence between the status quo and higherimplementation scenarios was already evident by 2030 and became substantially larger by 2040 and 2050. This temporal pattern is relevant because strengthening primary care, improving hypertension control, developing the cardiovascular workforce, and expanding hospital capacity all require time to implement. Delaying action until demand reaches its projected maximum may therefore constrain the ability of the health system to respond efficiently. At the same time, the projections should not be interpreted as certainty about the level of demand in 2050. The temporal holdout analysis demonstrated good agreement between observed and predicted admission patterns but also meaningful prediction error, and the probabilistic analysis showed increasing uncertainty over longer projection horizons. The most appropriate policy use of these findings is therefore not to plan a fixed system around a single 2050 estimate, but to use 2030, 2040, and 2050 as planning horizons and reassess trajectories as new data become available. This supports a proactive rather than reactive approach to cardiovascular health-system strengthening.

Adaptive planning and learning health systems

The uncertainty surrounding long-term cardiovascular demand further supports an adaptive approach to health-system planning. In the present analysis, uncertainty increased substantially toward 2050, and the one-way sensitivity analysis identified specific age-and disease-specific trends that contributed disproportionately to variation in projected outcomes. For example, the trend for cerebrovascular disease among adults aged 30-39 years produced the largest relative range for projected admissions, while cerebrovascular trends in several other age groups also contributed substantially to uncertainty. These findings have practical implications for surveillance priorities: improving the quality and completeness of age-specific cardiovascular data could directly strengthen future planning. Rather than treating the projections as a one-time forecasting exercise, health authorities could use the framework as part of an iterative planning cycle in which hospital trends, hypertension-control indicators, HEARTS implementation, and health-system capacity are periodically reassessed and projections updated. Such an approach is consistent with a learning health-system model in which routine data are continuously converted into information for policy adaptation (31, 32) . The value of the framework therefore lies not in eliminating uncertainty, but in making uncertainty explicit, identifying the parameters that matter most, and creating a mechanism through which improved surveillance and program implementation can progressively improve future decision-making.

Strengths

This study has several strengths that enhance its relevance for public-health policy and healthsystem planning. First, it uses a decade of routinely collected hospital data and disaggregates cardiovascular admission trends by disease and age group, allowing heterogeneous trajectories to be identified rather than assuming a uniform cardiovascular trend. Second, the framework integrates observed hospital trends with projected population growth and explicitly defined prevention and cardiovascular-care pathways. Third, the analysis links epidemiological projections to a real-world health-system intervention, HEARTS, while clearly distinguishing observed program indicators from modelling assumptions. Fourth, uncertainty was examined using both probabilistic sensitivity analysis and one-way sensitivity analysis, allowing the overall uncertainty of the projections and the parameters most influential to outcomes to be identified. Fifth, temporal holdout assessment provided an internal assessment of model performance against more recent observed admission patterns, while exclusion of the COVID-19 period assessed the influence of pandemic-related disruption on the long-term trend estimates. Finally, the framework is transparent enough to be updated as new routine health data and program indicators become available. This combination of routine surveillance, scenario modelling, and implementationoriented interpretation provides a potentially transferable approach for other health systems in the Americas where data are incomplete but policy decisions nevertheless require forward-looking evidence.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, the analysis was based on hospital discharge data from three major public hospitals and therefore does not constitute a complete national population-based registry. Although these hospitals represented approximately 71.8% of overall hospital admissions during the study period, disease-specific coverage may differ, particularly because cardiovascular admissions also occur in private hospitals. Second, the unit of analysis was the hospital admission rather than the unique patient; individuals with multiple admissions could therefore contribute more than once, and transfers between participating hospitals may result in more than one admission being recorded for a single clinical episode. Third, disease classification was based on discharge diagnoses and may be influenced by diagnostic and coding practices. Fourth, the historical trend model assumes that past age-and disease-specific trends provide an informative basis for long-term extrapolation, although future epidemiological, demographic, treatment, and health-system conditions may differ from those observed during 2013-2022. Fifth, the population projections were derived from UN-projected annual population growth rates and applied sequentially across age groups because age-specific annual growth rates were not used. Sixth, the status quo mortality scenario assumes that 2022 age-and disease-specific case-fatality rates remain constant throughout the projection period.

Seventh, the HEARTS scenarios combine observed program indicators with assumptions about future program reach, treatment/follow-up retention, and blood-pressure control; incomplete program reporting means that these indicators should not be interpreted as nationally representative estimates. Eighth, the assumed 80% program reach and the 20% case-fatality reduction in Scenario 5 are illustrative modelling assumptions rather than empirically established national effects. Ninth, the external hypertension-control associations were derived from an ecological study across countries in the Americas and were not specific to Suriname, HEARTS implementation, or hospital admissions. Finally, the PSA captures uncertainty in the stochastic parameters included in the model but does not encompass all forms of structural uncertainty, including future changes in disease incidence, treatment patterns, hospital utilization, program implementation, coding practices, or unforeseen health-system shocks. The projections should therefore be interpreted as scenario-based decision-support estimates rather than precise predictions of national cardiovascular admissions or deaths.

Conclusion

Suriname may face a substantial increase in cardiovascular hospital demand if the admission trends observed between 2013 and 2022 continue. Under the status quo pathway, combined annual IHD and cerebrovascular disease admissions were projected to reach 62,643 by 2050, with a 95% uncertainty interval of 46,351-102,113. The modelled policy pathways demonstrated that progressively stronger cardiovascular prevention could substantially alter this trajectory. Under the full HEARTS pathway, projected 2050 admissions were approximately 56% lower than under status quo. An additional illustrative improvement in hospital survival further reduced projected deaths without changing the admission trajectory. These findings should not be interpreted as precise forecasts or causal estimates of HEARTS effectiveness. Their principal value lies in demonstrating how routinely collected health data can be transformed into a transparent, policyrelevant decision-support framework. For Suriname, the findings support an integrated approach in which sustained investment in hypertension control and cardiovascular prevention is combined with continued strengthening of acute cardiovascular and cerebrovascular care. More broadly, the study illustrates how health systems with limited resources can use routine epidemiological data not only to describe current disease burden, but also to explore alternative futures and inform decisions about prevention, capacity, workforce, medicines, surveillance and resource allocation. Suriname's future cardiovascular hospital burden is therefore not predetermined. The trajectory will depend in part on the policy choices, implementation capacity and health-system investments made today.
