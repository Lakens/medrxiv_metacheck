Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Power Analysis Check

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Introduction: Anti-nephrin autoantibodies have been implicated in podocytopathies, including minimal change disease and focal segmental glomerulosclerosis. However, their prevalence, longitudinal dynamics, and clinical significance in first-onset nephrotic syndrome (NS) including steroid-sensitive NS (SSNS) and steroid-resistant NS (SRNS) remain unclear.

Methods: We established a highly sensitive anti-nephrin autoantibody assay using a modified recombinant nephrin antigen and the automated immunoassay platform, and applied it to a prospective Japanese pediatric, multicenter, cohort with first-onset NS and an independent validation cohort. Anti-nephrin autoantibody titers were evaluated before and after treatment and analyzed in relation to clinical phenotypes, genetic risk alleles, and tissue findings. The assay was validated using immunoprecipitation-Western blotting (IP-WB) in representative samples, assessing IgG-nephrin colocalization in evaluable kidney biopsy specimens, and antigen inhibition assays in all antibody-positive samples from the validation cohort.

Results: In the pediatric discovery cohort (n = 103), all patients with SSNS (n = 83, 100%) and nine of 16 with SRNS (56.3%) were positive for anti-nephrin autoantibodies, whereas all patients with IgA nephropathy (IgAN) with NS onset were negative (n = 4, 0%). Standard-range positivity was associated with SSNS (77/77, 100%), high-titer positivity with SRNS (9/15, 60.0%), and antibody negativity was restricted to non-SSNS diseases, comprising SRNS (7/11, 63.6%) and IgAN (4/11, 36.4%) (P < 0.0001). Similarly, in the independent validation cohort (n = 42), high-titer positivity was associated with SRNS (1/1, 100%), standard-range positivity with SSNS (30/33, 90.9%), and antibody negativity identified IgAN and membranous nephropathy (5/8, 62.5%) or SRNS (3/8, 37.5%). Orthogonal validation by IP-WB, IgG-nephrin colocalization, and antigen inhibition assays was concordant.

Conclusions: Anti-nephrin autoimmunity is a hallmark of NS and identifies distinct clinical phenotypes at disease onset. The automated immunoassay for anti-nephrin autoantibodies enables rapid, highly sensitive, and automated antibody measurement and may serve as a diagnostic triage tool for early etiologic stratification and clinical characterization of NS at disease onset.

Lay Summary

Nephrotic syndrome is a kidney disease in which proteins leak into the urine because of a damaged kidney filtration barrier. Recent studies suggest that some patients produce autoantibodies against nephrin, a key protein required for normal kidney filtration.

However, accurately measuring these antibodies has been difficult.

In this study, we developed a highly sensitive automated blood test for anti-nephrin autoantibodies and used it in Japanese children with newly diagnosed nephrotic syndrome. We found that all children with steroid-sensitive nephrotic syndrome were positive for anti-nephrin autoantibodies at disease onset. In contrast, children with very high antibody levels or no detectable antibodies were more likely to have steroidresistant disease or other kidney diseases. These findings were confirmed in an independent validation cohort. This blood test may help improve diagnosis, predict treatment response, and support more personalized management of nephrotic syndrome.

Introduction

Nephrotic syndrome (NS) in children is clinically and pathophysiologically heterogeneous, encompassing steroid-sensitive (SSNS) and steroid-resistant (SRNS) forms. 1 Most children achieve remission with corticosteroid therapy; however, some do not and develop SRNS, which is associated with a high risk of progression to chronic kidney disease. 2 The underlying mechanisms remain incompletely understood, especially in first-onset cases without identifiable monogenic defects.

Nephrin, encoded by NPHS1 gene, is a critical structural protein of the podocyte slit diaphragm, essential in maintaining the glomerular filtration barrier. 3 Pathogenic NPHS1 variants cause congenital NS of the Finnish type. 3 Moreover, genome-wide association studies of SSNS have identified several susceptibility genes, including loci within the HLA region, and risk alleles in NPHS1 have been reported. [4] [5] [6] [7] [8] [9] [10] These NPHS1 risk alleles are specific to East Asian populations. 8, 9 Beyond genetic defects, accumulating evidence suggests that nephrin can also become the target of pathogenic autoantibodies. Anti-nephrin autoantibodies have been identified in a subset of patients with minimal change disease and focal segmental glomerulosclerosis. [11] [12] [13] [14] [15] [16] However, several important issues remain unresolved. The prevalence of anti-nephrin autoantibodies in pediatric patients with first-onset NS and the longitudinal changes in their titers before and after treatment have not been systematically examined, and differences between SSNS and SRNS remain insufficiently characterized. Moreover, the relationship between anti-nephrin autoantibody titers and genetic background, including NPHS1 variants and HLA risk alleles, has not been adequately investigated.

Furthermore, anti-nephrin autoantibody detection remains technically challenging, and its prevalence varies. 17, 18 This discrepancy arises because circulating autoantibodies are of extremely low titer, making their detection highly susceptible to the timing of sample collection (i.e., the patient's treatment status) and the sensitivity of the assay system. To overcome these limitations, various highly sensitive and specific detection strategies have been developed, including signal-enhanced enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), 11, 19, 20 immunoprecipitation (IP) followed by Western blotting (WB), 11, 12 and a hybrid IP-ELISA quantification approach. 12 However, many of these assay systems inevitably sacrifice throughput to achieve sufficient sensitivity, and differences in glycosylation patterns arising from the source of recombinant nephrin can lead to substantial background noise and false-positive signal. 18, 21 Moreover, the nephrin antigen contains multiple free cysteines within its extracellular domain region, 22 which are generally known to negatively impact protein stability by inducing aggregation through unwanted intermolecular disulfide bonding. [23] [24] [25] Consequently, this structural . instability poses a significant risk of compromising antigen immunoreactivity and overall assay performance. 17 Given the inconsistent prevalence in previous reports, the trade-off between sensitivity and assay throughput, and the structural variations on the antigen side, careful assay validation, appropriate controls, and cross-validation using complementary methodologies are essential before anti-nephrin autoantibodies can be reliably implemented as clinical biomarkers or used to guide therapeutic decisionmaking. 17, 18, 26 To address these gaps, we established a highly sensitive and high-throughput automated immunoassay system using a modified nephrin antigen and applied it to a wellcharacterized, multicenter, Japanese pediatric cohort with first-onset NS. We compared pre-and post-treatment titers, evaluated associations with disease phenotype (SSNS, SRNS), genetic background, and biopsy findings.

Methods

Patients Pediatric idiopathic NS cohort (discovery)

We enrolled children aged ≥1 year and <15 years who developed first-onset NS between July 2024 and December 2025 at 29 participating medical institutions across Japan. NS was defined as serum albumin level ≤2.5 g/dL and urinary protein-to-creatinine ratio ≥2.0 g/g. 27 Serum or plasma samples were collected before treatment initiation. Posttreatment samples were obtained within 35 days after treatment initiation, either after remission or during this period, if remission was not achieved.

Pediatric idiopathic NS cohort (validation)

The independent validation cohort included children aged ≥1 year and <15 years who developed first-onset NS between January 2026 and July 2026 at 24 participating medical institutions across Japan. Serum or plasma samples were collected before treatment initiation.

Control cohort

To establish assay-specific positivity thresholds, we included 30 pediatric controls who presented to a pediatric emergency clinic with acute self-limited illnesses, such as upper respiratory tract infections and gastroenteritis. To assess disease specificity, we additionally included pre-treatment biopsy-proven pediatric IgA nephropathy (IgAN) and IgA vasculitis nephritis (IgAVN) identified outside the NS cohorts, as well as 30 healthy adult controls.

Treatment and outcomes

All patients with pediatric idiopathic NS were treated according to the Japanese clinical practice guidelines for pediatric idiopathic NS, 27 consistent with international guidelines. 28 Initial therapy consisted of oral prednisolone administered at 60 mg/m²/day or 2 mg/kg/day (maximum 60 mg/day). SRNS was defined as failure to achieve complete remission after 4 weeks of treatment initiation. Clinical data were collected from the electronic medical records at each participating institution.

Generation of recombinant wild-type and mutant nephrin proteins

Recombinant human nephrin (extracellular domain, amino acids 23-1058) and free cysteine-substituted mutants were generated using the pcDNA3.4 vector. Hexa histidine (6xHis)-tagged proteins were transiently expressed in Expi293F cells and purified from culture supernatants by His-tag affinity chromatography. NanoDrop spectrophotometry and sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) were used to assess protein concentration and purity, respectively. Supplementary Methods describe the detailed protocols.

Establishment of an automated immunoassay for anti-nephrin autoantibody detection

We established a chemiluminescent enzyme immunoassay using the HISCL-5000 system (Sysmex, Kobe, Japan) to quantify anti-nephrin autoantibodies. The assay was based on a two-step immunocapture principle using magnetic particles coated with recombinant human nephrin proteins. Serum or plasma samples were first diluted 40fold with a 4-(2-hydroxyethyl)-1-piperazineethanesulfonic acid-based sample diluent and filtered through a 0.22-μm membrane. These pre-processed samples were incubated with the nephrin antigen-coated magnetic particles. Autoantibodies captured on the microparticles were detected using an alkaline phosphatase-fused anti-human IgG antibody (Sysmex, Kobe, Japan) and the chemiluminescent substrate CDP-Star (Applied Biosystems, Bedford, MA). Detailed descriptions of the reagents, optimization of the antigen, and the specific measurement procedures are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

The positivity threshold was established using serum samples from pediatric controls.

During this evaluation, few of these control samples had non-specific signals due to non-specific adsorption of IgG. To eliminate these non-specific background signals, we adopted a subtraction approach to determine the ΔSU/mL value, wherein the signal measured using bovine serum albumin-coated magnetic particles was subtracted from the signal obtained using nephrin antigen-coated magnetic particles.

Analytical performance of the assay

We comprehensively validated the analytical performance of the automated immunoassay for anti-nephrin autoantibodies, evaluating the calibration curve, quantitative range, intra-and inter-assay reproducibility, sample stability, concordance between serum and plasma measurements, and potential interfering substances. Detailed experimental protocols are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

Detection of anti-nephrin autoantibodies by IP-WB

Representative positive and negative samples were evaluated using a previously reported IP-WB method to validate the automated immunoassay for circulating antinephrin autoantibodies. 11, 12 Recombinant nephrin protein was incubated with patient serum or plasma samples and immunoprecipitated using protein G beads.

Immunoprecipitated complexes were separated by SDS-PAGE and analyzed by Western blotting using an anti-nephrin antibody. Detailed protocols are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

Immunohistological assay

Tissue staining to validate the circulating anti-nephrin autoantibody assay was performed in evaluable cases from the pediatric NS discovery cohort as previously reported. 29 Double-immunofluorescence of nephrin and IgG was performed on frozen kidney sections using a premixed Alexa 546-anti-nephrin (Cat. No. 29050) (Immuno-Biological Laboratories, Fujioka, Japan) and Alexa 488-anti-IgG (not commercially available; Immuno-Biological Laboratories, Fujioka, Japan) antibody cocktail, with colocalization visualized as yellow fluorescence. Images were acquired using a KEYENCE BZX810 fluorescence microscope (KEYENCE Corporation, Osaka, Japan) with optical sectioning and analyzed with BZX800 software.

Antigen-inhibition assay

An antigen inhibition assay was performed to assess the specificity of anti-nephrin autoantibody reactivity. We incubated 9 μL of serum or plasma sample with 1 μL of recombinant human nephrin antigen solution at concentrations of 0 or 3,000 μg/mL, yielding final antigen concentrations of 0 or 300 μg/mL. After incubation at 37°C for 1 hour, the sample-antigen mixtures were diluted 40-fold with sample diluent, filtered, and analyzed immediately using the automated immunoassay platform according to the standard measurement protocol. A reduction in the anti-nephrin autoantibody signal following preincubation with nephrin antigen was interpreted as evidence of specific antigen-antibody binding.

Genotyping

In the pediatric idiopathic NS discovery cohort, genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood lymphocytes. Single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in HLA locus (rs3129888; tag SNP for HLA-DRB1*08:02 and HLA-DRB1*08:02-DQB1*03:02 in the Japanese population 5 ) and NPHS1 (rs2285450; synonymous variant) were assessed using SNP array analysis. 30 NPHS1 rs2285450 have shown genome-wide significant risk in Japanese, Korean, and South Asian populations, and their associations were further confirmed by trans-ethnic meta-analyses encompassing Japanese, Korean, South Asian, African, European, Hispanic, and Maghrebian populations. 8 The HLA risk variant (rs3129888) was analyzed using a carrier-based model, whereas NPHS1 risk variant (rs2285450) was analyzed according to genotype (non-carriers, heterozygotes, and homozygotes) to assess a potential allele dose effect. Targeted next-generation panel sequencing using a kidney disease gene panel was performed according to previously reported methods in patients who failed to achieve remission with multiple immunosuppressive agents. 31

Statistics

Patient background characteristics are expressed as median (interquartile range) for continuous variables. Categorical variables were compared using contingency table analyses. Fisher's exact test, including its extension for larger contingency tables when appropriate, was applied when expected cell counts were small, with corresponding odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI). Spearman's rank correlation was used to assess correlations between pre-treatment anti-nephrin autoantibody titers and time to remission, and to evaluate concordance between antibody titers measured in paired serum and plasma samples. Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed-rank test was used for paired comparisons of anti-nephrin autoantibody titers before and after treatment.

Comparisons of anti-nephrin autoantibody titers were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test for two-group comparisons and the Kruskal-Wallis test for three-group comparisons. Monotonic associations with NPHS1 allele dosage were assessed using Spearman's rank correlation. To determine the optimal cut-off value of the anti-nephrin autoantibody titer for differentiating SSNS from SRNS, a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was constructed with corresponding sensitivity and specificity.

Results

Establishment and analytical validation of an automated immunoassay for antinephrin autoantibody detection Optimization of the nephrin antigen for enhanced reactivity

To overcome the limitations of conventional assays regarding sensitivity and reliability, we optimized the antigen design by focusing on three free cysteine residues (Cys101, Cys721, and Cys974) within the nephrin molecule. We generated variants in which these residues were substituted with serine (SSS), alanine (AAA), or valine (VVV).

Each variant was incorporated into an automated immunoassay, and assay reactivity was compared using serum samples from patients with NS. All substituted variants produced stronger antibody signals than the wild-type antigen (CCC), with the Val-substituted variant demonstrating the highest signal intensity (Figure 1a). Western blot analysis showed that substitution of the three free cysteine residues reduced the slower-migrating species observed in wild-type under non-reducing conditions (Supplementary Figure S1, Supplementary methods). These findings suggest that substitution of these free cysteine residues improved antigen homogeneity, thereby enhancing assay sensitivity.

Further analysis of individual substitution sites revealed that mutations at Cys721 and

Cys974 particularly contributed to the enhanced reactivity with anti-nephrin autoantibodies (Figure 1b).

Analytical performance of the automated immunoassay

A calibration curve expressed in assay-specific units (SU/mL), was generated by gravimetric dilution of high-titer positive samples, with relative errors ranging from -4.9% to 3.2% (Supplementary Figure S2a, Supplementary Table S1). The assay demonstrated a wide quantitative range of 152 to 57,419 SU/mL based on the limit of quantitation (LoQ) and dilution linearity assessed using three high-titer NS samples (Supplementary Figure S1b, Supplementary Table S2). Precision was high, with intraassay and inter-assay coefficients of variation levels were 1.1-1.7% and 3.1-5.4%, respectively, across three concentration levels (Supplementary Figure S2c, S2d, Supplementary Table S3). Antibody titers remained stable within 93.3% to 108.5% of baseline values under various storage conditions (-20°C for 7 days, and 4°C or 25°C for 1 day) and after up to five freeze-thaw cycles, with no significant fluctuations observed in stability testing (Supplementary Figure S1e, S1f). Furthermore, we assessed 20 paired serum and plasma samples from pediatric patients with NS (14 in the active phase and 6 in remission) to evaluate sample matrix compatibility. Antibody titers in serum and plasma showed an excellent correlation (Spearman's ρ = 0.998), demonstrating that both sample types are equally suitable for high-precision evaluation (Supplementary Figure S2g). The assay showed no significant interference from major blood components, including free and conjugated bilirubin, hemolytic hemoglobin, chyle, and rheumatoid factor, even at the maximum concentrations specified by the commercial kits; the maximum deviation observed was -6.4% for chyle (Supplementary Table S4).

Validation of the Automated Immunoassay by IP-WB

Samples included pediatric anti-nephrin autoantibody-positive and -negative NS cases at disease onset and remission and healthy adult controls. Anti-nephrin autoantibody positivity determined by the automated immunoassay showed strong concordance with IP-WB. Representative immunoblots are shown in Supplementary Figure S3.

Comparison with renal tissue immunostaining

Detailed findings for the eight biopsied cases are presented in Supplementary Table S5 and Supplementary Figure S4. The status of IgG-nephrin colocalization was consistent with circulating anti-nephrin autoantibody titers. However, one case was considered inconclusive, potentially due to treatment-related modification of the pathological findings.

Anti-nephrin autoantibody titers at disease onset and clinical characteristics in pediatric idiopathic NS Pre-treatment anti-nephrin autoantibody titers and clinical outcomes in the pediatric discovery cohort

We enrolled 103 children with newly diagnosed NS during the study period. The clinical characteristics of the pediatric patients are shown in Supplementary Table S6.

Ultimately, 83 patients were diagnosed with SSNS, 16 with SRNS, and 4 with glomerulonephritis (GN). All GN cases were diagnosed with IgAN.

The positivity threshold was established using serum samples from 30 pediatric acute disease controls. Following background correction, a single control sample exhibited an explicitly elevated signal, presumably derived from anti-nephrin autoantibodies. This sample was statistically identified as a clear outlier using Tukey's fences method and was subsequently excluded. Among the remaining 29 control samples, the highest recorded value of 527 ΔSU/mL was established as the threshold for anti-nephrin autoantibody positivity (Figure 2, Supplementary Figure S5). All biopsy-proven IgAN (n = 8) and IgAVN (n = 11) cases as well as adult healthy controls (n = 30) were negative for anti-nephrin autoantibodies (Figure 2). Table 1a summarizes anti-nephrin autoantibody titers measured before treatment initiation and the corresponding final diagnoses. Among the 103 patients, 92 (89.3%) tested positive for anti-nephrin autoantibodies. All patients with SSNS were antibody-positive, whereas all patients with GN were antibody-negative. In contrast, patients with SRNS demonstrated antibody-positivity (n = 9) and antibody-negativity (n = 7). Among the seven patients with SRNS who were negative for anti-nephrin autoantibodies, two patients failed to achieve remission with multiple immunosuppressive agents. These two patients underwent targeted next-generation sequencing, which revealed no pathogenic variants (Supplementary methods). In contrast, the remaining five patients with anti-nephrin autoantibody-negative SRNS ultimately achieved remission. All nine SRNS patients with positive anti-nephrin autoantibody titers achieved remission after immunosuppressive and/or steroid pulse therapy.

Among antibody-positive patients, the maximum Youden index for discriminating SSNS from SRNS was observed at a cut-off value of 7,783 ΔSU/mL, yielding a sensitivity of 100% and a specificity of 92.77% for identifying SSNS (Supplementary Methods and Supplementary Figure S6). Based on this cut-off value, patients were further classified into high-positive, standard-positive, and negative groups (Table 1b and Figure 2). All patients classified as standard-positive (n = 77) were ultimately diagnosed with SSNS. In contrast, among high-positive patients (n = 15), 9 were diagnosed with SRNS. Among antibody-negative patients (n = 11), 4 were diagnosed with IgAN, and the remaining 7 were diagnosed with SRNS. Standard-positive anti-nephrin autoantibody status was strongly associated with SSNS. In contrast, SRNS comprised patients with antibody-negativity and high-positivity, whereas GN was exclusively observed in antibody-negative patients. Thus, in children with newly diagnosed NS, anti-nephrin autoantibody status at disease onset was predictive of clinical outcome: positivity within the usual range was associated with SSNS, whereas both high-titer positivity and antibody negativity were associated with an increased risk of SRNS and other glomerular diseases (Fisher's exact test, P < 0.0001).

Pre-treatment anti-nephrin autoantibody titers showed a weak positively significant correlation with time to remission in patients with SSNS (Spearman's ρ = 0.264, 95% CI 0.045-0.460, P = 0.0160, n = 83), indicating that higher titers were associated with delayed remission (Figure 3).

Post-treatment anti-nephrin autoantibody titers in the pediatric discovery cohort

Supplementary Table S7 and Figure 4 show the anti-nephrin autoantibody status (positive/negative) in 91 patients with serum samples within 35 days after treatment initiation, and the slope graphs showing pre-and post-treatment changes in antibody titers for each patient. anti-nephrin autoantibody titers in patients with SSNS (n = 78) and SRNS (n = 11) decreased significantly after treatment (Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed-rank test, P < 0.0001 and P = 0.0020, respectively). Although a subset of patients with SSNS remained positive for anti-nephrin autoantibodies during remission, antibody-positive cases had a significantly shorter interval from the initial blood sampling to remission-phase sampling than antibody-negative cases (median 14 vs. 25 days; Mann-Whitney U test, P = 0.0069, Supplementary Figure S7).

Genetic risk alleles and anti-nephrin autoantibody response in the pediatric discovery cohort

Table 2 shows the distribution of HLA and NPHS1 risk alleles across disease categories in the pediatric discovery cohort. HLA risk (rs3129888 G) alleles were more frequently observed in patients with SSNS than in those with SRNS or GN, although this difference was not statistically significant (Fisher's exact test, P = 0.133). Similarly, the distribution of NPHS1 risk (rs2285450 A) allele genotypes did not differ significantly among SSNS, SRNS, and GN (Fisher's exact test, P = 0.186). The NPHS1 risk allele was significantly more frequent in the SSNS group compared with controls 8 (OR = 1.93, 95% CI = 1.34-2.74; Fisher's exact test; P < 0.001; Supplementary Table S8a).

Moreover, the HLA risk allele showed a marked enrichment in SSNS cases compared with controls 32 (OR = 4.57, 95% CI = 2.80-7.45; Fisher's exact test; P < 0.0001) (Supplementary Table S8b).

Among patients with SSNS, baseline anti-nephrin autoantibody titers did not differ between HLA risk allele carriers and non-carriers (Mann-Whitney U test, P = 0.38, Supplementary Figure S8a). Baseline anti-nephrin autoantibody titers did not differ among NPHS1 risk allele genotype groups (null, heterozygous, and homozygous) with SSNS (Kruskal-Wallis test, P = 0.70; n = 83, Supplementary Figure S8b). Supplementary Table S9 shows the genotype distributions in anti-nephrin autoantibodypositive and -negative groups. However, no significant association was observed between the groups.

Validation of pre-treatment anti-nephrin autoantibody findings in an independent pediatric cohort

We enrolled 42 children with newly diagnosed NS in the independent validation cohort.

Among them, 1 patient was classified as high-positive, 33 as standard-positive, and 8 as negative for anti-nephrin autoantibodies at disease onset (Table 1c, Figure 2). All 30 patients who were ultimately diagnosed with SSNS were classified as standard positive.

In contrast, among the 8 antibody-negative patients, 5 were diagnosed with alternative glomerular diseases (3 membranous nephropathy and 2 IgAN) and 3 with SRNS. The remaining 4 patients with SRNS were antibody-positive, including 1 high-positive and 3 standard-positive cases. Among the three patients with standard-positive SRNS, two developed infectious complications during treatment, which may have contributed to delayed remission induction. Notably, all patients with SSNS were classified as standard-positive, whereas all alternative glomerular diseases cases were antibodynegative, consistent with the findings in the discovery cohort.

Antigen inhibition assay in the validation cohort

We performed antigen inhibition assays in all antibody-positive samples from the validation cohort (SSNS, n = 30; SRNS, n = 4) and observed no marked signal changes in antibody-negative samples (disease control, n = 2; glomerular disease, n = 3; SRNS, n = 1; Adult healthy control, n = 4). In contrast, preincubation with recombinant nephrin antigen reduced antibody signals in all antibody-positive samples, including all SSNS cases and antibody-positive SRNS cases, supporting the specificity of the assay (Supplementary Figure S9).

Discussion

In this study, we established a highly sensitive and automated immunoassay for the detection of anti-nephrin autoantibodies and prospectively evaluated anti-nephrin autoantibody titers in children with newly diagnosed NS. We found that pretreatment anti-nephrin autoantibody titers were strongly associated with clinical phenotype and treatment response. All patients with SSNS were antibody-positive, whereas non-SSNS patients, including SRNS and alternative glomerular diseases, most commonly exhibited either high-titer positivity or complete antibody negativity. These findings were reproduced in an independent validation cohort. Rather than representing a binary biomarker, anti-nephrin autoantibody titers could divide patients into distinct biological categories that differ in treatment responsiveness and disease phenotype.

Assay variability had been a major obstacle to the clinical application of anti-nephrin autoantibody testing. 17, 18 By optimizing the recombinant nephrin antigen and implementing the assay on an automated chemiluminescent platform, we achieved highly sensitive and reproducible measurements with rapid turnaround that showed strong concordance with IP-WB analyses, kidney tissue immunostaining findings, and antigen inhibition assay results.

The clinical implications of these findings may extend beyond diagnosis. Some antibody-negative patients had other glomerular diseases, suggesting that a negative anti-nephrin autoantibody result may prompt consideration of alternative diagnoses and further diagnostic evaluation. Conversely, higher antibody titers were associated with longer remission times, indicating that anti-nephrin autoantibody burden may reflect disease activity. Serial monitoring may therefore provide additional information regarding treatment response and support future individualized treatment strategies.

In contrast to previous reports, 12, 16 all children with SSNS in our cohort were positive for anti-nephrin autoantibodies. Several factors may explain this exceptionally high detection rate. First, the optimized recombinant nephrin antigen and highly sensitive automated chemiluminescent platform may have improved the detection of low-level antibody responses that could have been missed by earlier assays. Second, all samples were obtained at initial presentation before corticosteroid and/or immunosuppressive therapy, thereby minimizing the potential effects of treatment on antibody detection as well as pathophysiological changes associated with prolonged disease duration.

Although anti-nephrin autoantibody titers were not associated with the presence of NPHS1 or HLA risk alleles, the relatively high frequency of nephrin-associated susceptibility alleles in the Japanese population 5, 8 may have contributed to the remarkably high prevalence of anti-nephrin autoimmunity observed in this cohort.

Antibody-negative SRNS patients appeared to constitute a biologically heterogeneous group. Patients who subsequently achieved remission following immunosuppressive therapy may represent alternative immune-mediated forms of podocytopathy, including those associated with autoantibodies against other podocyte proteins, such as podocin and KIRREL1, 13 although these mechanisms were not evaluated in the present study, whereas those who remained treatment resistant may harbor underlying genetic or molecular abnormalities not detected by current diagnostic testing.

Some limitations should be acknowledged. First, the sample size was relatively small, particularly for patients with non-SSNS. Second, the study population consisted exclusively of Japanese patients, limiting the generalizability of the results. Further studies in ethnically diverse populations are warranted to determine the generalizability of these findings. Finally, although anti-nephrin autoantibodies were associated with clinical phenotypes, the present study was not designed to determine their direct pathogenic role.

In conclusion, our findings identify anti-nephrin autoimmunity as a central biological feature of SSNS and a subset of podocytopathies. Quantitative assessment of antinephrin autoantibody titers provides clinically relevant information that enables patient stratification and prognostic assessment. These data support a mechanism-based classification of NS and establish the automated immunoassay for anti-nephrin autoantibodies as a practical tool for future biomarker-guided management strategies.

Optimization of the nephrin antigen

To optimize the antigen for the assay, we conducted a two-step evaluation. First, to determine the optimal substituting amino acid, we compared the immunoreactivities of CCC and the three triple-mutants in which free cysteine residues were substituted (VVV, AAA, and SSS) using clinical samples from patients with pediatric nephrotic syndrome.

Subsequently, to identify the optimal substitution positions, we evaluated CCC alongside the single and double Val mutants, as well as VVV, using samples from pediatric nephrotic syndrome patients and healthy controls. Based on these evaluations, the triple Valsubstituted mutant was selected for the final assay formulation.

Western blotting analysis of recombinant nephrin variants under reducing and nonreducing conditions

Purified samples were mixed with reducing SDS-PAGE sample buffer (Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan, 09499-14) or non-reducing SDS-PAGE sample buffer (Nacalai Tesque, 09500-64), followed by heating at 96 °C for 5 min. The samples were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes using the iBlot 2 Dry Blotting System and iBlot 2 Transfer Stacks, PVDF (Thermo Fisher Scientific, IB24001).

After transfer, the membranes were blocked with Block Ace Powder (KAC, Kyoto, Japan,UKB80), prepared according to the manufacturer's instructions, for 1.5 h at room temperature. The membranes were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with a polyclonal sheep anti-nephrin antibody recognizing both human and mouse nephrin (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, AF4269), diluted 1:500 in tenfold-diluted Block Ace. After three washes with Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween 20 (TBS-T), the membranes were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated donkey anti-sheep IgG (Jackson ImmunoResearch, West Grove, PA, AB_234071), diluted 1:100,000 in tenfold-diluted Block Ace. The membranes were washed three additional times with TBS-T.

Chemiluminescent signals were developed using SuperSignal West Pico PLUS substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 34585), and images were acquired at multiple exposure settings using a FUSION SOLO S imaging system (Vilber GmbH, Collégien, France).

Preparation of antigen-coated magnetic particles (R² reagent)

Recombinant human nephrin antigens (CCC, and free cysteine-substituted variants SSS, AAA, or VVV) were immobilized onto carboxylated magnetic particles (3 μm in diameter) via the amine coupling method. The carboxyl groups on the particle surface were activated to form N-hydroxysuccinimide esters, followed by the addition of the recombinant nephrin proteins to form covalent bonds. The prepared R² reagents were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80°C until use. The reagents were thawed under running water immediately before loading. BSA-coated magnetic particles were prepared in the same manner by immobilizing BSA instead of the recombinant nephrin antigens to calculate the ΔSU/mL.

Measurement protocol

The measurement was conducted according to the following manual and HISCL-5000 system-based steps: 1.

Manual sample pre-processing: Serum or plasma samples were diluted 40-fold with a HEPES-based sample diluent (pH 6.5) and then filtered through a 0.22-μm filter.

2. Primary reaction (Step 1):

The filtered sample was first mixed with the R1 reagent. This mixture was then reacted with the R² reagent (antigen-coated magnetic particles) to capture anti-nephrin autoantibodies.

3. Washing: After magnetic separation, the particles were washed with HISCL Wash Solution (Sysmex) to remove unbound IgG and other matrix components.

4. Secondary reaction (Step 2):

The R3 reagent (ALP-fused anti-human IgG; Sysmex) was added to the particles to detect the captured autoantibodies.

5. Final washing and detection: After a second washing step, the R4 and R5 reagents were added and mixed.

6. Signal measurement: The chemiluminescence intensity was measured, and the antibody titer was calculated based on the luminescence signal.

Calibration curve

An assay-specific unit, designated as SU/mL, was adopted for quantitation since the measurement target is an autoantibody and the signal depends on its binding affinity. The calibrators were prepared by serially diluting a high-titer sample by weight using a HEPES-based sample diluent. The calibration curve consisted of five or six points (a blank and four or five concentration points) and was constructed using a four-parameter logistic regression model to convert the chemiluminescence intensity (HISCL counts) into SU/mL.

Determination of the quantitative range

To establish the quantitative range of the assay, the limit of quantitation (LoQ; the lower limit) and the upper limit of quantitation (ULoQ) were determined. A sample from a pediatric patient with nephrotic syndrome was serially diluted with the HEPES-based sample diluent to define the LoQ. Each dilution point was measured in five replicates (n = 5). The LoQ was defined as the lowest concentration at which the coefficient of variation 15% or less. To determine the ULoQ, dilution linearity was evaluated using three high-titer samples from pediatric patients with nephrotic syndrome. These samples were serially diluted with the HEPES-based sample diluent, and each dilution point was measured in two or three replicates (n = 2-3). The ULoQ was defined as the highest concentration that maintained dilution linearity.

Intra-and inter-assay reproducibility

To evaluate the precision of the assay, intra-assay and inter-assay reproducibility were assessed using three serum samples from pediatric patients with nephrotic syndrome.

These samples represented low, middle, and high anti-nephrin autoantibody concentrations. For the assessment of intra-assay reproducibility, each of the three samples was measured in 10 replicates (n = 10) within a single assay run. To evaluate inter-assay reproducibility, each sample was measured once a day over three days (n = 3).

Sample stability

To evaluate sample stability, two samples from pediatric patients with nephrotic syndrome (one with a low and one with a high antibody concentration) were aliquoted.

To assess the effects of storage conditions, the aliquots were stored at -80°C (as a control), -20°C for 7 days, 4°C for 1 day, or 25°C for 1 day. Furthermore, to evaluate the impact of repeated freezing and thawing, aliquots were subjected to 1, 3, and 5 freeze-thaw cycles.

A single freeze-thaw cycle was defined as maintaining the sample at room temperature for at least 30 minutes, followed by freezing at -80°C for at least 3 hours. All samples were measured in triplicate (n = 3).

Validation of sample types

Paired serum and plasma samples from 20 pediatric patients with nephrotic syndrome (14 in the active phase and 6 in remission) were analyzed to verify the comparability of measurement values between sample types. These measurements were conducted using the optimized protocol, which incorporated the 0.22-μm filtration step following sample dilution. All samples were measured in duplicate (n = 2).

Interference substances

"Interference Check A Plus" and "Interference Check RF Plus" (Sysmex) were used to evaluate the influence of interfering substances on the assay values. For bilirubin F (free), bilirubin C (conjugated), hemolytic hemoglobin, and chyle, each interfering substance was reconstituted with purified water and then mixed with serum samples from pediatric patients with nephrotic syndrome at a ratio of 9:1 to prepare spiked samples. As a control, the same sample was mixed with a blank solution at a ratio of 9:1. These mixtures were diluted 40-fold with the sample diluent and filtered through a 0.22-μm membrane before measurement.

The rheumatoid factor (RF) reagent or blank was reconstituted with the sample diluent, and a 39-fold diluted solution was prepared using the same diluent. Using this solution, serum samples from pediatric patients with nephrotic syndrome were diluted 40-fold and filtered through a 0.22-μm membrane.

In all evaluations, only the maximum concentration specified by the kits for each interfering substance was tested, and spiked samples (or RF-spiked samples) and control samples (blanks) were each measured in five replicates (n = 5). The maximum concentrations evaluated (calculated as concentrations in the original specimen) were as follows: • Bilirubin F: 20.1 mg/dL • Bilirubin C: 19.7 mg/dL • Hemolytic hemoglobin: 510 mg/dL

Plasmid construction

Genes encoding the WT human nephrin extracellular domain (CCC) and three triplemutants with Cys to Val, Ala, or Ser substitutions at positions 101, 721, and 974 (VVV, AAA, and SSS, respectively) were synthesized and cloned into the pcDNA3.4 vector (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). Based on the CCC sequence, single, double, and triple Val mutants (VCC, CVC, CCV, CVV, VCV, VVC, and VVV) were generated via PCR-based site-directed mutagenesis using synthesized primers. The resulting PCR products were fragmented with DpnI (Toyobo, Osaka, Japan, DPN-101), phosphorylated using T4 Polynucleotide Kinase (Takara Bio, Shiga, Japan, 2021A), and ligated using Ligation High-Ver.2 (Toyobo, LGK-201).

The generated plasmids and ligation mixtures were transformed into Competent high DH5α cells (Toyobo, DNA-913F). Single colonies were cultured in liquid media, and plasmids were extracted using a Maxiprep Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany, 12123). All nucleotide sequences were verified by Sanger sequencing using a pcDNA3.4 vector primer.

Protein expression and purification

Expi293F cells (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A14527) were maintained in Expi293 Expression Medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A1435101) at 37 °C and 5% CO2 with shaking at 125 rpm. Cells were transfected with the prepared plasmids using the ExpiFectamine 293 Transfection Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A14524). Twenty hours post-transfection, we added ExpiFectamine Transfection Enhancers 1 and 2 to the culture, and the cells were cultured for an additional 4 days.

The cell culture was centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 30 minutes to collect the supernatant, which was subsequently frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 °C. For purification, the supernatant was thawed under running water and dialyzed against a phosphate buffer containing 40 mM imidazole using a Slide-A-Lyzer Dialysis Cassette (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A52980). The dialyzed sample was loaded onto a HisTrap HP column (Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, 17524701) connected to an AKTA pure system (Cytiva). The column was washed with the 40 mM imidazole phosphate buffer, and the recombinant proteins were eluted using a phosphate buffer containing 300 mM imidazole. The eluted proteins were stored until use in subsequent experiments.
