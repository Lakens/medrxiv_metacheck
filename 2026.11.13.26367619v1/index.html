To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 6 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Effect Sizes in t-tests and F-tests works

The Effect Size module checks if effect sizes are correctly reported in t-tests and F-tests.

The Effect Size check searches for regular expressions that match typical ways in which effect sizes are reported. It subsequently checks different ways in which Cohen’s d, g, ηp2, and ωp2 can be computed against the reported value. If effects are missing, or might be incorrect, you the module provides a warning. This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Full text

Abstract

The FINGER trial demonstrated that a multidomain lifestyle intervention benefits cognition in older adults at-risk of dementia, yet the underlying biological mechanisms remain unclear. We measured neurodegeneration-related proteins using the NULISAseq CNS disease panel in 1158 FINGER participants to investigate associations between circulating proteins, cognitive trajectories, and intervention response. Independent of the intervention, ten proteins spanning immune, neuronal, and metabolic processes were associated with poorer cognitive trajectories, with TREM2 among the strongest signals. The intervention led to changes in eight proteins, including CRH, IL-7 and MME. Eleven proteins modified the cognitive response to intervention, with KDR and CRH showing the strongest effect. Complementary Elastic Net analyses confirmed CRH, and further identified PTN and CCL2 as markers of intervention responsiveness. Additional proteins showed APOE ε4-and sex-specific associations with the intervention response. Together, these findings identify proteomic signatures of cognitive response that could inform precision dementia prevention.

Introduction

The growing global burden of Alzheimer's disease and related disorders (ADRD) represents a profound health challenge, underscoring the need for effective interventions for preserving cognitive function among people at risk of dementia. ADRDs are driven by complex disease mechanisms 1 , with modifiable lifestyle and health-related factors accounting for an estimated 45% of the overall risk 2 . Despite recent advances in disease-modifying therapies for early Alzheimer's disease (AD), available treatments have modest clinical effects, are restricted to selected patient groups, and remain challenging to implement at population level [3] [4] [5] . Thus, lifestyle-based interventions have emerged as a promising strategy to mitigate cognitive decline and potentially delay the onset of ADRD, as emphasized in the updated 2026 WHO guidelines on dementia risk reduction 6 . Dietary patterns, physical activity, cognitive and social engagement, and effective management of vascular and metabolic risk factors have all been linked to better cognitive health and reduced dementia risk 2, 6, 7 . The Finnish Geriatric Intervention Study to Prevent Cognitive Impairment and Disability (FINGER) was the first clinical trial which provided proof of concept that targeting these factors simultaneously can improve cognition and exert other related health benefits in older adults at increased risk of dementia 8, 9 . This two-year landmark trial initiated the World-Wide FINGERS (WW-FINGERS) network of multidomain dementia risk reduction trials 10 throughout which the initial FINGER findings have been replicated in other geographical and cultural settings (e.g., in North America, US-POINTER 11 and South America, Latam-FINGERS 12 ). Yet an important question remains: what happens biologically when such an intervention has an impact on cognitive function, and why do some individuals benefit more than others? Blood-based biomarkers provide a window into these processes. The rapid advances in the biomarker field offer important insights into cognitive health and present significant opportunities for understanding and further developing the early intervention strategies in the context of ADRD. Plasma phosphorylated tau, amyloid-β, glial fibrillary acidic protein and neurofilament light are biomarkers currently used for tracking neuropathological and neurodegenerative processes 13, 14 . However, these established markers capture only a fraction of the molecular changes associated with cognitive aging and early neurodegenerative processes and may provide limited information on the broader biological mechanisms through which multidomain lifestyle interventions exert their effects 15 . Therefore, circulating proteins reflecting other molecular pathways that are central to brain health, such as inflammation, vascular function, metabolic homeostasis, and synaptic integrity, may help to understand how lifestyle interventions affect systemic and brain-related biology. They may also help identify individuals most likely to benefit from these interventions.

In this study, we characterized circulating protein profiles using the highly-sensitive, state-of-the-art NULISA technology 16 (NULISAseq™ CNS Disease 120 panel) in the FINGER trial. The panel included both established ADRD biomarkers and a broader set of mostly less explored multi-mechanistic proteins.

In a separate work we focused on the core markers 17 ; here, we focused on proteins reflecting broader disease biology. We first investigated the associations of these proteins with cognitive performance and cognitive change in the whole population. We further evaluated intervention-related changes in protein levels over time, identified baseline proteins associated with intervention responsiveness and examined whether these associations differed according to APOE ε4 carrier status and sex. Established ADRD biomarkers were examined separately as reference markers in correlation analyses. To capture the multidimensional nature of biological responses, we applied machine learning approaches to identify protein signatures predictive of intervention response. Finally, baseline protein profiles were compared between intervention-adherent responders and non-responders to identify molecular pathways associated with successful cognitive benefit from the lifestyle intervention.

Methods

Study Setting and Population

This research constitutes an exploratory analysis of the FINGER RCT (ClinicalTrials.gov ID NCT01041989), which evaluated the effectiveness of a multidomain lifestyle intervention on cognition among community-dwelling older adults at risk for dementia across six centers in Finland (N=1260, average age 69 years at baseline). Screening and baseline evaluations occurred between September 2009 and November 2011, and the core two-year RCT was completed in February 2014. Details on the FINGER protocol, and main trial findings are available elsewhere 8, 9 . The study received ethical approval from the Coordinating Ethics Committee of Helsinki and Uusimaa Hospital District (94/13/03/00/09, HUS/1204/2017) and Swedish Ethical Review Authority (2020-07058), with all participants providing written informed consent.

Participants were recruited from prior national surveys, with inclusion criteria set at ages 60-77, a CAIDE Dementia Risk Score 18 of at least six points, and cognitive function at or just below mean levels for their age. Key exclusion criteria included previously diagnosed or suspected dementia, significant cognitive impairment (Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) <20), and conditions that could compromise safe participation. This selection process aimed to represent the general Finnish older population with dementia risk factors but minimal cognitive impairment 19.

Participants were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to either the intervention group (intensive multidomain lifestyle program) or the control group (standard health advice). Randomization was executed using computer-generated allocations in blocks of four at each site (two participants to each group). Efforts were made to achieve double-blinding, with outcome assessors unaware of intervention specifics and group allocations remaining undisclosed. The primary outcome was change in global cognition measured by a total composite z-score from the Neuropsychological Test Battery (NTB). After two years, the intervention group exhibited greater cognitive improvement compared to controls and a reduced risk of cognitive decline. 9

Intervention

All participants received baseline lifestyle information (mini-intervention) and feedback regarding their vascular risk factors. Post-randomization, control participants were given routine health advice and risk factor information during main study visits at six months, one year, and two years, along with instructions to consult healthcare providers as needed. Those in the intervention group engaged in a comprehensive program encompassing dietary counseling, physical activity, cognitive stimulation, and monitoring of vascular and metabolic factors. The intervention details are described elsewhere 8, 9 . Interventions were delivered by a multidisciplinary team in accordance with national and international guidelines.

Intervention adherence was assessed according to a prespecified definition 20 based on participation in the activities offered to the intervention group, with high adherence defined as >50% overall participation across intervention domains.

In summary, the dietary intervention included three individual sessions and six to eight group sessions tailored to participants' needs. The exercise component included progressive strength training (one to three weekly group sessions) alongside individualized aerobic training (two to five weekly sessions).

Cognitive training encompassed ten group sessions (educational content) and individualized computerbased activities (in two six-month blocks, training three times weekly), targeting executive function, memory, and processing speed with distinct tasks from outcome assessments. Management of metabolic and vascular risks sought to enhance blood pressure, lipids, glucose, and weight, adhering to national care guidelines. Six additional meetings provided further measurements and lifestyle guidance, with no medications prescribed; participants were advised to reach out to healthcare providers if necessary.

Cognitive Assessment

Cognitive assessments using the NTB were carried out by blinded psychologists at baseline, one year, and two years. The trial's primary outcome was the change in the NTB total score, representing global cognition (mean of 14 tests, with at least eight required for calculations). Scores at each timepoint were converted to z-scores standardized to baseline mean and SD (higher scores indicating better performance).

Detailed description of the cognitive assessment has been published elsewhere 9.

Blood Sample Collection and Analysis

Fasting venous blood samples were obtained at baseline, six months, one year, and two years. Serum and plasma were separated immediately, frozen, and transferred monthly to the Finnish Institute for Health and Welfare laboratory, where samples were aliquoted and stored at -70°C for future analysis. APOE genotype was assessed using standard techniques 21 . Fluid biomarkers were analyzed in all available frozen plasma samples from baseline (n=1225) and the two-year follow-up (n=1105). Samples were shipped in February 2024 and analyzed at the Clinical Neurochemistry Laboratory at the University of Gothenburg, Gothenburg, Sweden. The assays included the clinically validated Fujirebio Lumipulse test for p-tau217, and the NULISAseq™ CNS Disease 120 Panel (Alamar Biosciences). The NULISA methodology (Nucleic acid Linked Immuno-Sandwich Assay) allows ultrasensitive multiplex detection and quantification of approximately 120 proteins at attomolar concentrations 22.

Participants samples were balanced (randomization group allocation and age) across study plates while keeping longitudinal samples of a single individual on the same plate. Sample were analyzed in the ARGO HT platform and resulting library pools sequenced on an Illumina NextSeq 2000 instrument (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA), as specified by the manufacturer. The sequencing output was processed using Alamar's proprietary software, which included rescaling and log transformation of the data, with the generation of NULISA Protein Quantification (NPQ) values 23, 24 . Therefore, NPQ units reflect relative protein abundances and differences are interpreted as log2 (fold change), whereas the Lumipulse p-tau217 assay yields absolute concentrations (pg/ml).

Proteins were manually assigned to biological categories (Inflammatory, Synaptic, Vascular, Metabolic, Neurodegeneration-related, or Amyloid & Tau-related) based on functional annotations from the Human Protein Atlas and hallmarks informed by the manufacturer (Alamar Biosciences). For multifunctional proteins, assignment was based on the predominant biological role most relevant to the central nervous system and neurodegenerative disease.

Statistical Analyses

Of the 1260 individuals randomized, 1,190 participants were included in the modified intention-to-treat (mITT) population, as previously defined by Ngandu et al 9 . For the present analyses, the study population was restricted to participants included in the mITT population with available baseline NULISA proteomic data (n=1158) to ensure consistency across analyses and facilitate comparability of the results and conclusions (Supplementary Figure 7).

All NULISA proteins plus Lumipulse ptau217 were used for the analyses. For each biomarker and timepoint, values were winsorized at the 1st and 99th percentiles to limit the influence of outliers. NPQ values are already log2-transformed and were therefore not transformed further, whereas Lumipulse p-tau217 was log-transformed. Baseline biomarker levels used as predictors were then standardized to a mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 1 (z-scores).

Baseline characteristics were compared between groups using chi-square tests for categorical variables (sex and APOE ε4 carrier status). For continuous variables, independent-samples t-tests were used for baseline and 2-year global cognition, whereas the Mann-Whitney U test was used for other continuous characteristics, according to the distribution of the data as assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test.

Linear mixed-effects models were fitted using maximum likelihood estimation and included participantspecific random intercepts and slopes for time. Unless otherwise specified, all models were adjusted for age, sex (reference: male), years of education, BMI and study site (reference: Helsinki). Analyses were performed using complete observations for the outcome, predictor and model covariates. For longitudinal cognitive analyses, participants were required to have baseline cognition and at least one cognitive assessment at either the 1-or 2-year follow-up. Two -sided P < 0.05 was considered nominally significant. To account for multiple comparisons, P-values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg FDR method and reported when FDR q < 0.05.

To assess intervention-associated changes in biomarkers over time, a linear model was used with outcome the 2-year biomarker change (biomarker level at 2 years) -(biomarker level at baseline)), adjusting for baseline biomarker levels and age, sex, BMI and study site (n = 1,090). Associations between baseline biomarker levels and longitudinal cognitive trajectories were evaluated by linear mixed-effect models, adjusting for randomization group (reference: control), group x time and the prespecified cofactors (age, sex, BMI, study site, education) (formula: ztotal_composite ~ year x biomarker+ cofactors + year x group + (1 + year | ID); n = 1,146). Cross-sectional associations between baseline biomarker levels and baseline cognition were examined with linear regression model used to evaluate the association between baseline cognition and baseline biomarkers, adjusting for the same cofactors. To investigate whether baseline biomarker levels modified the cognitive response to the intervention, we applied 3-way mixed-effect models with the interaction term baseline biomarker x time x randomization group (formula: ztotal_composite ~ year x biomarker x group + cofactors+ (1 + year | ID); n = 1,146).

To identify baseline proteins associated with cognitive responder status, a separate analysis was conducted among participants in the intervention group with high adherence (>50% overall adherence across intervention domains or >50% overall adherence in 3 of the domains and 0-50% in the last one; n = 363). Responders were defined as participants showing an improvement in the global cognitive composite score between baseline and the 2-year follow-up (change > 0), whereas participants with no improvement (change ≤ 0) were classified as non-responders. Associations between baseline protein levels and responder status were examined using multivariable logistic regression, adjusting for baseline global cognition, age, sex, education, BMI and study site. Results were expressed as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Heterogeneity according to APOE4 carrier status and sex was examined using four-way interaction models. APOE4-dependent models included a baseline biomarker x time x randomization group x APOE ε4 status interaction, whereas sex-dependent models included a baseline biomarker x time x randomization group x sex interaction (n = 1,075). Spearman correlations were calculated between the exploratory NULISA proteins and the core blood-based ADRD biomarkers (Aβ42/40, GFAP, NfL, p-tau181, p-tau217, p-tau231).

Analyses were conducted using R version 4.6.0 (stats package version 4.6.0) for linear regressions and multiple testing correction and lme4 version 2.0.1 for mixed models. Longitudinal biomarker plots were produced using python 3.11 and seaborn 0.13. All other plots were generated in R 4.6.0 using ggplot2.

Machine learning

All baseline plasma biomarkers measured using the NULISA platform, including established blood-based ADRD biomarkers were included as candidate predictors, together with the clinical covariates used in the mixed-effects models. Individuals with missing cofactors, baseline biomarkers and cognitive scores at baseline and two years were excluded from the analyses (Supplementary Figure 6). An Elastic net regression pipeline was implemented to test whether biomarkers identified through mixed-effects models remained predictive within a multivariable machine learning framework that accounts for correlations between predictors. This approach identifies sets of biomarkers that jointly predict cognitive change over two years, and, separately, biomarkers whose association with cognitive change depends on the intervention group. Elastic net combines L1 (Lasso) and L2 (Ridge) regularization 25 , allowing correlated predictors to be retained while shrinking predictors with limited contribution. This property is advantageous for proteomic data, in which correlated biomarkers may reflect overlapping biological pathways.

Analyses were performed in Python 3.11 using ElasticNet from scikit-learn 1.7. The outcome was twoyear change in the global cognitive composite score. As Elastic Net penalization is scale-dependent, all predictors were standardized (z-scored) using the mean and SD estimated from the training fold.

Model robustness was assessed using nested cross-validation, with an outer 4-fold loop for performance evaluation and an inner 3-fold loop for hyperparameter tuning. A 4-fold outer split was chosen to preserve adequate per-fold sample size given the limited size of the study cohort, particularly for the control-only and intervention-only analyses. 11 For each outer training fold, hyperparameters were optimized with a randomized search, by minimizing mean squared error (MSE) on the inner validation folds: the regularization strength (alpha) was searched on a logarithmic scale from 10 to 10², and the L1/L2 mixing ratio (l1_ratio) was searched uniformly between 0.1 and 1.0. Candidate hyperparameter combinations yielding fewer than five non-zero coefficients were excluded from consideration, to avoid overly sparse, unstable models and balance predictive performance against biological interpretability. For each outer fold, one final elastic net model was fit on the full outer training set using the selected hyperparameters, yielding four models per cohort.

Predictive performance was evaluated on each corresponding outer test fold using MSE, reported as mean ± SD across the four outer folds.

Predictor robustness was evaluated using two complementary perspectives, computed across the four fold-specific final models: (i) coefficient magnitude, reflecting each biomarker's direct contribution to predicted cognitive change within a given fold's model; (ii) shrinkage resistance, defined as the regularization strength (alpha) at which each biomarker's coefficient first reached zero along the Elastic net regularization path, with higher thresholds indicating greater robustness to penalization and lower likelihood of representing noise. Attention was paid to biomarkers nominally significant in mixed-effects models, repeatedly selected across Elastic Net folds with large coefficient magnitudes and persistent under strong regularization.

Analyses were performed separately in three cohorts: the control cohort (535 individuals), to identify biomarkers predictive of cognitive trajectories in the absence of intervention; the intervention cohort (536 individuals), to identify biomarkers associated with cognitive response under intervention; and the full cohort with biomarker x group interaction terms (1071 of individuals), to identify biomarkers whose association with cognitive change differed between intervention and control groups.

Results

Participant characteristics

The present study included all participants from the modified Intention-To-Treat (mITT) FINGER RCT population with available banked samples (N=1158 at baseline, N=1105 at 2 years follow up). The trial population has been described previously 9 . The mean age at baseline was 69.0 (SD 4.7) in the intervention group (N=580) and 68.6 (SD 4.7) in the control group (N=578). There were no significant differences between groups considering age, sex, education, APOE ε4 carrier status, plasma ptau217, body mass index or global cognition (total composite of a neuropsychological test battery, the primary outcome of the trial) at baseline (all P >0.05; Table 1). None of the plasma proteins differed significantly between the intervention and control groups at baseline (all P >0.05, Supplementary Table 1).

Associations between protein levels and age, sex, APOE ε4 genotype and cognition

This study included 115 proteins for the relationship with cognition and intervention response and utilized the canonical AD-biomarkers as reference biomarkers (Project overview, Fig. 1). Baseline protein levels showed correlations with age and sex, whereas correlations with APOE ε4 carriage status were more limited (Supplementary Fig. 1). Among the strongest associations that remained significant after FDR correction, FABP3, FOLR1 and VCAM1 were positively correlated with age (r = 0.25-0.28). NPTX2 levels were lower in women than in men (r = -0.42), whereas SAA1 levels were higher in women (r = 0.33).

Six biomarkers were nominally associated with cognitive performance at baseline (spanning neurodegeneration-related, inflammatory, vascular and synaptic pathways (P < 0.05; Fig. 2A-B, Supplementary Table 2). Specifically, higher MME levels were associated with better cognitive performance at baseline, whereas higher levels of VGF, UCHL1, VEGFA, NEFH and CHIT1, were associated with poorer baseline cognition.

A total of 35 baseline plasma proteins predicted change in global cognition over time, independently of randomization group, with half of them involved in inflammatory mechanisms (Fig. 2C-D). All significant associations were negative, indicating that higher baseline protein levels were associated with less favorable cognitive trajectories. Of these 35 proteins, 10 remained significant after FDR correction: 13 TREM2, AGRN, FOLR1, IGFBP7, MSLN, TNF, ARSA, CST3, FABP3 and IL18 (FDR-adjusted P<0.05; Figure 2C, Supplementary Table 3).

FINGER intervention effects on protein changes

To determine the effect of the multidomain FINGER intervention on circulating proteins, longitudinal changes were compared between the intervention and control groups. The intervention was associated with changes in plasma levels of eight proteins, at the nominal significance level (P < 0.05; Fig. 3A Supplementary Table 4), representing inflammatory (n = 2), vascular (n = 2), amyloid-and tau-related (n = 2), and metabolic (n = 1) categories (Fig. 3B). Compared with the control, participants in the intervention group had greater increases in CRH and FLT1 and a greater decrease in MME (Fig. 3C). IL7, PSEN1 and TREM1 levels increased in both groups, but to a lesser extent in the intervention. In contrast, CD40LG and FGF2 remained relatively stable in the intervention, whereas they were increased in the control group.

Baseline plasma proteins associated with cognitive response to the intervention

To identify candidate biomarkers predictive of intervention responsiveness, we used linear mixed-effects models examining interactions between baseline protein levels, time and randomization group. These models tested whether the association between baseline protein levels and cognitive change over two years differed between the intervention and control groups.

Eleven proteins showed nominally significant three-way interactions (P < 0.05; Fig. 4A; Supplementary Table 5). These proteins were predominantly synaptic (n = 6) and inflammatory (n = 3), with vascular (n = 1) and metabolic (n = 1) proteins also represented (Fig. 4B). Higher baseline levels of eight proteins (KDR, CRH, CCL2, PTN, BDNF, IL7, SNAP25 and NPTX1) were associated with a more favorable cognitive response to the intervention, whereas higher levels of TREM2, SFRP1 and GDNF were associated with a less favorable cognitive response (Fig. 4C). This was particularly pronounced for KDR, for which higher baseline levels were associated with less favorable cognitive trajectories in the control group but were shifted toward more favorable cognitive outcome in the intervention group (Figure 4D). In contrast, higher baseline TREM2 was associated with less cognitive improvement in the intervention relative to controls (Fig. 4D).

APOE ε4and sex-modification of biomarker associations with intervention-related cognitive change

We further investigated whether APOE ε4 status modified the association between baseline plasma protein levels and the intervention-related cognitive change over two years.

Using linear mixed-effects models, we examined four-way interactions between baseline protein levels, time, randomization group and APOE ε4 status. Six proteins showed nominally significant interactions (P < 0.05; Fig. 5A; Supplementary Table 6). These proteins were classified as neurodegeneration-related (n = 3), inflammatory (n = 1), vascular (n = 1), and synaptic (n = 1) (Fig. 5B). For four proteins (HTT, TARDBP, SNCA, NRGN), higher baseline levels were associated with larger intervention effect on cognitive trajectories among APOE ε4 carriers than non-carriers. In contrast, higher baseline CXCL1 and PDGFRB levels were associated with larger intervention effect among non-carriers than carriers.

Sex-dependent differences were also observed, with four proteins showing nominally significant four-way interactions (P < 0.05; Fig. 5C; Supplementary Table 7). Three proteins were inflammatory (IL6R, CXCL8 and CSF2), and one neurodegeneration-related (PARK7) (Fig. 5D). Higher baseline PARK7 and IL6R levels were associated with more favorable cognitive response to the intervention among women than men. In contrast, higher baseline CXCL8 levels were associated with a more favorable cognitive response to the intervention among men than women, whereas higher CSF2 levels were associated with a less favorable cognitive response to the intervention among women.

In addition to the mixed models, we applied Elastic Net regression with protein x intervention interaction terms as a final analytical step to identify candidate biomarkers predictive of intervention-related cognitive response. The machine-learning models included the established blood-based ADRD biomarkers alongside the broader NULISA biomarker panel (Supplementary Table 1), as candidate predictors, together with clinical and demographic variables. Concordance between biomarkers identified by the two complementary approaches (Elastic Net and mixed-effect models) was used to assess the robustness of our findings. Model development and evaluation were performed using four-fold nested cross-validation, with detailed results presented in Supplementary Results, Supplementary Tables 8 9 10 11 12and Supplementary Figures 2 3 4.

Discussion

The future of dementia prevention will likely require approaches that can be tailored to individuals' biological profiles and possibly integrated with disease-modifying therapies. FINGER, the first large multidomain lifestyle trial to demonstrate cognitive benefits in older adults at increased risk of dementia, provides a unique opportunity to investigate the molecular correlates of intervention response. Through longitudinal profiling of a panel of exploratory blood proteins quantified before and after the two-year intervention period, we identified changes in proteins involved in inflammation, synaptic integrity, pathological protein aggregation, vascular-and metabolic-processes with greatest effects on CRH, IL7 and MME. Several baseline proteins modified the cognitive response to the FINGER intervention, suggesting biological determinants of intervention responsiveness. Additional proteins exhibited APOE ε4-or sex-dependent effect modification, revealing heterogeneity in the biological mechanisms associated with cognitive response. Several proteins associated with intervention-related outcomes showed significant correlations with established ADRDs, although these associations were generally modest, suggesting that the biological pathways they reflect may partly overlap with, but are not fully captured by traditional ADRD biomarkers. Supporting these findings, machine learning analyses integrating multiple biomarkers and participant characteristics identified multivariable signatures predictive of intervention benefit. Finally, comparison of baseline biomarker profiles between intervention-adherent responders and non-responders provided further biological insight into pathways that may contribute to favorable cognitive outcomes following multidomain lifestyle intervention.

Given the limited availability of accessible biomarkers for predicting cognitive decline in older adults without dementia, we first examined associations between baseline plasma proteins and longitudinal cognition in the total FINGER cohort, adjusting for randomization group. We found that a set of proteins predicted less favorable longitudinal cognitive outcomes independent of intervention assignment. These markers are related to immune-and inflammatory processes 26 , neurodegeneration pathways, synaptic integrity 27, 28 , and metabolic regulation 29 . The broad range of biological processes captured by these proteins suggests that cognitive change is influenced by multiple interconnected mechanisms linked to dementia risk and age-related decline. Several of these proteins, including TREM2, IGFBP7, and IL18, have previously been associated with long-term cognitive decline and dementia risk in proteomic studies, lending further support to their relevance as early markers of cognitive aging and neurodegeneration. [30] [31] [32] [33].

Biomarkers associated with cognitive decline are not necessarily the same as those mediating intervention-related cognitive benefits. In a separate study, we investigated effects of the FINGER intervention on longitudinal changes in established ADRD blood biomarkers, including p-tau, Aβ42/40, GFAP and NfL 17 and found no effects on these markers. Within the broader CNS disease proteomic panel, the present study however identified nominal intervention-related changes in several proteins, particularly CRH (corticotropin releasing hormone) and MME (membrane metallo-endopeptidase, commonly known as neprilysin). In the brain, MME is one of the most significant amyloid-β (Aβ) degrading enzymes and known to decline with age and across several neurodegenerative disorders 34.

Levels of MME in the periphery, however, are generally elevated in association with poor metabolic health 35 consistent with its role in the regulation of multiple metabolic and vascular proteins including incretins (e.g. glucagon-like peptide-1, GLP-1 36 ). Thus, the intervention-related reduction in circulating MME may reflect favorable changes in peripheral metabolic regulation rather than diminished cerebral Aβ clearance. CRH was found to be increased by the FINGER intervention. CRH is produced in the hypothalamus where it induces release of corticosteroids 37, 38 . This stress related hormone has previously been shown to be decreased in plasma across several age-related neurodegenerative disorders 39, 40 and in postmortem brain material from AD patients 41, 42 , plausibly resulting from a general endocrine insufficiency and/or a negative feedback loop from cortisol over-activation. Furthermore, CRH is increased with physical exercise and with stress 43 , and is also linked with inflammation, energy homeostasis and metabolism 44 . The observed increase in circulating CRH may therefore reflect an adaptation in the participants' general metabolic and immune health, in alignment with the FINGER model. However, increased activation of stress-response pathways remains an alternative explanation, and further investigation is needed to establish the physiological significance of these protein changes. Thus, these results suggest that the early biological effects of multidomain lifestyle intervention may involve neuroendocrine, immune and metabolic processes not adequately captured by conventional markers of amyloid and tau pathology, at least over a two-year period in older adults at increased dementia risk.

Several baseline proteins modified the association between the intervention and subsequent cognitive trajectories, suggesting that the cognitive response to multidomain intervention may differ according to the underlying biological profile. Kinase insert domain receptor (KDR/VEGFR2), a key receptor involved in angiogenesis and vascular function 45 , was the strongest effect modifier among the proteins analysed, with higher baseline levels predicting more cognitive improvement under intervention. Interestingly, KDR peripheral levels were previously found lower in individuals with dementia compared to healthy controls, and decreased mRNA levels were observed in the brain of AD mouse models 46 . This may suggest that differences in vascular and angiogenic signalling are relevant to the capacity to respond to lifestyle modification. Several other identified effect modifying proteins were related to synaptic function and neurotrophic signalling (PTN, NPTX1, BDNF, GDNF and SNAP25) [47] [48] [49] or immune and inflammatory processes (CCL2, TREM2 and IL7) 50 . Importantly, the direction of these associations differed across proteins, suggesting that both baseline vulnerability and resilience may shape the potential for intervention benefit. Some protein profiles may reflect biological vulnerability that remains modifiable through lifestyle intervention, whereas others may indicate more established processes with less potential for modification, or conversely a more resilient state with less room for additional benefit.

This was apparent among neurotrophic proteins: the unfavourable association of higher BDNF with cognitive trajectories in controls was attenuated under intervention, whereas GDNF showed a more favourable association in controls but a less favourable pattern in intervention participants. In contrast, higher baseline TREM2 was associated with poorer cognitive trajectories in both groups, with a more pronounced negative association under intervention. Given the role of TREM2 in microglial responses and AD-related neurodegeneration 51 , higher circulating levels may identify a state of greater underlying disease or immune-related vulnerability that is less amenable to modification through lifestyle intervention. CRH also emerged as an effect modifier, with higher baseline levels associated with a more favourable cognitive trajectory under intervention. Whereas our previous FINGER analysis found no significant effect modification by established plasma ADRD biomarkers 17 , the present study highlights candidate synaptic, vascular, immune and neuroendocrine pathways that may contribute to heterogeneity in cognitive responses to multidomain lifestyle intervention.

The heterogeneous associations observed across these proteins suggest that intervention responsiveness may depend on multiple interacting biological processes rather than individual biomarkers in isolation. To explore this possibility, we complemented the mixed-effects analyses with machine-learning approaches integrating the broader proteomic panel, established ADRD biomarkers and participant characteristics. P-tau217 was one of the most consistently selected predictors of cognitive change, supporting its established prognostic relevance 52 . In contrast, CRH, CCL2 and PTN showed more intervention-dependent patterns, contributing prominently to predictions in the intervention group but less consistently in controls. Their interaction terms were also repeatedly retained in full-cohort models, providing complementary support for the effect modification observed in the mixed-effects analyses. Together, these findings suggest that CRH, CCL2 and PTN warrant further investigation as candidate biomarkers of differential intervention responsiveness, whereas p-tau217 may primarily capture broader cognitive vulnerability.

Non-modifiable individual characteristics may influence the associations between biomarkers and responsiveness to lifestyle interventions. We therefore, investigated whether baseline plasma proteins modified the FINGER intervention on cognitive trajectories according to APOE ε4 carrier status and sex.

These analyses identified additional effect modifiers particularly in APOE ε4 carriers. Higher baseline levels of proteins related to proteinopathies (HTT, TARDBP and SNCA) were associated with less favorable cognitive change in the control group, an effect that was neutralized by the intervention. These biomarkers were highly inter-correlated which could potentially be due to an overlapping biology. The APOE ε4 variant has been recognized as a mediator of "systemic biological vulnerability" across neurodegenerative disorders by altering inflammatory profiles 53 and impairing neuronal protein degradation systems 54, 55 . In this context, our findings indicate that multidomain intervention may partly counteract cognitive vulnerability associated with these processes in APOE ε4 carriers, offering insights for future tailoring of interventions according to genetic risk. A recent meta-analysis has also shown that APOE ε4 carriers benefit more from lifestyle-based dementia risk reduction strategies 56 which may at least partially be due to the herein identified effects. Given their higher risk of dementia and potential side effects from anti-amyloid treatment, APOE ε4 carriers represent a prioritized group which could be particularly relevant for future precision prevention strategies.

The impact of sex on effect modification revealed four biomarkers with differential associations with cognitive response between men and women. In women, the intervention attenuated a negative association of PARK7 (DJ-1) and a positive association of CSF2 with cognitive change. In men, CXCL8 was associated with poorer cognitive trajectories in the control group, whereas IL6R was positively associated with cognitive change.

Three out of four proteins were inflammatory; however no sex differences were observed for the many other inflammatory proteins assessed, suggesting that there is not a general sex-dependent inflammatory-related effect modification. In future analyses, a broader range of biomarkers may provide additional insight into potential sex-dependent differences.

To further characterize the biological profiles associated with favourable cognitive outcomes, we compared baseline plasma proteins between cognitive responders and non-responders among highly adherent FINGER intervention participants. Higher baseline levels of KDR, BDNF and PTN were associated with greater odds of cognitive improvement, potentially implicating vascular and neurotrophic pathways in cognitive outcomes following multidomain lifestyle intervention. Immune-related proteins showed contrasting associations, with higher TREM2 and IL12p70 associated with lower odds of improvement, whereas CCL2, CXCL1 and IL17A showed positive associations. This suggests that distinct immune pathways, rather than a uniform inflammatory profile, may be relevant to cognitive response. Higher baseline REST was also associated with lower odds of improvement. REST has been implicated in neuronal stress resistance and neuroprotection 57 and shown to be increased following mindfulness-based stress reduction 58 . In our cohort, however, higher REST correlated with older age and several established ADRD biomarkers, despite showing no significant associations with cognitive trajectories. These findings raise the possibility that circulating REST reflects age-related biological vulnerability or compensatory stress responses, although its physiological significance remains uncertain.

This study has several strengths. It represents a unique investigation of circulating proteomic markers within a randomized controlled multidomain lifestyle intervention. The relatively large and deeply phenotyped FINGER cohort, together with longitudinal cognitive assessments, allowed us to examine both changes in biomarkers following the intervention and their associations with cognitive trajectories.

The use of highly sensitive NULISA technology enabled the measurement of a broad range of proteins, including low-abundance circulating proteins that may not be captured by conventional proteomic approaches. Finally, the combination of mixed-effects models and Elastic Net provided two complementary approaches to identify intervention-related biomarker signals, while nested crossvalidation was used to improve the robustness of the machine-learning analyses.

Some limitations should also be considered. First, although the FINGER trial included a relatively large cohort, statistical power to detect protein-intervention interactions was limited, particularly for the more complex four-way interactions involving APOE ε4 status or sex. These exploratory analyses were not based on prespecified protein-specific hypotheses, and their interpretation is challenging given the number of comparisons and potential instability of higher-order interaction estimates. Several of the findings, particularly from the intervention and interaction analyses, did not survive correction for multiple testing and should therefore be considered exploratory and require replication. Although Elastic Net provided a complementary way to assess the consistency of the biomarker signals identified in the mixed-effects analyses, evidence of overfitting and the absence of an external validation cohort, limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, the FINGER eligibility criteria selected older adults at increased dementia risk with cognitive performance within a relatively restricted range, potentially limiting the generalizability of the findings and influencing cross-sectional associations between proteins and cognition. Furthermore, the responder analyses were restricted to highly adherent intervention participants. As adherence may be associated with participant characteristics influencing cognitive outcomes, these results should be interpreted cautiously. Finally, the relatively homogeneous Finnish study population may limit generalizability to more ethnically and socioeconomically diverse populations.

Future studies should prioritize adequately powered, hypothesis-driven analyses and independent validation of candidate biomarkers and predictive models across diverse populations.
