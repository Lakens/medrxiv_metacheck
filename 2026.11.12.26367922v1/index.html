Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Inequalities are usually reported one social dimension at a time. This hides how social characteristics combine and leaves open whether programmes should target social groups or strengthen services for all women. We measured intersectional inequalities in demand satisfied by modern methods among women in sub-Saharan Africa over three decades, assessed how well social position discriminates between women, and identified groups left behind.

We did a preregistered cross-sectional analysis of 139 Demographic and Health Surveys from 38 countries with fieldwork from 1994 to 2024. We included 675,584 women aged 15-49 years with demand for family planning and grouped them into 144 intersectional strata by age, union status, education, household wealth and residence. We fitted multilevel logistic models with random effects for strata and surveys (multilevel analysis of individual heterogeneity and discriminatory accuracy, MAIHDA) and estimated the variance partition coefficient (VPC), the area under the curve (AUC) and the proportional change in variance (PCV). Weighted demand satisfied rose from 27.5% in 1994-99 to 54.1% in 2018-24. Predicted demand satisfied ranged from 13.2% (95% CI: 11.0 -15.9) among married adolescents with no schooling in the poorest rural households to 68.0% (95% CI: 63.6 -72.0) among unmarried urban women aged 25-34 years with secondary schooling in the richest households.

The strata explained only 6.9% (95% CI: 5.6 -8.7) of individual variation, with small discriminatory accuracy (AUC = 0.65; 95% CI: 0.63 -0.66), and a Bayesian model gave a similar VPC (7.0%; 95% credible interval: 5.7 -8.9). Additive effects explained 95.1% of the betweenstratum variance. The VPC fell from 10.1% (95% CI: 8.0 -12.8) in 1994-99 to 4.5% (95% CI: 3.6 -5.8) in 2018-24. Five strata of married rural women with no schooling were in the lowest decile in every period. In the latest surveys, married rural women aged 15-24 years with no schooling had lower demand satisfied than other women in 29 of 30 countries (median gap 17.5 percentage points). The 15 most disadvantaged strata held a median 16.4% of unsatisfied demand, so targeting these groups alone would reach about one in six women whose demand is not met.

The main limitations are the cross-sectional design, self-reported contraceptive use and the exclusion of 15 early surveys.

Conclusions:

Intersectional gaps in demand satisfied by modern methods have narrowed in sub-Saharan Africa, and most of the remaining gap between groups is additive. Social position alone discriminates poorly between women, so programmes targeted only by social group would miss most women with unmet need. This supports strengthening universal services. Within universal services, married adolescents and young women with no schooling in poor rural households have been left behind for three decades in almost every country and need priority, country-specific programmes if the 2030 targets are to be met.

Why was this study done?

• Sustainable Development Goal 3.7 commits countries to universal access to family planning by 2030, and the share of women whose demand is satisfied by modern methods is the main indicator of progress.

• Gaps in this indicator are usually reported by one characteristic at a time, such as age or wealth, although women live at the intersection of several characteristics.

• Programme managers need to know whether to target particular social groups or to strengthen services for all women, and which groups, if any, have been left behind.

What did the researchers do and find?

• We analysed 675,584 women with demand for family planning in 139 national surveys from 38 sub-Saharan African countries between 1994 and 2024, grouped into 144 combinations of age, marital status, schooling, wealth and residence.

Introduction

The proportion of women whose demand for family planning is satisfied by modern methods is indicator 3.7.1 of the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs). It is a core measure of progress towards universal access to sexual and reproductive health care [1] [2] [3]. A threshold of 75% has been proposed as the level that countries should reach by 2030 [2, 4]. Sub-Saharan Africa has made the largest gains of any region since the 1990s. Yet it still has the lowest levels of demand satisfied, and progress has been uneven between and within countries [5] [6] [7] [8] [9]. The fertility transition in the region has also been slower than elsewhere [10, 11].

Studies of who is left behind usually describe gaps along one dimension at a time, such as age, wealth, residence or marital status [12, 13]. In multi-country analyses, adolescents, women with no schooling, poorer women and rural women have lower demand satisfied [12, [14] [15] [16]. These characteristics do not occur in isolation. A woman is not only young or poor or rural. She may be all of them at once.

Intersectionality theory holds that social positions combine to shape access to resources and services. The experience of people at particular intersections may therefore not be predicted by adding up single-axis disadvantages [17, 18].

Single-dimension analyses cannot show whether gaps combine additively or multiply. Conventional regression with many interaction terms also becomes unstable when strata are numerous and some are small [19, 20]. Multilevel analysis of individual heterogeneity and discriminatory accuracy (MAIHDA) addresses these problems [19, 21, 22]. Individuals are nested in intersectional strata, and the stratum random effects give shrunken and stable estimates for every stratum. The approach also measures how well the strata discriminate between individuals with and without the outcome, through the variance partition coefficient (VPC) and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) [23, 24]. It separates additive effects from interaction effects [25]. MAIHDA has been used for chronic disease, mental health and, recently, child anaemia in Latin America [24, 26]. We found no published study that has applied it to demand satisfied by modern methods or to other contraceptive outcomes in sub-Saharan Africa.

Knowing which combinations of characteristics leave women behind matters for programmes. If disadvantage is mostly additive, broad strategies that address each characteristic may be enough. If particular combinations fare worse than expected, programmes need to be designed for them. If social position discriminates poorly between women, targeting by social characteristics alone will miss many women in need. These questions have not been answered for family planning in sub-Saharan Africa.

We aimed to quantify intersectional inequalities in demand satisfied by modern methods among women in sub-Saharan Africa from 1994 to 2024. We estimated how demand satisfied varied across 144 strata defined by age, union status, education, household wealth and residence. We assessed how much of the individual variation lay between strata and how much of it was additive. We also examined how inequalities changed over time, how they differed between subregions and countries, and which groups were persistently left behind.

We preregistered five hypotheses. First, demand satisfied would be lowest among adolescent, unmarried, poorer, rural women with no schooling (H1). Second, the strata would have low to moderate discriminatory accuracy, with a VPC below 20% and an AUC below 0.70 (H2). Third, additive effects would explain more than 80% of the between-stratum variance, with some interaction effects (H3). Fourth, the between-stratum variance would be smaller in 2018-24 than in the 1990s, with the least improvement in strata combining adolescence, no schooling and rural residence (H4). Fifth, inequalities would be wider in west and central Africa than in east and southern Africa (H5).

Methods

Study design, data sources and ethics statement

This was a preregistered cross-sectional secondary analysis of Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS). The analysis plan was registered on OSF Registries on 19th October 2026, before any data were downloaded (doi:10.17605/OSF.IO/8M9GB; S2 Appendix). It is reported following the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guideline (S1 Checklist) [27]. DHS are nationally representative household surveys with two-stage stratified cluster sampling.

Trained interviewers administer standard questionnaires face to face [28]. We used the women's individual recode files of all standard DHS done in sub-Saharan Africa with fieldwork from 1992 to 2024 for the 41 countries approved by the DHS Program for this project. Interim, malaria indicator, AIDS indicator and subnational surveys were excluded. For 30 older surveys in the main analysis whose women's file did not include wealth quintiles, we merged the DHS wealth index file through the household identifier. This analysis used publicly available, anonymised DHS data, so no ethical approval was needed. Access to the data was granted by the DHS Program (project 246219, 19th October 2026). Informed consent was obtained from all respondents during the original surveys, and the authors had no contact with participants.

Of 154 eligible surveys, 13 did not measure demand for family planning among unmarried women. These were the 12 DHS-II surveys and the 1994 survey in Côte d'Ivoire, which asked fertility preferences only of women in union [29]. As the protocol specifies for ever-married samples, these were used only in a sensitivity analysis of women in union. Mozambique 2003 and Tanzania 1999 were excluded because our national estimates differed from the published values by more than 1 percentage point and the difference could not be resolved. Chad 2004 and Niger 2006 also differed from the published values, but our estimates agreed with each survey's own unmet-need variable, so we judged the published values to be in error and retained these two surveys. The main analysis therefore included 139 surveys from 38 countries with fieldwork from 1994 to 2024 (S1 Fig and S1 Appendix, Table S1). These surveys interviewed 1,697,188 women aged 15-49 years, of whom 675,897 had demand for family planning. We excluded 313 women (0.05%) with a missing stratification variable, which left 675,584 women.

Outcome

Women with demand for family planning were those currently using contraception and those with unmet need for spacing or limiting, according to the revised DHS definition [29]. Under this definition, women not in union are classified as having unmet need only if they had sexual intercourse in the four weeks before the survey, whereas women in union are classified regardless of recent sexual activity. The outcome was coded 1 if a woman with demand was using a modern method and 0 otherwise [27]. We used demand satisfied rather than contraceptive prevalence or unmet need because it is the SDG indicator and it compares groups with different fertility intentions on the same scale [2]. The revised unmet-need variable is included in the recode files of most surveys from 2010 onwards. For the 66 earlier surveys that did not include it, we computed it with a Python translation of the DHS Program's published Stata code, including its survey-specific rules. In 24 surveys that included the official variable, our classification agreed with it for 99.8% of women. Demand satisfied differed by 0.2 percentage points on average (S1 Appendix, Table S2). We also reproduced the national estimate of demand satisfied for each survey and compared it with the published STATcompiler value.

Intersectional strata

Strata were defined by five preregistered dimensions. These were age (15-19, 20-24, 25-34 and 35-49 years), union status (married or living with a partner, or not in union), education (none, primary, or secondary or higher), household wealth (poorest 40%, middle 20% or richest 40% of the survey's wealth index [30]) and residence (urban or rural). Their combination gives 144 strata, all of which were represented. The smallest stratum had 140 women. These dimensions are those used to monitor equity in family planning and were fixed in the protocol [12]. Religion and ethnicity were not included because their categories are not comparable across countries. Parity was examined as a sixth dimension in a sensitivity analysis.

Statistical analysis

Weighted descriptive estimates used the DHS sampling weights, rescaled so that each survey contributed equally. We followed the MAIHDA approach for the main models [19, 21, 22]. Model 1 (null) was a logistic model with random intercepts for intersectional strata and, cross-classified, for surveys. Model 2 (additive) added the main effects of the five dimensions. Model 3 added period (1994-99, 2000-09, 2010-17 and 2018-24). From Model 1 we calculated the VPC as the stratum variance divided by the sum of the stratum variance, the survey variance and π²/3. We calculated the AUC from predictions that included the fixed intercept and the stratum effects but not the survey effects, so that it measures the discriminatory accuracy of intersectional position alone. We also report the median odds ratio (MOR), which expresses the stratum variance on the odds ratio scale. It is the median odds ratio between a woman in a stratum with higher demand satisfied and a woman in a stratum with lower demand satisfied when two strata are chosen at random [23]. An AUC below 0.60 was interpreted as absent or poor discrimination, 0.60 to 0.70 as small, 0.70 to 0.80 as moderate and above 0.80 as large.

The proportional change in variance (PCV) was the reduction in stratum variance from Model 1 to Model 2. The stratum random effects in Model 2 were interpreted as interaction effects. They show how far a stratum departs from the prediction based on additive main effects. An interaction effect was considered present when its 95% interval excluded zero. Predicted probabilities for each stratum came from Model 1, with 95% intervals from 2000 simulations of the fixed intercept and the conditional distribution of the stratum effect. Models 1 and 2 were refitted within each period, each sub-region and each country.

Sub-regions were west and central Africa (WCA) and east and southern Africa (ESA), following the UNICEF grouping. Model 6 added random effects for stratumby-period combinations to estimate stratum trajectories. A stratum caught up or fell further behind if its change in predicted demand satisfied from 1994-99 to 2018-24 differed from the average stratum change and the 95% simulation interval of the difference excluded zero. Model 7 added random effects for stratum-by-country combinations. A stratum was persistently left behind if it was in the lowest 10% of predicted demand satisfied in every period.

We checked whether the trends were driven by changes in which countries were surveyed. We refitted the period models in the eight countries with a survey in every period and in the 11 countries with surveys in both 1994-99 and 2018-24. To make the results usable by national programmes, we did an analysis that was not preregistered and compared weighted demand satisfied in each country's latest survey between married rural women aged 15-24 years with no schooling and all other women with demand. This group combines the characteristics of the persistently left-behind strata. Confidence intervals used the effective sample size, and countries with fewer than 30 women in the group were not shown.

The outcome and all stratum-defining variables are categorical. The models were therefore fitted to binomial counts for each stratum within each survey, which gives the same likelihood as an individual-level model. Because the counts were aggregated to strata within surveys, survey clusters could not be included as an additional level. Models were estimated by maximum likelihood with the Laplace approximation in R version 4.3 using lme4 [31]. Equivalent Stata code using melogit is available from the corresponding author. Confidence intervals for the stratum variance and VPC were obtained by profile likelihood. Confidence intervals for the pooled VPC and AUC were also estimated by a cluster bootstrap in which surveys were resampled with replacement (200 replicates). At country level, we examined whether between-stratum inequality was lower in countries with higher overall demand satisfied, using Spearman correlation. Main multilevel models were unweighted, as preregistered, because weighting can bias variance components [32].

Sensitivity and exploratory analyses

Preregistered sensitivity analyses used wealth quintiles (240 strata) and parity as a sixth dimension (432 possible strata, 431 observed). We restricted the analysis to women in union, including eight of the 13 surveys that did not measure unmet need among unmarried women. The other five have no wealth index. We also fitted models weighted with scaled survey weights, excluded surveys with fieldwork in 2020-21, excluded strata with fewer than 30 women and fitted Bayesian models in brms with weakly informative priors [33]. These were a normal(0, 2.5) prior for the intercept and half-Student t(3, 0, 1) priors for the stratum and survey standard deviations, with four chains of 2,500 iterations including 1,000 warm-up iterations. R-hat was 1.02 for the stratum and survey standard deviations. Mixing of the intercept was slower (R-hat 1.09), which does not affect the VPC (S1 Appendix). We additionally excluded Chad 2004 and Niger 2006, whose published values differed from ours (see Results). Multiple imputation was not needed because no survey had more than 5% of women excluded. Surveys are nested in countries, and repeated surveys from one country are not independent. We therefore fitted a three-level sensitivity model, which was not preregistered, in which strata were crossed with surveys nested in countries. This model separates stable differences between countries from differences between surveys within a country. We compared its VPC, PCV and interaction effects with those of the main model and tested the country variance with a likelihood ratio test.

In an exploratory analysis that was not preregistered, we examined whether factors that may lie on the pathway between social position and contraceptive use explained between-stratum differences. These were parity, weekly exposure to mass media, hearing family planning messages, a visit from a family planning worker, being told about family planning at a health facility and current employment.

This analysis used the 105 surveys in which these variables were available for more than 90% of women. Deviations from the protocol are listed in S1 Appendix (section S3). To show what the stratum gaps mean for national coverage, we did a second exploratory analysis in the latest survey of each country. We defined the left-behind strata as the 15 strata in the lowest 10% of predicted demand satisfied in 2018-24.

For each country we estimated their weighted share of all women with demand and their share of all women whose demand was not satisfied by modern methods. The ratio of these shares shows whether unsatisfied demand is concentrated in these strata. We also estimated how much national demand satisfied would rise if women in these strata had the same level as other women in that country. We repeated this for married rural women aged 15-24 years with no schooling.

Results

The 139 surveys included 675,584 women with demand for family planning. There were 21 surveys (57,859 women) from 1994-99, 42 (166,848) from 2000-09, 46 (254,335) from 2010-17 and 30 (196,542) from 2018-24 (Table 1). Of these women, 80.2% were in union, 32.5% had no schooling and 60.3% lived in rural areas (weighted). National estimates were within 1 percentage point of the published value in 133 of the 137 main surveys with a published value. Rwanda 2010 and Uganda 2011 matched the published value for women in union exactly. For Chad 2004 and Niger 2006, our estimates were close to those from each survey's own unmet-need variable based on the original definition (6.3% and 19.2%). They were also consistent with the low modern contraceptive prevalence among married women in these surveys (1.6% and 5.0%). We therefore retained them, and excluding them did not change the results (S1 Appendix, Tables S1 and S6).

Weighted demand satisfied by modern methods increased from 27.5% in 1994-99 to 46.7% in 2010-17 and 54.1% in 2018-24 (Table 1). It was lowest among adolescents aged 15-19 years (35.0%), women with no schooling (26.0%), women in the poorest 40% (32.7%) and rural women (36.9%). It was higher among women not in union (54.3%) than among women in union (39.1%). The gap between women with no schooling and those with secondary schooling narrowed from 37.3 percentage points in 1994-99 to 13.9 points in 2018-24. Predicted demand satisfied varied more than five-fold across the 144 strata (Figure 1). It ranged from 13.2% (95% CI: 11.0 -15.9) among married adolescents aged 15-19 years with no schooling in the poorest rural households to 68.0% (95% CI: 63.6 -72.0) among unmarried women aged 25-34 years with secondary or higher schooling in the richest urban households. Of the 20 lowest strata, 18 were in union and 16 had no schooling. All 20 highest strata were women not in union, and 14 of them were urban. In the null model, 6.9% (95% CI: 5.6 -8.7) of the individual variation in the latent propensity to have demand satisfied lay between strata. The strata had small discriminatory accuracy (AUC = 0.65) (Table 2). The cluster bootstrap gave a 95% CI of 6.1 -8.0 for the VPC and 0.633 -0.662 for the AUC. The MOR was 1.70 for strata and 2.40 for surveys. Survey differences accounted for more variation than strata. A model with survey effects alone gave an AUC of 0.72, which rose to 0.76 when stratum effects were added. In the additive model, demand satisfied was higher among women aged 25-34 years (aOR = 1.94; 95% CI: 1.82 -2.07) than among adolescents. It was also higher among women with primary schooling (aOR = 1.48; 95% CI: 1.40 -1.57) or secondary or higher schooling (aOR = 1.96; 95% CI: 1.85 -2.07), women in the richest 40% (aOR = 1.58; 95% CI: 1.50 -1.67) and urban women (aOR = 1.29; 95% CI: 1.23 -1.35).

It was lower among women in union than among unmarried women (aOR = 0.56; 95% CI: 0.53 -0.58) (S1 Appendix, Table S8).

The additive effects explained 95.1% of the between-stratum variance.

Nevertheless, 59 strata had interaction effects whose 95% intervals excluded zero.

Of these, 31 had higher and 28 had lower demand satisfied than predicted by the additive effects (S1 Appendix, Table S4 and S2 Fig). The largest negative interaction was among unmarried rural adolescents with primary schooling in the richest households (-10.8 percentage points). Unmarried rural adolescents with primary schooling had negative interactions in every wealth group. Because stratum effects are shrunk towards zero, these interaction estimates are conservative.

Inequality was somewhat higher in west and central Africa (VPC = 9.0%; 95% CI: 7.3 -11.2) than in east and southern Africa (VPC = 6.3%; 95% CI: 5.0 -7.9). The confidence intervals overlapped slightly. Unweighted demand satisfied was higher in east and southern Africa (57.2%) than in west and central Africa (33.7%). In west and central Africa, the VPC stayed above 12% until 2010-17 and then fell to 5.9%. In east and southern Africa, the share of stratum variance explained by additive effects fell from 95.8% to 82.6%. Interactions therefore accounted for a larger share of the smaller remaining inequality. Across strata, predicted demand satisfied increased by an average of 27.1 percentage points between 1994-99 and 2018-24 (Figure 3). Thirteen strata caught up and gained 6 to 11 points more than average. All were rural, and ten had primary schooling. Fourteen strata gained less than average. Eleven of these were in the richest 40% and had started from relatively high levels. Most were urban women with secondary schooling, which is consistent with convergence rather than falling behind. The other three had started among the lowest and fell further behind. They were adolescents with no schooling in the poorest households. Discussion

In this preregistered analysis of 675,584 women with demand for family planning in 38 countries over three decades, demand satisfied by modern methods varied fivefold across intersectional strata. Intersectional position, however, explained only about 7% of the individual variation and had small discriminatory accuracy. Most of the between-stratum inequality was explained by the additive effects of age, schooling, wealth, residence and union status. This inequality halved between 1994-99 and 2018-24, and the decline was the same in countries surveyed in every period. These broad gains hide groups that have not benefited. Married adolescents and young women with no schooling in poor rural households were among the lowest-ranked groups in every period. In the latest surveys, this group lagged behind other women in almost every country.

These findings support four of our five preregistered hypotheses. The strata had small discriminatory accuracy (H2). Additive effects explained most of the between-stratum variance, alongside some interaction effects (H3). Betweenstratum variance fell over time, while the poorest adolescents with no schooling improved least (H4). Inequality was higher in west and central Africa than in east and southern Africa, although the difference was modest and the confidence intervals overlapped, so H5 was only partly supported. H1 was supported for age, schooling, wealth and residence. Contrary to our hypothesis, women in union, not unmarried women, had lower demand satisfied.

The narrowing of inequality is consistent with the inverse equity hypothesis. It predicts that new interventions first reach the better off and that gaps narrow once better-off groups approach saturation [34, 35]. Strata that started high, mostly urban women with secondary schooling in the richest households, gained less than average. Rural women with primary schooling gained most. The same pattern has been described for institutional delivery across hundreds of national surveys [35].

At country level, inequality between strata was lower where overall demand satisfied was higher, which fits the same pattern. These data are consistent with the hypothesis but cannot prove it. The strata that started lowest did not all follow this pattern. Married adolescents and young women with no schooling in poor rural households stayed among the lowest-ranked strata in every period. This suggests that general expansion of services does not resolve the mechanisms that exclude them.

Married adolescents face specific barriers. These include provider bias, lack of privacy, pressure to prove fertility early in marriage and limited decision-making power within the household [15, 16]. Exposure to family planning information is also lower among married adolescents in west Africa [36]. Programmes designed for this group can work. In a cluster randomised trial in Niger, a gender-synchronised programme for married adolescent girls and their husbands increased modern contraceptive use [37]. Our results suggest that such programmes are needed in almost every country in the region, not only in countries with low overall coverage.

The low discriminatory accuracy of the strata is an important result for policy. A small VPC does not mean that the gaps between groups are unimportant. Predicted demand satisfied still varied five-fold between strata. It means that women within the same stratum differ greatly from each other. An AUC of 0.65 means that knowing a woman's age, union status, schooling, wealth and residence gives only a limited indication of whether her demand is met. Interventions targeted only by these characteristics would therefore miss many women in need while also reaching many who are already served [21, 23]. This supports universal provision of services, combined with proportionately greater effort for the most disadvantaged groups. Survey context had more discriminatory value than intersectional position.

This points to the importance of national policy, commodity supply and health system factors [11, 38].

The large stratum-by-country variance shows that the same social position carries different risks in different countries. National programmes therefore need to identify their own left-behind groups rather than rely on regional averages. Our country estimates show that young married rural women with no schooling lag behind in almost every country. This includes countries with high overall coverage such as Kenya, Sierra Leone and Mozambique, where demand satisfied among other women was above 50% and the gap was 32 to 45 points. The gap for this group was no smaller in countries with higher overall coverage, which suggests that general expansion of services alone does not close it. Reporting SDG indicator 3.7.1 only as a national average, or by one characteristic at a time, would miss these gaps. Our impact analysis adds a further point. The left-behind strata hold more than their share of unsatisfied demand, yet most women with unsatisfied demand belong to other strata. Closing the gap for the left-behind strata alone would raise national coverage by a median of only 1.5 percentage points. Targeted programmes for these groups are therefore needed for equity, but they cannot replace the expansion of services for all women. This supports an approach in which services stay universal and their intensity rises with disadvantage.

Women in union had lower demand satisfied than women not in union with the same age, schooling, wealth and residence. Unmarried women count as having demand only if they are sexually active, so they are a selected group, and many unmarried users rely on condoms and other short-acting methods [8, 13]. For married women, partner and family expectations about fertility may limit use [39]. The restriction to women in union removes this selection, and in that analysis the VPC was 4.7%, while the AUC and the share of variance explained by additive effects were similar to the main analysis. This finding highlights married adolescents as a group in particular need. At the same time, the negative interaction effects among unmarried rural adolescents show that these adolescents face barriers that are not captured by the main effects.

The exploratory analysis showed that contact with family planning services, exposure to family planning messages and employment were associated with demand satisfied. These factors, however, explained little of the difference between strata beyond the additive effects. The disadvantage of the left-behind groups is therefore not simply due to less contact with services. It may reflect structural and normative barriers, such as partner opposition and early marriage, that our survey variables did not capture.

Strengths and limitations

The study has several strengths. The analysis was preregistered and used standard surveys from almost all countries in the region over three decades. We applied one definition of unmet need to every survey, rebuilt the indicator for older surveys with the DHS Program's own algorithm and validated it. We also checked every national estimate against the published value. MAIHDA gives stable estimates for small strata and separates additive from interaction effects. Results were consistent across preregistered sensitivity analyses, including Bayesian estimation, and in countries surveyed in every period. A three-level model that allowed for the nesting of surveys within countries gave the same results.

There are also limitations. First, the analysis is descriptive and cross-sectional, and the strata are markers of social position rather than causes. Second, the surveys included differ between periods, although the decline in between-stratum inequality was the same in the eight countries surveyed in every period. Third, contraceptive use, union status and sexual activity are self-reported. Use may be under-reported among unmarried women, which could bias their estimates in either direction. Fourth, the wealth index is relative within each survey, so a wealth group does not represent the same living standard across countries or periods. Fifth, the 13 surveys that did not measure unmet need among unmarried women and two surveys with unresolved differences from published estimates were excluded from the main analysis. This reduced coverage of the early 1990s, although the analysis of women in union that included eight of them gave similar results. Sixth, the interaction effects are descriptive and may partly reflect differences in composition within strata, such as parity or region. Seventh, the main models were unweighted and did not include survey clusters as a level, although models weighted with scaled survey weights gave similar results. As a result, differences between communities within a survey were not separated from individual variation. The categories of each dimension were also broad, and finer categories could reveal larger gaps. Finally, the exploratory variables were not available for all surveys and could not capture partner opposition, provider behaviour or service quality.

Conclusions

Intersectional inequalities in demand satisfied by modern methods have narrowed in sub-Saharan Africa, and most of the remaining gaps between groups are additive. Social position alone discriminates poorly between women, which supports universal services. Married adolescents and young women with no schooling in poor rural households have, however, been left behind for three decades in almost every country. Reaching universal access by 2030 is likely to require universal services combined with country-specific programmes that reach these women. Countries should report SDG indicator 3.7.1 by combinations of social characteristics rather than one dimension at a time.
