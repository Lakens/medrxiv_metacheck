Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

What we found

We found 8 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

INTRODUCTION

Universal health coverage (UHC) commits states to ensure that all people receive the health services they need without suffering financial hardship (World Health Organization 2023). In sub-Saharan Africa, social health insurance and community-based schemes have become the principal mechanisms for advancing this commitment, and countries such as Ghana and Rwanda have shown that meaningful coverage is achievable even at modest income levels (Lu et al. 2012; Wang et al. 2017). Liberia sits at the harder end of this spectrum. Two consecutive civil wars (1989 to 1996 and 1999 to 2003), followed by the 2014 to 2016 Ebola epidemic and the COVID-19 pandemic, have left the health system rebuilding from a near-zero baseline, and enrollment in the country's nascent social health insurance scheme remains below 15% of the population (Lee et al. 2011; Kentoffio et al. 2016; Ministry of Health, Republic of Liberia 2021).

Persistently low enrollment is conventionally explained through familiar levers: low income, limited information, geographic barriers to access and inadequate service quality at participating facilities (Carrin et al. 2008; Kutzin 2013). These factors map cleanly onto the Andersen Behavioral Model of Health Services Use, which organizes the determinants of careseeking into predisposing characteristics (demographics, social structure and beliefs), enabling resources (financial, organizational and social-support resources) and need (perceived and evaluated illness) (Andersen 1995; Andersen and Davidson 2014). The model has proven durable across more than four decades of refinement and remains the most widely applied theoretical framework in health services research (Babitsch et al. 2012).

In post-conflict and fragile-state contexts, however, the standard Andersen variables explain less of the variation in enrollment than the model would predict (Newbrander et al. 2011; Witter et al. 2015). Empirical work from Sierra Leone, the Democratic Republic of Congo and other conflict-affected settings repeatedly surfaces a category of barrier that the model does not name explicitly: a generalized and durable distrust of state institutions, of fund custodians and of the health system itself (Pieterse and Lodge 2015; Vinck et al. 2019). Where such distrust is high, even well-designed schemes with subsidized premiums and nearby facilities can fail to attract enrollment (Ozawa and Sripad 2013); where institutional credibility is being rebuilt, enrollment . can rise rapidly even amid resource constraints, as the expansion of Rwanda's communitybased health insurance after 1994 illustrates (Lu et al. 2012).

Trust is not absent from health services research. A substantial literature has examined interpersonal trust between patients and individual providers (Hall et al. 2001), trust in vaccination programs (Larson et al. 2018) and trust as a determinant of treatment adherence and service use (Whetten et al. 2006). What remains underdeveloped is the treatment of institutional trust, as distinct from interpersonal trust, as a structural enabling resource on a par with money, organizational capacity and social networks. In the Andersen framework as conventionally operationalized, trust appears, if at all, as a predisposing belief: an attitudinal variable measured by one or two Likert items and modeled as a covariate rather than as a category in its own right (Gilson 2003).

This paper argues that, in post-conflict settings, this treatment is inadequate. Using mixedmethods data from four Liberian counties, we show that institutional distrust is the most frequently reported barrier to enrollment among the uninsured, narrowly ahead of cost; that it is conceptually and empirically distinguishable from cost and access; that it has identifiable subtypes with distinct policy implications; and that it is rooted in experience of institutional behavior rather than in fixed individual attitudes. We also show that distrust operates alongside, not instead of, a steep socioeconomic gradient in enrollment. On this basis we propose a formal extension of the Andersen Behavioral Model that incorporates institutional trust as a fourth enabling resource, alongside the existing financial, organizational and social-support categories.

The paper makes three contributions. First, it provides an empirical mapping of distrust subtypes in a post-conflict African health system, grounded in primary survey and qualitative data from four contrasting counties. Second, it offers a theoretical extension to the most widely used framework in health services research, with a reasoned case for treating trust as an enabling rather than a predisposing factor. Third, it sets out a practical agenda for trust-sensitive enrollment policy in fragile states that distinguishes the interventions suited to each distrust subtype. The study addressed three questions: (1) how frequently do uninsured adults in Liberia name institutional distrust, relative to cost, access and information, as their primary barrier to . enrollment; (2) which socio-demographic characteristics independently predict enrollment; and (3) what forms does institutional distrust take, and how can it be located within the Andersen model?

Conceptual framework

The Andersen Behavioral Model: structure and limits

Andersen's Behavioral Model, first articulated in 1968 and substantially revised in later iterations, posits that an individual's use of health services is shaped by three sets of factors (Andersen 1995). Predisposing characteristics include demographic features (age, sex), social structure (education, occupation, ethnicity) and health beliefs (attitudes, values and knowledge about health and health services). Enabling resources are the means by which an individual can act on a need: personal and family resources (income, insurance status), community resources (provider availability, regional distribution) and, in later revisions, social support. Need is differentiated into perceived need (the individual's own assessment) and evaluated need (professional judgment of clinical requirement).

In its mature form, the model accommodates feedback loops: use influences outcomes such as perceived health and satisfaction, which in turn feed back into predisposing characteristics and perceived need (Andersen and Davidson 2014). It has been applied to almost every category of health service and in almost every type of health system, from emergency department use in the United States to outpatient service use in urban China (Babitsch et al. 2012; Bao et al. 2017). Its empirical durability is unusual in the social sciences. The model's limits become apparent, however, in settings where the institutional preconditions for service use are themselves contested. Andersen's framework implicitly assumes a functioning and broadly trusted institutional substrate: that fund custodians will honor claims, that providers will deliver the services for which they are reimbursed and that the state will not redirect pooled premiums to other purposes. Where this substrate is intact, distrust can reasonably be treated as one predisposing belief among many. Where it is not, distrust ceases to be merely a belief about the system and becomes a reasoned assessment of the system's past . behavior: in effect, a missing enabling resource that blocks action regardless of income, information or proximity (Newbrander et al. 2011; Vinck et al. 2019).

Why institutional trust is enabling, not predisposing

Treating institutional trust as a predisposing belief, the conventional approach, raises three problems in fragile-state contexts. First, it conflates trust with knowledge or attitude, whereas in post-conflict settings distrust is frequently the product of direct experience of misappropriated funds, denied claims or providers refusing to accept insurance cards (Pieterse and Lodge 2015). It is therefore not a stable trait but a contingent assessment that institutional behavior can raise or lower. Second, it implies that distrust can be remedied through information campaigns or attitudinal change, whereas in practice it responds chiefly to demonstrated institutional reliability over time (Vinck et al. 2019). Third, it separates trust analytically from the resources it most resembles: where the rule of law is partial, trust functions much as money or transport does, as a precondition that must be present for service use to occur. These characteristics (contingency on institutional behavior rather than individual attributes, responsiveness to structural rather than attitudinal intervention, and functioning as a precondition for use) are the defining features of an enabling resource in the Andersen taxonomy. Treating institutional trust accordingly is therefore not a departure from the model but a clarification of where, within its existing structure, trust most coherently sits.

Proposed extension: trust as a fourth enabling resource

We propose extending the enabling-resources category to comprise four parallel subcategories: In this formulation, institutional trust has identifiable subtypes that can operate independently and carry distinct policy implications. Drawing on our qualitative data and the fragile-state . literature, we distinguish four: (i) fund-stewardship trust, that pooled premiums will be safeguarded and not diverted; (ii) benefit-delivery trust, that promised services will be available when claimed; (iii) provider-quality trust, that participating facilities will deliver care of acceptable quality under insurance; and (iv) generalized-state trust, the diffuse expectation that state institutions act in good faith, which in Liberia is traceable to civil-war-era institutional collapse and uneven post-conflict recovery.

The extension is theoretically conservative: it displaces no existing category, leaves the model's underlying logic unchanged and remains testable with the model's standard empirical apparatus. It is nonetheless consequential. It implies that enrollment policy in fragile states should include trust-building as a programmatic line item rather than a soft adjunct, and that measurement of enabling resources should include trust subtypes alongside income and proximity. The remainder of the paper presents the empirical case for the extension and develops its policy implications.

METHODS

Study design

We used a concurrent mixed-methods design (Creswell and Plano Clark 2018) in which a household survey (quantitative strand) and key informant interviews and focus group discussions (qualitative strand) were conducted in parallel and given equal priority. The quantitative strand established the prevalence and rank order of enrollment barriers and the socio-demographic predictors of enrollment; the qualitative strand examined the meaning, subtypes and lived experience of institutional distrust. The strands were integrated at the interpretation stage through a joint-display approach (Guetterman et al. 2015). Reporting of the quantitative strand follows the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement for cross-sectional studies (von Elm et al. 2007), and reporting of the mixed-methods design and integration follows the Good Reporting of A Mixed Methods Study (GRAMMS) guideline (O'Cathain et al. 2008); both completed checklists are provided as Supplementary File 2.

.

Relationship to a companion analysis

This manuscript reports the barrier analysis from the same cross-sectional study whose health outcome findings are reported in a companion manuscript (Omisore et al., companion manuscript under review). The companion manuscript compares physical, psychological and social health outcomes between insured and uninsured respondents. The present paper addresses a distinct question, namely the barriers to enrollment and the place of institutional trust within the Andersen model, and is the only one of the two to report the forced-choice barrier item, the enrollment regression and the qualitative strand. Overlapping descriptions of the sampling frame and survey procedures are kept consistent with the companion manuscript.

Study setting

Four Liberian counties were purposively selected to span the country's geographic, ethnic and infrastructural diversity: Montserrado (urban; includes the capital, Monrovia), Margibi (periurban and industrial; rubber and port economy), Nimba (north-eastern; conflict-affected; ethnically heterogeneous) and Grand Gedeh (remote south-eastern; logistically constrained; Ebola-affected). Together these counties account for approximately 45% of Liberia's population and represent the principal contexts in which any national insurance scheme must operate.

Quantitative strand: sampling and survey

A stratified sample of 542 adults (aged 18 years or older and principal household decisionmakers on health expenditure) was recruited across the four counties, with equal allocation of 271 insured and 271 uninsured respondents to permit direct comparison between groups.

Participants were selected through a four-stage design. Within each county, settlements were stratified as urban or rural with proportional allocation; 48 communities (12 per county, balanced between strata) were selected with probability proportional to size from National Population and Housing Census enumeration lists; and households were selected by systematic random sampling from community household registers, with one eligible, consenting adult enrolled per household. Household selection continued within each community until the insured and uninsured quotas were filled; where a stratum could not be filled from households, . participants were recruited at health facilities and community gathering points serving the same community and through community health worker referrals, using the same eligibility criteria, consent procedure and instruments. The household stage is therefore best described as stratified quota recruitment within probability-selected communities, and the estimates are not presented as nationally representative. Participant recruitment and data collection took place in May 2026. Sample size was calculated for the insured versus uninsured comparison using the formula for two independent proportions: 246 per group provided 80% power at a two-sided significance level of 0.05 to detect a difference of 12.5 percentage points (60% versus 47.5%), and this was inflated by 10% for non-response to 271 per group (N = 542). The barrier analysis is restricted to the 271 uninsured respondents, a subset of the sample sized for the primary insured versus uninsured comparison; it was not separately powered to detect differences between individual barrier categories or counties. Questionnaires were completed on paper and, for some respondents, through a distributed online link.

The structured questionnaire collected demographic and socioeconomic data, current enrollment status and, for non-enrolled respondents, a forced-choice item on the primary barrier to enrollment ("What is the single most important reason you do not have health insurance?") with six pre-coded response categories adapted from barrier items used in comparable West African community-based health insurance research. Before fieldwork, the questionnaire was reviewed for cultural and linguistic appropriateness by two Liberian public health practitioners, cognitively tested with five community health workers from areas adjacent to the study counties, and piloted with 30 adults in non-study communities in Margibi; cognitive interviews confirmed that the six barrier options were understood and covered the reasons participants would give. Because respondents selected one primary barrier, category percentages sum to 100% and identify the binding constraint each respondent perceived rather than the full set of constraints experienced. The survey did not include multi-item trust scales; the four analytic subtypes of institutional distrust reported below were therefore developed from the qualitative strand, and no quantitative subtype estimates are reported.

.

Qualitative strand: sampling and data collection

Sixteen key informant interviews were conducted with purposively selected informants across four stakeholder categories: health insurance administration; county or district health services and health care provision; community health; and national policy and development partners.

Informants were selected for their direct experience of insurance design, implementation or uptake. Quotations are attributed using role and county labels only where these do not risk identifying an informant, and the distribution of labels does not imply equal representation of every role in every county.

Four focus group discussions were conducted, one in each of Montserrado, Margibi, Nimba and Grand Gedeh, each designed to include at least four participants drawn from enrolled and non-enrolled community members. The topic guide addressed knowledge of insurance, reasons for enrollment or non-enrollment, experiences with health institutions, expectations of fund stewardship and benefit delivery, and proposed remedies. Key informant interviews were conducted in English using an eight-question semi-structured guide with probes and lasted 45 to 75 minutes; each focus group was led by a trained facilitator with note-taking support.

Interviews and discussions were audio-recorded with consent, transcribed, checked against the recordings and field notes, and de-identified before analysis. Reporting of the qualitative strand follows the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) (Tong et al. 2007); the completed checklist is provided as Supplementary File 1.

Data analysis

Quantitative analysis

Questionnaire data were entered following a structured protocol, with 10% of questionnaires double entered to verify accuracy, and were checked for range and internal consistency. Itemlevel missingness was below 2% for all items and was not associated with insurance status, county or demographic characteristics, so complete-case analysis was used without imputation. insured and uninsured respondents were examined with Welch's t-test for age and Pearson's chi-squared tests for categorical characteristics. Primary barriers to enrollment were described as counts and percentages with Wilson 95% confidence intervals among uninsured respondents, overall and by county, and the distribution of barriers across counties was compared using Pearson's chi-squared test of independence, with Cramér's V as a measure of association. Multivariable logistic regression modeled current enrollment (enrolled versus not enrolled) among all 542 respondents, with age (per year), sex, education (no formal education, primary, secondary, tertiary), employment status (unemployed, informally employed, student, formally employed) and county of residence entered simultaneously. Results are reported as adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Because the barrier item was administered only to uninsured respondents and the survey did not include trust scales, institutional distrust was not entered as a regressor; its role was assessed through the barrier distribution and the qualitative strand, and the regression was used to characterize the structural socioeconomic gradient against which perceived barriers operate.

Qualitative analysis

Qualitative data were analyzed using reflexive thematic analysis (Braun and Clarke 2006; Braun and Clarke 2019) with a hybrid orientation: Andersen's predisposing, enabling and need domains provided initial sensitizing concepts, while the subtypes of institutional distrust were developed inductively from the data. Analysis was led by the principal researcher as an interpretive and reflexive process that moved through familiarization, systematic coding, generation and review of candidate themes, and definition and naming of final themes.

Consistent with this approach, statistical intercoder agreement was not used as a criterion of analytic quality. Rigor was supported through sustained engagement with the transcripts, reflexive memo-writing, transparent documentation of analytic decisions, deliberate attention to divergent and disconfirming accounts, and the use of illustrative participant quotations.

Integration

The strands were integrated at the interpretation stage. Survey barrier rankings were set against the qualitative themes in a joint display (presented in Results) that links each distrust subtype to its defining concern, representative quotations and the corresponding policy lever, and the . resulting meta-inferences informed the proposed extension of the Andersen model, which is set out at the end of Results.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was granted by the Research Ethics Committee of the University of Port Harcourt (December 2024; reference UPH/CEREMAD/REC/MM105/030). A letter of local institutional support for data collection in Liberia was obtained (May 2026). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before data collection, including specific consent for audio recording. Participation was voluntary, participants received no compensation beyond reimbursement of transport costs, and they could withdraw at any time without consequence.

Transcripts and survey records were de-identified before analysis.

Patient and public involvement

Community leaders in each study county were consulted during instrument design and advised on language, the framing of trust-related response options and culturally appropriate phrasing of sensitive questions. Preliminary findings were presented back to community focus groups in each county before manuscript preparation, and their reactions informed the policy implications set out in the Discussion.

RESULTS

Sample characteristics

Of approximately 600 eligible adults approached, 58 declined (response rate 90.3%); all 542 enrolled participants were included in the analysis, of whom 271 (50.0%) were currently enrolled in the national insurance scheme and 271 (50.0%) were uninsured. The insured and uninsured strata were of equal size by design, permitting direct comparison within a single multivariable model. Insured respondents were older than uninsured respondents (mean 44.6 years, SD 11.1, against 39.0 years, SD 12.5; p < 0.001). The groups differed markedly in education: 31.7% of uninsured respondents had no formal education and 42.4% primary education only, compared with 11.1% and 20.3% of insured respondents (χ² = 100.79; p < 0.001; Cramér's V = 0.431). They also differed in employment: 43.5% of uninsured . respondents were unemployed and 10.3% formally employed, compared with 23.2% and 45.8% of insured respondents (χ² = 86.56; p < 0.001; V = 0.400). The groups did not differ by sex (χ² = 0.36; p = 0.55) or county of residence (χ² = 3.14; p = 0.37). These unadjusted differences foreshadow the socioeconomic gradient identified in the multivariable analysis.

Primary barriers to enrollment among the uninsured

Among uninsured respondents (n = 271), institutional distrust was the most frequently reported primary barrier, named by 57 (21.0%; 95% CI 16.6 to 26.3), narrowly ahead of premium cost (49, 18.1%; 95% CI 14.0 to 23.1) and inaccessibility of services (47, 17.3%; 95% CI 13.3 to 22.3) (Table 1). These three leading barriers together accounted for 56.5% of primary reasons for non-enrollment. The remaining responses were that insurance was not necessary (45, 16.6%), not knowing how to enroll (38, 14.0%) and a preference for paying out of pocket (35, 12.9%).

The barrier distribution did not differ significantly across the four counties (χ² = 15.99, df = 15; p = 0.38; Cramér's V = 0.140), indicating that the ordering of constraints was broadly consistent across the study counties rather than confined to a single county. Two descriptive patterns are nonetheless notable. Distrust was most frequently cited in Nimba (30.8%), the county with the most intense exposure to civil conflict, whereas in Grand Gedeh the most frequent response was that insurance is unnecessary (25.0%). Because each respondent selected a single primary barrier, these figures identify the binding constraint each respondent perceived rather than every constraint faced; the qualitative strand indicates that distrust frequently coexists with cost and access constraints rather than substituting for them.

Subtypes of institutional distrust: qualitative findings

Qualitative analysis organized accounts of institutional distrust into four analytic subtypes, each with a distinct defining concern and a corresponding policy lever (Table 2). Three subtypes were directly illustrated by retained quotations; evidence for provider-quality distrust overlapped with broader distrust of the state health sector and this subtype should therefore be regarded as provisional. Together, the subtypes show that the single survey category "do not . trust the insurance scheme" conceals several different expectations of failure grounded in institutional experience.

Fund-stewardship distrust

Fund-stewardship distrust was the expectation that premiums would be misappropriated or diverted before reaching the benefit pool. It could be anchored in personal experience of earlier institutional failures involving household savings. As one Margibi focus group participant, who had lost savings when a financial institution collapsed, explained: "People lost everything. So when you come and say put your money in insurance, my mind goes to that experience." Here, reluctance to enroll reflected a reasoned transfer of experience from one failed custodian of household money to another prospective one.

Benefit-delivery distrust

Benefit-delivery distrust concerned whether promised services would be available when claimed. Informants described how delays in the claims process, once known in communities, undermined outreach regardless of its quality: "We have cases where claims were submitted months ago and are still pending. When communities hear about this, it doesn't matter how well we explain our scheme, the word gets around that insurance doesn't pay." (Montserrado insurance manager, key informant interview). This account illustrates how administrative performance is converted into community reputation, and why information campaigns alone cannot offset visible delivery failures.

Provider-quality distrust

Provider-quality distrust concerned whether insurance-affiliated facilities would deliver acceptable care to enrolled patients. Participants' accounts of this concern were closely bound up with the health sector's wider track record rather than with the clinical skills of individual providers, so the most illustrative quotation, from Grand Gedeh, is reported under generalizedstate distrust below.

Generalized-state distrust

Generalized-state distrust was the diffuse expectation that state institutions act in bad faith. A participant from Nimba, the county where distrust was most often named as the primary survey barrier, expressed it directly: "We have seen what governments do here in Nimba. They make . promises during campaigns and then forget us after. We have been burned too many times. This insurance is another promise from people who may not be here to keep it." (Nimba focus group participant). In such accounts, the insurance scheme was not evaluated on its own design but as one more instance of a long series of state commitments, which is why this subtype is likely to be the most resistant to short-term remedy. The same deficit extended to the health sector itself. A district health officer in Grand Gedeh traced it to the Ebola response: "During Ebola, people saw that the government system failed to protect health workers, who themselves became victims. When we now come asking people to invest their money in a health insurance scheme managed by that same sector, we inherit the credibility deficit that the EVD response created." (Grand Gedeh district health officer, key informant interview). Accounts of this kind show that judgments about the care an insured patient will receive are shaped by the state health sector's wider record, and we interpret them as evidence about institutional trust rather than about the clinical quality of individual providers.

Socio-demographic predictors of enrollment

Multivariable logistic regression with current enrollment as the outcome identified socioeconomic status as the strongest structural predictor (Table 3). Compared with unemployed respondents, those in formal employment had nearly nine times the odds of enrollment (aOR 8.78, 95% CI 4.90 to 15.76; p < 0.001). Compared with respondents with no formal education, the odds of enrollment were higher among those with tertiary education (aOR 8.17, 95% CI 4.12 to 16.20; p < 0.001) and secondary education (aOR 7.36, 95% CI 3.98 to 13.63; p < 0.001), whereas primary education was not associated with enrollment (aOR 1.40, 95% CI 0.77 to 2.55; p = 0.27). Each additional year of age was associated with a small increase in the odds of enrollment (aOR 1.05, 95% CI 1.03 to 1.07; p < 0.001). Informal employment (aOR 1.35, 95% CI 0.81 to 2.24; p = 0.25) and student status (aOR 1.14, 95% CI 0.53 to 2.45; p = 0.74) were not significantly associated with enrollment, nor were female sex (aOR 1.20, 95% CI 0.79 to 1.82; p = 0.40) or county of residence (all p ≥ 0.29).

Read together with the barrier data, these results show two complementary layers. The regression identifies who is structurally positioned to enroll, chiefly the formally employed and those with at least secondary education, while the survey and qualitative strands converge on institutional distrust as the barrier the uninsured themselves most often name. The absence of an association with informal employment is particularly relevant for policy, because informalsector households are the population that voluntary contributory schemes most need to reach and the population for whom trust in the scheme matters most when deciding whether to pay.

Table 4 sets out the proposed extension, juxtaposing the conventional Andersen enablingresources structure with the four-subcategory revision. The change is additive: predisposing and need factors are unaffected; the financial, organizational and social-support subcategories of enabling resources are retained unchanged; and institutional-trust resources are added as a fourth subcategory comprising the four subtypes identified above. Measurement implications follow directly: each trust subtype becomes a candidate variable for Andersen-framed studies, to be measured alongside existing income, proximity and social-support measures.

DISCUSSION

Principal findings

Three findings stand out. First, institutional distrust was the most frequently reported primary barrier to health insurance enrollment among uninsured adults in post-conflict Liberia, narrowly ahead of premium cost and inaccessibility, and the distribution of barriers did not differ significantly across four counties that differ markedly in urbanization, conflict exposure and remoteness. Second, distrust is not monolithic: the analysis generated four distinguishable subtypes with different policy implications, although the provider-quality subtype requires further direct validation. Third, distrust operates alongside, rather than in place of, a steep socioeconomic gradient: formal employment and secondary or tertiary education were the strongest independent predictors of enrollment. The two sets of findings are complementary.

Socioeconomic status describes who is structurally able to enroll; institutional distrust describes why many of those who remain outside the scheme say they have not joined.

.

Trust as an enabling resource: theoretical implications

These findings support the proposed extension of the Andersen Behavioral Model. The conventional treatment of trust as a predisposing belief understates its role in fragile-state contexts in three ways that the data make visible. It conflates a contingent assessment of institutional behavior with a stable individual attribute, whereas participants consistently linked their distrust to specific institutional events: a collapsed financial institution, delayed claims, the health sector's performance during Ebola and unkept political promises. It implies that distrust can be remedied through information, whereas informants described communities discounting well-explained schemes once delivery failures became known. And it separates trust analytically from the structural resources it most closely resembles, whereas in participants' accounts a credible expectation of institutional performance functioned as a precondition for paying into a scheme, in much the way that money or transport functions as a precondition for attending a facility.

The proposed reframing is theoretically conservative. It does not modify the model's underlying causal logic, displaces no existing category and remains testable with the model's standard empirical apparatus. It is nonetheless consequential: it implies that Andersen-framed studies of service use in fragile states that omit institutional-trust variables risk misspecification, and that interventions targeting only the conventional enabling categories may systematically underperform. The regression results sharpen rather than weaken this argument. Because the strongest structural predictors (education and formal employment) cannot be changed quickly by an insurance agency, institutional trust is among the few enabling resources that scheme managers can influence directly through their own conduct.

Comparison with prior literature

The prominence of distrust among enrollment barriers in a post-conflict African setting is consistent with evidence from Sierra Leone (Pieterse and Lodge 2015) and the Democratic Republic of Congo (Vinck et al. 2019). This study adds, first, a formal disaggregation of distrust into subtypes with distinct policy implications and, second, the theoretical integration of trust into a widely used service-use framework rather than its treatment as a standalone construct.

. Prior work on trust in health systems has tended either to treat trust as a unitary attitudinal variable (Gilson 2003; Ozawa and Sripad 2013) or to develop trust-specific frameworks that do not connect to mainstream service-use models (Gille et al. 2021). The extension proposed here is designed to bridge these traditions. The coexistence of distrust with a strong education and employment gradient also echoes the broader UHC literature, which emphasizes that voluntary contributory schemes struggle to reach informal-sector populations (Carrin et al. 2008; Kutzin 2013); our findings suggest that institutional distrust is one reason that struggle persists even where premiums are modest.

Policy implications

If institutional trust is an enabling resource, trust-building should appear as a programmatic and budgeted line item in enrollment policy rather than as a soft adjunct. Four implications follow, mapping to the four subtypes identified.

Fund-stewardship distrust is likely to respond primarily to demonstrable financial accountability: ring-fenced accounts, independent audit, public reporting of premium collection and benefit payout, and visible separation of the insurance pool from general government revenue. Because these measures are within the direct control of the scheme, they are plausible early-moving levers whose effect on stewardship trust could be tracked over the first one to two years of implementation.

Benefit-delivery distrust is likely to respond to reliable supply and credible grievance redress: stockouts should be tracked and published, claim-processing times and denial rates made transparent, and accessible mechanisms established for resolving delayed or denied claims.

Where benefit guarantees are not stated publicly and tracked, skepticism among potential enrollees is a reasonable response to the information available to them.

Provider-quality distrust is likely to respond to provider-payment design and quality monitoring. Payment structures that create incentives to under-serve enrolled patients should be redesigned, quality indicators tracked separately for enrolled and out-of-pocket patients, and complaint channels made accessible and demonstrably responsive. Because participants linked . expectations of care to the health sector's wider record, visible improvements in the protection and support of health workers may also contribute.

Generalized-state distrust is likely to be the most resistant to short-term remedy. It accumulates only through demonstrated institutional reliability across multiple programs over time. Multistakeholder governance, with donor, civil society and faith-based representation on oversight bodies, can partly substitute for state credibility during the rebuilding phase, but the principal lever is a sustained track record. In parallel, measures that lower financial barriers for informalsector and less-educated households, such as targeted premium subsidies and flexible contribution schedules, remain essential, because trust-building alone cannot offset the socioeconomic gradient identified in the regression.

Implications for research

Three research directions follow. First, a multi-item instrument measuring the four trust subtypes should be developed and psychometrically validated in Liberia and other fragile-state settings, with attention to cross-cultural translation and equivalence; this would allow institutional trust to be entered directly into enrollment models alongside socioeconomic variables. Second, longitudinal application of the extended Andersen model should test whether changes in trust subtypes predict changes in enrollment over time. Third, intervention studies should pair trust-building components (audit, grievance redress, provider-payment reform) with conventional enrollment interventions to estimate the effect on enrollment attributable to trust.

Strengths and limitations

The study has several strengths. The concurrent mixed-methods design allowed direct triangulation between the prevalence of perceived barriers and their meaning. The four-county sample spans substantial contextual variation within Liberia, from the capital to remote, conflict-affected and Ebola-affected counties, and the absence of significant county differences in the barrier distribution suggests that the pattern was not confined to one study county. The equal allocation of insured and uninsured respondents supported direct comparison of insured and uninsured adults within a single multivariable model of enrollment. The response rate was . high (90.3%), item-level missingness was below 2%, and the barrier item was cognitively tested and piloted before fieldwork. Finally, the theoretical extension is grounded in primary data rather than literature synthesis alone, and preliminary findings were presented back to participating communities. Several limitations should be noted. First, the findings come from Liberia and may not generalize to non-fragile sub-Saharan settings where trust dynamics differ. Second, institutional trust was captured in the survey through a single forced-choice barrier category rather than a validated multi-item scale, so trust could not be entered into the enrollment regression and the four analytic subtypes require independent validation, particularly the provisional provider-quality subtype. Third, because the barrier item recorded only one primary reason, the prevalence of distrust as a contributing barrier is likely to be higher than its prevalence as the primary barrier, while the small margin between distrust (21.0%) and cost (18.1%) means that the two should be regarded as comparably important. Fourth, the crosssectional design constrains causal inference, and longitudinal data would strengthen the case.

Fifth, the proposed model extension is at this stage a hypothesis with empirical motivation; full validation requires application in additional fragile-state settings.

The mixed-mode survey administration also has limitations. Participation through a distributed online link may have introduced differential access associated with digital literacy and connectivity, as well as self-selection, and responses collected online and on paper may be subject to mode effects. Supplementary recruitment at facilities and community sites, used where households could not fill an insurance stratum, may over-represent adults in recent contact with health services, and enrolling any eligible adult in each household rather than applying a within-household selection grid may over-represent adults who were at home or had access to a device. Because communities were the primary sampling units and the analyses did not adjust for clustering by community, the confidence intervals may be somewhat narrower than design-based estimates would be. Some qualitative reporting details recommended by COREQ, such as focus group size and focus group duration, were not systematically recorded and are reported as not available. These considerations qualify the representativeness of the sample where access to, or uptake of, the online option differed among eligible participants.

.

CONCLUSIONS

In post-conflict Liberia, institutional distrust emerged as the most frequently reported barrier to health insurance enrollment among the uninsured, narrowly ahead of cost (21.0% against 18.1%), and it operated alongside socioeconomic status and access as a distinct and policyaddressable constraint that conventional affordability measures do not reach. Socioeconomic status, particularly formal employment and secondary or tertiary education, remained the strongest structural predictor of enrollment. The analysis generated four distrust subtypes with distinct policy implications; three were directly anchored in retained participant quotations, while the provider-quality subtype remains provisional and requires further validation. The Andersen Behavioral Model, the most widely applied framework in health services research, conventionally treats trust as a predisposing belief; the evidence presented here suggests that in fragile-state settings it functions instead as an enabling resource on a par with financial, organizational and social-support resources. We have therefore proposed a conservative extension of the model that adds institutional trust as a fourth subcategory of enabling resources, with four analytic subtypes for further testing. The policy implication is direct: in fragile-state contexts, trust-building is health-system infrastructure that must accompany, not replace, measures that improve financial access, and enrollment policy that omits it is likely to underperform. The extension remains a hypothesis pending wider validation, but the survey, key informant and focus group evidence motivating it converge, and the cost of misspecification, persistent under-enrollment despite well-designed schemes, is too well documented to leave the framework unrevised.
