Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Effect Sizes in t-tests and F-tests works

The Effect Size module checks if effect sizes are correctly reported in t-tests and F-tests.

The Effect Size check searches for regular expressions that match typical ways in which effect sizes are reported. It subsequently checks different ways in which Cohen’s d, g, ηp2, and ωp2 can be computed against the reported value. If effects are missing, or might be incorrect, you the module provides a warning. The module was validated on APA reported statistical tests, and might miss effect sizes that were reported in other reporting styles. It was validated by the Metacheck team on papers published in Psychological Science.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Persistent inequalities in health outcomes continue to be a stubborn feature of many developed Western societies. To effectively understand health inequalities researchers must address two key criticisms: an overt focus on singular dimensions of disadvantage and a continuing need for longitudinal studies. Lifecourse intersectionality offers a framework to address these critiques through situating more nuanced and multidimensional understandings of the development of inequalities over time. We conduct a longitudinal Multilevel Analysis of Individual Heterogeneity and Discriminatory Accuracy (MAIHDA) examining both physical and mental health SF12 scores using UK Household Longitudinal Study data for adults (2009 to 2023). Forming intersections based on sex, ethnicity, generational cohort, education, and socioeconomic class, we identify a substantial degree of intersectional variation in our outcomes, and distinct differences in the development of intersectional inequalities in physical and mental health over the lifecourse. Intersectional inequalities are more pronounced for mental health compared to physical health, particularly at younger and older ages. In both cases, inequalities are additively patterned with generation and sex appearing most important to mental health and socioeconomic differences being stronger for physical health. However, our results also emphasise heterogeneity in the identified lifecourse intersectional effects, particularly in relation to how the patterning of the sociodemographic components for mental health vary by generational cohort. We therefore help reveal important complexity in the development of health inequalities in the UK and highlight the utility of longitudinal intersectional MAIHDA as a tool for quantitative intersectional researchers. Introduction

Many developed Western societies continue to be characterised by persistent and pervasive inequalities in health outcomes (Mackenbach, 2012; Case and Kraftman, 2024; Townsend and Davidson, 1982; Prior and Manley, 2018). A 'social gradient' is present, whereby those occupying positions of socioeconomic disadvantage typically experience worse health outcomes (Case and Kraftman, 2024; Costa-Font and Hernández-Quevedo, 2012; Scambler, 2012) such as lower life expectancy, poorer wellbeing and general health, as well as greater incidence of conditions such as cardiovascular disease or depression (Castelletti et al., 2024; Conrad et al., 2024; Lorant et al., 2003; ONS, 2025a). This morbidity burden places a strain on social and health services. For example, an estimated 148.9 million workdays were lost due to sickness in 2024 in the UK (ONS, 2025b). Additionally, avoidable differences in health outcomes along social, economic and environmental dimensions create a moral imperative to address such disparities to support thriving communities and a more equitable society. Therefore, understanding and addressing such social health inequalities should remain a key policy target (Ayres et al., 2024; Bibby et al., 2024; Bleich et al., 2012; GOV. UK, 2025; HM Government, 2022).

Part of the work to tackle inequalities involves firstly understanding their current state and distribution, as well as their relationship to different socioeconomic and demographic characteristics as markers of risk and exposure. To do so effectively researchers must address two key criticisms which have been levelled at studies of socioeconomic inequalities. The first is an overt focus on singular axes of disadvantage or individual 'risk factors' (Merlo et al., 2017), and a corresponding inattention to interactions and intersectionality (Richardson and Brown, 2016). The second is a continuing preponderance of cross-sectional studies (Jones and Rice, 2004; Van Ham and Manley, 2012), particularly in respect of multidimensional and intersectional articulations of lifecourse inequalities (Bell et al., 2024).

Beyond singular axes of disadvantage

Regarding the first criticism, a lack of attention to multidimensionality in the factors associated with different health outcomes may come with several drawbacks. Such an approach may encourage 'blaming the victim' through framing inequalities as individuallevel issues (Evans et al., 2018). The tendency to attach categories of exposure to individuals, without thought to the social context in which such exposures are embedded, is reflected in policy interventions which focus on addressing lifestyle factors over attempts to shift 'upstream' societal and economic pressures which place different groups at heightened risk (Holman et al., 2021). Additionally, singular, broad categories of identity are likely to mask important aspects of heterogeneity as individuals are enmeshed in multiple overlapping and entangled systems of oppression and marginalisation. Where there is significant heterogeneity in outcomes, relying on the 'average' for singular group categories could also lead to weak inferential or discriminatory accuracy in the identification of those at risk of poor health (Subramanian et al., 2018; Merlo, 2018; Merlo et al., 2017). This could feedback to policy interventions: policy could be ineffective when implemented on a more universal basis where variation around overall group averages would suggest a more targeted or proportionally tailored solution to be beneficial (Merlo et al., 2019). Overall, an overt focus on singular facets of (dis)advantage in studies of health inequalities is to the detriment of knowledge on the distinct vulnerability or resilience that may be present around the 'on average' story.

Intersectionality is a framework in which to situate more nuanced and multidimensional understandings of inequalities in health and other social outcomes.

Intersectionality articulates the mutually constituting nature of social systems and centralises marginalised and 'invisible' groups to expose and critique configurations of power within social systems, both of privilege and disadvantage (Prior et al., 2025; Crenshaw, 1989; Choo and Ferree, 2010). Quantitative intersectional research relies primarily on an 'inter-. categorical' (McCall, 2005) approach that adopts categorical definitions of identity (such as male/female or White or Black ethnicity etc.) to explore variation within and between groups and their interactions. There is a body of thinking that critiques the treatment of social categories, emphasising their inability to capture the messy realities of lived experience and highlighting their ultimately social construction (Cho et al., 2013; Choo and Ferree, 2010, McCall, 2005). However, such identity markers continue as societal signifiers of persistent hierarchies of power relations (MacKinnon, 2013). Therefore, bringing forward intersectional perspectives remains a promising avenue for advancing the study of health inequalities (Holman and Walker, 2021).

Emerging techniques for analysing intersectional dynamics in health outcomes, such as Multilevel Analysis of Individual Heterogeneity and Discriminatory Accuracy (MAIHDA) (Evans et al., 2024a; Evans et al., 2020; Evans et al., 2018; Merlo, 2018) may be particularly appropriate here. This approach has been growing in popularity within epidemiology and the social sciences (Prior et al., 2025; Persmark et al., 2019; Fisk et al., 2018; Moreno-Agostino et al., 2024; Evans et al., 2023; Hernández-Yumar et al., 2018; Keller et al., 2023; Alvarez and Evans, 2021). It facilitates the operationalisation of intersectional thinking within quantitative studies in a manner more aligned with the tenets of intersectionality, situating intersectional identities as contexts within which individuals are embedded and socialised. This implementation helps avoid 'blaming the victim' and maintains an awareness for the structural power hierarchies which shape the identity categories we utilise within quantitative research (Evans et al., 2020). Methods such as intersectional MAIHDA offer ways to explore and identify diverse patterns of intersectional dynamics as part of toolkit of approaches to comprehend health inequalities in a multidimensional manner.

Lifecourse perspectives on intersectionality.

Cross-sectional studies still predominate the literature on health inequalities, necessarily limiting the capacity for research to uncover insight into the development of inequalities through time. Fortunately, there has been a considerable drive towards lifecourse research on health, with lifecourse epidemiology and conceptual models for health pathways emerging since the 1990s (Blane et al., 2007; Kuh et al., 2003; Wadsworth, 1997), as well as a growing body of cohort and other longitudinal datasets by which to explore trajectories of health relationships (Blaxter, 1986; CLOSER, 2025). However, extensions of lifecourse research to consider intersectional entanglements (or conversely of intersectional literature to deliberate the nature and impact of ageing and lifecourse transitions) are less prevalent (Holman and Walker, 2021).

The exploration of health trajectories through an intersectional lens will provide insights beyond those of static cross-sectional studies. An intersectional framework on longitudinal and lifecourse health studies would forefront conceptualisations of the heterogeneous nature of ageing, including differing trajectories, transitions and life events (Ferrer et al., 2017; Holman and Walker, 2021). Differing lifecourse hypotheses are posited for how health inequalities will evolve with time, such as ageing as leveller, persistent inequality, cumulative inequality, sensitive/critical periods, and chains of risk (Ferraro and Farmer, 1996; Kuh et al., 2003). These may be present to a greater or lesser degree for different intersections of status/identity and may also vary by health outcome measured. For instance, evidence from older adults in the United States has suggested that Black women may experience an accelerated increase in functional limitations compared with Black men, White or Mexican American individuals, who show more constant disparities through middle to old age according to the 'persistent inequality hypothesis' (Warner and Brown, 2011). Brown et al., (2016) examined intersections of age, race/ethnicity, gender and socioeconomic status and revealed that the gap between higher status Black and White individuals increased . with age as the highly educated Black grouping experienced heightened declines in their health, in line with a cumulative disadvantage perspective.

Combining intersectionality theory with lifecourse studies would help situate dynamic understandings of identity within diverse contexts of space, place and time and how changing social systems shape developing health inequalities along intersectional lines. For example, Ferrer et al. (2017) highlighted the interplay of ageing, caring labour and immigration with racialized and gendered inequalities through a case study of a Filipino community in Canada. Brown (2018) noted the potential role of racialised immigrant integration processes that serve to marginalise and stigmatise Black and Mexican American immigrants, with deleterious consequences for their health trajectories through middle to later life. Therefore, the synthesis of lifecourse intersectionality will provide insights into the production, maintenance or breakdown of health. In turn, these new insights could inform policy decisions on where, when and for whom to target interventions for tackling inequalities. However, space remains for further research utilising a lifecourse intersectional framework, particularly for work which considers the whole lifecourse, rather than focusing on later-life ageing. Recent extensions to the MAIHDA methodology have shown how this promising technique can be combined with longitudinal analyses (Bell et al., 2024) and are particularly pertinent to bringing forward lifecourse understandings of the development of sociodemographic intersectional health inequalities.

Heterogeneity across different outcomes

An appreciation for complexity is vitally important both to providing nuanced empirical understandings of health inequalities and to intersectional thinking. One aspect of heterogeneity in health inequalities is how intersectional variation may present differently across different health dimensions. Gendered and culturally shaped attitudes towards physical and mental health conditions could manifest in intersectional differences between these outcomes. For example, a stigma can still exist around mental illness and this may be more apparent in reporting patterns for men where there are gendered expectations of stoic masculinity (Gorski, 2010). Additionally, physical health and mental health conditions can often show comorbidity (Rai et al., 2016; Wienand et al., 2024). An intersectional framework could help reveal multiple marginalised communities at risk of social, environmental and other stressors that may contribute to an enhanced burden of multiple conditions, for instance through a chronic weathering on the body (Seeman et al., 2014; Forde et al., 2019).

Furthermore, introducing temporality to the discussion could reveal extra dimensions of heterogeneity between physical and mental health. For example, societal and economic conditions such as a recession may have a more marked effect on mental health for certain groups, such as young adults poised to enter the job market (Alam and Bose, 2022). In general, older persons and generations may see declining physical health but intersections of privileged characteristics may offset some of this decline through maintenance of positive mental wellbeing whilst disadvantaged groups suffer from their history of socialised discrimination and marginalisation (Gilmore-Bykovskyi et al., 2021; Sadana et al., 2016).

Through examination of both physical and mental health trajectories under an intersectional lens we can uncover these important aspects for understanding the development of inequalities.

This study

This paper will help address the dearth of longitudinal intersectional studies and thus tackle the two key critiques of health inequalities studies discussed above. We will use a longitudinal MAIHDA analysis to examine physical and mental health trends over the lifecourse for individuals in the UK. The overall aim of the study is to examine sociodemographic intersectional variation in these two interrelated dimensions of health and quality of life, and how they develop over time.

Data

This study utilises the UK Household Longitudinal Survey (UKHLS) dataset.

UKHLS is a panel survey of households, where all individuals within a selected household are surveyed. We will be using 14 waves of data running from 2009 to 2023. Due to the survey design, we can assess a broad range of ages (16 to 96) through a compilation of different generational cohorts.

The two outcome health measures are the SF12 mental and physical health metrics.

These are short-form versions of the widely used SF36 health measures and are quality of life measures that express the degree to which these dimensions of health impact on everyday life (Ware et al., 1995). The physical health metric covers: physical functioning, role-physical, bodily pain, and general health. The mental health metric covers: vitality, social functioning, role-emotional, and mental health. Both are continuous scores ranging from 0 to 100 where higher numbers are indicative of higher functioning or better health, and the two scores can be meaningfully compared (Ware et al., 2002).

UKHLS also features a variety of social and demographic measures that we can use to define intersectional groups. For this study we focus on facets of identity that proxy for key systems of marginalisation and discrimination, as well as axes which are routinely linked to health differentials. These are sex (male, female), ethnicity (White, Black, Asian, Mixed, Other), highest educational qualification achieved (degree or other higher, A level/GCSE/other qualifications, no qualifications) and socioeconomic class (management and professional, intermediate, routine). Additionally, as we are assessing intersectional variation over time we need to consider the temporal identities of our study population. Therefore, generational cohort will also be included as we can reasonably expect there to be differences in health outcomes across cohorts as they progress through life under different economic, political, social and technological climates. The generational cohorts are: Silent Generation (birth years 1945 and earlier), Baby Boomers (born 1946 to 1963 ), Generation X (born 1964 to 1979 ), Generation Y (born 1980 to 1994) and Generation Z (born 1995 or later), following the example of Bell et al., (2024).

Recorded changes in ethnicity or birth year (used to define generational cohort) were treated as erroneous and cases were dropped on this basis. In the case of education, we chose to use the highest achieved qualification (a time invariant variable) as this aligns more with the educational status concept we are trying to capture. To operationalise the concept of socioeconomic class we utilise the highest modal category across observations (also time invariant) as this is thought to better reflect overall social status, rather than potentially shortterm changes in class based on occupational positions. Changes in recorded sex may reflect genuine experiences and highlight the potential marginalisation of transgender communities.

However, as there were so few instances of this in our population (<0.1%) we chose to drop these cases and continue with a static formulation of sex identity. Age will be included in the fixed part to model trajectories in mental and physical functioning and will be centred around age 50, as a 'middle-aged' individual. A descriptive summary of the outcomes, age and intersectional components is shown in Table 1, with further details on missingness, category changes over time and the distribution of occasions provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Our final sample of those with complete non-missing information across all our sociodemographic variables was 298,911 occasions, nested in 50,622 individuals, within 372 strata.

Methods . This study relies on the MAIHDA methodology which offers many advantages over traditional approaches to studying intersectional relationships (notably the inclusion of interaction terms between two or more axes of identity in regression analyses). These include: easily and parsimoniously accommodating several different dimensions of status: directly quantifying the degree to which intersectional grouping account for total variation in outcomes; reducing the risk of overinterpreting strata predictions that may have arisen by chance; and enabling the analysis of variation within and between strata, affording appreciation for multiple strands of heterogeneity (Prior et al., 2025; Bell et al., 2019; Leckie et al., 2025; Merlo, 2018).

We employ a longitudinal intersectional MAIHDA in this study. Under the longitudinal design the two-level formulation of individuals nested within their intersectional strata is extended to three-levels: occasions nested within individuals within strata. This setup is possible as we are considering our intersectional strata as unchanging through time for this exploratory examination of health inequalities.

For a cross-sectional intersectional MAIHDA two models are typically run. The first is a variance components model without any covariates which assesses the overall degree of variation present at the level of your intersectional strata (Evans et al., 2024a). The second model then adds the components of the intersectional strata as covariates. This model allows researchers to analyse the degree to which the intersectional variation is composed of main effects versus interaction effects (additive versus multiplicative effects) with any remaining intersectional level variation being the result of two or higher-way interactions between the intersectional components.

For the longitudinal formulation as used in this study, these models are extended by the inclusion of age terms in the fixed part of the model and through allowing these age . effects to vary at higher levels. This enables us to examine trajectories of health through time and how these trajectories vary between individuals and by intersectional groups. The first set of models can be written as follows: 𝐻𝑒𝑎𝑙𝑡ℎ = 𝛽 + 𝛽 𝐴𝑔𝑒 + 𝑣 + 𝑣 𝐴𝑔𝑒 + 𝑢 + 𝑢 𝐴𝑔𝑒 + 𝑒 [1] This first model features a linear (𝛽 ) effect of age, which will we centre around age 50 so that the intercept 𝛽 provides an estimate of the overall average physical or mental health for 'middle-aged' individuals. For simplicity we only present the linear age effect, but the model could also be extended by further polynomial terms. We allow the intercepts to vary at the stratum level (level-3) (𝑣 ) as well as allowing the age slope (the linear portion) to vary across intersectional strata (𝑣 ).

Thus, we are modelling stratum specific mean differences in the average level of health (the intercepts) but also how health develops and changes over time (the age slopes/trajectories). Similarly, we allow the intercepts and age trajectories to vary at the individual level (level-2) through the 𝑢 and 𝑢 terms, capturing further heterogeneity within intersectional strata. At the lowest level (level-1, occasions) the residual 𝑒 summarises remaining variation not accounted for by higher levels, that is within individuals, between occasions.

Examination of the variance is an important element to the analysis. The stratum-and individual-level random effects of models 1 and 2 fall under the typical normality assumptions of a multilevel model. As these are random slope models the variance at the higher levels (strata and individuals) are expressed as a variance function. This function shows how the variance, which can be considered here a measure of the extent of inequalities across or within intersections, changes with age.

An important statistic is the Variance Partitioning Coefficient (VPC). A stratum-level VPC assesses the proportion of overall variation that is situated between intersections, providing quantification for the importance of the chosen intersectional identities to outcome inequalities. In this longitudinal study, the stratum-level VPCs will vary as a function with age. Alongside the overall stratum VPC, we can also calculate separate VPC's for the intercepts and slopes. The intercept VPC will calculate the extent to which intersectional differences account for overall mean differences in health for the reference 50-year-old person. The Slope VPC explains the degree that differences in the changes in physical or mental health over time are intersectional in nature.

The motivation for fitting the second model is to determine the extent to which additive or multiplicative effects between the intersectional components explain overall intersectional variation. This helps distinguish whether intersectional effects are operating on a more universal basis across different stratum axes or whether there is heterogeneity in how intersectional inequalities operate. The relative importance of additive effects (the sociodemographic main effects) is given by the Proportion Change in Variance (PCV), which will vary with age. Additionally, intercept and slope specific PCVs will be calculated. The intercept PCV will demonstrate how overall mean intersectional differences are explained by additive rather than interactive effects. The slope PCV will do the same but in reference to differences in the health slopes.

In this study we will be examining two outcomes: physical and mental health.

Separate sets of the models above will be run for physical and mental health, before comparisons are made between the outcomes. Further detail on the methodology, including algebraic expressions of the variance components, functions and calculation of VPCs and PCVs is provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Results

Variance components

Tables 2 and 3 provide the model results predicting the physical health (Table 2) and mental health (Table 3) SF12 scores. Intersectional variation is higher for the mental health score where our strata account for approximately a quarter of variation in the mean scores for our middle-aged individuals (intercept VPC: 26.9%) and age trajectories (slope VPC: 22.6%).

In contrast, for physical health the proportion of intercept (mean score) and slope (age trajectory) variation explained by intersectional strata is lower at 10.2% and 6.1% respectively. This shows that our intersectional effects are likely to be much more influential to the overall mental health and functioning of individuals and how this develops over time than they will be to physical health. The results do indicate a large share of variability in outcomes lies within each intersection, between individuals and/or across occasions. Thus, the differences between individuals in any given stratum exceed the mean differences across strata, see Supplementary Figures S1 and S2 for VPC and variance plots including all three levels.

Plotting the stratum VPC function by age from the unadjusted model further demonstrates the differences between outcomes (Figure 1). The variance function plot is provided in Supplementary Figure S3. The overall higher proportion of stratum-level variation for mental health in comparison to physical health is evident. Figure 1 also reveals that the proportion of overall variation situated between strata is higher at both younger and older ages for mental health. In contrast, we see that younger ages (around 20 to 30 years old) show the lowest proportions of stratum-level variation for physical health with this increasing as persons transition through middle age (30 to 60) before plateauing for older ages.

Model 2, where we include the stratum defining components and interactions of these with age in the fixed portion of the model, allows us to evaluate the degree to which additive effects versus multiplicative effects account for variation in the mean health and age trajectories. The majority of the intersectional effects we identify are composed of additive effects. For physical health our model additions explain 92.0% of the intercept variance and 76.5% of the slope variance. The PCVs for mental health are higher at 98.0% and 86.2% for the intercepts and slopes respectively. Figure 1 additionally shows the percentage of variability across intersections which is non-additive (that is due to interactions) by age. It is apparent that interaction or multiplicative effects are likely to be more prominent for physical health, particularly so at younger ages where for example around 30% of variation is nonadditive at age 20. Mental health sees a slight increase in the proportion of intersectional variation which is non-additive from around 60 years of age, reaching around 10% nonadditive effects. Therefore, whilst the intersectional variation we identify in Model 1 is acting on a largely universal basis, with little variation across the other axes of strata identities, there is a suggestion of interactive intersectional effects and that these may vary in importance over the lifecourse.

Predicted stratum effects

Figure 2 shows the predicted stratum effects from Model 2 (adjusted for age, stratum components and age by component interactions) coloured by sex. The difference in the overall trajectories of physical and mental health is stark. For physical health the.

intersectional strata show a clear downward trajectory throughout the lifecourse where the decline accelerates as individuals approach later life. In contrast, mental health shows an overall slightly positive trend over the lifecourse, but the generational effects are prominent in defining clusters of strata and show varied trends (Supplementary Figure S4 shows the generational clusters). For example, the youngest generations (Generations Z and Y) show generally declining mental health through time with strata defined by the youngest generation showing consistently worse mental health scores than Generation Y at comparable ages.

Where we can compare across cohorts at the same age points there is a tendency for older generations to show better mental health than their younger generation counterparts.

Generation X also shows a declining trend, but this is shallower than for Generation Y or Z.

The strata group defined by the Baby Boomer cohort (ages from around 50 to 75) show flatter trajectories through time, suggesting that this group consistently maintains their mental health functioning for the ages monitored. Finally, the strata containing those of the Silent Generation start the survey period with relatively good mental health functioning, but this group then declines on an accelerating curve as they enter very old age (80 plus years).

Figure 2 also highlights there is a more evident sex effect for mental than physical health whereby the scores for females are consistently worse within the generational cohort groupings. In comparison this tendency is less clear for physical health where the lines for males and females across different strata groups overlap considerably along the age spectrum.

We see this trend reflecting in the additive main effects where the gap between females and males for the middle-aged reference person is -1.921 in mental health score compared with -1.177 in physical health score.

The predicted stratum effects coloured by ethnicity are shown in Figure 3 (plots coloured by the remaining stratum components (generation, highest qualification, highest socioeconomic class) are provided in the Supplementary Materials). Here again we can identify differences between physical and mental health in how the effect of ethnicity plays out between strata. For physical health there is a tendency for groups containing White individuals to have generally better physical functioning than stratum defined by ethnic minorities, especially those with Asian identities. This can be seen as a reflection of the dominant main effects, where we see a trend for minority ethnicities to have worse physical health scores than White individuals, ranging from -1.084 for Mixed ethnicities, to -2.591 for Asian individuals for the reference 50-year-old. In contrast, for mental health the ethnicity patterning shows distinct clusters, where each ethnicity tends to appear in two distinct bands.

This corresponds to combinations of ethnicity with sex, highlighting the strength of the sex effect where males consistently show better mental health than females.

Examining the ethnicity by sex strata groupings in isolation (Supplementary Figures . where generation also appears to matter. For the youngest cohorts (Generations Z and Y), the Asian males and Asian females typically have better mental health scores than the respective male and female strata composed of White individuals. For Generation X strata the Asian and White groupings are overlapping (still maintaining the overall sex difference) and then for Baby Boomers the two ethnicities begin to diverge so that White strata start to show better mental health. This patterning for Baby Boomers would align with a cumulative disadvantage hypothesis where the more marginalised groups show accelerated declines in health. There is clearly heterogeneity in how intersectional inequalities may be expected to play out for different cohorts and through the lifecourse.

Of note for both physical and mental health is that most strata present for the oldest generational cohort (Silent Generation) are defined by White individuals. Supplementary Figure S11 examines the degree to which our strata our fully populated shown by generation, sex and ethnicity. It highlights the stark underrepresentation for minority ethnicities for the Silent Generation cohort, particularly so for females. This could be a true representation of the composition of the Silent Generation, potentially due to lower levels of immigration in the pre-war period. However, there may also be an aspect of differential selection into the study, or it could relate to intersectionally patterned attrition or survivorship.

For the socioeconomic components of the intersectional strata (highest educational qualification and socioeconomic class, Supplementary Figures S5 and S6) we see some evidence of a gradient by status for physical health. The highest status strata (those with the highest qualifications and professional or managerial positions) tend to maintain better health scores throughout the lifecourse. Whereas, for mental health there is no clear patterning by status. These trends are reflective of the additive main effects we identify where the gradients along socioeconomic dimensions for physical health are more pronounced and statistically significant. For example, for a reference middle-aged person, those with no qualifications are expected to have physical functioning that is -1.975 points lower than those with a degree level qualification, whilst for those with A level/GCSE or other qualifications this difference is -1.068.

Correlations

The overall correlation between the intersectional stratum intercepts (overall mean health scores) for physical and mental health is 0.36 in Model 1. Thus, intersections with generally worse physical health tend to also have worse mental health but only moderately so. The correlation between the stratum slopes (age trajectories) is much weaker at just -0.06, meaning there is a very little consistency between the two dimensions of health in how they develop over time. However, it is important to consider how the overall correlation between the stratum effects for physical and mental health may vary by age. Figure 4 demonstrates how the correlation varies as we consider 20-year age slices from 20 to 80 years old. It shows that the correlation increases as we consider older individuals, from -0.17 for 20-year-olds to 0.76 for adults in their 80s. Therefore, we expect physical and mental health outcomes to become more aligned with each other as people age.

Additionally, we can consider those strata which show particularly divergent effects between their physical and mental health outcomes. Here we will focus on the intercept correlations (evaluated for the age-centred 50-year-old) from Model 1, as they dominate the overall stratum effects, and we consider outlying or divergent strata to be those where the difference between the physical and mental health score is 2 or more times the standard deviation of the average difference between the two outcomes. This equates to a difference of around 6.10 points or more between intercepts. Taking these criteria, we see that most divergent strata are from the youngest generations (Generation Z (11 out of 17, 65%) and Generation Y (4 out of 17, 24%) and these are showing worse mental health than physical.

Additionally, we see around half (9 out of 17, 53%) of the divergent strata feature females of either White or Mixed ethnicities. Therefore, we see many divergent cases where White or Mixed ethnicity females from the youngest generations are showing particularly worse mental health in comparison with their physical health.

Discussion

This study used a longitudinal MAIHDA analysis to explore intersectional variation and trajectories in physical and mental health functioning scores for adults in the UK. We found significant and substantial variation in individual outcomes between our intersectional strata. This showcases the continuing importance of intersectionality as a means to avoid the 'tyranny of averages' and the inefficient provision of interventions based on group averages of singular disadvantage metrics (Merlo et al., 2017; Merlo et al., 2019). For instance, intersectional inequalities accounted for 27% of variation in mental health scores for middleaged individuals and 10% for physical health. By the grading scale suggested by Fisk et al.

(2018) for social epidemiological applications, this would qualify our results as "good" to "very good" in terms of the potency of our intersectional strata. In contrast, Bell et al., (2024), in their study of General Health Questionnaire (GHQ) responses (a mental health metric), reported that only 9% of the outcome variation for similarly middle-aged individuals (48.13 years). This contrast may in part be due to differences between our two mental health measures, with the SF-12 potentially covering a broader view of quality of life status and with a wider range of potential values (0-36 for GHQ compared with 0-100 for SF-12).

Therefore, our strata constitute a potentially important social context by which to delineate (dis)advantaged groups in terms of their health outcomes and it is likely worthwhile for policymakers to have greater consideration for multiple dimensions of identity in aiming to tackle inequalities.

It is clear from our results that the expression of intersectional inequalities is highly heterogeneous, with no single inequality model applicable for all strata or at all ages. We found that the importance of intersectional inequalities to physical health was less potent at younger ages, but more consistent through the rest of the lifecourse, showing some agreement with a persistent inequality hypothesis. In contrast, the proportion of variation in mental health attributable to our intersectional strata increased for the oldest ages. Widening inequalities into old age could align with a cumulative disadvantage hypothesis (Brown et al., 2016), whereby those in already advantaged positions are able to accumulate greater resources or socioeconomic capital to mitigate health risks, whilst already disadvantaged groups suffer an exacerbation of risks and deleterious exposures as they age. We found that intersectional inequalities in mental health may also be especially pronounced among the younger aged participants. This could reflect a particular vulnerability, or sensitive period, at this life stage (Rod et al., 2025). Key transitions, such as those away from family environments, or into the labour market could represent important moments where intersectional differentials in social and resource capital could impact mental resilience.

The complexity of our results demonstrates the useful knowledge that can be gained from lifecourse intersectionality research using MAIHDA. It can highlight periods of heightened vulnerability for disadvantaged groups to aid with tackling inequalities. The increased inequalities at older ages (from around 60 years old) suggests that working to achieve healthy ageing for all should be a target for policymakers, particularly considering ageing populations in the UK and around the world (ONS, 2018; Sadana et al., 2016).

Additionally, our comparison between physical and mental health showcased how the strata predictions can be used to identify divergent cases which may be prudent for further investigation, especially as ill health on one dimension may negatively impact on the other.

For example, it appears that some intersectional groups (White females) from the youngest generations may be suffering particularly in their mental health in comparison to physical health. Addressing the mental wellbeing of young adults appears prudent as the time between 18 and 29 is now considered an important developmental period (Arnett et al., 2014). Poor mental health during this time may not only have knock-on effects for a greater burden of later mental health, as well as physical illness through comorbidities (McCloughen et al., 2012; Wienand et al., 2024), but impede successful life achievements in terms of education, relationships and occupational life which are then detrimental to later inequalities (Gustavson et al., 2018).

The MAIHDA approach also revealed that most of the intersectional effects operate on an additive basis, meaning that, for example, inequalities according to sex were operating similarly across different categories of ethnicity or socioeconomic status. Whilst the conceptual language of intersectionality prioritises the terminology of 'interaction(s)', the finding of dominant additive effects does not discount the importance of intersectionality to our outcomes (Evans et al., 2024b). Furthermore, our longitudinal analysis revealed that nonadditive effects may vary in prominence depending on an individual's age. Therefore, it is vitally important to incorporate time as a further dimension of heterogeneity in studies of how health inequalities develop and are maintained. In other words, the results here encourage the use of more bespoke or precision-targeted interventions, such as proportionate universalism (Marmot, 2010), to improve physical and mental health outcomes.

Overall, it is evident that longitudinal MAIHDA techniques offer a promising avenue to expand lifecourse intersectionality research. This study did not extend into a deeper study of the potential mechanisms behind any identified inequalities, offering instead an initial mapping to highlight where critical timepoints and marginalised groups may exist in overall physical and mental health functioning. Opportunities for further analysis also exist in ways to push the MAIHDA methodology forward. For example, here we considered two outcomes but ran separate models as we found the combination of random slopes with bivariate models too computationally burdensome for this dataset. However, extensions to bivariate or multivariate models (Prior et al., 2021) could be possible and would provide opportunities for more direct comparisons across outcomes in terms of the operation of intersectional effects.

Similarly, the incorporation of lagged dependent variables could be uses to assess the relevant importance of state vs. trait effects including how this also potentially differs by intersectional identities (Steele, 2008). In this study we considered our intersectional strata as fixed entities, focusing on how health developed over the lifecourse for those singularly defined groupings. However, adjustments to the MAIHDA multilevel set-up to allow shifts in social belonging over time, analogous to students changing schools for example (Heck et al., 2022), would allow more nuanced understandings of intersectional inequalities. It could reveal groups whose social mobility is stymied for example. Researchers should therefore continue to progress the work of lifecourse intersectionality through these promising techniques.
