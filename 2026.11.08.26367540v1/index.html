How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background: Extreme heat is an increasingly pressing public health threat, particularly in South Asia. Yet the temperatures at which mortality risk begins to increase in Indian cities remain poorly characterized. Operational heat wave definitions are based on meteorological criteria and may not reflect epidemiologically relevant temperature thresholds. Widely used distributed lag non-linear models are designed to flexibly estimate expected mortality as a function of temperature, but do not directly target the onset of excess risk from heat, a quantity central to early warning and planning.

Introduction

Extreme heat events are among the most pressing public health challenges of the twenty-first century, particularly due to projected increases in their frequency, duration, and intensity (5). South Asia, notably India, faces rising risks from extreme heat, as seen in severe episodes in 2010, 2015, and 2022, which led to thousands of excess deaths (4; 7; 16). A ten-city study across varied agroclimatological zones in India reported a 14.7% increase in daily all-cause mortality associated with recorded temperatures above the 97th percentile for two or more consecutive days (6).

A key challenge in the study of the health effects of heatwaves is the varying criteria used to define heatwaves: absolute or relative daily maximum temperature thresholds exceeding a predetermined duration (12). The India Meteorological Department (IMD) uses climatological thresholds, including daily maximum temperatures above 40 °C in inland cities or significant (4.5-6.4 °C) departures from historical temperature trends for several consecutive days (18). These definitions serve operational forecasting but may not fully capture population health effects (21). Research shows mortality-based heat thresholds in Europe, North America, and East Asia are often lower than such operational standards (13; 19; 8; 23). Indian evidence agrees: the ten-city mortality study found significant mortality risk accumulates well before heat events qualify under IMD's absolute temperature criteria (6). Health-based thresholds may therefore inform timely warnings and help institutions respond to heat-associated health impacts.

Contemporary epidemiological analyses use Minimum Mortality Temperature (MMT), defined as the temperature at which mortality risk is lowest, as a core metric for heat-related mortality thresholds and adaptation tracking (9; 36). Distributed lag non-linear models (DLNMs) relate daily mortality to temperatures on the same and preceding days, adjusting for seasonal trends. The MMT is derived from the cumulative exposure-response curve (estimated from a DLNM) by aggregating lagged effects (10), corresponding to a counterfactual of constant temperature over consecutive days rather than the observed lag distribution.

MMT research has motivated mortality-based heatwave definitions and yielded U-or J-shaped temperature-mortality profiles informative for policy (22). In persistently warm climates, however, relatively constant risk across wide temperature ranges, sharp increases only at high temperatures, and the need to collapse multi-lag relationships into single-temperature summaries make MMTs difficult to estimate and potentially unrepresentative of typical exposure patterns. Flexible DLNMs may also capture residual seasonal variation not fully removed by the time trend, distorting the estimated curve and producing an apparent cold effect when truly cold days are rare.

These limitations are acute in India. We analyze mortality data for Ahmedabad, Delhi, Chennai, Kolkata, and Mumbai, five of India's seven most populous cities, over periods of 4 to 10 years depending on data availability. These tropical and subtropical cities experience predominantly warm to very hot conditions with few cold days, where flexible models may produce noisy temperature-mortality curves, making MMT difficult to estimate and potentially ill-defined.

Here, we introduce the heat-risk onset temperature (HOT): the temperature at which the mortality risk curve begins to rise above baseline, directly targeting the onset of excess risk for early warning in warm-climate settings. Estimating HOT poses two challenges: residual seasonal variation can distort the temperature-mortality relationship, and conventional approaches often require extensive lag structures inconsistent with the short duration of heat effects on mortality (33; 20). To address these limitations, we propose a structured model in which the temperature-mortality association is constant up to an unknown threshold and increases thereafter. By focusing on a single, well-defined exposure, HOT is directly interpretable, as it does not require aggregating temperature effects across multiple days and corresponds to observable temperature conditions. This approach reduces sensitivity to residual seasonal confounding and enables stable identification of the temperature at which risk begins to rise.

Threshold-based models have been proposed and applied in earlier temperature-mortality studies (3; 14; 25; 19).

Although they have largely been replaced by more flexible approaches such as DLNMs, their simpler structure can be advantageous in settings with short and noisy time series, such as those considered here. Our approach retains the core modeling strategy underlying DLNMs, separating smooth seasonal and long-term variation from temperature-associated risk, but replaces the flexible temperature-mortality function with a structured threshold-based parameterization.

Establishing epidemiologically grounded temperature thresholds addresses an urgent governance gap with direct impact on population health in India. Heat Action Plans (HAPs), the primary governmental heatwave response framework, rely on IMD thresholds as triggers for coordinated emergency response measures. HAPs lack local, evidence-based threshold calibration, potentially delaying protective measures during heatwaves (30). The generation of more granular, health-based thresholds, as is the case with the HOT estimates in this study, can directly inform the recalibration of HAP trigger thresholds (29; 2).

1 Methods

1.1 Data

We obtained city-level daily mortality counts from municipal health departments or vital registration systems. Due to variation in record-keeping practices and data access, the study period differed by city: Ahmedabad (2001-2010), Chennai (2012-2017), Delhi (2011-2015), Kolkata (2011-2018), and Mumbai (2011-2015). Based on information from news reports, we removed periods coinciding with major non-heat-related shocks (such as epidemics, floods, or earthquakes) to avoid confounding with heat (Supplementary Table S1). Death registration completeness in the urban areas of the states/union territories containing these five cities is well over 90%, supporting confidence in the representativeness of the mortality data used in this study (28).

We used daily maximum temperature from the India Meteorological Department's 1º ! 1º gridded dataset , selecting for each city the grid cell closest to the urban core with continuous coverage (Supplementary Figure S1).

Maximum rather than minimum temperature was used as it better explains heat-wave-associated mortality (13).

1.2 Motivation

We applied a distributed lag non-linear model (DLNM) framework to assess its suitability in our setting: short time series from warm climates with limited cold exposure. Following standard practice, long-term and seasonal trends were modeled using natural cubic splines, with the number of degrees of freedom per year selected based on exploratory analysis (three for Ahmedabad, Chennai, Delhi, and Kolkata, and four for Mumbai). The temperature-mortality association was modeled using a distributed lag structure, with lag-specific effects parameterized by natural cubic splines (four equally spaced knots in the observed temperature distribution) and summarized as a cumulative effect across lags. The resulting curves showed sharp risk increases at high temperatures and mild increases at low temperatures (Figure 1). Furthermore note that for Chennai and Mumbai the curves moves up and down in the 25-30ºC range, challenging epidemiological expectations. In addition, for Ahmedabad and Kolkata, the relative risk estimate exceeds 1.2 in the 20-25ºC range, which are mild/comfortable conditions. To assess whether the estimated temperature-mortality curves reflect underlying structure or overfitting by the DLNM, we examined residuals from a model that adjusted for long-term and seasonal patterns but excluded the temperature effect. The residuals do not support a full U-or J-shaped relationship. Instead, they are consistent with a simpler pattern: mortality remains approximately constant across most temperatures and increases only beyond a high-temperature threshold, with a smooth, accelerating rise at higher temperatures (Supplementary Figure S2).

We examined the structure of temperature across lags and its relationship with mortality to better understand the role of the lag structure. A two-dimensional smoother of residual mortality against temperatures at lags t and t → 1 showed little distinct variation along either dimension when conditioning on the other, consistent with strong collinearity across lags up to 7 days (Supplementary Figures S3, S4-S8). To identify the most informative lag, we fit loess smoothers to mortality residuals at each lag and compared residual error; model fit with lag 1 consistently explained more variability than lag 0 and lags 2-7 (Supplementary Figures S9-S13, S14). After accounting for lag 1 temperature, residuals showed no systematic association with remaining lags (Supplementary Figures S15-S19), confirming that the model fit with lag 1 captures most of the relevant temperature signal.

We also examined the added explanatory power of humidity on all-cause mortality after accounting for temperature. Exploratory analysis did not reveal a statistically independent effect of humidity in the cities studied (Supplementary Section 2.1).

These observations motivate a more structured model that directly targets the epidemiologic quantity of interest: the temperature at which mortality risk begins to rise above its baseline. In the next subsection, we propose a structured threshold-based model that parameterizes the temperature-mortality association using temperature at lag 1, assuming the relationship is approximately constant up to an unknown threshold and increases thereafter.

1.3 Statistical Model

In temperature-mortality time-series analyses, daily death counts are typically modeled through overdispersed Poisson regression. In DLNMs, the temperature effect is modeled as a sum of lagged contributions: allowing temperatures on previous days to influence current mortality.

Both g(t) and the s l (x t→l )s are specified using natural cubic splines (10). The function g(t) is assigned a fixed number of degrees of freedom per year to control for long-term and seasonal variation, while the s l (x t→l )s are defined using spline bases with knots placed at quantiles of the temperature distribution.

Motivated by the approximately flat temperature-mortality relationship at moderate temperatures, the sharp increase at high temperatures, and the strong correlation among lagged temperatures described in Section 1.2, we replaced the flexible representation used by DLNMs with a structured parameterization that directly targets the onset of heat-related excess mortality. This specification uses temperature at lag 1, x t→1 , motivated by exploratory data analysis indicating that a single lag captures most of the relevant variation in mortality risk. Under this model, the temperature-mortality association is constant for x t→1 ↓ ϑ and increases smoothly for x t→1 > ϑ, with larger ε values indicating greater sensitivity to heat above the threshold.

Note that the threshold parameter ϑ renders the model non-linear. To enable standard optimization algorithms, we approximate s(x t ) with a smooth differentiable (Supplementary Section 2.2.1). Degrees of freedom for g(t) were similarly selected by visual inspection to capture seasonal patterns without absorbing short-term heat-related mortality signals (Supplementary Section 2.2.4). Given these model components, excess mortality for any set of days A is defined as t↑A (Y t → µ t ), where µ t = exp{ĝ(t)} is the estimated baseline in the absence of heat effects. Days above the estimated HOT are further classified into low, moderate, and high risk categories based on s(x t ), corresponding to increases in expected mortality of less than 5%, between 5% and 10%, and at least 10%, respectively (Supplementary Section 2.2.5. A simulation study based on Chennai data confirmed that the structured threshold model improves the bias-variance tradeoff relative to existing approaches (Supplementary Section 2.3).

2 Results

2.1 Heat-risk onset temperature estimates

We estimated the heat-risk onset temperature (HOT) for five Indian cities, along with the corresponding temperature-mortality relationships, through a model specification that uses lag 1 temperature as predictor, x t→1 (Figure 2).

This model choice was motivated by exploratory data analysis indicating that a single one day lagged temperature captured most of the relevant variation in mortality risk . Model fit was assessed using Pearson residual Q-Q plots (Supplementary Figure S21), which which were consisted with model assumptions. Estimated HOT values were 37.1 °C (95% CI: 36.5-37.7) in Ahmedabad, 35.5 °C (35.0-36.1) in Chennai, 36.5 °C (35.4-37.7) in Delhi, 33.5 °C (31.2-35.8) in Kolkata, and 35.1 °C (34.3-35.8) in Mumbai. Estimated HOT values occurred within the upper range of the observed temperature distributions in each city.

Across cities, estimated HOT values were close to or below 37 °C and lower than the operational heat wave thresholds defined by the India Meteorological Department (IMD), which specify 37 °C for coastal cities (Chennai and Mumbai) and 40 °C for inland cities (Ahmedabad, Delhi, Kolkata).

At higher temperatures, mortality risk increased across cities. The magnitude of this increase varied, with the largest estimated effect in Ahmedabad ( ε = 0.0135, 95% CI: 0.0102-0.0167), followed by Mumbai ( ε = 0.0128, 0.0045-0.0211). Chennai showed a moderate increase ( ε = 0.0089, 0.0073-0.0106), while smaller estimated effects were observed in Delhi ( ε = 0.0037, 0.0025-0.0049) and Kolkata ( ε = 0.0059, 0.0038-0.0114).

Based on the periods studied, we estimate annual excess deaths of 1,469, 1,369, 773, 580, and 411 for Chennai, Delhi, Ahmedabad, Kolkata, and Mumbai, respectively. 3 Discussion

We used a structured threshold model to estimate the temperature at which heat-related mortality risk rises above seasonal levels in five Indian cities. In all cities, the estimated heat-risk onset temperatures (HOTs) were lower than the operational heat wave thresholds used by the India Meteorological Department (IMD) (18). This implies that under current definitions, heat wave warnings based on maximum temperature are not issued until after mortality risk is already elevated. This is consistent with Indian and international evidence showing significant mortality risk accumulating well below IMD's absolute temperature thresholds (21; 26; 27; 35).

Excess mortality increased with temperature in most cities. Higher-intensity heat days (defined as > 10% increased mortality risk above baseline), which are less common, accounted for a larger share of the excess mortality. This pattern appeared in all five cities, except Kolkata and Mumbai where extreme temperatures were less frequent compared to the others. Although overall excess mortality was lower in Kolkata and Mumbai, the relative increase in mortality at higher temperatures remained consistent across cities. Notably, the range of HOTs across cities is within 4 °C, raising the possibility of a common temperature threshold range at which heat stress begins to drive heat-related mortality in a homogeneous population. Disentangling climate, acclimatization, and vulnerability contributions requires study across diverse cities.

These findings make a case for revisiting India's heatwave declaration thresholds, as these thresholds are significantly higher than the HOTs estimated. While current IMD thresholds may have operational advantages given the clear delineation of thresholds between inland, coastal, and hilly areas, there is a strong case for recalibrating these thresholds using epidemiological evidence. Heat warning systems designed around meteorological convention rather than observed mortality risk are likely to leave populations exposed and systematically under-protect the population during dangerous early-phase heat exposure. Emerging evidence shows that the microenvironments where those most at risk live, work, and sleep are hotter than recorded meteorological temperatures (15; 32; 24), a gap that may have widened in recent decades with growing urban density, heat island effects, and changes in construction practices.

Balancing epidemiological accuracy with operational manageability is a concern, however, with considerations of alert fatigue and state capacity to respond to alerts being of greatest relevance to policymakers (29). The tiered risk categorization of low, moderate, and high risk days above the HOT developed in this study offers one practical resolution.

A graduated response ladder would allow government capacity to scale with risk level. Triggering public warning systems only at moderate or high risk levels would also ameliorate concerns of alert fatigue, as would, when feasible, calibrating warning thresholds to specific population risk groups.

The implications of these findings extend beyond national threshold-setting to the broader architecture of heat governance in India. The HOT estimates reported in this study provide the kind of city-specific evidence that can guide the development and implementation of tiered HAP responses contextualized for local conditions; a gap in heat policy in India currently (30). We note that the model used in our analysis did not include covariates to account for potential effect modifiers such as air pollution, age structure, or socioeconomic vulnerability. However the model can be readily adapted to incorporate these. The aggregated daily counts of death used in this study are all-cause totals, precluding disaggregation by age or sex. Systemic challenges with cause-of-death coding precludes disaggregation by specific causes.

Humidity was excluded from the final model, as exploratory analysis did not reveal a statistically independent effect on all-cause mortality in the cities studied (Supplementary Section 2.1), consistent with findings from multi-country analyses (11). This absence of an effect should not be interpreted as evidence that no association exists; the relatively short time series and all-cause mortality outcome likely limited statistical power to detect a humidity effect. Furthermore, the present analysis focuses on mortality and does not address the likely morbidity burden associated with combined heat and humidity exposure.

This study is part of a small but growing body of locally grounded heat epidemiology in India. The approach underlying this analysis, with mortality data obtained from municipalities, could feasibly be replicated across other cities in India.

Extending this analytical framework across other cities, and strengthening the linkages between epidemiology and heat policy should be priorities for research and policy communities. Our results demonstrate that epidemiologically derived thresholds are feasible and informative across Indian cities. Aligning heat warning thresholds with observed mortality risk is a concrete, actionable step toward more effective heat-health warning systems, requiring collaboration between the epidemiological research community, the IMD, and disaster management authorities (1).
