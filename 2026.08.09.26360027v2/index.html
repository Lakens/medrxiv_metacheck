Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. Abstract Background

Blood biomarkers are increasingly used to support the diagnosis and monitoring of neurodegenerative diseases. However, their interpretation is complicated by physiological determinants, including age, sex, body-mass index, and renal function, and by differences in absolute concentrations between analytical methods. We aimed to develop population-based reference equations allowing individualized interpretation of selected blood biomarkers blood biomarkers used in neurology.

Methods

In this cross-sectional study, we analysed plasma samples from cognitively unimpaired participants selected from the French CONSTANCES and Three-City population-based cohorts. Generalized additive models for location, scale, and shape were used to model neurofilament light chain (NfL), glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), phosphorylated tau 181 (p-tau181), amyloid-β40, amyloid-β42, and their ratios according to age, sex, body-mass index, and renal function. The resulting equations provided individualized expected concentrations, percentiles, and Z-scores. Previously established disease-specific concentrations were converted into Z-score. Cross-calibration equations were developed for NfL measurements across analytical methods and sample matrices.

Findings

The final reference populations comprised 5123 participants for amyloid biomarkers and p-tau181 and 5122 for NfL and GFAP; median age was 52.3 years and 49.7% were women.

Between ages 40 and 80 years, expected NfL and GFAP concentrations increased by an average of 2.6% and 2.2% per year, respectively. Renal function, body-mass index, and sex had additional biomarker-specific effects. Application of the equations to clinical cohorts Background Biological biomarkers represent a major asset in medical decision-making, as they represent quantifiable and reproducible elements contributing not only to the diagnosis of pathologies but also to risk assessment, prognosis, and therapeutic choice (1). Among biological fluids, blood is the most widely used and accessible medium for biological analysis, carrying a broad range of circulating proteins that reflect systemic physiological and pathological processes. In neurodegenerative and neuroinflammatory diseases, such as Alzheimer's disease (AD), metabolites and proteins associated with pathological processes in the central nervous system (CNS) can cross the blood-brain barrier and reach the bloodstream, allowing their use as blood-based biomarkers.

The detection of CNS-derived biomarkers in blood has historically been challenging because their concentrations are substantially lower than those observed in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), mainly due to dilution. The emergence of ultrasensitive approaches, such as SIMOA (2), has opened new avenues for the detection of CNS biomarkers in the blood. After an initial phase of validation, the demonstration that blood-based biomarkers could match the clinical performance of CSF biomarkers initiated a major transformation in the diagnosis of neurological diseases and was accompanied by new assays on clinical routine analyzers, whose sensitivity has been improved.

For example, the biological diagnosis of AD, which relies on the detection of pathologically relevant amyloid-beta (Aβ) peptides and phosphorylated tau (p-tau) proteins in the CSF, is rapidly shifting toward blood-based biomarkers (3) (4) (5) (6). Detection of the astrocytic glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), as a marker of astrocytic activation and damage, is moving to the blood (7) (8) (9). Importantly, plasma GFAP has shown stronger associations with ADrelated amyloid pathology than CSF GFAP (10), whereas CSF GFAP has demonstrated limited diagnostic performance across dementia syndromes (11). Neurofilament light chain protein (NfL), which is released upon axonal damage of various origins, is another example of a blood-based biomarker (12, 13), with in vitro diagnostic (IVD)-certified methods now available in routine lab settings. Thus, we observed an exponential and rapid development in the use of these biomarkers for the biological diagnosis of neurological diseases.

The interpretation of blood-based biomarkers remains complex (12), as their values are affected by several physiological factors, such as age, sex, body mass index (BMI) and renal function (14). Thus, covariate-adjusted reference values are needed to guide interpretation, and to distinguish normal from abnormal changes. Several studies have developed these reference values, mostly for NfL and GFAP. For NfL, age has been consistently associated with higher concentrations (9, (15) (16) (17) (18) (19) (20), while other studies also reported an inverse association with BMI (9, 20). Renal function may represent another important determinant, with lower estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) associated with higher NfL concentrations reported (9). Similar age-related increase has been reported for GFAP, while lower eGFR, lower BMI, and female sex have also been associated with higher concentrations, even though the results are not consistent across studies (9, 15, 16). Importantly, most available reference data for NfL and GFAP were generated using Simoa-based assays, which may limit their applicability across analytical platforms.

In addition, reference values for other biomarkers are still limited, notably for AD-related biomarkers, including Aβ and p-tau. Indeed, their interpretation are now well-established in a pathological context. For example, in AD and related pathologies, Aβ42 and the Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio generally decrease, whereas p-tau tend to increase (18). However, among cognitively healthy participants without underlying pathologies, the change of these biomarkers is yet to be characterized. Cooper et al. reported that, among Canadian participants aged 3-79 years, the Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio showed a slight decrease with age, whereas p-tau181 followed a Ushaped association, with concentrations decreasing during childhood and increasing in later adulthood cooper (16). Accounting for other physiological determinants is needed for these biomarkers, as previous studies have shown that plasma levels of Aβ proteins and p-tau tend to increase with impaired renal function and decrease at higher levels of BMI (21, 22).

In this study, we developed reference values for a selection of relevant blood biomarkers of neurodegenerative diseases, including Aβ and p-tau proteins, NFL and GFAP using a large population-based cohort, measured with Roche Elecsys assay. We accounted for the effects of age, sex, BMI, and renal function, which are known to influence blood biomarker concentrations to varying degrees depending on the analyte (23). We also determined the optimal thresholds for normalized biomarkers using different IVD assays to detect AD, ALS, and MS. And finally, we provided cross-calibration equations to facilitate harmonization across different analytical platforms and biological fluids. The final goal is to provide the community with accessible equations and reference plots for direct clinical use, thereby improving the quality, interpretation, and performance of blood-based biomarkers for patient management in neurodegenerative and neurological disorders.

METHODS

Study design and populations

This cross-sectional study included participants from two French population-based cohorts, CONSTANCES and 3C. Ethical approval for the CONSTANCES study was obtained from the French Data Protection Authority (CNIL) and the INSERM Institutional Review Board, while the 3C study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the University Hospital of Kremlin-Bicêtre (No. 99-28). CONSTANCES is a nationwide cohort of over 200,000 adults aged 18-69 years at inclusion, randomly selected from the French National Pension Insurance Fund between 2012 and 2019 (24). The participants underwent standardized health examinations at 21 health screening centers and were followed up with periodic visits and annual questionnaires. Since 2018, biological samples have been collected and stored in the CONSTANCES biobank (25).

The 3C study enrolled 9,294 community-dwelling adults aged ≥ 65 years from Bordeaux, Dijon, and Montpellier between 1999 and 2001 (26). Participants underwent cognitive assessments, clinical examinations, and blood sampling at baseline and longitudinal followup.

Biomarker measurements were performed within the INTERVAL project in participants selected from two population-based cohorts: CONSTANCES, which includes more than 200,000 participants, and 3C. Given the logistical and financial constraints of measuring biomarkers in the entire cohorts, participants were randomly selected using stratified sampling based on age, sex, and BMI to ensure adequate representation across these major physiological determinants of biomarker concentrations. In CONSTANCES, 4,890 participants were included using 24 sampling strata, with additional inclusion of participants with impaired renal function (eGFR <60 mL/min per 1.73 m²) outside the stratified sampling to ensure adequate representation of this subgroup. In 3C, 483 participants were sampled across 36 strata. In CONSTANCES, a target of 200 participants was randomly selected per stratum, or all eligible participants when fewer than 200 were available, with additional inclusion of participants with impaired renal function. Consequently, selection probabilities varied across strata. Inverse probability weighting was applied to account for the sampling design and non-response.

For this analysis, we included all the participants from the INTERVAL project after excluding those with neurodegenerative diseases or substantial cognitive impairment (defined as a Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) score <24) at the time of blood sampling. This conventional threshold was chosen to exclude potentially pathological cognitive impairment while preserving the population-based nature of the reference sample. Covariates included age, sex (coded1=female,0=male), BMI, and markers of renal function (creatinine in µmol/L), estimated using the 2021 CKD-EPI creatinine equation (27).

Datasets for clinical thresholds of normalized biomarkers

The ALZAN cohort, which was used to compute the AD thresholds, has been published (28).

Briefly, it includes 423 participants who consulted memory clinics for cognitive complaints. The demographic data are presented in Supplementary Table 3. Only patients with available age, sex, BMI, and creatinine levels were included to compute Z-scores. NfL and GFAP values were originally quantified in serum using Roche Elecsys and converted to plasmaequivalent values (see Supplementary Table 2). The Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS) was evaluated at withdrawal, and disease activity corresponded to either a recent relapse (<3 months) or new magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) lesions.

Biochemical analysis

For biomarker measurement, blood aliquots were thawed at +4°C, gently homogenized, and centrifuged for 5 min at 2000 × g before analysis. One aliquot from each participant in each cohort was used for p-tau181, Aβ42, Aβ40, GFAP, and NfL measurements using Elecsys® assays on the e402 Cobas analyzer, as described elsewhere (32). Plasma measurements were performed across 56 analytical runs between April and November 2024, using the same reagent lot for each analyte and internal quality controls in each run. Between-run variability was evaluated as detailed in the Supplementary Methods. Intra-and inter-run coefficients of variation (CVs) were evaluated using plasma pools of clinical samples analyzed within a single run and across consecutive assay runs. For p-tau181, the intra-and inter-assay CVs were 2.5% and 3.7%, respectively, with an LLOQ of 0.30 pg/mL, respectively. For Aβ40, the corresponding values were 0.9%, 7.1%, and 10.0 pg/mL; for Aβ42, 3.2%, 4.5%, and 0.668 pg/mL; for GFAP, 1.63%, 2.93%, and 2.85 pg/mL; and for NfL, 1.92%, 9.70%, and 0.5 pg/mL. The AD, ALS and MS cohort samples were analysed using the same assays (3, 6, 7, 33). NfL and GFAP values were quantified in serum using Roche Elecsys and converted to plasma-equivalent values using cross-calibration, showing excellent agreement across platforms (ICC>0.9) (Supplementary Table 2).

Impact of covariables

Relative covariate-related percentage changes were estimated using the fitted median (Z-score=0) biomarker values for women relative to men for each additional 1 year between 40 and 80 years, each unit increase in BMI between 20 and 40 kg/m², and 5 units increase in creatinine between 50 and 120 µmol/L. The 40-80-year interval was selected to provide a standardized summary of age-related changes across biomarkers. As age effects may be nonlinear, these average annual changes should not be interpreted as constant rates across the full age range covered by the reference curves.

Statistical analyses

Population-based reference distributions were estimated using inverse probability weighted (IPW) generalized additive models for location, scale, and shape (GAMLSS) (34). These models allow the location, dispersion, skewness, and kurtosis of biomarker distributions to vary according to individual characteristics and are therefore well-suited for the derivation of age-and covariate-specific reference values.

Inverse probability weights were used to account for the sampling design and non-response.

CONSTANCES participants received three weights (sampling cohort, sampling substudy, and nonresponse), whereas 3C participants received a rescaled weight based on national demographic distributions. The final weights were computed by multiplying the components.

Four distributions: Box-Cox Cole Green (BCCG), Box-Cox T (BCT), Box-Cox power exponential (BCPE) and normal distributions were tested, with BCT providing the lowest Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) for all the biomarkers. BCT includes four distribution parameters: median µ, scale parameter σ (approximation of the coefficient of variation), skewness v, and kurtosis τ. Creatinine and eGFR were compared as alternative markers of renal function using the AIC. Creatinine-based models provided a better fit for all biomarkers except the Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio and were therefore retained in the final models to ensure consistency across biomarkers. Two sets of candidate covariates were considered for each biomarker: Model 1 included age, sex, BMI, and creatinine, whereas Model 2 considered only age and sex. Within each model, covariates were retained according to their contribution to model fit, as determined by the predefined selection procedure. For µ, covariates were added incrementally to the model and retained if they significantly reduced the AIC. Additionally, cohort effects were tested. For the selected continuous variables, linear and fractional polynomial terms (degrees 1 or 2) were tested. The same selection procedure for variables as for µ was applied for σ and v. To avoid overfitting, τ was not regressed on the covariates. The estimated distribution parameters (μ, σ, ν, and τ) were used to calculate the adjusted percentiles and Z-scores through a four-step procedure, allowing the interpretation of an individual concentration relative to the expected distribution in the reference population.

Finally, age-specific reference curves were generated from the fitted models and plotted for selected Z-scores (0, 1, 1.5, and 2), stratified by creatinine concentrations (50, 80, and 100 µmol/L), BMI levels (20, 25, and 30 kg/m²), and sex. These values were selected for illustrative purposes: BMI values of 25 and 30 kg/m² correspond to the conventional thresholds for overweight and obesity, respectively, whereas creatinine concentrations of 50, 80, and 100 µmol/L represent different physiological profiles rather than established clinical cut-offs.

To evaluate the impact of physiological adjustment, we compared unadjusted Z-scores with Z-scores adjusted for age and sex (Model 2) and those additionally adjusted for BMI and creatinine (Model 1).. Linear regression models were then used to investigate the associations between diagnostic group and Z-score values for each approach. Disease-specific thresholds previously established in independent studies performed on the same analytical platforms were converted into Z-score thresholds using the reference equations developed in the present study.

Deming regression was performed to derive cross-calibration equations that allowed conversion between analytical platforms (35). This cross-calibration is important to convert biomarker measures performed on other fluids (serum vs. plasma) or with other analytical platforms before applying the equation. The Deming regression parameter λ was estimated using the ratio of x to y standard deviations (Sxx/Syy), where x and y correspond to those in the cross-calibration equations (36). The bootstrap method was used to estimate the 95% CI of the intercept and slope of the Deming regression equations. Agreement was assessed using intraclass coefficient (ICC) estimates and their 95% confidence intervals (CI) calculated based on single-rating, absolute agreement, and a 2-way random-effects model (ICC (2,1)) (37).

Statistical analyses were performed using R 4.4.1 statistical software (R packages: gamlss, pROC). Using these cross-calibration equations, the Z-score values were transformed to the Lumipulse IVD assay scale. Age-specific reference curves were then generated from the fitted models and plotted for selected Z-scores (0, 1, 1.5, and 2), stratified by creatinine concentrations (50, 80, and 100 µmol/L) for NfL levels. In addition, equations were used to calculate the Z-scores in the clinical cohorts. Linear regression models were fitted to assess the association between diagnosis and Z-scores. Finally, threshold values for the relevant biomarkers were estimated for each diagnosis by maximizing the Youden index.

RESULTS

Reference population

Of the 5,373 individuals initially considered, 134 were excluded at the time of blood sampling due to neurodegenerative diseases (n=64) or potential cognitive impairment (MMSE score <24; n=70). After further exclusions for missing data, values below the LLOQ, and outliers, the final reference populations comprised 5,123 and 5,122 cognitively unimpaired participants for amyloid biomarkers and p-tau, and for NfL and GFAP, respectively (Figure 1; Table 1).

After IPW, the median age was 52.3 years (Q1-Q3, 42.3-64.0). The weighted age distributions of the two analytical populations are presented in Supplementary Figure 2. Both distributions showed the highest weighted frequencies in middle-aged participants, with lower frequencies at older ages.

Reference values for the different blood biomarkers

The reference equations are presented in Supplementary Table 1. In multivariable Model 1, age, sex, and renal function were retained as determinants for all biomarkers, whereas BMI was retained for all biomarkers except the p-tau181/Aβ42 ratio. p-Tau181 is used as an example in Table 2 to illustrate the four-step procedure for calculating p-tau181 z-scores. The influence of physiological covariates on the absolute biomarker concentrations corresponding to a given Z-score is further illustrated for NfL in Supplementary Figure 1.

Impact of covariates on biomarker concentrations

Figures 2, 3 and 4 present age-dependent reference curves for the selected biomarkers, illustrating the influence of creatinine, BMI, and sex on their expected concentrations. P-tau181, the p-tau181/Aβ42 ratio, NfL, and GFAP increased with age, with a steeper increase observed around 50-60 years of age (respectively Figure 2C, 2D, 2E, 2F). The largest increases were observed for NfL and GFAP, with concentrations rising by 2.6% and 2.2% per year, respectively, between 40 and 80 years (Table 3). The Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio gradually declined with age (Figure 2B), with a 0.2% decrease per year. For Aβ42, only modest agerelated changes were observed (+0.3% per year) (Figure 2A). The age-dependent effects of physiological covariates on NfL are further illustrated in Supplementary Figures 1.

Regarding creatinine effects, higher creatinine levels were associated with higher concentrations across all biomarkers (Figure 2), with the largest effects observed for p-tau181 and NfL (+3.9% and +3.6%, respectively, per 5 µmol/L increase in creatinine). For BMI, an increased BMI was generally associated with lower biomarker concentrations (Figure 3), particularly for NfL (-1.6%) and GFAP (-1.3%) (Table 3), whereas its effect was more limited for amyloid biomarkers and p-tau181. Female sex was associated with higher expected concentrations across all biomarkers (Figure 4), with the strongest effects observed for GFAP (+16.6%), p-tau181 (+13.2%), and Aβ42 (+12.0%) (Table 3).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we established population-based reference values for five individual blood biomarkers of neurodegenerative diseases, including NfL, GFAP, Aβ40, Aβ42, p-tau181, and two biomarker ratios, Aβ42/Aβ40 and p-tau181/Aβ42. Using a large reference population comprising more than 5,000 cognitively unimpaired individuals' representative of the French population, we developed percentiles and Z-scores adjusted for major physiological determinants. To facilitate implementation in clinical and research settings, we provide both mathematical equations and graphical reference curves, allowing the direct conversion of biomarker concentrations into standardized scores. Their clinical relevance has been illustrated in independent cohorts, including AD, FTD, ALS, and MS. Importantly, although physiological adjustment modifies disease-control differences, this does not necessarily translate into improved diagnostic accuracy, which was not directly assessed in this comparison.

Population-based reference studies have increasingly extended beyond NfL. Cooper et al.

established age-specific reference intervals for Aβ42/Aβ40, p-tau181, NfL, and GFAP (16), while Rousset et al. developed multivariable reference intervals for NfL and GFAP (9). More recently, the large HUNT study characterized age-related plasma p-tau217 distributions and the prevalence of biomarker-defined AD pathology in 11,486 participants aged 58 years and older (38). Direct numerical comparisons of reference limits across studies remain challenging because of differences in analytical platforms, sample matrices, population characteristics, and covariate adjustment strategies. Our study complements these investigations by providing covariate-adjusted reference equations and individualized Zscores for five blood biomarkers and two ratios measured using Roche Elecsys assays. The covariate effects observed in our study were consistent with those reported in previous studies of biomarker reference values, which showed that NfL (9, 15-20), GFAP (9, 15, 16), p-tau181 (16) levels increased with age and with impaired levels of renal function marker and lower BMI. In addition, the annual percentage changes estimated in our study were within the range previously reported in the literature, with increases of approximately 3-4% per year for NfL (16, 19), 2% for GFAP (16), and approximately 3% for p-tau181 among individuals aged 60 years and older (16). We also observed consistently higher levels of these biomarkers among women compared with men. Our findings are consistent with those reported by Rousset et al. for GFAP, although other studies have not observed a significant association between sex and GFAP levels. This discrepancy may be attributable to differences in study populations, age distributions, analytical platforms, or other unmeasured biological or methodological factors. Physiological factors influence circulating biomarker concentrations to markedly different extents. NfL and GFAP, which are non-specific biomarkers to neurodegenerative disease, are the most influenced by age, while creatinine has strong influence on NfL and p-tau181. The inverse associations with BMI may partly reflect a blood-volume dilution effect, as previously proposed for NfL (14). Age-related changes may partly reflect alterations in biomarker clearance (39). The association with age remained significant, even after adjusting for renal function. However, renal function was assessed using creatinine, which is an imperfect surrogate of kidney function, particularly in older individuals, where creatinine levels are strongly influenced by muscle mass (40). Recent studies have highlighted the important influence of kidney function on blood-based neurological biomarkers (23), and our study confirmed and quantified this effect. However, the influence of renal function on biomarker concentrations does not necessarily translate into improved diagnostic accuracy after adjustment. Arslan et al. (41) reported that adding eGFR to models including plasma biomarkers, age, and sex did not improve amyloid-positivity discrimination, although their cohort predominantly included individuals with preserved or mildly impaired renal function. Sex-related differences were also observed across most biomarkers, with women generally exhibiting higher expected concentrations than men after adjusting for other covariates, particularly for GFAP, NfL, and p-tau181. Previous population-based reference studies have reported inconsistent sex effects ranging from no detectable differences across biomarkers (16), to higher GFAP concentrations in women (9), suggesting that these associations may depend on the population studied and the biomarker considered. The biological basis for this difference remains incompletely understood. The persistence of a sex effect after adjustment for BMI and renal function suggests that sex captures additional biological determinants that are not fully explained by these variables.

Population-specific differences in cumulative behavioural and health-related exposures may also contribute to the heterogeneity observed across studies.Consequently, sex appears to be an important component of individualized biomarker interpretations.

The influence of age, renal function, BMI, and sex was not uniform across all biomarkers. Aβ peptides, which have both central and peripheral origins, were less influenced by BMI.

Notably, Aβ42/Aβ40 and p-tau181/Aβ42 were less strongly influenced by these physiological determinants. This likely reflects the fact that both analytes composing the ratio are influenced in similar ways by these physiological factors, such that calculating the ratio tends to offset their effects (42) (43) (44).

The establishment of normative equations made it possible to express biomarker concentrations as standardized Z-scores rather than relying solely on absolute concentrations. Standardized Z-scores reduce physiological variability attributable to age, sex, BMI, and renal function, thereby improving the comparability across individuals. In addition, because all biomarkers are expressed in standard deviation units, Z-scores enable direct comparison of abnormalities across biomarkers, despite their different analytical scales and concentration ranges. Similar to pediatric growth charts or bone mineral density Z-scores, this framework expresses biomarker concentrations relative to the expected distribution for an individual with similar physiological characteristics. Z-scores also facilitate longitudinal monitoring by enabling changes to be interpreted relative to the expected biological variation. This normalization may improve diagnostic and prognostic assessments by distinguishing diseaserelated biomarker abnormalities from physiological variations. Individualized reference equations may be useful in several clinical settings. First, they may facilitate the interpretation of biomarker results in routine laboratory practice by providing standardized measures of abnormalities. Second, they may improve harmonization across clinical centers by reducing the variability attributable to differences in patient characteristics. Third, they may support patient selection, risk stratification, and treatment monitoring in the clinical trials. This approach may be especially valuable in older populations, where physiological increases in biomarker concentrations complicate the interpretation of the absolute values. The potential clinical benefit of these reference equations is likely biomarkerdependent. For NfL and GFAP, which are relatively nonspecific and strongly influenced by physiological factors, adjusted Z-scores may help identify elevations beyond those expected for an individual's physiological profile. In contrast, for AD-related biomarkers such as p-tau181, disease-associated changes may substantially exceed physiological variability, and established assay-specific diagnostic cut-offs validated against amyloid PET or CSF biomarkers should remain the primary basis for diagnostic classification. Adjusted Z-scores may provide complementary information, particularly for borderline values or patients with relevant comorbidities, but their additional diagnostic value remains to be established. Such individualized interpretation aligns with the broader movement toward precision medicine and may facilitate the integration of blood biomarkers into routine neurological care in the future.

Disease-associated biomarker profiles remained largely consistent after adjustment for physiological covariates, without clear evidence of improved diagnostic discrimination.

Nevertheless, adjusted Z-scores provide a standardized measure of biomarker deviations relative to the expected distribution for individuals with comparable physiological characteristics. Benkert et al. (20) demonstrated that age-and BMI-adjusted serum NfL Zscores improved the identification of disease activity and prognostication in MS compared with absolute concentration cutoffs. In our study, we found that accounting for creatinine and BMI may slightly attenuate NfL association with MS clinical phenotypes, underlying that part of the differences may be due to underlying comorbidities. However, overall patterns are consistent. Rather than masking disease-related biological differences, Z-scores enable biomarker abnormalities to be interpreted relative to the expected value for an individual with a similar age, sex, BMI, and renal function. However, physiological normalization does not confer disease specificity. Even biomarkers considered relatively specific for AD, such as p-tau181 and p-tau217, may be elevated in other neurological disorders, including Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease (45) and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (46) . As blood biomarkers are generally measured in patients with a clinical suspicion of neurological disease, Z-scores should be interpreted in the context of the clinical presentation and complementary diagnostic investigations, rather than considered as standalone evidence of AD.

In addition to individualized interpretation, this study also provides assay conversion equations between major analytical platforms, including Elecsys®, Lumipulse®, Ella®, and Simoa® assays. Although blood biomarkers are increasingly used in both research and clinical settings, substantial inter-method variability remains a major obstacle to their implementation. Absolute concentrations may differ considerably between assays, despite excellent correlations. Combined with assay conversion equations, expressing biomarker concentrations as percentiles or Z-scores relative to a common reference population provides a unified interpretative framework across the analytical platforms. Such harmonization could facilitate the comparison of results between studies, laboratories, and healthcare systems.

Standardized reference values may contribute to future international efforts aimed at biomarker standardization and regulatory implementation.

This study had several important strengths. First, it is based on a large reference population exceeding 5,000 participants, making it one of the largest normative studies of blood neurodegeneration biomarkers to date. Another strength of this study is the simultaneous evaluation of several clinically relevant biomarkers using a unified statistical framework.

Finally, the use of GAMLSS modeling allowed us to estimate not only the mean biomarker values but also the age-dependent changes in variability, skewness, and kurtosis, providing a more flexible representation of biomarker distributions than conventional linear approaches.

Limitation

This study has several limitations. First, the study population was predominantly composed of French individuals of European ancestry, and external validation in more diverse populations is necessary. Second, the analyses were based on cross-sectional data and therefore, cannot fully characterize within-subject biomarker trajectories over time. Third, only a single biomarker measurement was available for each participant, preventing the assessment of biological and analytical variability at the individual level. Fourthly, cognitive impairment was defined using an MMSE cut-off score of 24, a threshold intended to exclude substantial cognitive impairment while preserving the population-based nature of the reference sample.

Consequently, some participants with subtle cognitive impairment may have remained in the reference population.

In addition, relatively few participants were older than 85 years, which limited the precision of the reference estimates at the extreme upper end of the age distribution. Future studies should evaluate the performance of these equations in independent cohorts and assess their impact on diagnostic accuracy and clinical decision-making. The same methodological framework could also be extended to emerging biomarkers such as p-tau217, brain derived (BD) p-tau217, endogenously cleaved tau species from the microtubule-binding region (eMTBR), α-synuclein, and other markers of neurodegeneration and neuroinflammation. The integration of these equations into laboratory information systems, web-based calculators, and automated reporting tools may further facilitate their adoption in routine clinical practice.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we developed population-based reference values and standardized Z-scores for the principal blood biomarkers of neurodegenerative diseases. By accounting for age, sex, BMI, and renal function, this framework enables the individualized interpretation of biomarker concentrations and addresses important sources of physiological variability. The proposed framework may facilitate harmonized reporting across laboratories and analytical platforms and improve clinical interpretation by expressing biomarker abnormalities on a common standardized scale, enabling direct comparisons across patients, neurological diseases, and biomarkers.
