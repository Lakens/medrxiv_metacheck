Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. Abstract

Background: Zika virus local transmission has been reported in at least 97 countries and territories, but the sources, routes, and determinants of cross-country spread remain incompletely understood. We reconstructed a phylogeographic network, characterized source activity across epidemic phases, and examined factors associated with identified crosscountry transmission events.

We analyzed a time-scaled Nextstrain phylogeny of 1,043 Zika virus genomes collected between February 2013 and March 2026. Parent-child branches assigned to different locations were screened and consolidated into 81 sourcedestination-year events involving 39 locations. Network analysis characterized temporal and geographic patterns, routes, hubs, and communities. For the analysis of associated factors, 68 events with complete covariate data were compared, using mixed-effects models, with eligible year-matched control records for which no event was identified, with all 68 events retained and a new control set drawn in each of 200 iterations. Asia contributed the earliest event, from Thailand in 2012. Most events occurred within the Americas (79.0%) and were concentrated during 2014-2016, with Brazil as the principal source hub. All six events from 2019 onward originated from Thailand or India and reached destinations in Asia, Europe, and Africa. Thailand's source activity spanned 2012-2025. Event identification was positively associated with high source-country incidence (odds ratio [OR], 2.66; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.39-9.69), greater air travel (OR, 2.52; 95% CI, 1.58-5.67), and higher destination-country GNI per capita (OR, 1.70; 95% CI, 1.20-4.50). It was negatively associated with greater geographic distance (OR, 0.11; 95% CI, 0.02-0.20) and higher source-country GNI per capita (OR, 0.64; 95% CI, 0.45-0.94). Fourteen source countries showed elevated residual source propensities, with Brazil and Thailand among the highest.

The transmission network showed a shift in dominant source hubs, from early activity in Asia to the Americas during the 2014-2016 epidemic and back to Asia in recent years. High source-country incidence, greater air travel, and shorter distance can help identify country pairs requiring closer surveillance, alongside established routes in the Americas and persistent or emerging Asian sources, particularly as El Niño may increase vector-borne transmission potential in 2026-2027.

1 Introduction

Zika virus is transmitted primarily by Aedes mosquitoes and can cause congenital Zika syndrome and neurological complications. The 2015-2016 epidemic in the Americas brought Zika to global attention as a major public health threat, and transmission has continued at lower reported levels since the epidemic peak [1, 2]. The 2026 WHO update reported evidence of current or previous autochthonous transmission in 97 countries and territories [3].

Previous studies have examined how Zika virus can spread through environmental suitability, human mobility, and viral evolution. Environmental models have mapped ecological conditions conducive to transmission [4], while models combining air travel and vector distributions have assessed introduction and transmission potential in Africa and the Asia-Pacific region [5]. Genomic studies reconstructed the establishment and cryptic spread of Zika virus in Brazil and the Americas [6], demonstrated that the virus circulated undetected in several American locations for months before the first locally transmitted cases were confirmed [7], and identified multiple introductions into the United States [8]. Earlier and broader phylogeographic studies traced the long-term emergence and movement of Zika virus across Africa, Asia, the Pacific, and the Americas [9, 10].

Existing evidence has largely focused on transmission suitability, importation risk, or epidemic reconstruction in specific settings. What remains less clear is how cross-country viral spread is structured over longer periods, how countries differ in their source and destination roles, and how bilateral and country-level factors are associated with individual transmission events. Climatic anomalies add urgency to this question. Exceptionally warm conditions associated with the 2015 El Niño increased modeled suitability for Zika virus . transmission in South America [11]. In September 2026, El Niño was intensifying, with a greater than 90% probability of becoming very strong during the Northern Hemisphere fall and winter of 2026-2027 [12]. Identifying historical routes and their associated factors can therefore provide timely evidence for proactive surveillance.

Accordingly, we aimed to characterize cross-country Zika virus transmission patterns and associated factors to inform risk assessment and early warning in a changing climate. We used a Nextstrain time-scaled phylogeny and ancestral geographic-state annotations to identify phylogenetically inferred cross-country transmission events. Network analysis described when and where these events occurred and how they accumulated into routes, country-level hubs, and communities. The case-control analysis compared observed events with eligible country-pair-year controls to examine source, destination, and bilateral factors associated with event identification.

2 Methods

2.1 Identification of Phylogenetically Inferred Cross-Country Transmission Events

We conducted a country-level observational study that combined transmission-network analysis with a case-control design. On 17 May 2026, we downloaded a time-scaled phylogeny from the Nextstrain Zika page [13]. The Nextstrain Zika workflow draws primarily on publicly available viral genome sequences and metadata from NCBI GenBank, supplemented by curated sequence data from the United States Virgin Islands. The downloaded tree contained 1,043 viral genomes collected between February 2013 and March 2026.

. Internal-node country states were reconstructed in the Nextstrain workflow using maximum-likelihood discrete-trait inference implemented in TreeTime [14, 15]. Using R, we traversed directly connected parent-child branches and identified 131 candidate records of cross-country transmission in which the parent and child nodes were assigned to different countries or territories. When multiple geographic states were available for a node, the country or territory with the highest support was selected. Event year was based on the inferred branch date and could therefore precede the earliest genome sampling date. These candidate geographic transitions represent changes between inferred viral locations and are not counts of notified imported cases or epidemiologically confirmed transmission episodes.

The geographic annotations used by Nextstrain required additional consideration in the identification of cross-country transmission events. The Zika workflow derives geographic metadata primarily from NCBI GenBank and applies standardized geolocation rules and accession-level annotations. For known travel-associated sequences, Nextstrain aims to assign the likely country of infection rather than the country in which the sample was collected. However, when travel history is unavailable or has not been manually curated, a record may retain the sampling location reported in GenBank [13]. To reduce this potential misclassification, we applied geographic eligibility criteria based on WHO documentation of local mosquito-borne transmission through December 2025. Eleven records involving China, Japan, Australia, or Spain were excluded because these locations had imported cases but no documented local mosquito-borne transmission, leaving 120 eligible branches.

To limit the influence of uneven genome sampling and submission, we consolidated records with the same source, destination, and year. Multiple phylogenetic branches representing the same pathway in the same year may reflect the availability of several related genomes rather than independent transmission events. Consolidation removed 39 duplicate . records and yielded 81 country-pair-year transmission events for network analysis. Fig 1 shows the selection process and the locations of eligible branches within the phylogeny.

To assess robustness to uncertainty in ancestral geographic-state assignment, we additionally recorded the reconstructed-state probabilities at the parent and child nodes for each cross-country branch. Events were re-consolidated after retaining only branches for which both node probabilities were at least 0.80 or 0.95, and the resulting networks were compared with the primary analysis (S1 Data).

2.2 Network Construction and Description

Countries and territories were represented as nodes, and inferred transmission events from source to destination were represented as directed edges. The same directed country pair was aggregated across years into one edge, weighted by the number of its occurrences among the 81 event records. In-degree and out-degree were the numbers of distinct source and destination connections, respectively; total degree was their sum. In-strength and out-strength represented incoming and outgoing event counts, and total strength was their sum. Network density was calculated as the number of observed directed edges divided by all possible directed edges without self-loops. We also calculated reciprocity, clustering coefficient, weakly and strongly connected components, mean shortest path among reachable node pairs, and network diameter.

Source and destination events were summarized annually by continent. Following the United Nations M49 geographic classification, the Americas were further divided into South America, Central America, the Caribbean, and Northern America for the regional transmission matrix [16]. On the geographic network maps, node area was proportional to unweighted total degree, defined as the total number of distinct incoming and outgoing connections, irrespective of the number of events along each connection. Edge width was proportional to the number of country-year transmission events along each directed connection. Community structure was assessed using the Infomap algorithm on the directed network [17] and the Girvan-Newman edge-betweenness algorithm on the undirected projection of the network [18].

2.3 Candidate Factors and Analytical Sample

Country-level factors included source-country annual reported Zika incidence, GNI per capita in source and destination countries, the Global Health Security Index (GHSI), and the proportion of land with tropical climate. Annual counts of reported Zika cases were compiled from three source categories: (1) official surveillance and case-reporting data issued by the Pan American Health Organization, national health authorities, and other public health agencies; (2) published literature; and (3) event-based reports from ProMED, WHO Disease Outbreak News, and news media. The case counts were converted to annual reported incidence per 100,000 population, with annual population estimates from the World Bank [19]. GNI per capita, measured using the World Bank Atlas method, was obtained from the World Bank [19]. GHSI is a composite assessment of national capacities to prevent, detect, and respond to epidemic and pandemic threats. We used GHSI scores from the 2019 and 2021 editions, applying the 2019 scores through 2019 and the 2021 scores from 2020 onward [20]. Tropical climate coverage was calculated from the 1-km-resolution Köppen-Geiger raster published by Beck et al. [21] as the proportion of valid land pixels within each country or territory classified as Af (tropical rainforest), Am (tropical monsoon), or Aw (tropical savanna).

Bilateral factors included capital-to-capital geographic distance and annual air travel volume. Distance was represented by the capital-to-capital distance (km) variable from the CEPII Gravity Database [22]. Air travel volume was measured using OAG Traffic Analyser annual passenger-booking counts [23]. Because booking data were available only for 2025, we used 2025 values as a proxy for bilateral mobility throughout the study period. This approach was based on previous findings that international air travel patterns are relatively stable across years, including during the 2015-2016 Zika epidemic, with substantial deviations occurring only during exceptional global disruptions such as the COVID-19 pandemic [24].

The analysis of associated factors was restricted to events with complete covariate data.

Thirteen events involving Martinique, Guadeloupe, Saint Barthélemy, French Guiana, the United States Virgin Islands, or American Samoa were excluded because covariate data were unavailable for these locations. The final modeling dataset therefore included 68 observed events involving 31 destination countries and territories. These 68 events were a completecovariate subset of the 81 events used to construct the network and served as cases in the analysis of associated factors.

2.4 Negative-Control Sampling and Model Estimation

The network analysis summarized routes and country-level hubs, whereas the analysis of associated factors compared identified events with eligible controls at the source-destinationyear level. The 68 identified events were treated as positive records (cases), whereas negative records (controls) were sampled from eligible source-destination combinations for which no event was identified in the corresponding year. Candidate sources were drawn from the 97 countries and territories listed in the WHO 2026 update [3] as having evidence of current or previous autochthonous Zika virus transmission and were required to have reported at least one Zika case in the corresponding year. Candidate destinations were restricted to the 31 countries and territories represented among the 68 positive events to reduce potential bias arising from geographic differences in genomic surveillance and sequence reporting.

Negative-control sampling excluded within-location pairs and any source-destination-year combination already identified as a positive event.

From this candidate pool, year-matched negative records (controls) were sampled at a 1:5 case-to-control ratio. In each iteration, all 68 positive events (cases) were retained, and 340 controls were selected by simple random sampling without replacement. The full candidate pool was restored between iterations, and the sampling procedure was repeated 200 times to assess the stability of estimates across alternative control samples.

We fitted a generalized linear mixed-effects model with a binomial distribution, logit link, and crossed random intercepts for source and destination locations. The random intercepts accounted for residual heterogeneity in each location's propensity to act as a transmission source or destination after adjustment for the fixed effects, as well as the nonindependence of records sharing the same source or destination. The model was specified as: where P ijt denotes the conditional probability that a cross-country transmission event was identified from source location i to destination location j in year t.

The fixed effects comprise bilateral (X ijt ), source (Z it ), and destination (W jt ) covariates; β 0 is the fixed intercept; β, γ, and δ are the corresponding coefficient vectors; and u i and v j are random intercepts for source and destination locations, respectively.

To reduce right-skewness, annual air travel volume was transformed as ln(x+1), with 1 added to accommodate records with zero travel volume, while GNI per capita for source and destination locations was natural-log transformed. All continuous covariates were subsequently standardized as Z scores (mean 0 and standard deviation 1) to place them on a common scale. Annual reported Zika incidence in the source location was categorized as low (<5 cases per 100,000 population), medium (5-100 cases per 100,000, including both cutoffs), or high (>100 cases per 100,000), with the low-incidence group as the reference.

Odds ratios (ORs) for continuous covariates therefore represent the change in odds associated with a one-standard-deviation increase in the corresponding variable, on the transformed scale where applicable.

Collinearity among candidate covariates was assessed using pairwise Pearson correlation coefficients and variance inflation factors (VIFs). When collinear covariates were identified, variables were selected for inclusion in the final model based on data coverage and epidemiological relevance. For each fixed effect, coefficients were exponentiated to ORs in each of the 200 modeling iterations, and the results were summarized using the median OR and empirical 95% CIs defined by the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles. Associations were considered statistically significant when the empirical 95% CI excluded the null value (OR = 1). Random intercepts were exponentiated to express each location's residual effect on the odds relative to a random-intercept value of zero. Analyses were conducted in R version 4.5.1, and generalized linear mixed-effects models were fitted using the lme4 package [25].

3 Results

3.1 Phylogeographic Network of Cross-Country Zika Virus Spread

3.1.1 Overall Network Structure

We identified 81 phylogenetically inferred cross-country events involving 39 countries and territories. After recurring source-destination connections observed in different years were aggregated, these events represented 65 unique directed routes (Fig 2). Most routes were temporally limited: 51 were observed in only one year, 12 in two years, and two in three years. Brazil-to-United States and Nicaragua-to-Honduras were the only routes observed in three separate years. Network density was 0.044, meaning that only 4.4% of all possible directed connections among the 39 locations were observed. Despite this global sparsity, the mean shortest-path length among location pairs connected by a directed path was 2.49, indicating that reachable destinations were typically connected to sources within two to three cross-country transmission steps. Transmission events showed marked temporal concentration (Fig 3). 4 Discussion

This study reconstructed more than a decade of phylogenetically inferred cross-country Zika virus spread and examined factors associated with identification of individual transmission events. Three findings stand out. First, source activity varied across epidemic phases. Asia contributed the earliest inferred event through Thailand in 2012; Brazil and the wider Americas dominated the high-volume 2014-2016 epidemic; and all events identified from 2019 onward originated from Thailand or India in Asia. Second, the network was highly concentrated: it was sparse and predominantly directional, most routes appeared in only one year, and a small number of source hubs and recurrent routes carried much of the activity.

Third, at the event level, transmission events were more likely to be identified when sourcecountry incidence was high, bilateral air travel was substantial, and geographic distance was short; source-country random effects were also concordant with the principal hubs identified in the network.

4.1 Phase-Specific Source Patterns and Shifting Hubs

Cross-country Zika virus transmission events were concentrated in the Americas during 2014-2016, with South America serving as the main source region and the Caribbean receiving the largest number of incoming events. This pattern is consistent with genomic evidence on the establishment and rapid spread of Zika virus in the Americas [6]. Bayesian phylogeographic analyses further reconstructed the underlying directed movements: after its establishment in northeast Brazil, the virus reached Haiti around mid-2014 and subsequently Colombia, the Dominican Republic, and other American countries [33, 34]. Genomic analyses have also shown that the virus circulated undetected in several locations before locally transmitted cases were first reported [7], underscoring how case reports may lag behind actual transmission. By applying a consistent country-pair-year unit, our analysis describes a historical network in which intensive within-Americas transmission was connected to a smaller number of intercontinental routes. This event-based network approach follows frameworks developed for other viruses, in which global influenza spread was represented as a network of seeding events between regions [31] and viral genetic data were combined with human transportation data to explain dissemination [32].

Brazil was the leading source node, the United States was predominantly a destination node, and several Caribbean countries and territories played both incoming and outgoing roles. Genomic evidence of multiple introductions into the United States [8] supports the observed multi-source pattern. The dense Caribbean connections in our network further indicate that the region served not only as a destination but also as an intermediate hub for onward transmission.

Asia was not a newly emerging source after 2019. Thailand generated the earliest inferred source event in the network in 2012, followed by the inferred French Polynesia-to-Brazil event in 2013. This sequence is consistent with the broad movement of the Asian lineage through the Pacific to the Americas described in phylogeographic research [9, 10], although unsampled locations or transmission steps may lie between the inferred events. The post-2019 pattern instead represents a renewed concentration of identified source activity in Asia after the epidemic peak in the Americas. All six events identified from 2019 onward originated from Thailand or India, linking Asia to Europe and Africa as well as destinations within Asia. India's emergence as a recent source is consistent with documented outbreaks since 2018 caused by Asian-lineage viruses and with evidence of continued circulation across multiple Indian states [26, 27]. Independent surveillance evidence supports Thailand's persistent source role: nationwide testing in 2016-2017 confirmed widespread focal transmission across 29 of 76 provinces, and time-resolved phylogenetic analysis indicated that Zika virus had circulated in Thailand at a low but sustained level since at least 2002, with viral diversity approaching endemic patterns [28]. A comprehensive phylogenomic analysis likewise identified Thailand as a crucial hub for Zika virus spread across Asia [36].

4.2 Factors Associated With Identified Cross-Country Transmission Events

The analysis of associated factors complements the network analysis by examining the conditions associated with the individual transmission events that collectively produced the observed routes and hubs. Annual air travel volume was positively associated with event identification, whereas geographic distance was negatively associated, highlighting the joint roles of human mobility and spatial proximity. These findings provide empirical support for early travel-based risk models, which anticipated that international air movement could introduce Zika virus into vector-suitable areas [5].

High source-country incidence was significantly associated with an identified crosscountry transmission event, whereas medium incidence did not differ significantly from low incidence. This pattern suggests a possible threshold effect: increases within the low-tomedium range may not markedly change exportation risk, but once incidence exceeds a higher threshold, the number of infections available for export, and thus the probability that a cross-country transmission event is identified, may rise substantially. These categories do not define a precise biological threshold, but they suggest that rapid escalation into the highincidence range is a useful signal for surveillance in destination countries.

Two mechanisms could underlie such a threshold pattern. First, the pool of viremic travelers grows approximately with the product of source-country incidence and travel volume, so the probability that at least one infection is exported may follow a saturating function of pool size, remaining low while the pool is small and then rising steeply once incidence enters the high range. Second, reported incidence partly reflects reporting propensity: countries experiencing recognized outbreaks intensify case finding and sequencing, which raises both recorded incidence and the likelihood that an exported infection is detected and genetically linked to its source. These mechanisms cannot be separated with the present design, but they yield a testable prediction: if the threshold reflects a true biological process, exportation probability should rise steeply within a defined incidence range once continuous case counts and dense traveler sampling become available; if it is purely an artifact of reporting propensity, the pattern should attenuate once reporting effort is held constant.

Tropical climate coverage in source and destination countries was not significantly associated with transmission. This null result may reflect the study design. Both candidate pools were restricted: source countries were required to have current or previous local transmission, and destination countries were limited to those represented among the positive events. Broad ecological suitability was therefore already common across the sampled countries, and tropical climate coverage varied little within the analysis sample. Moreover, a static measure cannot capture year-specific variation in climatic suitability. This finding therefore does not imply that climate is unimportant, but rather that a time-invariant national measure with limited within-sample variation is insufficient for detecting climatic effects. A previous study projected that longer-term climate change would redistribute the geographic and seasonal risk of Aedes-borne viruses [29], and with a very strong El Niño forecast for 2026-2027 [12], near-term climatic influences on Zika virus transmission warrant close attention.

Source-country GNI per capita was negatively associated with transmission events, whereas destination-country GNI per capita was positively associated. At the source, lower national income is often accompanied by poorer housing, greater vector exposure, and less intensive vector control, which can increase local transmission intensity and thus the pool of infections available for export. At the destination, higher income reflects not only stronger international travel connections but also greater diagnostic and genomic surveillance capacity, so events involving wealthier destinations may be both more likely to occur and more likely to be detected. The source-side association is consistent with an ecological assessment in which lower regional GDP per capita predicted greater Zika virus transmission potential [35].

4.3 Study Contributions and Limitations

This study integrates phylogenetic evidence, network analysis, and a case-control mixed model within a single framework. The two analytical components address complementary levels: network metrics aggregate identified transmission events to characterize routes and hubs, whereas the mixed model estimates factors associated with identification of those events and captures residual source and destination propensity. Together, they connect historical reconstruction with prospective risk alerts and help identify surveillance priorities.

The study has several limitations. The analysis was conditioned on a single published tree, and uncertainty in the tree topology itself was not propagated. Ancestral geographic-state assignments vary in confidence across the tree. To assess robustness to this uncertainty, we re-consolidated events from branches whose parent-and child-node state probabilities were both at least 0.80 or 0.95 (S1 Data): 60 of 81 events (74.1%) were retained at 0.80 and 53 (65.4%) at 0.95. Events with lower assignment confidence were concentrated among deep nodes of the 2014-2016 expansion in the Americas, whereas all six events from 2019 onward were retained at the 0.80 threshold and four of the six at 0.95. The eight leading source countries and the phase-specific pattern-dominance of the Americas during 2014-2016 and Asian origin of all identified post-2019 events-were unchanged at both thresholds, indicating that the main conclusions are robust to geographic-assignment uncertainty.

Public genome coverage varied across countries and years, and the analysis was limited to samples collected and made publicly available from February 2013 onward, which may have affected event detection and network structure [30]. Terminal-node country annotations may represent either a curated infection location or, when travel history was unavailable or had not been manually reviewed, the sampling location reported in GenBank. Excluding records involving countries without documented local mosquito-borne transmission reduced the likelihood that uncorrected imported cases were interpreted as transmission locations, although it may not have eliminated all such misclassification. Coverage and reported incidence alike were shaped by surveillance and sequencing intensity, which were uneven across countries and declined after the 2015-2016 epidemic [2]. Even at the epidemic peak, the majority of infections in the Americas were not reported, with estimated reporting probabilities varying substantially by year [37, 38]. The concentration of identified events in Asia after 2019 may consequently reflect, in part, where surveillance and sequencing remained most active rather than the complete absence of transmission elsewhere. Thailand's prominence is consistent with its comparatively strong arbovirus surveillance and genomic capacity [28], which makes it both a genuine source and a sentinel through which wider circulation across Southeast Asia becomes visible. The events should therefore be interpreted as phylogenetically inferred geographic transitions rather than as evidence that transmission did not occur between other country pairs, and observed events may condense longer chains whose intermediate stops were never sampled.

Finally, the factor analysis has its own limitations. Negative-control sampling was restricted to the candidate pools, so any residual bias from unmeasured factors influencing pool membership would be toward the null. Covariate measurement adds two further constraints: 2025 air travel data served as a proxy for mobility during the study period, and the climate covariates, defined as fixed climate-type proportions, did not vary over the study period, so climatic effects could be identified only from cross-country contrasts. Future studies should expand genome coverage and incorporate annual mobility, time-varying climate variables, and vector-density measurements to evaluate the main associations and improve the precision of risk alerts.

5 Conclusions

Source activity varied markedly across the study period. Asia contributed the earliest inferred event through Thailand in 2012; intensive within-Americas activity dominated the 2014-2016 epidemic, with Brazil as the principal source; and all events identified from 2019 onward originated from Thailand or India in Asia. The events forming the network were concentrated along a small number of routes and were more likely to be identified when source-country incidence was high, bilateral air travel was substantial, and geographic distance was short. Fourteen source countries retained elevated residual source propensities after adjustment, including the principal network hubs Brazil and Thailand, indicating that the measured factors partly, but not fully, accounted for their prominence. Surveillance should continue monitoring established routes in the Americas while prioritizing persistent and.

emerging Asian sources, issuing alerts when source-country incidence enters the highincidence range, and strengthening case detection and genomic data sharing across these routes, particularly in resource-constrained settings.
