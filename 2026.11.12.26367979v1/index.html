How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

Abstract

Background. The American Heart Association defined Cardiovascular-Kidney-Metabolic (CKM) syndrome in 2023. Census-tract studies often examined one condition without spatial modeling. It is unclear where combined Stage 1-2 burden concentrates and how it relates to aging, environment, and socioeconomic conditions.

Methods. We built a CKM burden index for 70,336 tracts from five CDC PLACES crude prevalences (obesity, diabetes, hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, chronic kidney disease) and compared it with NHANES 2017-2020. In 69,530 contiguous-U.S. tracts, we fit Bayesian spatial (BYM2) models with demographic, socioeconomic, and environmental predictors and aging × environment interactions, plus descriptive machine learning. The primary model excluded behavioral measures sharing the outcome's BRFSS source; a second added them. Credible intervals (CrIs) are conditional on PLACES estimates.

Results. Agreement with NHANES was modest. Burden clustered spatially (Moran's I = 0.657) and highest in the South. Behavioral measures had the largest machine-learning importance (52.6%), partly from shared sourcing. Each percentage-point increase in adults ≥65 years was associated with 0.112 units higher burden (95% CrI 0.111-0.113) outside low-income, lowaccess (LILA) tracts. LILA tracts had 0.10-0.14 units higher burden from the 25th aging percentile but not consistently in the youngest decile. Aging and socioeconomic associations held across sensitivity analyses. The primary model's residual Southern excess approached zero after adding behavioral measures, which may reflect shared measurement, real differences in these behaviors, or both.

Conclusions. CKM burden is spatially clustered and highest in the South. LILA tracts had modestly higher burden above the youngest tracts. These cross-sectional estimates support area-level surveillance and needs assessment.

What Are the Clinical Implications?

1. Introduction

In October 2023, the American Heart Association issued a presidential advisory that recast the conditions of obesity, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, hypertension, chronic kidney disease (CKD), and atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease into a single integrated term: Cardiovascular-Kidney-Metabolic (CKM) syndrome 1 . The advisory introduced a four-stage framework, from Stage 0 (no risk factors) through Stage 1 (excess or dysfunctional adiposity), Stage 2 (metabolic risk factors and/or moderate-to-high-risk CKD), Stage 3 (subclinical cardiovascular disease or very-high-risk CKD), and Stage 4 (clinical cardiovascular disease, including kidney failure subcategory 4b), and called for action on the social determinants of health that pattern progression through these stages. Because the intensity of monitoring and treatment escalates across these stages, identifying which populations and places carry the greatest CKM burden is central to directing screening, prevention, and management.

Population aging, structural disadvantage, and environmental exposure might shape the geography of CKM burden. For instance, adults aged ≥65 years make up about 16.8% of the U.S. population, and the prevalence of every CKM component rises steeply with age 2, 3 . The unequal geography of structural disadvantage is captured by Krieger's Index of Concentration at the Extremes (ICE) 4 , whose association with cardiometabolic outcomes persists after individuallevel adjustment 5, 6 . Long-term ambient air pollution is associated with cardiopulmonary mortality 7 , summarized in an AHA scientific statement 8 , and with chronic kidney disease and diabetes 9, 10 ; prospective evidence links pollutant mixtures to the onset and progression of cardio-renal-metabolic multimorbidity itself 11 . The nearest prior nationwide tract-level analysis compared seven composite neighborhood indices against median household income as predictors of coronary heart disease, stroke, and chronic kidney disease, with obesity, hypertension, and diabetes as secondary outcomes, and found similar associations 12 . Two of those outcomes, coronary heart disease and stroke, are Stage 4 clinical endpoints in the AHA framework. However, where the combined Stage 1-2 CKM burden that precedes them concentrates, once spatial dependence is modeled, has not been described nationally at the census-tract level. It is also unknown whether this burden is highest in tracts whose populations are older and more exposed to environmental hazards.

We construct a continuous tract-level index of the AHA CKM Stage 1-2 burden using CDC PLACES estimates of the five core cardiometabolic indicators and compare it against National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 2017-2020 individual-level co-occurrence patterns. Then, we relate the index to demographic, socioeconomic, and environmental predictors in a hierarchical Bayesian BYM2 spatial regression, cross-checked by ordinary least squares with residual-autocorrelation diagnostics, and in an XGBoost/SHAP machine-learning model ( §S2.4). All formal inferential claims rest on the spatial regression models; the machinelearning model is exploratory and is not used to test population-level hypotheses.

We address four aims: 1.

Construction and external comparison. Construct a tract-level CKM burden index that summarizes the prevalence of five conditions in the AHA Stage 1-2 framework and characterize its agreement with independent individual-level NHANES estimates. 2. Correlate decomposition. Quantify the relative contribution of demographic, socioeconomic, and environmental predictors to the spatial distribution of CKM burden using inference from the spatial regression and exploratory decomposition from the machine-learning model. 3. Aging × environment interaction. Examine whether associations between neighborhood environmental characteristics and CKM burden differ according to the proportion of residents aged ≥65 years. 4. Behavioral-block contrast. Quantify how much of the socioeconomic-concentration signal is carried by the co-reported behavioral and healthcare-engagement block, by comparing an otherwise identical pair of spatial models fit with and without that block.

2. Methods

This is a cross-sectional, ecological analysis of U.S. census tracts using publicly available, deidentified secondary data; it did not constitute human-subjects research requiring institutional review board approval. Dr Batobara had full access to all the data in the study and takes responsibility for its integrity and the data analysis. Reporting follows the STROBE and RECORD guidelines (Supplemental Methods). The outcome is a continuous tract-level CKM burden index: the first principal component of five crude, non-age-standardized CDC PLACES 2022 tract prevalences (obesity, diabetes, hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, and self-reported chronic kidney disease), oriented so that higher values denote greater burden 13 . We used the 2022 release because the 2023 release has no Florida estimates for measures based on 2021 BRFSS data and the 2024 release no longer includes chronic kidney disease. Agreement with independent individual-level data was assessed against NHANES 2017-2020 14 ( §3.1).

We grouped the predictors into four blocks. Table S1a lists the source, year, spatial resolution, and units of each variable. The demographic block includes the percentage of adults aged ≥65 years and the percentage of Hispanic or Latino residents. The socioeconomic block includes Krieger's Index of Concentration at the Extremes for race and for income, the percentage of adults with a bachelor's degree or higher, and rurality from USDA Rural-Urban Commuting Area codes 15 . The environmental block includes air pollution (PM₂.₅ and ozone, from the EPA Downscaler), warm-season heat (mean daily maximum temperature from May to September, from PRISM 16 ), walkability (EPA National Walkability Index 17 ), and food access. For food access, we used the USDA flag for low-income, low-access (LILA) tracts (1-mile/10-mile definition), because it requires both low income and poor access to food. The behavioral and healthcareengagement block includes four more PLACES measures: physical inactivity, smoking, routine checkups, and lack of health insurance. Supplemental Methods explains how we assigned the air pollution and temperature values to each tract.

Our main analysis used a hierarchical Bayesian spatial regression. Each tract had a random effect with a scaled BYM2 prior, which lets tract effects be spatially structured, unstructured, or a mix of both. Neighbors were each tract's 8 nearest tracts (k = 8). We fit the models by integrated nested Laplace approximation (INLA) with penalized-complexity priors. Continuous predictors in interaction terms were mean-centered.

We report each association as a posterior mean with a 95% credible interval (CrI) and call it credible when the interval excludes zero. When a result combines several coefficients, such as the LILA contrast at a given age composition, we computed it from the joint posterior rather than from the separate intervals. The CrIs are conditional on the PLACES estimates; that is, they treat those estimates as fixed. They do not include the uncertainty of the PLACES model itself, whose shared county-and state-level random effects make errors correlated across tracts ( §S3.7).

We fit two models on the same tracts. Model 1, the primary model, excludes the behavioral and healthcare-engagement block; Model 2 includes it. The two models answer different questions and comparing them is one of our aims. Model 1 is primary because the behavioral measures come from the same BRFSS survey cycles as the outcome, so adjusting for them may remove part of the associations we want to study. Several demographic and socioeconomic predictors are also inputs to the PLACES model ( §4.5), but we keep them because they are the structure under study, whereas the behavioral measures are candidate mediators.

We used ordinary least squares, with checks for residual spatial autocorrelation, and eigenvector spatial filtering as cross-checks. We also fit a tuned XGBoost model with SHAP values, which we report descriptively only. To test the shape of the aging association, we refit Model 1 with a natural spline for % age ≥65 years (df = 4, with df = 3 and 5 as sensitivity checks; §S2.4) and compared the fits by log marginal likelihood ( §3.3; Table S13). We also refit Model 1 with food-store counts from the National Neighborhood Data Archive 18 in place of the LILA flag ( §S3.10). Supplemental Methods describes the sensitivity analyses, and their results appear in Supplemental Results (Tables S8-S10 and S14) and Supplemental Discussion (Table S12). All analyses used R 4.6.0 with the sf, spdep, spmoran, R-INLA, and xgboost packages.

3. Results

>Results

3.1 Sample, index construction, and comparison with NHANES

Figure S13 shows how we built the analytic sample. Joining CDC PLACES with the American Community Survey (ACS) 19 gave 72,335 tracts. We excluded New Jersey, whose tracts lack PLACES estimates for hypertension and high cholesterol, which left 70,336 tracts for building the CKM burden index. Then, we excluded Alaska, Hawaii, and tracts with missing covariates, which left 69,530 tracts (Table 1). We used this sample for both the OLS baseline ( §3.2) and the BYM2 regression ( §3.3). The first principal component (PC1) explained 76.1% of the variance in the five indicators, and all five loadings were positive and similar in size (range 0.36-0.49; Table 2).

We compared PLACES with individual-level NHANES 2017-2020 data (Table 2, Figure 1; Supplemental Results). National prevalences were similar for diabetes, hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, and chronic kidney disease, differing by 0.30 to 2.23 percentage points. Obesity was 8.95 percentage points lower in PLACES, as expected, because BRFSS relies on selfreported height and weight, whereas NHANES measures them. The relationships among the indicators agreed less well. When we compared the ten pairwise correlations in PLACES with those in NHANES, they agreed only weakly (r = 0.37), and the PC1 loadings agreed even less (r = 0.16). This is consistent with how PLACES estimates are produced: the five indicators are much more correlated in PLACES (mean |r| = 0.69) than in NHANES (mean |r| = 0.16), and all ten pairs are more correlated in PLACES (Supplemental Note 2).

3.2 OLS baseline and global Moran's I diagnostics

The baseline OLS model included only the demographic and socioeconomic predictors (n = 69,530) and explained 72.4% of the variance. Older neighborhoods had higher burden, and both ICE measures had the expected protective (negative) sign. When the behavioral and healthcare-engagement block is added, both ICE coefficients change sign; §3.3 examines this change. Supplemental Results reports the coefficients and the collinearity and normality checks (Figures S11-S12). Figure 2 maps % adults aged ≥65 years, ICE_Income, and ICE_Race.

CKM burden was clustered in space. Global Moran's I was 0.657 (p < .001) across the 69,854 contiguous-U.S. tracts with a burden index (Figure S13), using row-standardized weights based on each tract's 8 nearest neighbors. Burden was highest across the U.S. South, including the Mississippi Delta, the Black Belt, Appalachia, and the Rio Grande Valley. It was lowest in the Mountain West, the Pacific Northwest, and the urban core of the Northeast (Figure 3). The OLS residuals were also clustered (Moran's I = 0.470, p < .001; Figure S1): observed burden was higher than the model predicted in South Texas, the Mississippi Delta, and Appalachia. This remaining clustering is why we used a model with spatial random effects.

Supplemental Results (Figures S7-S10) reports local clusters (LISA), a correlogram by lag order, and robust Lagrange multiplier tests. Both robust tests, for spatial lag and for spatial error, were significant, suggesting that both types of spatial dependence are present.

3.3 BYM2 fit, ESF cross-check, and residual absorption

We fit the BYM2 model on all 69,530 tracts; no further tracts were excluded. Log marginal likelihood was higher for Model 2 (-20,271 vs -73,551; Table S15), but we do not use this difference to choose between the models, because Model 2 includes predictors that come from the same BRFSS data as the outcome ( §S2.4; §4.5). Table 3 gives the estimates for both models, Figure 4 compares them, and Table S2 gives the OLS and ESF cross-check estimates for the Model 2 predictor set.

Demography. Population aging was the strongest demographic predictor. In the XGBoost model, it accounted for 19.3% of the total SHAP contribution, second only to physical inactivity ( §S3.4). In the BYM2 models, its association was positive in both models. In Model 1, each 1percentage-point increase in adults aged ≥65 years was associated with 0.112 units higher burden (95% CrI 0.111-0.113) in non-LILA tracts at average levels of the other exposures. This is a linear average: a natural spline for aging fit better (log marginal likelihood -69,642 vs -73,551), and 3,776 of that 3,909-unit gain came from the aging main effect (Table S13), so the age gradient is not constant. The association was weaker in Model 2 (Table 3; Figure 4). Across the middle 90% of tracts (6% to 29% aged ≥65 years), this slope means a difference of about 2.5 index units, or about 1.3 standard deviations of the outcome. On a per-standard-deviation scale, aging was the largest single effect in Model 1 (+0.90, or 46.5% of an outcome standard deviation; Table S3).

Structural socioeconomic conditions.

In Model 1, both ICE measures had the expected protective sign (ICE_Income β = -0.99 [-1.03, -0.94]; ICE_Race β = -1.63 [-1.66, -1.60]), as they did in the OLS baseline ( §3.2) and the county-level re-analysis ( §S3.5). In Model 2, which adds the behavioral and healthcare-engagement block, both became positive (Table 3; Figure 4). Percent Hispanic or Latino changed in the same way, from -0.005 [-0.005, -0.004] to +0.009, and the coefficient for a bachelor's degree or higher shrank almost to zero (from -0.0359 to -0.0019). For ICE_Income, the sign change also appears in the OLS and ESF models with the Model 2 predictors (Table S2), so it follows from adding the behavioral block. For ICE_Race, OLS and ESF with the same predictors stayed negative (β = -0.80 and -0.07), so the spa al random effect also contributes to its sign change. The data cannot tell us why adding the behavioral block changes these estimates. The behavioral measures come from the same BRFSS data as the outcome and may lie on the pathway between structural disadvantage and CKM burden. Adjusting for them could therefore cause over-adjustment, common-method bias, or collider bias; alternatively, Model 1 may be confounded by factors that the behavioral block captures ( §S2.4). Supplemental Results reports the full comparison.

Rurality. Burden rose from metropolitan to rural tracts in both models. In Model 1, rural tracts had higher burden than metropolitan tracts (β = +0.200 [+0.159, +0.241]), small-town tracts less so, and micropolitan tracts did not differ from metropolitan tracts (Table 3; Figure 4). PM₂.₅ was negative in both models, but only 0.6% of its variation in Model 1 and 0.1% in Model 2 remained after the spatial term (Table S15), so we do not interpret its sign. Supplemental Results reports ozone, warm-season heat, walkability, and the LILA flag.

Environmental exposures.

Aging × environment interactions. With aging entered linearly, the aging × LILA interaction was positive (Model 1 β = +0.019 [+0.016, +0.021]; Model 2 β = +0.016 [+0.015, +0.017]). This linear form implies that the LILA difference keeps rising with age composition and becomes negative in the youngest tracts. A natural spline for aging (df = 4) fit better (log marginal likelihood -69,642 vs -73,551) and showed a different shape: LILA tracts had 0.10 to 0.14 units higher burden from the 25th percentile of aging upward, with no credible difference in the youngest decile (Table S13). However, 97% of this gain came from the aging main effect: a spline main effect with a linear interaction recovered 3,776 of the 3,909, and freeing the interaction added 132. The shape in the youngest tracts therefore depends on the functional form. With foodstore counts per resident in place of the LILA flag, the higher burden above the 25th percentile of aging did not appear, and adding the store counts left the LILA contrasts nearly unchanged ( §S3.10; Table S14). The other four interactions were small (Table 3; Figure 4). Aging × ozone was negative and aging × walkability was positive in both models. Aging × PM₂.₅ (positive in Model 1) and aging × heat (not different from zero in Model 1) both became negative in Model 2.

Supplemental Results reports the ESF cross-check, the comparison of fit across modeling frameworks (Table S4), the focal-versus-neighborhood (Wx) analysis (Tables S5-S6), and the Southern excess that remains after adjustment, with its dependence on the model (Figure 6; Table S11).

3.4 Sensitivity analyses

Supplemental Results reports seven sensitivity analyses in full: county-level re-aggregation (Table S8, Panel A), alternative spatial weights (Table S8, Panel B), aging × environment interactions refit within rurality strata (Table S8, Panel C), an index without chronic kidney disease, refit for Model 2 (Table S9), precision weighting using the CDC PLACES confidence intervals (Table S10), an extended index that adds coronary heart disease and stroke, refit for Model 1 ( §S3.9), and food-store counts in place of the LILA flag, refit for Model 1 ( §S3.10; Table S14). Four of these analyses re-estimate the main effects: county re-aggregation, the index without chronic kidney disease, precision weighting, and the extended index. In all four, the main effects of population aging and the two ICE measures kept their direction. The aging × environment interactions were less stable. Aging × heat changed the most: it was not different from zero in the primary tract-level model, but it was negative (nominally significant) at the county scale ( §S3.5) and in every rurality stratum ( §S3.8), and positive in the precision-weighted and extended-index refits of Model 1 ( §S3.7, §S3.9). The county analysis tests whether results depend on the spatial scale of analysis, which is one part of the Modifiable Areal Unit Problem.

4. Discussion

>Discussion

4.1 Principal findings

The tract-level index of AHA Stage 1-2 CKM burden was clustered in space ( §3.2) and agreed only modestly with NHANES individual-level data. Between the two sources, the correlations among the five indicators agreed at r = 0.37, and the PC1 loadings at r = 0.16. Chronic kidney disease accounted for most of the disagreement: its PC1 loading was 0.48 in PLACES but 0.26 in NHANES. This gap may partly reflect a difference in case definition, because NHANES asks about "weak or failing kidneys" ( §S2.2), but it is also consistent with the model-based smoothing behind PLACES tract estimates. We highlight three main findings.

First, the behavioral and healthcare-engagement measures ranked highest in the descriptive XGBoost analysis, with 52.6% of total SHAP importance ( §S3.4; Figure 7). Physical inactivity ranked first, and smoking and routine checkup were among the top four features; population aging ranked second and was the leading demographic feature. These measures come from the same BRFSS/PLACES data as the outcome. Their high ranking therefore at least partly reflects common-method variance and diagnostic ascertainment ( §4.5), not an ordering of causes, and we read them as descriptive correlates. In the same model, the socioeconomic measures (ICE_Income, ICE_Race, education, and rurality) accounted for 11.5% of total importance. On a per-standard-deviation scale, Model 2, which uses the same predictors as XGBoost, ranked the same two leading blocks first, but the order of the environmental and socioeconomic blocks differed (Table S3).

Second, environmental associations were modest, and evidence that they vary with age composition was limited. In the primary model, LILA tracts had 0.10 to 0.14 units higher burden from the 25th percentile of aging upward, with no credible difference in the youngest tracts; the shape in the youngest tracts depends on functional form ( §3.3). This LILA pattern was also specific to the USDA flag, which combines low income with distance to a supermarket; foodstore counts per resident did not reproduce it ( §S3.10). The aging × PM₂.₅ interaction was positive in the primary model and matched the metropolitan stratum in sign ( §4.3). The aging × ozone interaction was negative in the pooled models but reversed sign between metropolitan and small-town/rural tracts (Table S8; §4.3). The aging × walkability interaction was small and positive in all three rurality strata, although it reversed sign at the county scale ( §S3.5). The aging × heat interaction was the least robust. It was negative in every rurality stratum, in Model 2, and at the county scale, but not different from zero in the primary pooled model and positive in the precision-weighted, spatial+, and extended-index refits of Model 1 ( §S3.7, §S3.9; Table S12). Its direction is therefore unresolved. The SHAP dependence plots support these estimates only in part ( §S3.4; Figure 8).

Third, the Southern excess in CKM burden depended on the adjustment set. In Model 1, which excludes the behavioral block, the Southern regions named in §3.2 remained the areas of highest residual burden in the BYM2 spatial random effect (Figure 6). In Model 2, which includes the block, the excess approached zero ( §S3.3). The block comes from the same BRFSS survey as the outcome, and its care-engagement measures point in the direction expected from diagnostic ascertainment (routine checkup positive, lack of insurance negative; Table 3; §4.5). We therefore consider Model 1 the more interpretable map of residual geography. The loss of the excess in Model 2 may reflect shared measurement and ascertainment, real regional differences in these behaviors, or both, and the data cannot separate them.

4.2 Comparison with prior literature

Our finding that structural socioeconomic conditions are associated with tract-level CKM burden, after adjustment for environmental exposures, is consistent with the neighborhoodeffects literature [4] [5] [6] 20 . In Model 1, which excludes the behavioral block, both ICE measures were protective, in the same direction as that literature ( §3.3; Table 3). In Model 2, both were positive but small: per standard deviation, +0.26 for ICE_Income and +0.14 for ICE_Race (Table S3). Because the ICE indices range from -1 to +1, their raw coefficients cannot be compared with effect sizes from other studies, so we compare our results with that literature in direction and ordering, not magnitude. Supplemental Discussion gives the rescaling and the same adjustment sensitivity for percent Hispanic or Latino.

The closest prior study is the nationwide analysis by Krishnan et al. (2026), which compared seven composite neighborhood indices with median household income as predictors of coronary heart disease, stroke, and chronic kidney disease and found similar associations. Our study differs in four ways. First, their primary outcomes, coronary heart disease and stroke, are Stage 4 clinical disease, whereas our index measures the earlier Stage 1-2 CKM burden; they analyzed obesity, hypertension, and diabetes only as separate secondary outcomes. Second, we model a single index aligned with CKM Stages 1-2 and compared with NHANES, rather than comparing social indices with income. Third, they adjusted for tract median age, whereas we used the share of adults aged ≥65 years, the group in which CKM conditions are most common. We also test this share as an effect modifier, and because our outcome is not age-standardized, our aging terms reflect both the age makeup of tracts and age-related risk. Fourth, our BYM2 model accounts for spatial autocorrelation, whereas their non-spatial regressions treat tracts as independent. When we refit our primary model on an extended index that adds their Stage 4 endpoints, every coefficient kept its sign and the Southern excess remained ( §S3.9). Our study therefore addresses two questions theirs did not: where Stage 1-2 burden concentrates once spatial dependence is modeled, and whether age composition modifies its environmental associations.

We know of no direct precedent at the U.S. tract scale for the LILA difference, which was present across most of the age distribution but absent in the youngest tracts ( §3.3). Older adults' greater reliance on food sources near home 21, 22 could contribute to this pattern, although the difference did not increase further with age composition above the 25th percentile.

4.3 Spatial heterogeneity: scale-dependent interactions, residual geography, and neighborhood effects

The main effects of the ambient exposures (PM₂.₅, ozone, and warm-season heat) are difficult to estimate in this model. These exposures vary mainly across regions, and the BYM2 spatial random effect absorbs broad regional patterns in any smoothly varying predictor. A smooth surface over tract locations (thin-plate spline) explains 92% of the variation in warm-season heat, 91% in ozone, and 88% in PM₂.₅, but only 14% in % adults aged ≥65 years (Table S12). The ambient main effects are therefore estimated mostly from differences within regions, and we do not interpret them as exposure-response relationships. The aging × environment interactions are less affected, because they rely on tract-to-tract differences in age composition within regions of similar exposure. Even so, not all of them held up. When we removed the spatial trend from each covariate (spatial+) 23 , the LILA and walkability interactions remained credible, the ozone and PM₂.₅ interactions lost credibility, and the heat interaction, which was not credible in the primary model, became credible and positive (Table S12). Supplemental Discussion reports the spatial+ and state fixed-effect refits, the rural-urban differences in the ozone and PM₂.₅ interactions, and the neighborhood-effect (Wx) estimates.

The Southern excess that remained in Model 1 raises the question of what this residual geography represents. Unmeasured contextual factors may contribute, such as historical segregation and disinvestment, including the legacy of Home Owners' Loan Corporation (HOLC) redlining 24 , and regional differences in healthcare access and capacity, such as state Medicaid expansion status in 2019-2020 25 . Our ICE_Race, RUCA, and education measures do not fully capture these factors.

4.4 Clinical and public-health implications

CKM burden is geographically patterned at the tract level. For clinicians and health systems, area-level information of this kind may complement, but not replace, the individual risk factors assessed in the clinic. The AHA PREVENT equations already accept an optional ZIP code-level Social Deprivation Index, which improved discrimination only modestly 26 . Whether tract-level CKM burden improves individual risk prediction beyond that term has not been tested.

Physical inactivity and smoking are also more common in the neighborhoods with the greatest burden ( §S3.3; highest vs lowest burden quintile: physical inactivity 34.0% vs 17.2%, current smoking 24.0% vs 11.9%). Both were associated with higher burden in Model 2, the only model that includes them (Table 3), and both are established modifiable CKM risk factors 1 . That external evidence, rather than their ranking in our analysis, supports non-pharmacological approaches such as community-based physical-activity and tobacco-cessation programs. The care-engagement measures cannot be read the same way. Higher routine-checkup prevalence was associated with higher measured burden (+0.2790), and lack of health insurance with lower burden (-0.0379; Table 3), a pa ern consistent with diagnos c ascertainment, so we do not present them as intervention targets ( §4.5).

LILA tracts had modestly higher burden across most of the age distribution (0.10 to 0.14 units, about 5% to 7% of an outcome standard deviation; §3.3). This may help guide area-level needs assessment, but it does not show that pairing screening with food-access interventions would improve individual outcomes. Similarly, the Southern excess in Model 1 supports using geography to plan services, but it does not make geography a cause.

4.5 Limitations

Four limitations matter most for interpretation. First, the design is ecological. It compares tracts, not individuals, so our coefficients should not be read as individual-level effects. Second, the outcome and several predictors are partly circular. The CDC PLACES model predicts tract prevalence from age, sex, race and ethnicity, education, and county poverty, which overlap with predictors in our models. Part of the demographic and socioeconomic associations may therefore come from the PLACES model itself, and the index should be read as a proxy for burden, not a measure of clinical CKM co-morbidity. Our credible intervals also treat the PLACES estimates as fixed, so they do not include the uncertainty of the PLACES model ( §S3.7).

Third, each model is open to a different bias. Model 1 omits the behavioral block and may be confounded by it. Model 2 adjusts for the block, which shares a data source with the outcome and may lie on the causal pathway, so it may be over-adjusted. Fourth, the routine-checkup and health-insurance estimates may reflect ascertainment and reverse causation. PLACES measures diagnosed conditions, so tracts with better access to care can show higher measured burden at the same level of disease. The cross-sectional design also cannot rule out people moving because of their health. Supplemental Note 2 describes these limitations in full, along with sensitivity to spatial scale, exposure measurement error, multiple comparisons, the crude (not age-standardized) outcome, and the exclusion of New Jersey.

4.6 Future directions

Future studies should test whether these patterns hold in later CDC PLACES releases, use difference-in-differences designs to test whether changes in food access precede changes in CKM burden, and use individual-level data, such as NHANES and geocoded cohorts, to separate confounding from mediation (Supplemental Discussion).

4.7 Conclusions

At the U.S. census-tract scale, CKM burden is spatially clustered. In the analysis, the behavioral and healthcare-engagement measures ranked highest, followed by population aging, with smaller contributions from environmental exposures and structural socioeconomic conditions. This ranking is not causal since the behavioral measures come from the same BRFSS data as the outcome. LILA tracts had modestly higher burden across most of the age distribution, but evidence that age composition changes environmental associations was limited. A Southern excess in burden remained after adjustment for demographic, socioeconomic, and environmental factors and approached zero only when the shared-source behavioral measures were added, so its causes remain unknown. These estimates support area-level surveillance and needs assessment.
