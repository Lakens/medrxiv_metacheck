To fix Exact P-Values

What we found

We found 2 imprecise p values out of 36 detected p values.

Show details

Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 28 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background: No dural enhancement sign centered on the inferior sagittal sinus (ISS) has been characterized. We observed a frequent, previously undescribed finding-para-ISS dural space enhancement (PISE)-at the free inferior margin of the falx cerebri. Objective: To define PISE, determine its detection rate in a large cross-sectional cohort, and characterize its correlates, in particular whether the pattern of association is compatible with an atherosclerosis-related dural mechanism. Methods: Consecutive contrast-enhanced intracranial vessel-wall MRI (VWI) examinations (May 2015-December 2025) were reviewed; 835 of 875 examinations were evaluable, corresponding to 811 patients. The first outcome-evaluable examination for each patient was used for all primary analyses. PISE was defined as dural enhancement strictly at the falx free margin adjacent to the ISS, exceeding the ISS vascular lumen. Forty-seven demographic, clinical-history, and imaging variables were tested univariately with Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction; multivariable logistic regression with patient-grouped 5-fold cross-validation was triangulated with LASSO and random forest. Dose-response across plaque grades was assessed with the Cochran-Armitage trend test, and circulation-specific associations were compared in models adjusted for age and sex (inter-reader Cohen's κ=0.93). Results: PISE was detected in 609/811 patients (75.1%); detection rose from 50.0% (<40 years) to 81.7% (>70 years; trend p=5.6×10⁻⁶). The strongest univariate correlate was vulnerable intracranial plaque (OR=8.29, 95%CI 5.54-12.40). Detection increased across plaque grades 0/1/2 (38.7%→77.2%→90.3%; Z=11.09). Anterior-circulation plaque showed a stronger association than posterior-circulation plaque (OR=4.42 vs 2.76). Vulnerable plaque (OR=2.37), plaque grade (OR=2.84 per grade), age (OR=1.38 per SD), and inpatient status (OR=3.18) remained independently associated (patient-grouped cross-validated AUC=0.755). Moyamoya disease was inversely associated (OR=0.25). Findings were consistent across imputation and all-examination sensitivity analyses. Conclusions: PISE is a frequently detected novel dural enhancement sign whose independent, graded, anatomically concordant association with intracranial atherosclerotic burden supports an atherosclerosis-related dural mechanism and warrants prospective validation.

1. Introduction

On contrast-enhanced intracranial vessel-wall MRI (VWI) we repeatedly observed a distinctive pattern of dural enhancement that does not correspond to any entry in published catalogues of meningeal enhancement patterns [1, 2]: tubular or band-like enhancement situated precisely at the free inferior margin of the falx cerebri, centered on the inferior sagittal sinus (ISS), within the peri-ISS dural space of the falx. We termed this finding the para-ISS dural space enhancement (PISE) sign. Physiological dural enhancement, as characterized in prospective and early contrast MRI studies, is thin, linear, discontinuous, and located along the body of the falx, the tentorium, and the convexity dura [3] [4] [5]; the enhancement we describe occupies a territory that does not overlap these descriptions. We therefore sought to define PISE operationally and to determine what it signifies. Representative MR images and corresponding schematic illustrations of its anatomical location are shown in Figure 1.

The ISS courses within the free edge of the falx cerebri, receives falcine veins and anterior pericallosal tributaries, and drains posteriorly into the vein of Galen and the straight sinus [6, 7]. Thin-section contrast MRI depicts the ISS in approximately 97% of adults, with substantial morphological variability [7]; a persistent falcine sinus is a recognized variant that must be distinguished from peri-sinus dural findings [8]. Functionally, the ISS sits at a junction between the deep venous drainage of the anterior circulation and the dura that invests it, making the peri-ISS dura a candidate interface between vascular, interstitial, and meningeal compartments.

Over the past decade the dura has been re-framed from a passive covering to an active drainage and signaling interface. Meningeal lymphatic vessels were characterized in rodent dura [9, 10] and visualized non-invasively in human and primate meninges by MRI [11]. Intrathecal tracer studies demonstrated cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) efflux to the parasagittal dura in humans [12], and dynamic contrast-enhanced (DCE) MRI showed that the parasagittal dural space enhances slowly with delayed washout, a kinetic distinct from venous structures [13]; cross-sectional MRI studies further showed that the parasagittal and peri-sinus dural space enlarges and its washout decreases at older age [13] [14] [15].

Histologically, lymphatic vessels accompany the dorsal dural sinuses-including the ISS-in human specimens, with larger-caliber vessels along the dorsal sinuses [16], and meningeal lymphatic circuits are conserved between mice and humans [17], although their distribution is widespread and not restricted to sinus walls [18]. Notably, a published 7-Tesla MRI atlas of the meningeal lymphatic network assessed fifteen peri-vascular and peri-sinus sites but did not include the ISS region [19]; the peri-ISS dura remains a blank spot in published imaging atlases. Several recent studies and reviews now place the parasagittal dura at the center of CSF transport, neuroimmune surveillance, and brain clearance frameworks [20] [21] [22] [23].

Direct imaging contact points between dural enhancement and cerebrovascular disease are scarce.

Delayed vessel-wall MRI in 11 patients with recent cerebral infarction showed ipsilateral delayed . dural/meningeal enhancement in all five large infarctions and in none of six small infarctions or six moyamoya patients [24]; DCE-MRI studies have demonstrated impaired meningeal lymphatic function in cerebral small vessel disease [25, 26], complemented by indirect diffusion-based glymphatic markers [27]; and dural thickening on FLAIR was found in 34% of a neurodegenerative cohort, largely unrelated to vascular risk factors [28]. A recent review concluded that human MRI evidence on post-stroke meningeal lymphatics remains exploratory [29]. No study has systematically characterized a dural enhancement sign on contrast-enhanced MRI in relation to intracranial atherosclerotic burden in a large cohort.

We therefore asked a descriptive-mechanistic question: in a large cross-sectional cohort of contrast-enhanced VWI examinations, what is the detection rate of PISE, what are its correlates, and does the pattern of correlates point to an atherosclerosis-related dural mechanism? Specifically, we tested whether PISE is independently associated with atherosclerotic burden (strength), whether detection rises monotonically with plaque grade (dose-response), whether the association is stronger for the anterior than the posterior circulation (anatomical correspondence with ISS drainage), and whether a non-atherosclerotic arteriopathy-moyamoya disease-behaves differently (natural control).

2. Methods

>Methods

2.1 Study design, setting, and participants

This single-center cross-sectional study included consecutive contrast-enhanced VWI examinations performed at Shantou Central Hospital between May 2015 and December 2025. Of 875 examinations, 40 (4.6%) were excluded: 6 because image quality precluded evaluation of the falx free margin, and 34 because of coexisting conditions that could confound dural-enhancement assessment (cranial trauma, intracranial hemorrhage, post-operative changes, or pathological meningeal/meningeal-vascular thickening), leaving 835 evaluable examinations from 811 patients. For every primary analysis, only the first outcome-evaluable examination of each patient was included; the other 24 repeat examinations were reserved for all-examination sensitivity analyses. Exam-level exclusion was non-differential with respect to age (61.7±13.8 vs 60.7±11.8 years; Student t-test, p=0.608), male sex (65.0% vs 62.4%; continuity-corrected chi-square, p=0.869), and emergency presentation (17.5% vs 16.6%; continuity-corrected chi-square, p=1.000). Twenty-two patients contributed two or more evaluable examinations and were included in the repeat-examination reliability analysis.

2.2 PISE definition and image reading

PISE was defined operationally as dural enhancement located strictly at the free inferior margin of the falx cerebri, in the peri-ISS dura, whose extent exceeds the contour of the ISS vascular lumen on post-contrast T1-weighted black-blood VWI. All examinations were acquired with three-dimensional T1-weighted black-blood vessel-wall sequences before and after contrast administration. The black-blood preparation of VWI suppresses intraluminal vascular signal, which facilitated the separation of ISS luminal opacification from peri-ISS dural enhancement; comparable black-blood vessel-wall sequences were used in the ISS visualization study of Tsutsumi et al. [7] and in the parasagittal dural study of Joo et al. [13]. Because the ISS lumen itself opacifies in approximately 97% of contrast MRI examinations [7], the reading protocol explicitly separated two fields-ISS luminal opacification and peri-ISS dural enhancement-and PISE was scored only when enhancement extended beyond the luminal contour (tubular or band-like enhancement within the peri-ISS dural space). The thin, linear, discontinuous physiological enhancement described along the falx body, tentorium, and convexity [3] [4] [5] lies outside the falx free margin and does not overlap the PISE territory; location restriction was therefore sufficient to distinguish the two. Pericallosal artery opacification was excluded by correlation with angiographic sequences; a persistent falcine sinus (located posteriorly, connecting to the superior sagittal sinus) [8] and falcine calcification were excluded morphologically. PISE is a pachymeningeal (dural) finding; it was distinguished from leptomeningeal (pial) enhancement, which follows cortical sulci and has a different anatomical basis [2]. Examinations showing diffuse supra-and infratentorial smooth dural thickening prompted clinical review for intracranial hypotension [30, 31]; nodular or irregular dural thickening prompted consideration of hypertrophic pachymeningitis; and recent surgery or lumbar puncture was recorded because post-procedural meningeal enhancement is common [1, 32]. PISE is not part of routine clinical reporting; all examinations were re-read for this study under a pre-specified two-stage protocol. First, before study reading began, a reliability subset of 60 examinations (30 PISE-positive and 30 PISE-negative on preliminary assessment) was randomly selected in advance; within this subset, two readers, each with more than 5 years of professional experience, independently and blindly scored PISE without communication, producing separate datasets. Inter-reader agreement between the two independent blinded readings was almost perfect (Cohen's κ=0.933, 95%CI 0.842-1.000; observed agreement 96.7%, 58/60), and intra-reader agreement on repeat assessment at a different time was perfect (κ=1.000; 60/60); the two discordant pairs (one positive-to-negative and one negative-to-positive) were resolved by consensus. Second, all 875 examinations-including the reliability subset-were then read jointly by both readers, with discrepancies resolved by consensus, and the consensus values constituted the analytical dataset of this study.

Forty-seven variables were abstracted from the same examination episode: demographics (age, sex); vascular risk factors (hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, hyperuricemia, smoking, alcohol use); care-pathway variables; intracranial atherosclerotic measures on angiographic/vessel-wall imaging-plaque grade (0-2), vulnerable plaque (binary), regional plaques (left anterior, right anterior,.

3.1 Cohort and PISE detection rate

Of 875 consecutive contrast-enhanced VWI examinations, 835 were evaluable (40 excluded: 6 for image quality and 34 for coexisting conditions confounding dural-enhancement assessment; non-differential exclusion as detailed in Methods), corresponding to 811 patients.

In the patient-level primary analysis, PISE was detected in 609/811 patients (75.1%); 202/811 (24.9%) were PISE-negative. Detection rose monotonically with age: 50.0% (22/44) in those <40 years, 70.0% (196/280) at 40-60 years, 79.3% (230/290) at 60-70 years, and 81.7% (161/197) in those >70 years (logit-linear trend p=5.6×10⁻⁶; OR=1.55 per ascending age stratum) (Figure 2). Twenty-two patients had two or more evaluable examinations (median interval 206 days between the first and last examination). PISE status was concordant in 16 (73%); all six discordant pairs showed incident PISE at follow-up (0→1), accompanying stable or increased atherosclerotic burden or older age-an exploratory observation consistent with temporality between burden accumulation and sign emergence.

In the pre-specified reliability subset (60 examinations, two independent blinded readers), inter-reader agreement for PISE was almost perfect (κ=0.933, 95%CI 0.842-1.024; 96.7%, 58/60), and intra-reader agreement was perfect (κ=1.000, 60/60); consensus values from dual reading were used for all analyses.

3.2 Baseline characteristics

Median age was 63 years (IQR 54-69); 62.0% were male and 93.7% of patients with recorded encounter type were inpatient (Table 1).

Compared with PISE-negative patients, PISE-positive patients were older (median 64 [56-70] vs 59 [48.25-67] years; p=1.1×10⁻⁶, q=8.6×10⁻⁶), more often inpatient (95.9% vs 87.2%; q=2.8×10⁻⁴), and more often emergency referrals (19.5% vs 9.9%; q=0.008). Among vascular risk factors, hypertension history survived FDR correction (84.0% vs 75.7%; q=0.043), whereas diabetes (40.9% vs 33.0%; q=0.126), hyperlipidemia (70.6% vs 65.9%; q=0.363), and hyperuricemia (25.9% vs 18.5%; q=0.126) did not; smoking history was less frequent among PISE-positive patients but did not survive FDR correction (21.3% vs 29.3%; q=0.091). Sex distribution did not differ (p=0.383).

3.3 Univariate association spectrum

Of 47 variables screened, 21 were significant after FDR correction (q<0.05) (Figure 3).

The strongest signals came from the atherosclerotic burden spectrum: vulnerable plaque (90.9% vs 54.5%; OR=8.29, 95%CI 5.54-12.40; q<1×10⁻¹⁵), plaque grade (r=0.478; q<1×10⁻¹⁵), left anterior plaque (OR=2.85, 2.00-4.07), right anterior plaque (OR=2.67, 1.87-3.83), posterior-circulation plaque (OR=2.41, 1.70-3.42), and stenosis degree (r=0.158; q=0.002). A second cluster comprised parenchymal and perfusion alterations: encephalomalacia (OR=3.11, 1.40-6.92), perfusion abnormality (OR=2.11, . 1.34-3.34), prolonged TTP (OR=2.02, 1.31-3.11), microbleeds (OR=1.96, 1.33-2.90), prolonged MTT (OR=1.82, 1.18-2.80), recent infarction (OR=1.92, 1.39-2.65), posterior-circulation infarction (OR=1.83, 1.14-2.93), absence of perfusion deficit on perfusion imaging (OR=1.69, 1.12-2.55), lacunar lesions (OR=1.64, 1.10-2.44), and higher white matter hyperintensity grade (r=0.172; q=3.1×10⁻⁴). A third cluster comprised demographic, clinical-history, and care-pathway variables: inpatient status (OR=3.41, 1.89-6.17), emergency presentation (OR=2.21, 1.34-3.66), hypertension history (OR=1.68, 1.11-2.56; q=0.043), and age (r=0.228; q=8.6×10⁻⁶). Moyamoya disease was inversely associated (1.2% vs 4.5%; OR=0.25, 0.09-0.68; Fisher exact; q=0.020), serving as a natural control.

3.4 Dose-response and anatomical correspondence

PISE detection increased stepwise with plaque grade: 38.7% (53/137) for grade 0, 77.2% (247/320) for grade 1, and 90.3% (280/310) for grade 2 (Cochran-Armitage Z=11.09, p<1×10⁻¹⁵; n=767 evaluable patients) (Figure 4). In the regional model adjusted for age and sex, right anterior plaque (OR=2.74, 1.86-4.05; p=4.23×10⁻⁷), left anterior plaque (OR=2.95, 2.00-4.34; p=4.20×10⁻⁸), and posterior-circulation plaque (OR=2.49, 1.69-3.68; p=4.48×10⁻⁶) were each associated with PISE. In the combined model, anterior-circulation plaque showed a stronger association (OR=4.42, 3.06-6.39; p=2.84×10⁻¹⁵) than posterior-circulation plaque (OR=2.76, 1.86-4.10; p=5.25×10⁻⁷)-an approximately 1.6-fold ratio consistent with the anterior-circulation deep drainage territory of the ISS.

3.5 Multivariable model

The final multivariable model retained six variables (Figure 5): vulnerable plaque (OR=2.37, 1.07-5.22; p=0.033), plaque grade (OR=2.84 per grade, 1.77-4.57; p=1.6×10⁻⁵), age (OR=1.38 per SD≈11.4 years, 1.15-1.66; p=7.1×10⁻⁴), and inpatient status (OR=3.18, 1.05-9.61; p=0.041) as positive independent correlates; stenosis degree (OR=0.83 per grade, 0.69-0.99; p=0.035) as an inverse correlate; and male sex (forced in; OR=0.76, 0.51-1.15; p=0.194, not significant).

Apparent AUC was 0.775, with good calibration (Hosmer-Lemeshow p=0.670); patient-grouped 5-fold pooled OOF AUC for the complete modelling pipeline was 0.755 (95%CI 0.713-0.799, patient-cluster bootstrap; valid OOF n=767). LASSO with patient-grouped cross-validated penalty selection retained five variables (plaque grade, age, vulnerable plaque, inpatient status, and microbleeds), including all four positive independent correlates of the main model, supporting model stability. The random forest did not outperform the logistic modelling pipeline (patient-grouped OOF AUC=0.742, 95%CI 0.696-0.787; valid OOF n=760), suggesting predominantly linear/additive associations; its out-of-fold permutation importance ranking (plaque grade 0.0433, age 0.0287, vulnerable plaque 0.0186) mirrored the logistic model, and partial dependence plots showed a high plateau of PISE probability beyond approximately 55 years and a near-linear increase across plaque grades. As an exploratory quantification of association strength, PISE alone discriminated vulnerable plaque with AUC=0.727 . (sensitivity 0.838, specificity 0.616, positive predictive value 0.909, negative predictive value 0.455, accuracy 0.798; vulnerable plaque frequency 0.820), and adding PISE to age+sex raised the AUC from 0.619 to 0.770 (ΔAUC=+0.151; PISE coefficient 2.056, p=2.80×10⁻²²).

3.6 Sensitivity analyses

All sensitivity analyses were directionally concordant with the main model.

With simple imputation in the full patient sample (n=811), age (OR=1.37, p=2.9×10⁻⁴) and plaque grade (OR=3.03, p=1.7×10⁻⁶) remained significant; vulnerable plaque (OR=1.93, p=0.077), inpatient status (OR=1.66, p=0.155), and stenosis degree (OR=0.86, p=0.056) were not significant. With multiple imputation, age (OR=1.37, p=4.0×10⁻⁴), plaque grade (OR=2.44, p=5.7×10⁻⁴), vulnerable plaque (OR=2.69, p=0.014), and stenosis degree (OR=0.83, p=0.030) remained significant, whereas inpatient status did not (OR=1.70, p=0.147). In the all-examination final-variable model (n=791 complete cases, 602 events; apparent AUC=0.780), age (OR=1.45), plaque grade (OR=2.74), vulnerable plaque (OR=2.53), and stenosis degree (OR=0.82) remained significant (all p<0.05), whereas inpatient status did not (OR=1.55, p=0.203). When all 835 evaluable examinations were used for univariate sensitivity analysis, all 21 patient-level FDR-significant findings remained FDR-significant after Benjamini-Hochberg re-correction across the 47-variable set (maximum q=0.048).

4. Discussion

In 811 patients with evaluable contrast-enhanced VWI, we defined and characterized a previously Why should enhancement concentrate at this particular site? The concentration of the sign at the falx free margin has a coherent anatomical logic. The ISS drains the falx itself and the medial hemispheric deep territory of the anterior circulation toward the vein of Galen and straight sinus [6], and histological work has confirmed lymphatic vessels accompanying the ISS in human dura [16]. The . arterial supply of the anterior and inferior falx derives from anterior-circulation sources-the anterior falcine artery (a meningeal branch of the anterior ethmoidal artery [6]) and distal dural branches of the anterior cerebral artery-demonstrated angiographically and anatomically [36, 37]. PISE therefore sits at a functional junction where anterior-circulation deep drainage meets the dura. This anatomy predicts precisely what we observed: anterior-circulation plaque associated more strongly than posterior-circulation plaque. We do not claim that the ISS is a unique lymphatic site-dural lymphatics are widely distributed [16, 18]-but that the peri-ISS dura is a plausible hub for anterior-circulation drainage-dural interface signaling, a hub that published 7T atlases happened not to sample [19].

The correlate pattern constrains mechanism in an informative way. PISE was associated with established vascular lesion burden-plaque grade, plaque vulnerability, and downstream parenchymal/perfusion injury-whereas most risk-factor exposures (diabetes, hyperlipidemia, hyperuricemia) did not survive multiple-testing correction and hypertension history was only modestly associated (OR=1.68; q=0.043). The sign thus tracks end-organ disease rather than risk-factor exposure, and it does so in a graded fashion. One parsimonious interpretation is a dural drainage-interface response: atherosclerotic and parenchymal brain injury increases interstitial solute and fluid load, which is cleared partly through glymphatic and intramural peri-arterial routes toward meningeal lymphatic and parasagittal dural compartments [9, 10, 12, 20, 22]; sustained load at the peri-ISS interface could remodel the local dura (vascularity, permeability, or lymphatic caliber), rendering it visibly enhancing on contrast-enhanced VWI. This hypothesis is compatible with evidence that CSF tracer effluxes to parasagittal dura [12], that parasagittal dural kinetics change with age [13] [14] [15], that experimental augmentation of dural lymphatics alters ischemic brain outcomes [38], and that the parasagittal dura functions as a neuroimmune interface [21]. The high background detection rate should be read together with the physiological-enhancement literature: PISE may represent a pathological amplification of a physiological phenomenon (a graded, load-related continuum) rather than an all-or-none abnormality; the dose-response gradient argues against a fixed anatomical variant. We explicitly refrain from claiming exclusion of alternative mechanisms, which requires targeted prospective designs.

The inverse association in moyamoya disease (OR=0.25) is mechanistically informative. Moyamoya displays prominent leptomeningeal change-the ivy sign of slow pial collateral flow on FLAIR and post-contrast images [39]-yet spontaneous pachymeningeal enhancement is not a feature; indeed, all six moyamoya patients in the delayed vessel-wall MRI series of Umehana et al. showed no dural enhancement outside postoperative areas [24]. This leptomeningeal-pachymeningeal dissociation indicates that PISE is neither a reflection of pial slow flow nor of collateralization per se. Within our framework, terminal anterior-circulation occlusion in moyamoya reshapes deep drainage demand and.

pathways, plausibly reducing load at the peri-ISS dural interface-the mirror image of the atherosclerotic-burden association.

The closest published observation is the delayed ipsilateral dural/meningeal enhancement after large cerebral infarction reported by Umehana et al. [24]. The two phenomena are best regarded as complementary rather than identical, differing on four dimensions: theirs is a delayed-phase (1-6 h post-injection) vessel-wall MRI finding, triggered by acute large infarction, distributed over the ipsilateral cortical dura, and described in 11 patients, whereas PISE is detected at standard post-contrast timing on VWI, tracks chronic atherosclerotic burden, is centered on the midline ISS at the falx free margin, and was assessed in 811 patients. Their compensatory-drainage interpretation after acute drainage-route injury is conceptually aligned with the chronic load-related interface response we propose.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to define an ISS-centered dural enhancement sign and to examine its correlates systematically. More importantly, the coherence of the clinical and anatomical findings, together with their robustness across complementary analytical approaches, supports the interpretation of PISE as a reproducible imaging marker associated with vascular burden rather than an incidental enhancement pattern. Because PISE can be assessed on the same VWI examination used for plaque evaluation, it could be incorporated into existing imaging workflows without additional scanning.

Several limitations qualify these findings. First, the study was primarily cross-sectional; exploratory repeat examinations in 22 patients provided preliminary temporal context for the emergence of PISE, but were insufficient to support robust temporal or causal inference. Second, the cohort is a single-center, indication-enriched clinical spectrum (93.7% inpatients); the 75.1% figure is a detection rate within this spectrum and is not generalizable to the general population. Third, VWI is not a lymphatic-specific sequence, so the drainage-interface mechanism remains inferential; delayed-phase and DCE sub-studies are needed; moreover, the visibility of PISE on conventional post-contrast T1-weighted sequences has not been verified, and extrapolation of the present detection rate to non-VWI protocols should be made with caution. Finally, the inverse association of stenosis degree with PISE should be interpreted cautiously given its divergent univariable result.

5. Conclusions

The para-ISS dural space enhancement (PISE) sign is a frequently detected, previously undescribed dural enhancement at the falx free margin on contrast-enhanced intracranial vessel-wall MRI. It is independently associated with intracranial atherosclerotic burden, with a monotone dose-response across plaque grades and an anterior-predominant anatomical correspondence that matches ISS drainage territory, while the non-atherosclerotic arteriopathy moyamoya disease shows markedly lower detection. The correlate pattern is compatible with an atherosclerosis-related dural . drainage-interface response. Prospective studies with dedicated delayed/DCE sequences, formal reliability testing, and histological correlation are warranted to validate the sign and test the proposed mechanism.
