Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Full text

Abstract

Blood and bone marrow smears are central to diagnosing hematological disorders. While whole-slide scanning enables automated single-cell profiling at scale, the computational burden of gigapixel images has confined automated analysis to manually pre-selected regions of interest. Here, we present HemAutomaton, a framework built on neural cellular automata that extracts white blood cells across entire whole-slide images without manual region selection and with minimal annotation requirements. HemAutomaton matches the in-domain detection accuracy of Faster R-CNN while surpassing it under domain shifts, operating at a fraction of the computational cost and with over 10,000-fold fewer parameters. Across three blood cancer cohorts, we extract over 100 million single-cell images and achieve annotation-efficient differential counting via fewshot classification using a pretrained hematology foundation model. Slide-wide spatial analysis uncovers consistent blast-frequency gradients across smears and demonstrates that automated blast counts match expert evaluations within inter-expert variability. Scaling automated profiling from hundreds to tens of thousands of cells per smear overcomes conventional sampling limits and establishes the foundation for more sensitive AI supported hematological assessments.

Introduction

Peripheral blood (PB) and bone marrow (BM) smears serve as the primary first-line diagnostic modality for the identification and characterization of hematological malignancies and cytopenias. Diagnosis of these acute and chronic blood disorders-including myeloid and lymphoid leukemias, myelodysplastic syndromes, and myeloproliferative neoplasms-requires high-resolution, single-cell morphological assessment to differentiate specific lineages and evaluate maturation stages [1] [2] [3] [4]. In clinical practice, hematopathologists analyze cell-type composition, identify aberrant cellular populations, and quantify blast frequencies to establish definitive diagnostic and prognostic markers [5, 6]. Crucially, a comprehensive manual review extends beyond the mere analysis of isolated single-cell images; it incorporates macroscopic and contextual slide-level indicators-such as specimen hemodilution (contamination of bone marrow aspirates with peripheral blood), preparation artifacts, and staining quality-to validate the clinical integrity of the specimen before specific regions are selected for differential counting [7, 8].

While a single smear contains up to hundreds of thousands of evaluable single white blood cells [9], conventional manual workflows utilize only a minute fraction of this data. To maintain operational throughput despite the time constraints of manual microscopy analysis, guidelines standardize manual blood count differentials to a limited sample size of up to 200 cells [7, 10, 11]. This introduces substantial statistical variance, causing high sampling error for rare cell populations and inherently limiting the detection of minimal residual disease. Furthermore, because manual inspection relies on subjectively selected fields of view, the comprehensive characterization of the cellular composition and macroscopic spatial organization across the entire smear remains impractical. Routine digitization of smears into whole-slide images (WSIs) offers a path to overcome these limitations by enabling computational processing at the slide-wide scale [12, 13].

To automate smear assessment, deep learning methods have recently extended hematopathological analysis from manually selected regions of interest (ROIs) to WSIs by introducing automated pipelines for cell extraction [14]. Extracted single-cell images form the foundation for downstream analyses, including automated differential counting [9, [15] [16] [17] [18] [19] [20], disease prediction [21] [22] [23] [24], and visionlanguage modeling [25]. Most existing extraction pipelines mirror the manual clinical workflow, passing WSIs through sequential, specialized stages: ROI (pre)-selection, quality assessment, and high-resolution single-cell detection and segmentation.

These approaches, however, present several limitations: First, supervised cell detection typically relies on extensive expert annotations, such as manually bounded ROIs [9, 16, 26], single-cell bounding boxes [9, 16, 18, 24, 27], or pixel-level segmentation masks [21], which severely restricts training data scale and diversity. Second, cell detection is typically performed at full resolution using deep-learning architectures such as Faster R-CNN [9], YOLO variants [16, 18, 19, 24, 28], U-Net approaches [21], large vision foundation models such as SAM [20, 25], or other CNN-based architectures [26, 27], requiring substantial computational resources when processing entire WSIs. Third, external validation is often restricted to downstream task performance, such as differential counts or disease classification, rather than analyzing the underlying single-cell extraction pipeline itself [19] [20] [21]. Heavily parameterized networks trained on small, single-center cohorts tend to overfit to local staining profiles and scanner optics, making them vulnerable to performance degradation under domain shifts that are always present in real-world data from laboratories and clinics.

Beyond diagnosis, large-scale single-cell extraction is critical for training self-supervised hematology foundation models, such as DinoBloom, which learned morphological features from 380,000 curated white blood cell images [29]. By offering unified, transferable representations without taskspecific retraining, learned single-cell embeddings form the foundation for diverse downstream tasks, including single-cell classification [30] [31] [32] and disease prediction [33] [34] [35] [36] [37] [38]. However, scaling these architectures to millions of cells across heterogeneous cohorts remains constrained by the inability of existing extraction pipelines to adapt to varying staining protocols and scanning hardware. Limited dataset diversity causes self-supervised methods to focus on institutional signatures rather than 2 . biologically relevant morphological features. Efficient, slide-wide single-cell extraction is thus a key prerequisite for assembling the high-volume datasets required to advance the generalizability of future hematology foundation models.

To address these challenges, a whole-slide extraction framework must satisfy the following three criteria: (i) computational efficiency at the gigapixel scale, (ii) annotation efficiency to eliminate site-specific expert relabeling, and (iii) robustness against varying staining protocols, scanner hardware, and sample preparation. Neural cellular automata (NCAs) are uniquely suited to meet these constraints [39]. Originally introduced as a bio-inspired architecture for image generation through local cell-to-cell interactions [40], NCAs have recently demonstrated competitive performance in medical image segmentation, requiring substantially fewer trainable parameters than conventional deep learning models [41]. In hematology, NCAs have been applied to single-cell classification [42] [43] [44] and weakly supervised white blood cell segmentation [45], demonstrating strong robustness under cross-domain evaluation.

Here, we present HemAutomaton, a lightweight pipeline for large-scale white blood cell extraction directly from WSIs. Built upon a parameter-efficient NCA model optimized for cell localization, HemAutomaton operates on the premise that cell identification relies on local context rather than long-range features, enabling high-throughput detection at the full-slide scale. By processing the complete slides without requiring ROI pre-selection, the pipeline eliminates the need for manual intervention. We demonstrate that HemAutomaton enables the extraction of 108 million singlecell images across three independent clinical cohorts with minimal expert supervision. Using the extracted cells, we further demonstrate its utility in two downstream applications: automated slidelevel differential cell counting and quantification of macroscopic spatial blast gradients across entire smears.

Results

HemAutomaton whole-slide single-cell extraction pipeline

HemAutomaton implements a multi-stage pipeline for scalable white blood cell extraction across gigapixel WSIs, without manual region selection (Figure 1). We exemplified the framework using three independent datasets spanning BM and PB smears from five diseases and normal controls, acquired on four scanning systems (Figure 1A and Methods): (i) an in-house B-cell precursor acute lymphoblastic leukemia (B-ALL) dataset containing paired BM and PB WSIs from 120 patients; (ii) a BM smear dataset released by Höfener et al. [18] comprising 246 patients diagnosed with acute lymphoblastic leukemia (n = 100), acute myeloid leukemia (n = 95), and chronic myeloid leukemia (n = 51); and (iii) a publicly available PB dataset released by de Almeida et al. [21] containing 362 cropped WSIs from individuals diagnosed with myelodysplastic syndromes (n = 233), anemias (n = 71), and hematologically normal controls (n = 58). WSIs from different datasets exhibit substantial heterogeneity arising from differences in specimen type (BM versus PB), laboratory workflows, cell density, staining intensity, and preparation artifacts, illustrated by representative whole-slide views and magnified regions. An overview of the available annotations for each dataset and the usage throughout the study is provided in Supplementary Figure 1. Because diagnosis of the disorders in these datasets relies on white blood cell rather than red blood cell morphology, we focused exclusively on leukocyte detection and analysis.

In the first processing stage, raw WSIs are partitioned into non-overlapping tiles, followed by a brightness-based filter that discards uninformative background regions and densely aggregated cell areas where individual cells cannot be resolved (Figure 1B). Next, to optimize computational efficiency during slide-wide inference, the remaining tiles are downsampled to a standardized resolution and processed using an NCA-based detection model that iteratively generates cell-shaped activation maps with peaks at white blood cell centers. These activation maps are smoothed and converted into cell center coordinates via local-maxima detection, which are then mapped back onto the original full-resolution WSIs for the extraction of single-cell patches. Finally, an automated quality-filtering model discards blurred, damaged, or otherwise unsuitable crops, yielding a collection of high-quality single-cell images for downstream analyses (Methods).

We validated HemAutomaton's utility for large-scale dataset generation, automated differential cell counting, and spatial analysis of blast distributions (Figure 1C).

Robust and scalable white blood cell detection

Accurate white blood cell localization across heterogeneous preparation and scanning protocols is essential for automated whole-slide analysis, given the variability across clinical laboratories and within individual slides. To enable efficient whole-slide processing, we performed detection at a reduced standardized resolution of approximately 1 µm per pixel and benchmarked our NCA-based detection model against Faster R-CNN [9, 46] and a simple blob detection baseline (Methods) based on color and intensity (Table 1). We evaluated this localization task in a three-domain cross-validation setup (Methods).

In domain, Faster R-CNN achieved the highest average ROI-level F1 score (0.87 ± 0.02; mean ± standard deviation over n = 3 in-domain settings), with HemAutomaton's NCA detection model performing competitively (0.85 ± 0.01), whereas the blob baseline showed substantially lower overall performance (0.59 ± 0.16). Under domain shift, i.e., when the model is trained on one dataset and applied to another, the NCA model achieved the highest average cross-domain performance (F1 score of 0.79 ± 0.11 across n = 6 cross-domain settings), outperforming Faster R-CNN (0.65 ± 0.18) and the blob baseline (0.51 ± 0.18). Consistent with this, the NCA model showed the smallest average gap between in-domain and cross-domain F1 scores (0.06 ± 0.10), indicating more stable performance across datasets than Faster R-CNN (0.21 ± 0.14) and the blob baseline (0.08 ± 0.26). Notably, the relatively small gap of the blob baseline reflected uniformly poor absolute performance rather than strong transferability. Together, these results indicate that the proposed NCA-based detection model achieves strong cross-domain generalization while maintaining competitive in-domain performance. In addition to detection performance, we evaluated the computational efficiency of the proposed approach and compared it to Faster R-CNN and the blob baseline (Table 1). Efficient processing is critical for practical deployment in laboratories and clinics, where large numbers of images must be analyzed every day. The NCA-based detection model requires four orders of magnitude fewer parameters than Faster R-CNN (3.3k versus 41.4M) and reduces computational cost in terms of FLOPs per tile by a factor of 12.6 (10.0G versus 126.1G). This translates into 0.55-fold lower latency and 1.8-fold higher throughput during inference, demonstrating substantially faster processing compared to Faster R-CNN, while maintaining competitive detection performance. The NCA-based approach of HemAutomaton also minimizes GPU memory footprint during inference, facilitating slide-wide analysis on resource-constrained hardware. While the blob baseline exhibits excellent computational efficiency, its low localization accuracy (Table 1) limits applicability.

Together, these results show that the NCA-based detection model matches Faster R-CNN's indomain performance and exceeds it under domain shift at reduced computational cost.

Annotation-efficient automated blood counts

Extracted single-cell images form the basis for slide-wide morphological analyses, such as differential blood counts. However, cell classification typically requires extensive expert annotation. Pretrained hematology foundation models can reduce these annotation requirements by providing transferable morphological representations learned from large collections of single-cell images. To minimize expert annotation effort, we therefore used DinoBloom [29] as a frozen feature extractor and investigated whether sparse supervision suffices for cell-type classification (Figure 3A).

To maximize annotation efficiency, representative cells were selected by clustering the targetdomain embeddings using k-means. Annotating a fixed number of cells per cluster ensures coverage across the full morphological spectrum, avoiding bias toward high-frequency cell populations. To compensate for cell types that are absent or only sparsely represented in few-shot reference training data, we additionally incorporated class prototypes derived from publicly available labeled datasets [18, 31] with a custom weighting scheme. Cell-type prediction for the remaining cells was performed using a nearest-neighbor classifier (Methods).

We first evaluated the few-shot approach on the Matek et al. [31] and the Höfener et al. [18] singlecell datasets (Figure 3B). To establish a common label space across datasets, annotations from the two datasets were harmonized using a common set of cell types including blast cells, neutrophils and neutrophil progenitors, eosinophils and basophils, nucleated erythroid cells, mononuclear cells and progenitors, and other cells and artifacts (Supplementary Figure 4). As a zero-shot baseline, cells from each dataset were classified using only labeled cells from the respective other dataset. Incorporating limited target-domain supervision improves classification performance over the zero-shot baseline (Figure 3B). Annotating only 20 cells per cluster increases macro-F1 scores by 0.19 (from 0.48 to 0.67) on the Matek et al. dataset and by 0.31 (from 0.37 to 0.67) on the Höfener et al. dataset, demonstrating that high classification accuracy can be achieved with minimal annotation effort. The full confusion matrices and results from an ablation experiment for varying numbers of labeled single-cell images per cluster are provided in the Supplementary Material (Supplementary Figure 5, Supplementary Table 4).

We next applied the few-shot workflow to automatically extracted cells from PB and BM WSIs of the B-ALL dataset. The resulting single-cell image features form a morphology landscape in which visually similar cell types cluster together, providing a basis for nearest-neighbor cell-type classification (Figure 3C). Applying the classifier to all extracted cells enables automated estimation of slide-level cell-type frequencies. Qualitative inspection of the predictions via UMAP revealed coherent regions of predicted cell types within the morphology landscape (Figure 3D). The resulting distributions are biologically plausible with elevated blast frequencies reflecting the disease, while PB samples exhibit relatively higher proportions of mature hematopoietic cells than the corresponding BM samples (Figure 3E).

Discussion

Diverse computational tasks in hematology-including automated differential counting [9, [15] [16] [17] [18] [19] [20], disease classification [21-24, 33-36, 38], and representation learning [29, 32, 48, 49]-share a common prerequisite: the extraction of high-fidelity single-cell patches from raw whole-slide data. Existing approaches, however, are rarely designed for exhaustive whole-slide extraction. Instead, most pipelines rely on multi-stage workflows that restrict computationally demanding full-resolution cell detection to selected regions [9, 16, 21, 23, 26], while publicly available models often show limited transferability to external datasets (Supplementary Table 3). By enabling fast, robust white blood cell localization directly across complete slides without manual intervention, HemAutomaton overcomes these limitations, reducing the need for site-specific retraining or manual adjustments when translating to new laboratory settings.

For practical slide-wide deployment, computational hematology workflows must combine throughput and robustness to variable preparation conditions. NCA models, which are at the core of HemAutomaton, are particularly well suited to these demands. By operating locally, HemAutomaton avoids the heavy parameterization typical of standard vision architectures-such as Faster R-CNN [9], YOLO variants [16, 18, 19], or semantic segmentation networks [21]-which are prone to domain-specific overfitting. Consequently, it matches the in-domain accuracy of Faster R-CNN while maintaining greater robustness under domain shifts, at a reduced computational cost. In addition to computational scalability, annotation efficiency represents a key practical advantage: HemAutomaton achieves near-peak detection performance from as few as 100 annotated cells, substantially lowering the barrier for adapting the model to new datasets.

Large-scale single-cell extraction also provides the necessary data foundation for self-supervised representation learning in computational hematology. Existing curated reference datasets typically range from approximately 28,000 [18] to 171,000 single-cell images [31], and the hematology foundation model DinoBloom was pretrained on a multi-dataset corpus of approximately 380,000 curated white blood cell images [29]. By automatically extracting 108 million single-cell images across the three whole-slide image datasets used in this study, HemAutomaton demonstrates the feasibility of expanding training data volume by nearly three orders of magnitude beyond current curated collections. This scaling capability facilitates the training of broader, more generalizable models [29, 48]. Our few-shot classification experiments demonstrate that pairing pretrained feature representations with sparse target-domain supervision substantially improves cell-type classification over zero-shot transfer, showing that even minimal manual labeling can markedly enhance adaptation to new clinical settings. Together, these findings indicate that HemAutomaton can support both the data curation required to train hematology foundation models and their downstream sample-efficient deployment.

Moving beyond single-cell detection and classification, automated whole-slide analysis enables the assessment of macro-spatial patterns of cell-type distributions. In this work, we used HemAutomaton to map the spatial heterogeneity of blast cells within BM and PB smears. While the non-uniform distribution of different cell types across smears-particularly the accumulation of larger cells such as blasts near the feathered edge-is recognized in clinical practice [47], measuring these macroscopic patterns has remained impractical with manual workflows. HemAutomaton overcomes these sampling constraints by profiling cell distributions across entire slides, systematically quantifying the blast gradient and providing an empirical characterization of local sampling biases.

Several limitations of our approach should be acknowledged. First, the macro-spatial analyses rely directly on model predictions and may be subject to localized detection biases; specifically, high cell density in crowded smear regions can disproportionately hinder the detection of larger cells, such as blasts, potentially confounding the observed spatial gradients. Second, the framework involves an inherent trade-off between throughput and image resolution: our lightweight NCA architecture is optimized for exhaustive slide-wide scanning, whereas highly parameterized models or models operating at higher resolution may be preferable when the clinical workflow is restricted to small, pre-selected ROIs. Third, our preprocessing relies on brightness-based heuristics for tile filtering, which may require manual tuning for variable slide backgrounds. Fourth, the current implementation of HemAutomaton focuses on white blood cells exclusively. The detection and analysis of other diagnostically relevant components such as red blood cell morphology, platelet clumping, and smudge cells are beyond the scope of this work and left for future analysis. Fifth, while our three-cohort evaluation validates the framework's cross-cohort generalizability, its translation into clinical practice requires broader testing across diverse scanners and disease profiles.

In summary, HemAutomaton enables exhaustive single-cell profiling across whole-slide hematology smears. By eliminating manual region selection and requiring minimal annotation for training, the pipeline overcomes conventional sampling constraints to capture complete cellular populations across entire specimens. The parameter-efficient architecture facilitates both large-scale dataset curation for future foundation model training and downstream clinical evaluation, providing a framework for more sensitive AI-supported hematological assessments.

Methods

Extraction Pipeline

Overview

Single-cell detection is performed using a multi-resolution approach, in which cells are detected at a lower resolution to reduce computational cost. All computations are defined relative to a fixed physical target scale corresponding to the resolution at which the detection model was trained. For each input WSI in a pyramidal format, the effective resolutions of the available pyramid levels are determined, and detection is performed at the pyramid level whose resolution most closely matches the target scale. This strategy avoids unnecessary interpolation while ensuring that the detection model operates at a reasonably consistent physical scale across slides. Non-pyramidal images are rescaled to the target scale.

WSIs or large input images are subdivided into regions with optional overlap, and regions containing little relevant information are excluded prior to inference using brightness heuristics. The NCA-based detection model produces spatial activation maps from which single-cell locations are extracted. When overlap is used, spatially redundant detections are resolved using a distance-based merging criterion. Final detected locations are subsequently mapped back to the full-resolution image. An optional quality filter is included, which is implemented using MobileNetV3 [50] to identify and remove low-quality patches, containing smudged cells, blurry images, and other artifacts.

NCA-based cell detection

At the core of HemAutomaton lies an NCA designed for white blood cell center detection. The NCA embeds the RGB image into a latent state and refines this state through repeated local update steps [40]. In our implementation, the model uses a 6-channel state, a hidden size of 128, and a fire rate of 0.5. Each update step applies two learned 3 × 3 convolutions, concatenates the resulting local features with the current state, and passes them through a two-layer multilayer perceptron to complete the update step. The RGB channels are reset to the original image content after each update so that the image information remains available while the latent channels evolve.

The NCA is trained from simple cell-center point annotations rather than bounding boxes or segmentations. Point labels are converted into Gaussian heat maps that serve as training targets, and during optimization, a weighted mean squared error is minimized between the predicted response map and the Gaussian target map (Supplementary Figure 6). Training augmentation consists of random horizontal and vertical flips, and the automaton is unrolled for a randomly sampled number of update steps during training. At inference, one latent channel is interpreted as a response map for cell presence. Candidate cell centers are extracted by Gaussian smoothing followed by local-maxima detection and very closely spaced detections are removed by radius-based deduplication.

Datasets

We assembled data from four independent hematology imaging datasets, comprising whole-slide images (WSIs), manually annotated regions of interest (ROIs), and labeled single-cell images. These data were curated and combined in different configurations for our experiments. An overview of the datasets and their use across this study is provided in the Supplementary Material (Supplementary Figure 1).

WSI data

We used three independent whole-slide microscopy datasets comprising blood and bone marrow smears from different institutions and imaging systems (Figure 1A). The B-ALL dataset comprises 240 WSIs, including 120 BM and 120 PB smears in MRXS format, acquired using a 3DHISTECH P1000 scanner. The Höfener et al. dataset comprises 246 BM WSIs in DICOM format, acquired using 3DHISTECH PANNORAMIC MIDI II and PANNORAMIC 250 Flash scanners. The de Almeida et al. dataset comprises 362 cropped PB WSIs in TIFF format, acquired using a Hamamatsu NanoZoomer 2.0 scanner.

ROI data

Detection annotations were assembled from the B-ALL, Höfener et al., and de Almeida et al. datasets and harmonized to a common one-class white blood cell detection task. For the B-ALL dataset, manual annotations were generated from 10 patients, each represented by one BM and one PB smear. Two ROIs were selected from each of the 20 smears, yielding 40 ROIs in total. Each ROI was constructed to contain exactly 100 annotated white blood cells, by annotating cells sequentially from left to right within a preselected rectangular region and cropping the ROI immediately after the 100th cell, resulting in 4, 000 annotated cells (Figure 4E). The Höfener et al. dataset comprised 226 manually annotated BM ROIs with 22, 631 WBC annotations after excluding instances labeled as artifact, erythrocyte, giant platelet, megakaryocyte, neutrophil extracellular trap, spicule, thrombocyte, or thrombocyte aggregate. For the de Almeida et al. dataset, we used the AC1 subset comprising 158 image patches from 31 acquisition groups. As AC1 provides segmentation masks rather than pointbased detection annotations, binary masks were converted to cell-center annotations by identifying connected components and using their centroids as detection targets. Components smaller than 16 pixels were discarded, yielding 2, 839 annotated cells.

Single-cell data

Single-cell annotations were available for the B-ALL, Höfener et al., and Matek et al. datasets. The 4, 000 manually annotated B-ALL cells described above were independently classified by two experts as blast or non-blast cells. The Höfener et al. and Matek et al. datasets provide multiclass annotations for approximately 27, 000 and 171, 000 single-cell images, respectively. As the two datasets use different original annotation schemes, their labels were mapped to a shared set of six morphological categories: blast cells, neutrophils and progenitors, eosinophils/basophils, nucleated erythroid cells, mononuclear cells and progenitors, and other. The complete mapping between the original annotations and the harmonized cell-type scheme is provided in the Supplementary Material (Supplementary Figure 4).

Three-domain detection benchmark

To evaluate robustness across domains, we established a three-domain benchmark using the curated B-ALL, Höfener et al., and de Almeida et al. ROI datasets described above. All datasets were evaluated as a common one-class center-detection task. Images were processed at an 8× downsampled resolution. Each ROI was converted into overlapping 512 × 512 pixel tiles with stride 384, with edge padding to full tile size. Empty tiles were retained with a probability of 0.25 during training, whereas all tiles were retained during validation.

Five-fold cross-validation was performed independently for each source domain, with ROIs originating from the same patient or acquisition group assigned to the same fold. For each fold and source domain, one learned detection model was trained on the source-domain training partition and evaluated both on the held-out in-domain partition and on the two target domains. The same trained checkpoint was reused unchanged for the two cross-domain evaluations from that source domain.

Training and baselines

The NCA was trained for 20 epochs with Adam (lr = 10 -3 , batch size = 2). Point annotations were converted into Gaussian center maps with standard deviation 3.0 pixels and truncation 6.0, and training minimized a weighted mean squared error loss with positive and negative weights of 8.0 and 1.0, respectively. Both detection models were optimized using the same training data augmentation, consisting of random horizontal and vertical flips of the image patches. During training, the NCA was unrolled for 6 to 10 update steps, with the exact number randomly sampled for each batch. At inference time, full-tile response maps were reconstructed from overlapping 256 × 256 patches with stride 128. The post-processing threshold was selected on the training split by sweeping thresholds between 0.03 and 0.30 to maximize the F1 score. The last training checkpoint was used for evaluation.

We compared HemAutomaton's NCA-based detection model with Faster R-CNN [46] and a blob baseline evaluated on the same tile datasets. The Faster R-CNN model used a ResNet-50-FPN backbone with random initialization [46, 51]. The model was trained for 20 epochs with stochastic gradient descent (learning rate 5 × 10 -4 , momentum 0.9, weight decay 1 × 10 -4 , and batch size 2). Anchor sizes were set to 8, 16, 32, 64, and 128 pixels with aspect ratios of 0.75, 1.0, and 1.33. Detection thresholds were calibrated on the training split by maximizing center-based F1 scores using a match radius of 8 pixels over thresholds between 0.01 and 0.20. For evaluation, predicted boxes were reduced to their center coordinates and greedily deduplicated within 8 pixels. Because NCA and Faster R-CNN represent different detector families with different parameterizations and loss landscapes, each model was optimized using an optimizer commonly used for that architecture rather than enforcing a shared optimizer across methods.

The classical blob baseline used no learned parameters. Instead, it generated a hand-crafted response map from each RGB tile by combining a purple-favoring chromatic term, grayscale darkness, and an HSV-based mask. The resulting response was Gaussian smoothed (σ = 1.2), percentile normalized, and converted to detections by local-maxima extraction. Its absolute response threshold was calibrated on the source-domain training split by maximizing center-based F1 scores.

Evaluation metric

All experiments were evaluated as center-detection tasks. Predicted and ground-truth centers were matched one-to-one if their Euclidean distance was within 8 pixels (∼ 8µm); unmatched predictions were counted as false positives and unmatched annotations as false negatives. We regrouped tiles into their parent ROIs and computed F1 scores per ROI. Supplementary Table 1 summarizes the mean and standard deviation across the five folds.

Computational efficiency evaluation

We benchmarked the computational efficiency of NCA, Faster R-CNN, and the classical blob baseline using an identical tile-based inference pipeline. Experiments were conducted on tiles of size 512 × 512 with a physical resolution of approximately 1µm per pixel. All measurements were performed on a system equipped with an Intel Xeon Gold 6226R CPU and an NVIDIA Tesla V100S GPU (32 GB VRAM). Benchmarks were executed using Python 3.10 with PyTorch and CUDA support. For each method, inference was evaluated on the same set of 12 tiles with batch size 1. Two warm-up passes were performed before timing, followed by two repeated measurements per tile. To ensure consistent measurements, CPU threading was restricted to a single thread. We reported the number of model parameters, floating-point operations (FLOPs) per tile, latency per tile, throughput in tiles per second, and peak memory usage during inference.

Low-data generalization experiment

To quantify sample efficiency under increasing domain shifts, we conducted a low-data experiment where we fixed the training domain to B-ALL BM and systematically varied the amount of single-cell training data. Using the grouped five-fold split from the detection benchmark, we constructed nested budgets of k = 1, ..., 8 source slides. For each selected slide, one ROI was retained for training, so that each budget increment contributed one additional source slide and no two selected ROIs originated from the same slide.

To standardize annotation volume and eliminate intra-slide sampling bias, each training patch was extracted from a distinct source slide and contained exactly 100 annotated white blood cells. Specifically, cells were annotated sequentially from left to right within a pre-selected rectangular ROI, with the patch cropped immediately following the 100th annotated white blood cell. Consequently, each incremental budget step contributed exactly 100 additional annotated cells originating from a unique slide, ensuring no two patches shared a common slide origin. For each budget and fold, NCA and Faster R-CNN were trained from scratch with the same model configurations, augmentation scheme, optimization settings, and threshold-selection procedures used in the full detection benchmark. The blob detection baseline was excluded from this experiment, as the full-data benchmark had already established its inability to achieve generalizable cell localization.

Trained models were evaluated across four distinct validation scenarios: (i) held-out B-ALL BM (in-domain), (ii) B-ALL PB from the same cohort (cross-modality), (iii) the independent BM smear dataset from Höfener et al. [18] (cross-center), and (iv) the independent PB smear dataset from de Almeida et al. [21] (combined cross-modality and cross-center shift). Detection performance was quantified using F1 scores and reported as mean and standard deviation across the five validation folds.

Few-shot classification

Few-shot classification was evaluated using the curated and harmonized Matek et al. and Höfener et al. single-cell datasets described above. Single-cell images were embedded using the frozen pretrained DinoBloom-S feature extractor. To obtain a morphologically diverse set of target-domain cells for annotation, embeddings were clustered using k-means and representative cells were selected from each cluster.

Prototype-and example-based classification

Following annotation of the selected examples, the remaining target cells were classified using cosine similarity in the DinoBloom embedding space. For each query cell, the five nearest annotated target cells were identified and their labels combined by majority vote. To account for cell types that are absent or only sparsely represented among the labeled target cells, source-domain prototypes were additionally incorporated. Source-domain prototypes were computed by averaging feature embeddings within each fine-grained source class and subsequently mapped to the harmonized target label space. Source-and target-derived class probabilities were combined according to p final = (1 -α)p source + αp target , α = n anchors n anchors + τ where n anchors denotes the number of labeled target examples available for the corresponding harmonized cell type and τ is a regularization parameter. As additional target annotations become available, the prediction progressively shifts from source-domain prototypes towards target-domain examples.

Experimental setup

For each target dataset, five folds were generated, where one fold was held out for testing while the remaining cells formed the target pool for cluster-guided exemplar selection. The number of clusters was set to the number of fine-grained cell categories present in the respective dataset, and we set τ to 25. We evaluated annotation budgets of k ∈ {1, 2, 5, 10, 20} representative cells per cluster. Performance was summarized using macro-F1 scores. Supplementary Table 4 reports results for all annotation budgets, while Supplementary Figure 5 shows the confusion matrices of the best-performing configurations.

Spatial variance of blast frequencies

Spatial analyses were performed on the B-ALL dataset using single-cell images automatically extracted by the proposed pipeline. For the descriptive whole-slide analyses, the few-shot classifier was adapted to binary blast versus non-blast classification using all 4, 000 expert-annotated single-cell images from the B-ALL dataset. Evaluation of the classifier for blast-frequency estimation was performed separately using patient-level cross-validation.

Local blast frequency

For each detected cell, the local blast frequency was estimated from its 500 nearest neighboring cells. The local blast frequency was defined as the fraction of cells predicted as blasts within this neighborhood. In this way, each detected cell serves as a discrete sampling point that represents the local cellular composition.

Smear position

To quantify spatial trends along the smear, each detected cell was assigned a relative position within the analyzable smear region. Tiles passing the brightness filter were grouped into contiguous vertical smear segments. For each segment, the mean grayscale intensity of the uppermost and lowermost three tiles was compared to identify the bright (feathered edge) and dark (dense smear) ends. The relative smear position of cells was defined as the normalized distance between these two ends, yielding values between 0 (dense smear region) and 1 (feathered edge). Cells that could not be assigned to a valid smear segment were excluded from smear-position analyses.

Two independent hematology experts (A and B) classified the same 100 cells in each of two ROIs per slide as blast or non-blast cells.
