Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

P = 0.057 Metastatic disease at diagnosis was numerically more common in MC3high than in MC3-low patients (Fisher’s exact test, adjusted P = 0.057), but high MC3 remained associated with shorter OS after adjustment for metastatic status.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Clear cell sarcoma (CCS) is a rare soft-tissue sarcoma with poor prognosis and limited therapeutic options. Its tumour microenvironment and tumour cell heterogeneity remain largely unexplored. Within the Clear Cell Sarcoma Consortium, we used imaging mass cytometry with two antibody panels to map more than 5 million cells from a multi-institutional, clinically annotated cohort of 52 patients. Together, this work provides a detailed map of the spatial proteomic landscape of CCS and highlights candidate markers for patient stratification and potential therapeutic targets.

INTRODUCTION

Clear cell sarcoma (CCS) is a rare, translocation-associated soft-tissue sarcoma that predominantly arises in the tendons and aponeuroses of the lower extremities in young adults (Enzinger, 1965; Gonzaga et al., 2018; Kawai et al., 2007). Although it may initially present as an indolent lesion, CCS is clinically aggressive, with early metastatic spread, frequent local recurrence and poor long-term survival; overall survival rates across all stages are approximately 50% at five years and 40% at ten years (Bianchi et al., 2014; Clark et al., 2008; Gonzaga et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2025). Therapeutic options for advanced disease remain extremely limited (Wetterwald et al., 2023). CCS is largely refractory to conventional chemotherapy and radiotherapy, and targeted and immune-based therapies have shown only modest clinical activity (Gronchi et al., 2021; Jones et al., 2011; Martin-Broto et al., 2020; Martin-Broto et al., 2026; Merchant et al., 2016; Schöffski et al., 2017). CCS is classified as an ultra-rare cancer, with a reported annual incidence of fewer than 0.5 cases per million people, which limits clinical trials and the discovery of actionable biological targets (Stacchiotti et al., 2021). CCS tumour tissue samples from multi-institutional cohorts, with linked clinical data, are therefore also sparse and particularly valuable for advancing the understanding of this disease.

At the molecular level, CCS is defined by recurrent EWSR1 rearrangements, most commonly EWSR1::ATF1, with rarer fusions involving genes encoding other members of the CREB transcription factor family (Straessler et al., 2013; Yamada et al., 2013; Zucman et al., 1993). These oncogenic fusions encode aberrant transcription factors that result in melanocytic differentiation, accounting for melanocytic marker expression and the historical designation of CCS as melanoma of soft parts (Straessler et al., 2013; Yamada et al., 2013; Zucman et al., 1993). Recent genomic and transcriptomic studies have begun to define the genomic landscape of CCS (Lee et al., 2021; Panza et al., 2021; Rasmussen et al., 2023). However, these studies have relied almost exclusively on bulk profiling approaches, masking cellular heterogeneity and eliminating spatial context. As a result, fundamental questions regarding the composition, spatial organization, and functional interplay of the CCS tumour microenvironment (TME) remain unanswered. Here, we applied high-dimensional, spatially resolved single-cell proteomic profiling to specimens from a multi-institutional cohort of clinically characterized CCS patients assembled through the Clear Cell Sarcoma Consortium. This retrospective exploratory approach enabled systematic mapping of the tumour and immune compartments in CCS and the identification of prognostic features that might aid patient stratification and the development of individual treatment approaches.

RESULTS Clear cell sarcoma cohort

The aim of the study was to use spatial proteomics to analyse the tumour ecosystem in CCS and to relate it to prognostic features and potential therapeutic targets (Fig. 1A). We assembled formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) samples from a clinically annotated cohort of CCS patients for spatial proteomic profiling (Fig. 1B; Sup. Table S1). Diagnoses were established according to the 2020 WHO Classification of Soft Tissue and Bone Tumours (WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board, 2020) and were confirmed by an expert sarcoma pathologist who reviewed H&E-stained slides of samples from each case. To minimize treatment-related confounding, only specimens obtained before any systemic therapy were included in our analysis. After central case review, 56 patients were retained for imaging mass cytometry (IMC) analysis. Regions of interest (ROIs) were selected by a pathologist and classified as tumour centre, invasive front, or lymph-node metastasis for downstream analyses (Fig. 1E). From these ROIs, we constructed three tissue microarrays (TMAs). Laser-microdissected regions from a subset of these patients were additionally profiled by deep visual proteomics (DVP), and H&E-stained sections of the same ROIs were used for deeplearning analysis (Fig. 1A; Methods).

A single-cell, high-dimensional spatial atlas of CCS

To characterize phenotypic heterogeneity and the spatial organization of tumour cells relative to the TME, we performed high-dimensional, spatial proteomic profiling using IMC (Giesen et al., 2014) (Fig. 1C). TMA sections were stained with two metalconjugated antibody panels (Fig. 1D) designed to characterize both the tumour and the immune microenvironment. Panel I included broad immune-lineage markers, activation, exhaustion and proliferation markers for T cells (TCF1/7, PD-1, CTLA-4, LAG-3, FOXP3, granzyme B, Ki-67) and for myeloid cells (HLA-DR, PD-L1, IDO1, arginase-1, CD11c), together with tumour-state and architectural markers (Fig. 1D; Sup. Table S2; Fig. S1A). Panel II was designed to spatially screen tumour-antigen expression and identify candidates for antibody-drug conjugate (ADC) targeting (Fig. 1D; Sup. Table S3; Fig. S2A). With panel I, we acquired 460 high-dimensional images, which were segmented into approximately 3 million single cells. Image preprocessing and single-cell segmentation were performed using established IMC analysis pipelines (Windhager et al., 2023) (see Methods). After quality control and exclusion of control cores, we retained 427 images from 52 patients, comprising 2,870,352 cells; four patients were excluded from downstream analysis following quality control. With panel II, 412 images from the same 52 patients, comprising 2,513,554 cells, were retained after quality control. No batch effects were apparent on visual inspection of the uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) embeddings with respect to TMA, sample origin, patient identity, or ROI category (Fig. S1B, Fig. S2B).

To define cellular phenotypes, we trained an image-based neural network using the CellSighter framework (Amitay et al., 2023) on our dataset (Fig. S3A-G; see Methods). This approach enabled classification into eight broad cell types: B cells, plasma cells, CD4⁺ T cells, CD8⁺ T cells, myeloid cells, tumour cells, vessels, and stromal cells. UMAP visualization revealed clear separation of the major cell types into distinct clusters (Fig. 1F), accompanied by coherent expression of canonical lineage and state markers (Fig. 1G). Tumour cells constituted the dominant population across most samples (Fig. 1H). This single-cell, spatially resolved proteomic atlas of CCS provides a framework for studying tumour-cell heterogeneity and the organization of the immune microenvironment in this rare malignancy. Next, we assessed tumour-immune interactions in the tumour centre and invasive front using a neighbourhood-based model that accounts for cell abundance (see Methods). Across both regions, raw tumour-immune interaction counts were dominated by macrophages, with interactions between tumour cells and CD68⁺ macrophages, CD163⁺ HLA-DRhigh, and CD163⁺ HLA-DRlow macrophages together constituting about three-quarters (76%) of all tumour-immune cell contacts (Fig. 2J; Fig. S4H). We then focused on these macrophage populations and used an approach that accounted for regional differences in cell abundance to compare infiltration scores between the tumour centre and the invasive front (see Methods and Fig. S5A-E). Infiltration scores were comparable between regions for CD163⁺ HLA-DRhigh, CD163⁺ HLA-DRlow and CD68⁺ macrophages (Fig. 2K and Fig. S5A-E), suggesting similar tumour-macrophage interactions across tumour regions, despite differences in cell density. Deconvolution of bulk transcriptome data (Chen et al., 2018) in a subset of the primary cohort (n = 23 patients) supported a predominantly macrophage-derived myeloid signal in these tumours (Fig. S4K-M). Next, we assessed whether the immune cell composition was associated with patient outcome. In Cox proportional hazards models adjusted for age, sex and metastatic status at diagnosis, no immune cell population was associated with overall survival (OS) at both cut-points (mean and median split); CD15⁺ granulocyte abundance was associated with OS at the mean split only (Fig. S4I-J). For validation of these findings, we performed IMC on an independent CCS cohort from KU Leuven (n = 20 patients passing quality control; Sup. Table S4) using the same antibody panel and an analogous analytical approach. Consistent with the primary cohort, this validation cohort exhibited low immune infiltration, dominated by myeloid cells (Fig. S6A-F). We also fitted survival models for the immune subtypes in this cohort. However, with only 13 patients with usable follow-up and 10 events, the analysis had limited statistical power, so the lack of a detected association does not provide evidence that no association exists (Fig. S6K-L). CCS was historically referred to as "malignant melanoma of soft parts". Given the phenotypic similarities between CCS and melanoma, a long-standing question is whether their tumour immune microenvironments also share features. To address this question, we compared the CCS TME with that of cutaneous melanoma (CM), an immunogenic malignancy driven by distinct intrinsic genetic mechanisms and exogenous mutagenic influences of ultraviolet light (Fig. S7A) (Akbani et al., 2015; Wetterwald et al., 2023). We analysed a CM cohort (n = 38 patients; 152 ROIs) (Fig. S7B; Sup. Table S5), stained with the same IMC antibody panel used for CCS. Using the same phenotyping framework as for CCS, we then jointly analysed immune cells from both cohorts; UMAP analysis showed no cohort-specific segregation suggestive of batch effects (Fig. S7C). Compared with CM, CCS showed lower immune cell density and diversity (Shannon entropy), but a higher myeloid fraction (Fig. S7D-G).

Definition of CD8⁺ T cells, CD4⁺ T cells, and myeloid/macrophage populations across both cohorts (Fig. S7H) revealed enrichment of CD68⁺ and CD163⁺ macrophages in CCS upon differential abundance analysis, whereas lymphoid populations, including exhausted PD-1⁺ CD4⁺ and PD-1⁺ CD8⁺ T cells, were enriched in CM (Fig. S7I). These macrophages constituted a larger immune cell fraction in CCS despite comparable or lower absolute densities (Fig. S7J-K).

Collectively, these findings indicate that CCS tumours are "immune-cold" with a myeloid-dominated, spatially compartmentalized microenvironment and a more immune-diverse invasive front compared with the tumour centre. Despite shared melanocytic differentiation, CCS and CM have distinct immune ecosystems, with greater immune cell abundance and diversity in CM, further supporting the classification of CCS as "immune-cold". A proliferative tumour cell state is associated with CCS patient outcome Next, we focused on the tumour compartment to determine whether distinct tumour cell phenotypes have prognostic relevance in CCS. We identified 34 tumour clusters via graph-based clustering of tumour cells using markers of cell state (Fig. 3A), and subsequently metaclustered these based on mean marker expression into 5 tumour metaclusters (MCs) (Fig. 3A, Fig. S8A-C). These tumour MCs separated distinctly on the UMAP embedding (Fig. 3B) and were further evaluated by visual inspection of the corresponding IMC images (Fig. S8D). Each MC displayed a distinct expression pattern with specific dominant markers: MC1 was characterized by NGFR expression, MC3 by Ki-67, MC4 by HLA-DR and MC5 by TYRP1; only MC2 lacked a defining marker-expression pattern from our antibody panel (Fig. 3A, Fig. S8D). All tumour MCs were present both in the tumour centre and at the invasive front, and most ROIs were dominated by one to three MCs (Fig. 3C). Shannon entropy was higher at the invasive front than in the tumour centre, indicating greater phenotypic diversity at the tumour margin (Fig. 3D). However, no MC was significantly enriched at one location versus the other after correction for multiple testing (Fig. 3E; Fig. S8H). In contrast to the immune infiltrate, therefore, tumour MCs in CCS do not exhibit spatial compartmentalization of their composition. We then asked whether specific tumour MCs were associated with patient outcomes. In Cox proportional-hazards models adjusted for age, sex and metastatic status at diagnosis, with each MC fraction dichotomized at either its median or its mean, a high MC3 fraction and metastatic status were associated with shorter OS at both cut-points (Fig. 3F-G). Metastatic disease at diagnosis was numerically more common in MC3high than in MC3-low patients (Fisher's exact test, adjusted P = 0.057), but high MC3 remained associated with shorter OS after adjustment for metastatic status. Kaplan-Meier estimates confirmed the association between MC3 and OS (Fig. 3H). The association was also present when MC3 was kept continuous rather than dichotomized (P = 0.041; adjusted model, see Methods). Consistent with the high Ki-67 expression that characterized MC3, mean tumour-cell Ki-67 expression was also associated with OS (Fig. 3I). Because high Ki-67 expression defines MC3, the two analyses capture the same underlying proliferation signal, with the Ki-67 analysis expressing it in terms of a routinely measured marker (Fig. 3I). We found no evidence of a correlation between MC3 and any individual immune cell population (Fig. S8E) or of MC cooccurrence beyond that expected from compositional constraints (Fig. S8F-G

, see Methods).

The five tumour metaclusters were also detected in the external validation cohort (Fig. S6G-J). In this cohort, both the proliferative MC3 metacluster and Ki-67 expression were associated with poorer overall survival (Fig. S6M-N); however, these associations did not reach statistical significance, likely owing to the limited number of survival events in the external cohort (10 events in 13 patients) and the resulting low statistical power. A proliferation-associated proteomic signature is present in CCS patients with poor outcomes Next, to extend our analysis beyond the markers measured by IMC, we used DVP, that is, LC-MS/MS proteomic profiling of laser-microdissected tissue, to characterize global proteomic signatures in CCS. For this, we used the IMC data to guide the selection of tumour regions from the same FFPE TMAs for laser microdissection and subsequent proteomic profiling, selecting a subset of patients from our cohort stratified by the MC3 fraction defined in the IMC analysis: patients were sampled from above and below the MC3 median, which in this cohort also separates shorter from longer overall survival (Fig. 3H; Fig. S9). An initial pilot experiment involving four patients (42 cores: 26 tumour and 16 stromal regions) confirmed the suitability of the material for untargeted mass spectrometry-based proteomics and revealed coherent, compartment-specific biological signals (Fig. S10A-G). In the final experiment, 7,276 proteins quantified across 141 cores from 21 patients were retained for downstream analysis (Fig. 4A; Fig. S9). Paired tumour-centre and invasive-front regions from all 21 patients with both regions profiled, pooled across tumour MCs, showed similar proteomic profiles (Fig. S10H-J) and strong overall correlation (Fig. 4B). However, pathway-level analysis revealed enrichment of metabolic pathway terms in the tumour centre, including glycolysis/Warburg metabolism, oxidative phosphorylation, fatty-acid metabolism, mTORC1 signalling, and hypoxia responses, whereas the G2/M checkpoint and cellmotility pathways were enriched at the invasive front (Fig. 4C; Fig. S10K-L). These findings are consistent with spatially compartmentalized pathway activity in CCS tumours. Next, to investigate associations with survival, we compared the proteomes of patients stratified by the median MC3 fraction from the IMC analysis into MC3-high/short-OS and MC3-low/long-OS groups (Fig. 4A, Fig. S9). Protein coverage was comparable between the two subgroups (Fig. 4D). Notably, the CCS proteomes separated according to these survival groups in a principal component analysis (PCA), when analysed at the level of individual TMA cores (Fig. 4E) and when biological replicates were aggregated per patient (Fig. 4F). PC1, which separated the survival groups and explained 8.4% of total variance, was primarily driven by proteins associated with cellcycle progression, DNA replication, and proliferation (the minichromosome maintenance complex MCM2-MCM7, PBK, KPNA2, CDK1, TOP2A, and NCAPG) in the short-survival group, consistent with a proteomic proliferation signature in MC3enriched tumours. In contrast, the long-survival group was enriched in proteins involved in mitochondrial and metabolic processes (CA5B, FABP3, MOXD1, and GLUD1) (Fig. 4G). Differential expression analysis revealed survival group-specific protein enrichments (Fig. 4H). Protein-set enrichment analysis showed that shortsurvival proteomes were enriched for the terms G2/M checkpoint, E2F targets, DNA repair, and unfolded protein response, whereas long-survival proteomes were enriched for oxidative phosphorylation and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (Fig. 4I). Unsupervised hierarchical clustering based on the most differentially abundant proteins also separated the two survival groups (Fig. 4J). Next, to assess associations with survival, we calculated protein module scores for all Hallmark pathways with sufficient quantified proteins and tested each score in a separate Cox model. A higher E2F-target protein module score was associated with shorter overall survival (Fig. 4K). The E2F-target module score also correlated with the proportion of metacluster MC3 in the same patients (Fig. 4L), linking the proteomic proliferation signature to the IMC-defined MC3 state. The oxidative phosphorylation module, which had the highest enrichment score in the long-OS subgroup, was not associated with survival (Fig. 4M). In summary, spatial profiling with DVP extended the characterization of the IMCdefined MC3 state from a single proliferation marker to a proteome-wide proliferation signature that includes replication licensing (MCM2-MCM7), cell-cycle regulation, DNA repair, and unfolded protein response. H&E-stained images of CCS tumours contain prognostic information

Because MC3 is measured by IMC, which is not available in routine diagnostics, we next asked whether the MC3 state can be recognized in routine H&E-stained slides, which would make it usable for future clinical decisions. We trained machine-learning models to predict, from H&E images alone, whether a patient's tumour had a low or high MC3 fraction (below or above the median used in Fig. 3H). For this analysis, we used H&E-stained sections consecutive to the IMCprofiled samples. Because pathology foundation models analyse small image tiles rather than whole cores, each core image was divided into non-overlapping tiles (patches), and each patch was converted into a feature vector by Phikon-v2 (Filiot et al., 2024), using the TRIDENT pipeline (Zhang et al., 2025) (Fig. 5A). The patch features of each core were then combined and used to classify the core as MC3-low or MC3-high with three machine-learning approaches: support vector machine (SVM), random forest, and an attention-based multiple-instance learning (MIL) model (Attn-MIL) (Fig. 5A). Before classification, we removed patches without tumour cells. The remaining patch feature vectors were grouped into ten clusters of similar morphology by k-means clustering (Fig. S11A-D). Representative patches of each cluster were manually inspected, and the one cluster consisting predominantly of non-tumour tissue was excluded (Fig. S11A). All three models predicted low versus high tumour MC3 abundance above chance (patient-level area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC-ROC) 0.81 for SVM, 0.81 for random forest, and 0.80 for Attn-MIL; 95% bootstrap confidence intervals 0.67-0.93, 0.66-0.94 and 0.64-0.93, respectively), and all three exceeded a null distribution obtained by permuting the patient labels 2,000 times (Fig. 5B-C). Performance varied across folds, especially for Attn-MIL (fold-wise AUC-ROC 0.60-0.85 for SVM, 0.54-0.86 for random forest, and 0.53-0.78 for Attn-MIL), consistent with the modest cohort size (Fig. 5D). At a 0.5 threshold, all models classified MC3low versus MC3-high H&E cores with distinct sensitivity profiles (Fig. 5E). Because MC3 is associated with high Ki-67 expression, we next asked whether its abundance could be read directly from this marker, which is routinely quantified in clinical practice. As expected, per-patient mean tumour-cell Ki-67 expression correlated closely with MC3 abundance (Fig. 5F-G). Because Ki-67 is in effect the defining marker of MC3, the higher Ki-67 in MC3-high patients follows from how the groups are defined rather than being an independent finding. H&E classifiers trained on the Ki-67 median split therefore performed similarly to those trained on the MC3 split (patient-level AUC-ROC 0.81, 0.76, and 0.76 for SVM, random forest, and Attn-MIL, respectively; Fig. 5H-I), indicating that the morphological signal is linked to Ki-67 expression. Together, these results show that H&E morphology identifies the IMC-defined, poorprognosis tumours with Ki-67 expression serving as a potential surrogate marker. Tumour composition and proteome are largely preserved between primary tumours and lymph-node metastases

To assess whether primary and metastatic tumours resemble one another, we compared tumour metacluster composition and proteome profiles between matched primary tumours and lymph-node metastases in paired samples from our cohort (n = 11 patients). Primary tumour phenotypic compositions were broadly conserved at the metastatic sites. The pairwise correlation of tumour metaclusters was similar within primary tumours, between primary and metastatic samples, and within metastases (Fig. 6A). Further, PCA on the tumour metacluster composition showed substantial overlap between primary and metastatic samples (Fig. 6B). Compositional diversity was also similar between sites, with no differences in any of the indices examined (Fig. 6C). The proliferative metacluster MC3 was more abundant in metastases in 8 of 11 patients, although this was not statistically significant (Fig. 6D-E). Similarly, proteomic profiles of paired samples showed no clear separation by PCA (n = 9 patients) (Fig. 6F; Fig. S10M-O). However, we identified 42 proteins as differentially abundant between primary and metastatic samples (Fig. 6G); the protein most significantly enriched in metastasis was SERPINB9 (Fig. 6G, H), an intracellular serine protease inhibitor previously described as promoting metastasis in different types of neoplasm (Bian et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2024). A separate paired analysis yielded a similar result, with higher SERPINB9 abundance in metastases than in matched primary tumours in 8 of 9 patients. Further, gene-set enrichment analysis revealed enrichments of proliferationand cell-cycle-related programmes, the proteasome axis, and the G2/M checkpoint pathway in metastases and of the oxidative phosphorylation pathway in primary tumours (Fig. S10P). We therefore investigated whether CCS tumour cells express surface antigens that could serve as targets for ADCs. Our IMC panels included 15 antigens that have previously been evaluated in clinical trials and preclinical models of solid tumours (HER2, HER3, c-MET, FOLR1, MUC16, cadherin-6, LY6E, TROP2, EGFR, NaPi2b, tissue factor, claudin-6, AXL, CD70 in panel II; B7-H3 in panel I; Fig. S3H-M). Across individual cores, there was heterogeneous expression of these proteins on tumour cells with B7-H3 and c-MET exhibiting the highest expression levels (Fig. 7A). To rank the target antigens, we scored each on five criteria: tumour selectivity, expression homogeneity, patient coverage, percentage of tumour cells positive for the antigen, and median expression on tumour cells. Each score was min-max normalized to 0-1 across all 15 antigens, and the five scaled values were averaged into a composite score. This analysis identified B7-H3 (composite score 0.91) and c-MET (composite score 0.68) as the two highest-ranking targets (Fig. 7B); expression of both was then confirmed visually (Fig. 7C). For both proteins, tumour-cell expression did not differ significantly between the tumour centre and the invasive front and was correlated between the two regions (Fig. 7D-E), indicative of spatially stable target availability within tumours. In the matched primary and metastatic samples, expression of neither antigen differed significantly (Fig. 7F). Both antigens were also detectable by immunohistochemistry (Fig. 7G). Finally, neither B7-H3 nor c-MET was associated with overall survival (Fig. 7H-I), an important consideration when evaluating clinical relevance as the target of an ADC. These data nominate c-MET and B7-H3 as candidate ADC targets in CCS.

DISCUSSION

CCS is a rare and aggressive soft-tissue sarcoma (Cornillie et al., 2016; Ikuta et al., 2023; Wetterwald et al., 2023). To characterize the microenvironment of this type of tumour, we analysed samples from a multicentre cohort of 52 CCS patients resulting in a single-cell, spatially resolved proteomic atlas of the disease. Together with global proteomic analysis of laser-captured tissue, this atlas enabled detailed spatial characterization of the tumour and immune compartments in CCS and assessment of their associations with clinical outcome. We showed that CCS tumours are immune-cold with sparse, myeloid-dominated immune infiltrates especially in the tumour centre, where macrophages are the main immune cells interacting with tumour cells. Consistent with the immune-low/immunedesert sarcoma classes described by Petitprez et al. (Petitprez et al., 2020), which respond poorly to checkpoint blockade, we observed few lymphoid features and no tertiary lymphoid structures in tumours from this CCS cohort. In contrast to studies on carcinoma, where a myeloid-to-lymphoid gradient with lymphoid enrichment at the tumour margin has been reported in colorectal cancer (Lin et al., 2023) and hepatocellular carcinoma (Ruf et al., 2023), the immune organization at the invasive front remains virtually unexplored in sarcomas. We made use of our unique dataset to study this. Our analysis revealed spatial compartmentalization of the immune infiltrate in CCS. The invasive front was consistently more immune-diverse than the tumour centre, with higher immune entropy, greater lymphoid density, and a broader spectrum of T-cell states, including exhausted PD-1⁺ and TCF1/7⁺ naïve-like T cells. In contrast, the tumour centre was dominated by myeloid cells, mainly CD68⁺ macrophages and CD163⁺ HLA-DRhigh or HLA-DRlow macrophages; together, these accounted for about three-quarters of all tumour-immune cell contacts in these tumours. Notably, once the regional difference in abundance was accounted for, the infiltration score of all three macrophage populations was comparable between the tumour centre and the invasive front, indicating that the higher relative myeloid presence in the tumour centre is carried by macrophage abundance rather than by a difference in how closely macrophages engage tumour cells. Overall, the spatial compartmentalization of immune cells suggests that there is some feature of CCS tumours that acts as a barrier to immune infiltration, with myeloid-lineage cells being the main immune population able to enter the tumour core. However, whether this reflects a physical barrier formed by tumour cells or by stroma, chemokine gradients, or vascular access cannot be resolved from these data. To our knowledge, this is the first study to characterize the immune-cell composition at the invasive front in a sarcoma subtype. Our IMC analysis further identified distinct tumour cell phenotypes within CCS, including a proliferative cell type associated with poor outcome. In contrast, immune composition, whether at the invasive front or in the tumour centre, showed no robust association with outcome. Our DVP analysis extended the characterization of the IMCdefined MC3 proliferative tumour cell state from a single marker to a broader proteomewide signature involving replication licensing (MCM2-MCM7), cell-cycle regulation, DNA repair, and the unfolded protein response. Together, these complementary modalities support the presence of a subgroup of CCS patients characterized by high proliferative activity and poor clinical outcome. As no established classification or grading system exists for CCS, Ki-67 may serve as a simple, clinically applicable surrogate marker for the proliferative cell type and its associated proteome-wide proliferation signature. Furthermore, we showed that this prognostic information was also partially captured by standard H&E images, further paving the way for personalized patient stratification. From a therapeutic perspective, our atlas indicates that CCS lacks key features typically associated with checkpoint inhibitor response: T cells are sparse, and macrophages dominate the immune compartment. This is consistent with the limited activity of immune-checkpoint blockade in CCS, including in the OSCAR trial (NCCH1510; trial registration UMIN000023665), in which nivolumab achieved no objective responses among the 12 CCS patients enrolled (Nishikawa et al., 2024). Our data are also consistent with the limited activity of PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors across sarcoma (D'Angelo et al., 2018; Tawbi et al., 2017). Given the myeloid-rich immune microenvironment observed in our cohort, strategies that target macrophage-mediated immune exclusion, such as CSF1R inhibition combined with PD-1 blockade (Zhu et al., 2014), warrant investigation. We note that very recently, the single-arm phase II IMMUNOSARC II trial (NCT03277924) demonstrated clinical activity of sunitinib combined with nivolumab in advanced CCS with partial responses in 3 of 21 radiologically evaluable patients (14.3%), a median progression-free survival of 6.2 months, and a 6-month progression-free survival rate of 50.1% (Martin-Broto et al., 2026). These findings support further investigation of antiangiogenic therapy combined with checkpoint blockade; however, the non-randomized, single-arm design precludes determining the contribution of either agent to the observed activity. In our cohort, the presence of PD-1⁺ and TCF1/7⁺ T cell populations at the invasive front may offer a biological rationale for response in a subset of CCS patients. Because none of the cases included in this study received such drugs, treatment response could not be assessed. Antigen-directed therapies such as bispecific T cell engagers have recently emerged as potential treatment strategies for CCS. Unlike checkpoint inhibitors, T cellredirecting therapies can target tumour-restricted antigens. Based on this rationale, tebentafusp, a gp100-HLA-A*02:01-directed CD3-directed T-cell engager with clinical activity in metastatic uveal melanoma (Nathan et al., 2021), is being evaluated in a phase II trial (NCT06942442) in patients with HLA-A*02:01-positive unresectable or metastatic CCS. Our data nominate c-MET and B7-H3 as candidate antigen-directed therapeutic targets in CCS. Because antibody-drug conjugate activity depends primarily on cellsurface antigen expression rather than inhibition of the target's signaling function, ADCs may be effective even when the antigen is not a key oncogenic driver (Drago et al., 2021; Tarantino et al., 2022). They may also retain activity at antigen-expression levels below the thresholds used for kinase-directed therapies (Modi et al., 2022). Relevant to this, trastuzumab deruxtecan, an ADC targeting HER2, has shown activity in desmoplastic small round cell tumour (Slotkin et al., 2026), which, like CCS, is a fusion-driven rare sarcoma. c-MET has been shown to be upregulated in CCS (Davis et al., 2010; Ikuta et al., 2023). The prospective EORTC 90101 'CREATE' trial (NCT01524926) evaluated the MET inhibitor crizotinib in advanced CCS and reported limited clinical activity, with few objective responses by RECIST (Schöffski et al., 2017). Given the limited clinical activity of crizotinib in CCS, an alternative strategy would be to use an ADC that binds cell-surface c-MET to deliver a cytotoxic payload. Telisotuzumab vedotin, a c-MET-directed ADC, has shown efficacy in different types of tumours (Camidge et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2017). In CCS, we observed uniformly high c-MET expression across the tumour centre and invasive front and in both primary and metastatic samples. This consistency supports investigating c-MET as a target for ADC-based approaches. Our other proposed target, B7-H3, is broadly expressed across soft-tissue sarcomas (Lynch et al., 2024), and B7-H3-directed agents are in clinical development: the antibody-drug conjugate ifinatamab deruxtecan achieved a confirmed objective response rate of 48.2% in previously treated extensive-stage small-cell lung cancer (Rudin et al., 2026). CCS is listed among the eligible diseases of an ongoing paediatric and young-adult phase I B7-H3 CAR T-cell trial (NCT04483778). As for c-MET, our study provides the spatial expression pattern of this target, demonstrating homogeneous expression across tumour regions and between primary and metastatic samples. Also, neither B7-H3 nor c-MET expression was associated with overall survival, so selecting patients on target expression would not by itself enrich for a prognostic subgroup, a point relevant to the design and interpretation of potential clinical trials. In conclusion, our single-cell spatial proteomic analysis identifies CCS as an immunecold, myeloid-dominated tumour with a spatially compartmentalized immune infiltrate and a Ki-67-high tumour cell state associated with both a related proteomic signature and poorer survival. Further, we propose c-MET and B7-H3 as candidate targets for antibody-drug conjugates and other antigen-directed approaches in CCS. This study has limitations. First, the number of patient samples was limited due to the inherent challenges of studying ultra-rare neoplasms like CCS, for which high-quality, clinically annotated tissue is extremely difficult to obtain. Therefore, this study should be interpreted in the context of its exploratory design. Second, the spatial analyses relied on TMA cores, which sample only a limited fraction of the tumour and may therefore underestimate intratumoural heterogeneity. To mitigate this limitation, multiple cores were analysed for all cases. Third, as this study was based on retrospectively collected human samples, it was descriptive in nature and could identify associations but not establish causality. Fourth, the prognostic associations of MC3 abundance and tumour-cell Ki-67 expression require independent validation before clinical application. The median cut-offs used to define high and low groups were exploratory and derived from the study cohort. Future studies should use a prespecified scoring protocol to assess tumour-cell Ki-67 by immunohistochemistry and quantify mitoses on whole-slide sections in a large, independent CCS cohort. Finally, and more broadly, these limitations reflect structural challenges inherent to research in ultra-rare sarcomas rather than weaknesses specific to the present study. Patient-partnered initiatives have demonstrated the potential to accelerate recruitment in this setting (Painter et al., 2020). Looking forward, prospective, standardized collection of CCS tissue alongside plasma and blood samples will be an important next step towards a more comprehensive understanding of CCS. The present study, conducted within the Clear Cell Sarcoma Consortium initiative, lays the groundwork for these efforts.

Image acquisition

Images were acquired on a Hyperion XTI Imaging System (Standard BioTools). Each core was laser-ablated at a frequency of 800 Hz. Cores were selected for acquisition in random order, and each TMA was acquired in one continuous run.

Image processing, segmentation, and quality control

Raw data were processed with steinbock (v0.16.1) following the published IMC workflow (Windhager et al., 2023). MCD files were converted to OME-TIFF, and cells were segmented with the pre-trained Mesmer model (Greenwald et al., 2022) using nuclear (Ir191 and Ir193) and cytoplasmic (S100b, MelanA, SMA, CD31, CD45, and CD68) channels. Whole-cell masks containing zero or more than one nucleus were removed, and nuclei outside a whole-cell mask were expanded by 2 μm to generate cells. Mean marker intensities, morphological features (area and eccentricity), and neighbourhood information were extracted per cell, the last from a spatial cell graph constructed by pixel expansion in steinbock, as previously described (Meyer et al., 2026; Windhager et al., 2023). Channel spillover was compensated using a spillover slide (Chevrier et al., 2018). Single-cell data and images were imported into R with imcRtools and cytomapper (Eling et al., 2020) and stored as SingleCellExperiment objects (Amezquita et al., 2020). Mean intensities were arcsinh transformed with a cofactor of 1 (arc = asinh[raw]). Quality control was performed using pre-established criteria. At the cell level, objects with a segmented area of 5 μm 2 or less were discarded as segmentation debris. At the image level, control cores containing normal tissue and cores with insufficient tissue or retraction artefacts were excluded following visual inspection. For panel I, 427 images from 52 patients (2,870,352 cells) were retained; for panel II, 412 images from the same 52 patients (2,513,554 cells). Marker performance was assessed per channel by fitting a two-component Gaussian mixture (mclust (Scrucca et al., 2016)) to the arcsinh values and taking the ratio of the mean raw intensities of the positive and negative components as the signal-to-noise ratio; a ratio of 2 was used as the reference line for flagging weak channels. Remaining ROIs and patients were then screened for outliers without further automatic exclusion: ROIs whose mean cell area or whose mean expression of any marker deviated from the cohort median by more than three median absolute deviations, and patients contributing fewer than two ROIs were flagged for manual review. Batch effects were examined by comparing marker distributions, ROI-level principal components, and silhouette widths across the three TMAs, and cell density and nearest-neighbour distance distributions were inspected per ROI.

Cell type classification

Cell type classification was performed with a customized version of an image-based neural network approach (CellSighter, Fig. S3A) (Amitay et al., 2023). A training set was created by labelling a subset of all detected cells by manual gating: cells that could be unambiguously assigned to one of eight broad types (tumour cells, vessels, stromal cells, CD4 + T cells, CD8 + T cells, myeloid cells, B cells, and plasma cells) on the basis of key lineage markers were added to the training set, and all other cells were labelled 'undefined' (Fig. S3B). A network was trained and tested on this dataset with an 80/20 split (Fig. S3D), and the trained network was then used to predict the type of every cell that had not been manually labelled. To confirm that the phenotype of network-labelled cells was consistent with that of manually labelled cells, the expression patterns of the two subsets were compared visually (Fig. S3C, F). The weighted F1 score of the network trained on manually labelled cells was 0.988 on the held-out test set. This evaluation systematically overestimates performance on the full dataset, because manually labelled cells are easier to classify by construction. To obtain a better estimate of performance on the full dataset, a second network was trained and tested using both manually labelled and network-labelled cells as ground truth; this quantifies the internal consistency of the classification used in all downstream analyses. As expected, the performance was lower, but the weighted F1 score remained above 0.9 (Fig. S3G). Classification was finally assessed by visual inspection of the classified images. The same approach was applied to panel II (Fig. S3H-M). Immune cell state clustering/metaclustering CD4 + T cells, CD8 + T cells, and myeloid cells were clustered separately on lineagespecific state markers: CD4 + T cells on TCF1/7, CTLA-4, LAG-3, PD-1, FOXP3, 4-1BB, Ki-67, and HLA-DR; CD8 + T cells on granzyme B, TCF1/7, CTLA-4, LAG-3, PD-1, 4-1BB, and Ki-67; and myeloid cells on CD15, IDO1, CD68, arginase-1, CD163, CD11c, PD-L1, and HLA-DR. Each lineage was clustered in two steps. In the first step, cells were clustered on their state markers with Rphenoannoy v0.1.0, a PhenoGraph implementation that builds a Jaccard-weighted nearest-neighbour graph and partitions it by Louvain community detection (Blondel et al., 2008; Levine et al., 2015). The number of nearest neighbours was swept over k = 50, 70, 90, 110, 130, and 160, and the resulting partitions were compared by cluster number, root-mean-square deviation of the cluster mean profiles, and silhouette width. The same value, k = 110, was used for all three lineages. This gave 23 clusters for CD4 + T cells, 18 for CD8 + T cells, and 19 for myeloid cells. In the second step, each first-step cluster was summarized by its mean arcsinh expression over the markers used for that lineage, and these cluster profiles were metaclustered by k-means. The number of metaclusters was chosen separately for each lineage over a range of 2 to 20 by internal cluster validation (clValid, Euclidean distance; connectivity, Dunn index, and silhouette width), with silhouette width as criterion. This gave 4 CD4 + T cell metaclusters, 5 CD8 + T cell metaclusters, and 5 myeloid metaclusters, which were then annotated by their marker expression and are the immune cell states used throughout. For the melanoma comparison, immune cells from both cohorts were clustered jointly by the same two-step procedure and annotated with the same scheme, giving 19 immune cell states.

Tumour cell clustering/metaclustering

Tumour cell phenotypes were defined in two steps, following the approach used previously (Meyer et al., 2026). First, tumour cells were clustered on tumourcompartment markers with the PhenoGraph algorithm (Jaccard index-based weights, Louvain algorithm for community detection) (Blondel et al., 2008; Levine et al., 2015), yielding the 34 tumour clusters. Clustering used the markers shown in Fig. 3A (CDK4, β-catenin, NGFR, HLA-DR, TYRP1, caveolin-1, HLA-ABC, mTOR, PD-L1, CA9, CDK6 and Ki-67), with k = 110 selected from a sweep of the neighbourhood size (Fig. S8A). Second, these fine clusters were grouped into tumour metaclusters (MCs) using CLARA clustering (Kaufman & Rousseeuw, 1990) with k = 5 on z-scored arcsinh expression of Ki-67, HLA-DR, NGFR, and TYRP1 resulting in 5 tumour metaclusters. In the validation cohort, tumour cells were clustered with the same procedure at k = 70, and the resulting clusters were assigned to MC1-MC5 by their defining markers.

ADC target scoring

Five criteria were computed per antigen on tumour cells: (1) median arcsinh expression; (2) percentage of positive tumour cells; (3) patient coverage, the percentage of patients with at least 25% positive tumour cells (sensitivity analyses at 10% and 50%); (4) homogeneity, the inverse coefficient of variation of raw tumour-cell intensity; and (5) selectivity, the median expression in tumour cells minus that in non-tumour cells.

Positivity thresholds were set per marker where the weighted densities of a twocomponent Gaussian mixture (mclust (Scrucca et al., 2016)) crossed. Five markers (FOLR1, claudin-6, TROP2, NaPi2b, and EGFR) were scored 0 for positivity-based criteria. Two further antigens, cadherin-6 and tissue factor, had positive cells in fewer than 1% of tumour cells and no patient reaching the 25% coverage threshold, so seven of the fifteen antigens contribute essentially no positive signal. Each criterion was minmax scaled across all 15 antigens, pooled over both panels, and the composite score was their unweighted mean. Cross-panel comparisons used the cores present in both panels. Peptides were separated on an Evosep One liquid chromatography system (Evosep Biosystems) with an Aurora Rapid 5 × 75 XT C18 column (IonOpticks), using the predefined 80 samples-per-day Whisper Zoom method (16.2-min gradient; solvent A, 0.1% formic acid in water; solvent B, 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile), and analysed on an Orbitrap Astral mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in data-independent acquisition (DIA) mode. MS1 spectra were acquired in the Orbitrap at a resolution of 240,000 (maximum injection time 5 ms; normalized AGC target 300%, corresponding to 3,000,000 charges). MS2 spectra were acquired in the Astral analyzer from 299 sequential, non-overlapping 2-m/z isolation windows over 380-980 m/z with optimized window placement, with higher-energy collisional dissociation at a normalized collision energy of 25%, a fragment range of 150-2,000 m/z, a scan time of 3 ms, a normalized AGC target of 300% (30,000 charges), and one microscan; multiplexed ion acquisition was disabled.

Raw data analysis with Spectronaut

Raw spectral data were analysed with Spectronaut (Biognosys, analysis version 20.5.260227) in library-free directDIA mode against the human Swiss-Prot FASTA database (release November 2021; 20,360 entries). Carbamidomethylation of cysteine was set as a fixed modification, and protein N-terminal acetylation and methionine oxidation as variable modifications; enzyme specificity was trypsin and LysC with up to two missed cleavages, and the minimum peptide length was seven amino acids. Proteins were inferred with ID Picker and quantified with the automated label-free quantification settings. The resulting library comprised 107,007 precursors, 95,773 peptides, and 7,365 protein groups (7,452 proteins). Across the 164 runs the mean number of missed cleavages was 0.29 (25.8% of peptides with at least one). The mean absolute mass deviations were 5.35 ppm at the MS1 level and 2.72 ppm at the MS2 level, and the median chromatographic peak width was 0.074 min. Filtering approach per analysis

Data were processed in R 4.5.1, with no inter-sample normalization applied beyond the normalization performed during Spectronaut processing. STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

General principles

The unit of analysis for all between-region, between-cohort, and survival comparisons was the patient: cell-and ROI-level values were aggregated per patient (and per region where applicable) before testing. The numbers of cells, ROIs, and patients underlying each panel, the statistical test, the centre and dispersion shown, and the multipletesting family are given in the figure legends. Tests were two-sided. Multiple testing was corrected with the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) method (Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995) within the families named in each legend; edgeR analyses were called at a false discovery rate (FDR) below 0.1 and all other analyses at 0.05. Given the cohort size, all analyses are exploratory, and P values are interpreted as evidence of association rather than confirmation.

Composition, density, and diversity

Cell-type fractions were computed per ROI among all cells or among immune/tumour cells. Densities (cells/mm 2 ) used an assumed ROI area of 0.785 mm 2 (the area of a 1mm core) for CCS and 1.0 mm 2 for melanoma. Diversity was quantified per ROI by Shannon entropy (Shannon, 1948), H = -Σ pi ln pi, over cell-type proportions and averaged per patient; Gini-Simpson index and Pielou's evenness (H/ln S) were also computed. Paired region comparisons used the Wilcoxon signed-rank test on patientlevel means in patients with both regions (40 patients); cohort comparisons used the Wilcoxon rank-sum test on patient-level values. Tumour MC co-occurrence (Fig. S8F-G) was assessed using MC fractions at the ROI and patient levels. Zero fractions were replaced with half the smallest positive fraction in the matrix before centred log-ratio transformation. Spearman correlations were then calculated for all ten MC pairs. To account for the dependence introduced by fractions summing to one, correlations were compared with a permutation null model: each MC's fractions were independently shuffled across ROIs or patients, rows were rescaled to sum to one, and the transformation and correlations were recalculated. Z-scores were then calculated to show how far each observed correlation was above or below the mean correlation expected under the null model.

Differential abundance

Cell counts were summed per patient and region, normalized by TMM (Robinson & Oshlack, 2010), and tested with edgeR (Chen et al., 2025; Robinson et al., 2010) quasi-likelihood F-tests (design ∼patient + region; dispersion estimated without trend; robust quasi-likelihood fit). The same approach was used for primary tumour versus metastasis (∼patient + site) and for CCS versus melanoma (∼cohort, on patientsummed counts).

Spatial interaction analysis

Tumour-partner interaction counts were computed with imcRtools (countInteractions, method "classic") on the precomputed neighbourhood graph (Meyer et al., 2026; Windhager et al., 2023). For each ROI and each ordered pair (tumour → target) this returns the contact rate ct, the number of observed directed tumour → target edges divided by the number of tumour cells in that ROI (Fig. S5A). Tumour metaclusters were collapsed to a single tumour label. To compare tumour-macrophage engagement between regions independently of abundance, the contact rate was modelled across ROIs by ordinary least squares as ct ~ target-cell frequency + tumour-cell frequency + cell density, where cell density is the mean number of spatial neighbours per cell in the ROI (mean undirected degree). The covariates are shown in Fig. S5B and Fig. S5C. One model was fitted per target population, within the analysis set (Fig. S5D; adjusted R² = 0.712, 0.881 and 0.692), and the infiltration score was defined as log2(ct / predicted ct) (Fig. S5E). Regions were compared with paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests on the per-patient scores, the patient being the unit of analysis, with Benjamini-Hochberg correction across the three macrophage populations.

Survival analysis Outcome definition.

Overall survival (OS) was defined with death from disease as the event; patients recorded as alive with or without disease were censored. The event indicator was constructed from the status (death from disease = 1; alive with no evidence of disease or alive with disease = 0). Survival time in days and status were extracted from the clinical data. Median follow-up, estimated by the reverse Kaplan-Meier method, was 81 months (2,465 days; 47 patients). Hazard ratios were estimated with Cox proportional-hazards models (Cox, 1972) using the survival package, and Kaplan-Meier curves were compared by log-rank tests (survminer). The proportionalhazards assumption of the reported models was assessed with scaled Schoenfeld residuals (Grambsch-Therneau test) (Grambsch & Therneau, 1994). Tumour metacluster analysis. The analysis set comprised patients with at least 50 tumour cells and usable follow-up. Of the 52 patients analysed by IMC, three contributed only lymph-node metastasis ROIs and therefore have no primary-tumour metacluster composition; of the remaining 49, two were excluded for missing or nonpositive survival time and none by the cell-count floor, leaving 47 patients with 35 events. Each MC fraction was dichotomized at the cohort median and, separately, at the cohort mean; both cut-points were specified in advance and both are reported. Five separate models were fitted per cut-point, one per MC, each adjusted for age (per 10 years), metastatic status at diagnosis, and sex. BH correction was applied over the five MC terms within each cut-point. As a sensitivity analysis without a cut-point, the MC3 fraction was also entered as a continuous variable (hazard ratio per 10 percentage points) in a Cox model with the same covariates, in the same 47 patients with 35 events. Per-patient Ki-67 was the mean of min-max-scaled arcsinh Ki-67 over tumour cells, dichotomized at the median. ADC target expression. c-MET and B7-H3 were summarized per patient as the mean arcsinh expression over tumour cells in primary-site cores, with lymph-node metastases excluded, and dichotomized at the cohort median. The cut-point is the median and was prespecified. The reported P value is the log-rank P, BH-corrected. Proteomic module scores. Patients were first assigned to a short-survival or a longsurvival group. A module score was then computed for each protein module and patient. Each protein was standardized across patients, and the score is the mean of those standardized values over the proteins of the module. Only modules with at least 10 proteins quantified in at least 70% of patients were scored, which left 47 modules. Each module score was tested against overall survival as a continuous variable (per standard deviation) in a separate Cox model. Because the modules shown were selected on the same patients, family-wise error was assessed by permutation: the survival outcome was permuted across patients 2,000 times, the maximum absolute Cox z statistic over the 47 modules was recorded for each permutation, and p_FWER is the fraction of permutations in which that maximum reached the observed |z|.

External validation cohort.

This cohort contributed 13 patients with usable follow-up, among whom 10 died. With 10 events no covariate can be estimated alongside the variable of interest, so all models in this cohort are unadjusted and the cut-point is the cohort median, as in the discovery cohort. Interpretation. The discovery cohort comprises 52 patients, of whom 47 enter the survival analysis with 35 events, and the validation cohort 13 patients with 10 events. With cohorts of this size the survival results are therefore exploratory and hypothesisgenerating.

Mass spectrometry proteomics statistics

Differential protein abundance was tested with limma (Ritchie et al., 2015) at core level with patient as a blocking factor via duplicateCorrelation (Smyth et al., 2005), requiring at least four observed values overall and two per group; BH correction was applied over all tested proteins with thresholds of FDR < 0.05 and |log2 fold change| ≥ 1. Geneset enrichment used fgsea (Korotkevich et al., 2016) on the moderated t statistic (the most extreme value per gene symbol) with the MSigDB Hallmark collection (Liberzon et al., 2015) (minimum size 10, maximum size 500). For tumour centre versus invasive front (Fig. 4C), proteins were ranked by a paired moderated t statistic (limma, ∼patient + region, on patient-by-region median profiles) and tested against 28 curated sets. For primary tumour versus metastasis (Fig. 6), the core-level test was complemented by a patient-level test: the identifier-level values were differenced within each patient (metastasis minus primary), and a moderated one-sample t test (limma) was applied to proteins with a difference in at least three patients, with BH correction over all proteins tested. Gene-set enrichment for this comparison used 21 gene-sets (tumourintrinsic programmes, therapeutically targetable axes, and neural crest development), ranked on the core-level moderated t statistic. Primary tumour versus metastasis MC counts were converted to centred log-ratios (log2[(count + 1)/total], centred per sample) (Aitchison, 1982). Composition was compared by PERMANOVA (Anderson, 2001) on Euclidean distances of centred log-ratio values (Aitchison distance) with the adonis2 function of the vegan R package (Oksanen et al., 2026). Individual MCs were compared by paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with BH over five MCs and by edgeR as above.

Classifier evaluation

Performance was summarized from out-of-fold predictions by AUC-ROC, average precision, weighted F1 score, Matthews correlation coefficient, and confusion matrices at a probability threshold of 0.5. Out-of-fold core probabilities were averaged per patient to give the patient-level scores reported in the Results, since cores from one patient are not independent. Confidence intervals on the patient-level AUC-ROC are percentile bootstrap intervals over 2,000 resamples of patients, and significance was assessed against a null obtained by permuting the patient labels 2,000 times. No external validation cohort was available for the H&E models.
