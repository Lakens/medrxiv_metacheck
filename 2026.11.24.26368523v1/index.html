How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Glial tumours are the most common primary malignant brain tumours in adults and comprise molecularly and histologically distinct entities with diverse outcomes. Despite this diversity, they arise within a shared anatomical environment, suggesting that common organisational principles may shape tumour heterogeneity. However, how cellular states are organised across gliomas and whether this organisation relates to clinical outcomes remains unclear. To that end, we generated a single-cell resolution spatial transcriptomic atlas of 2.6 million cells from 275 patients spanning IDH-wildtype glioblastoma, IDH-mutant and chromosome 1p/19q codeleted oligodendroglioma, IDHmutant astrocytoma and ependymoma. Across diagnoses, we show how heterogeneous tumour cell states are spatially organised into recurrent neighbourhoods with distinct immune and glial composition, which in turn assemble into a higher-order architecture. Among these cellular neighbourhoods, some, such as the astrocyte-like (AC-like), are conserved across entities, whereas others segregate by clinicopathological parameters. AI-powered histopathology further reveals selective association of neighbourhoods within histological clusters, providing a link between molecular spatial organisation to routine H&E morphology. In astrocytoma, neighbourhood abundance is associated with patient survival independently of WHO grade, with AC-like enrichment linked to favourable outcomes. Same-cell high-plex spatial transcriptomics and multiplexed protein imaging in an independent astrocytoma cohort validated these neighbourhoods and further confirmed enrichment of oligodendrocytes within the TBI neighbourhood. Collectively, our findings reveal that glial tumours are spatially encoded through recurrent multicellular neighbourhoods across different molecular entities, showing clinical relevance beyond conventional histology and providing a foundation for analogous investigation across cancer types.

Main

Despite decades of research, brain tumours remain among the deadliest cancers. Glial tumours, which are the most common primary malignant brain tumours, encompass a biologically and clinically diverse group of central nervous system (CNS) tumours. To account for this diversity, the current World Health Organisation (WHO) classification integrates histopathological features, DNA methylation profiling, and defining genetic alterations to stratify patients into distinct diagnostic groups [1] [2] [3] . Even with this refined molecular taxonomy, clinical outcomes remain highly variable within diagnostic categories, motivating efforts to understand the cellular organisation of tumour tissue as a complementary axis of glioma biology. Single-cell transcriptomic studies have identified recurrent malignant cell states -including astrocyte-like (AC-like), oligodendrocyte-progenitor-like (OPC-like), neural-progenitor-like (NPC-like), and mesenchymal-like (MES) states -within specific glioma subtypes, most notably glioblastoma [4] [5] [6] [7] [8] [9] [10] [11] . However, whether these states recur across the full spectrum of glioma entities remains unclear; distinguishing conserved from entity-specific features is therefore essential for understanding glioma heterogeneity. Similarly, resolving how tumour phenotypes are spatially organised within their shared anatomical environment could reveal organisational principles not captured by cell-state composition alone. Spatial omics offer the opportunity to resolve this organisation in situ, directly linking transcriptional identity to tissue architecture and the local microenvironment 12- 15 . Recent work using such methods has shown grade-dependent patterns of high-level tissue architecture in isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH)-mutant gliomas, shifting from location-driven structure in low-grade tumours to hypoxia-driven organisation resembling glioblastoma in high-grade tumours 16 . This suggests that tissue-level architecture may carry clinically relevant information. However, these studies lack single-cell spatial resolution, precluding identification of specific malignant states and cell-cell interactions underlying these spatial patterns, a gap that advancements in single-cell spatial profiling can now resolve 17 . Here, we apply a multimodal approach -combining single-cell spatial transcriptomics, DNA methylation and genetic profiling, multiplexed protein imaging, and computational histopathology -to systematically characterise recurrent tumour cell states and their spatial organisation across adult gliomas. To assess how broadly such organisational principles are shared, we study a diverse set of glial tumours. These include IDH-wildtype glioblastomahttps://paperpile.com/c/B39PCokq/rsEn IDHmutant astrocytoma, chromosome 1p/19q-codeleted oligodendroglioma, thought to originate from neural stem or glial progenitor cells 1 , alongside ependymomas, which are thought to arise from ependymal cells lining the ventricular system [18] [19] [20] [21] [22] . By integrating these complementary modalities, we investigate how tumour-intrinsic programs and the local microenvironment jointly shape glioma tissue architecture. We further assess the clinical relevance of this spatial organisation for patient stratification.

High-resolution single-cell spatial profiling across a diverse cohort of adult gliomas

To comprehensively characterise the cellular and transcriptional heterogeneity and recurrence patterns of tumour cells and the tumour microenvironment (TME) across adult gliomas, we assembled a cohort of 275 patients, from whom 282 tumour samples and 309 tissue cores were obtained for spatial transcriptomic profiling (Figure 1A, B, C; Supplementary Table 1). The cohort spans adult-type diffuse gliomas, including IDH-mutant (mut) and 1p/19qcodeleted oligodendroglioma (n=27), IDH-mutant astrocytoma (n=170), IDH-wildtype (wt) glioblastoma (n=24), as well as ependymal tumours, comprising subependymoma (n=15), myxopapillary ependymoma (n=19), and spinal ependymoma (n=20). One oligodendroglioma with sarcomatous features (oligosarcoma) showed no 1p/19q codeletion by copy-number analysis; oligosarcomas can retain 1p/19q loss of heterozygosity in a copy-number-neutral state 23 . Of the patient samples profiled, 205 were primary and 74 were recurrent, including 5 patients with matched primary and recurrent samples. For a subset of patients, multiple tumour cores per tumour were included, allowing direct comparison of transcriptional variation across distinct regions within the same tumour (Extended Data Figure 1). As the majority of intra-patient comparisons showed consistently higher similarity than inter-patient comparisons, cores from the same patient were aggregated for downstream analyses. DNA methylation profiling and panel sequencing data for recurrent driver gene mutations were available as part of routine diagnostic testing (Figure 1C). DNA methylation-based classification further refined these diagnoses, stratifying tumours into 13 molecular subtypes, including Receptor Tyrosine Kinase class I (RTK I), RTK II, and Mesenchymal (MES) glioblastoma subclasses (Figure 1B). Two patients contributed samples to both groups, as their tumours progressed from low-grade to high-grade astrocytoma at recurrence. The recurrent tumour states of adult gliomas

While tumour cell states have been described within individual glioma subtypes in previous studies 10 https://paperpile.com/c/B39PCokq/HlXU their comparative characterisation across glioma entities remains limited. To systematically address this, we fit a linearly decoded variational autoencoder (LDVAE) 24, 25 model to the expression profiles of the tumour cells. This identified nine malignant cell states that recur across our cohort (Figure 2A, B; Methods). This analysis recovered canonical tumour states previously described in glioblastoma, such as AQP4⁺ astrocyte-like (AC-like), OLIG1⁺ oligodendrocyte-progenitor-like (OPC-like), and SOX11 + neural-progenitor-like (NPC-like) states, as well as mesenchymal-like (CD44⁺ MES1 and VEGFA⁺ MES2) tumour cell states (Figure 2B; Supplementary Table 4). Notably, we resolved the OPC-like population into two substates, OPC-like I and II, with OPC-like I characterised by higher PLP1 expression (Figure 2B). PLP1 encodes a protein of CNS myelin 26 . We further identified a proliferating state (TOP2A⁺, MKI67⁺), a stressed state (HSPA1A⁺), and a tumour state expressing ciliary markers (KLHDC8A⁺, FANK1⁺) enriched in ependymal tumours hereafter termed ependymal (EPN)-like tumour cells (Figure 2A, B). Genes defining different tumour states showed low co-expression within individual tumour cells (Figure 2C). The genes defining each state were consistent across diagnoses, allowing direct comparison of cell-state composition across glioma entities (Extended Data Figure 4). Comparing tumour cell state abundance across diagnoses, we found that some states were diagnosis-restricted -OPC-like I, for instance, was largely specific to oligodendrogliomawhile others were broadly shared across entities, albeit at varying frequencies (Figure 2D). Proliferating and AC-like tumour states were the most recurrent states, suggesting that these represent universal glioma programs. The occurrence of an AC-like tumour state in various tumour entities is in line with previous reports identifying astrocytic-like transcriptional programs in paediatric ependymomas 22 . MES1/2-like states were most prevalent in highgrade astrocytoma and glioblastoma, occurring at lower frequency in other diagnoses except myxopapillary ependymoma (Figure 2D). In contrast, OPC-like and NPC-like states were largely restricted to diffuse gliomas, whereas the stressed and ependymal-like states were mostly found in ependymomas. Ependymal-like cells were also detected in glioblastoma, albeit at lower frequencies, consistent with studies reporting a subset of tumour cells exhibiting ciliaassociated features 27 . These findings reveal substantial differences in tumour state diversity across diagnoses, with glioblastoma exhibiting particularly high heterogeneity. At the same time, they highlight tumour state recurrence across genetically and epigenetically distinct glioma entities and provide new insights into less extensively characterised tumours, such as spinal ependymomas, which were predominantly composed of AC-like and ependymal-like states (Figure 2D). Incorporating spatial information, we observed that these tumour cell states tend to occupy distinct, non-overlapping regions (Figure 2E). Glioma tumour states occupy distinct spatial neighbourhoods characterised by a unique TME

Building on the observation that tumour states exhibit spatial segregation rather than being uniformly intermixed, we next sought to characterise their spatial organisation and associated microenvironment. To do so, we defined spatial neighbourhoods as local environments characterised by the composition of neighbouring cells, using Delaunay-triangulation graphs constructed from cell coordinates (Figure 3A, Methods). In total, we identified 11 spatial neighbourhoods with distinct compositions of malignant and non-malignant cells (Figure 3B, C). Most neighbourhoods were dominated by tumour cells and were distinguished primarily by the relative abundance of specific tumour states (Figure 3C, D; Extended Data Figure 5A). Among these, MES1-like cells predominantly occupied the gliosis neighbourhood 12 and MES2 the hypoxia neighbourhood 28 . In contrast, one neighbourhood was characterised by a low tumour burden and the presence of perivascular and endothelial cells, resembling vasculature. A separate neighbourhood comprised tumour cells alongside brain-resident cell types, including oligodendrocytes, which we termed the tumour-brain resident interface (TBI) neighbourhood (Figure 3C, D). Overall, the cellular composition of these neighbourhoods was largely conserved across diagnoses (Extended Data Figure 5B). Beyond their tumour cell state composition, neighbourhoods also differed in their relative abundance of brain-resident and peripheral immune cells (Figure 3D). The association of specific tumour states with immune-enriched neighbourhoods may indicate that some tumour phenotypes may be shaped or reinforced by microenvironmental cues. Specifically, MES1and MES2-like states were associated with neighbourhoods enriched for peripheral immune cells such as CD163⁺ myeloid cells, consistent with previous reports linking mesenchymal and hypoxic states to myeloid-rich microenvironments (Figure 3D) 12, 28 . In contrast, the TBI, OPClike, NPC-like, and AC-like neighbourhoods were enriched for brain-resident cells. These observations demonstrate that tumour states are spatially organised within the tissue microarchitecture rather than uniformly distributed throughout the tumour, with distinct states associated with specific microenvironmental cell populations, suggesting that tumour cell phenotypes may be influenced by local cellular interactions. Across tumour diagnoses, neighbourhoods exhibited distinct distribution patterns, with some being entity-specific and others shared across histological subtypes (Figure 3E, F). In particular, the AC-like and proliferating neighbourhoods appear in all entities, including ependymomas, whereas other neighbourhoods were predominantly observed in individual entities, such as the OPC-like I neighbourhood in oligodendroglioma and the EPN-like neighbourhood in ependymomas. Within astrocytomas, DNA methylation profiling distinguishes two major subclasses, corresponding to lower-and higher-grade tumours https://paperpile.com/c/B39PCokq/3rxN . AClike neighbourhoods were more abundant in low-grade astrocytomas, whereas hypoxia neighbourhoods were mostly observed in high-grade astrocytomas, corresponding largely to WHO grades 3 and 4 (Figure 3F). Interestingly, hypoxia neighbourhoods were also detected in myxopapillary ependymoma, a CNS WHO grade 2 tumour, indicating that these spatial programs are not restricted to high-grade tumours. We next examined how these neighbourhoods relate to each other. This analysis revealed higher-level spatial layering among neighbourhoods: gliosis and hypoxia neighbourhoods frequently colocalised, NPC-like states are often adjacent to OPC-like regions, and AC-like cells were often positioned near OPC-like II and gliosis neighbourhoods (Figure 3G, H). The spatial proximity between AC-like and gliosis states may reflect a gradual transcriptional shift from astrocytic to mesenchymal programs, consistent with prior reports of reactive AC transitioning to MES states under proinflammatory stimuli 15, 29 . This spatial association was observed across multiple diagnoses, with particularly pronounced patterns in ependymal tumours and higher-grade gliomas (Figure 3G, H; Extended Data Figure 6). Collectively, our data reveal that glioma tumour states occupy distinct spatial neighbourhoods characterised by specific malignant and non-malignant cell populations, highlighting how shared cellular programs are organised within distinct tissue microenvironments across entities.

Spatial neighbourhood composition associates with clinical parameters in adult gliomas

The diagnosis of adult gliomas integrates histopathological features, DNA methylation profiles, and genetic alterations to establish tumour type and grade (Figure 4A). We therefore asked whether the spatial neighbourhood composition of tumours was associated with these established clinicopathological and molecular characteristics. We first investigated associations between recurrent genetic alterations and spatial neighbourhood composition (Figure 4B; Extended Data Figure 7; Supplementary Table 5).

Given the central role of IDH mutation status in glioma classification, association analyses were performed separately for IDH-wildtype and IDH-mutant gliomas. Within IDH-mutant gliomas, the 1p/19q codeletion, a defining molecular feature of oligodendroglioma, was strongly associated with increased OPC-like neighbourhood abundance and reduced AC-like and gliosis neighbourhood abundance. Beyond this diagnosis-defining alteration, CDKN2A/B homozygous deletion was associated with differences in several spatial neighbourhoods, including increased representation of NPC-like, stressed, and ependymal-like neighbourhoods (Figure 4B). These associations are particularly relevant given the established role of CDKN2A/B homozygous deletion in the grading of IDH-mutant astrocytomas 30 . In IDH-wildtype glioblastoma, fewer significant associations were detected. EGFR alterations were associated with differences in EPN-like neighbourhood abundance, corresponding to a tumour cell population with cilia-associated features, whereas PDGFRA amplification was associated with reduced gliosis neighbourhood abundance (Figures 2B, 4B). Previous studies have linked PDGFRA amplification to increased OPC-like tumour states 27 https://paperpile.com/c/B39PCokq/S41X a trend also observed in our data, although it did not reach statistical significance. These findings are compatible, as the relative abundance of OPC-like neighbourhoods may increase at the expense of gliosis neighbourhoods. Given the cohort size of 23 glioblastoma patients, additional genotype-neighbourhood associations may not have reached statistical significance. Since DNA methylation-based classification plays a central role in glioma diagnosis and prognosis, we next investigated whether spatial neighbourhood composition was associated with molecular subtypes. In glioblastoma, methylation subclasses exhibited distinct neighbourhood compositions, with Receptor Tyrosine Kinase subtype I (RTK I) tumours enriched for OPC-like II neighbourhoods, RTK II tumours for AC-like neighbourhoods, and MES tumours for gliosis neighbourhoods (Figure 4C). Beyond molecular classification, we examined whether spatial neighbourhood composition was associated with WHO grade https://paperpile.com/c/B39PCokq/sFTh . Notably, WHO grade 4 IDH-mutant astrocytomas exhibited neighbourhood compositions more similar to glioblastomas, which are also WHO grade 4, than to lower-grade astrocytomas (Figure 4D, Extended Data Figure 8A). This suggests that spatial neighbourhood composition may reflect grade-associated tumour characteristics that extend across distinct molecular diagnoses. Lastly, we sought to identify histopathological correlates of the transcriptionally defined neighbourhoods that could be detected using routinely generated hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained sections. While necrotic regions, typically associated with MES2-tumour states and hypoxia, could be readily recognised by visual assessment, other neighbourhoods had less evident characteristics (Figure 4E, Extended Data Figure 9). To quantify the association between neighbourhood prevalence and histopathology, we extracted embeddings from H&E images using the TITAN histopathology foundation model 31 (Figure 4E). The histopathological feature space was only partially separated by diagnostic groups and IDH-mutant astrocytoma and IDH wildtype glioblastoma formed a continuum, which could be represented by four subclusters (Figure 4F). Spatial neighbourhood abundances varied noticeably across the histopathological feature space (Figure 4E). Within the histopathological continuum of astrocytoma and glioblastoma, neighbourhoods discriminated the set four subclusters more accurately than the diagnostic group alone (macro AUC 0.71 vs 0.62; Figure 4H; Methods). Cluster 1 contained tumours with a high abundance of the TBI and a depletion of other neighbourhoods, but was only weakly associated with diagnosis (Figure 4I). Similarly, cluster 3 was enriched for the gliosis neighbourhood. These findings link transcriptionally defined neighbourhood composition to H&E derived histomorphological patterns. Collectively, our data demonstrate that spatial neighbourhoods capture clinically relevant tumour heterogeneity, associated with established molecular and clinicopathological features. AC-like and TBI neighbourhoods predict survival in IDH-mutant astrocytoma

Our histomorphological analysis revealed substantial heterogeneity among astrocytomas, which were distributed across multiple histological clusters and showed considerable overlap with glioblastomas (Figure 4D, F). At the same time, WHO grade provided limited prognostic separation between grade 2 and grade 3 IDH-mutant astrocytomas (Extended Data Figure 8B). Together, these observations prompted us to investigate whether neighbourhood composition could provide additional prognostic information beyond the established WHO grading. Given the broad representation of IDH-mutant astrocytomas in our cohort (n=175), spanning WHO grades 2-4, we focused subsequent analyses on this tumour type. To assess the relationship between spatial organisation and clinical stratification, we stratified tumours based on their most prevalent neighbourhood (Figure 5A). This revealed a dichotomy in lower-grade IDH-mutant astrocytomas, with tumours primarily characterised by either the tumour-brain-resident interface (TBI) or the AC-like neighbourhood (Figure 5B, C). This separation became progressively less pronounced with grade and was largely absent in grade 4 tumours, where additional neighbourhoods, such as the gliosis neighbourhood, became more prevalent (Figure 5C). To further dissect these neighbourhoods, we profiled an independent cohort of 18 low-grade IDH-mutant astrocytoma samples using same-slide high-plex spatial transcriptomics and multiplexed immunofluorescence (5,096 genes and 15 proteins; Figure 5D; Extended Data Figure 10; Supplementary Tables 6, 7). As both assays were performed on the same section, we were able to perform joint analysis and feature transfer across modalities (Methods). The resulting multimodal dataset is publicly accessible at https://gliomap.dkfz.de/ for interactive exploration. This validated our spatial annotations at the protein level and provided additional morphological insights, including vascular structures surrounded by astrocytic endfeet, consistent with blood-brain barrier structure (Figure 5D). Our analysis confirmed that AC-like neighbourhoods were enriched for brain resident cells such as microglia, but contained few oligodendrocytes, which were instead enriched in the TBI neighbourhood (Figure 5E). Accordingly, TBI neighbourhoods were characterised by tumour cells interspersed among oligodendrocytes, with enrichment of myelin associated marker MAG, suggesting that these tumour cells may reside in proximity to myelinated axons (Figure 5E). Having characterised these neighbourhoods and their association with astrocytoma grade, we next asked whether spatial composition holds prognostic value independent of existing clinical and molecular parameters. Survival analysis and multivariate Cox proportional hazards modelling revealed that enrichment for the AC-like neighbourhood was associated with significantly longer overall survival (OS), while dominance of the TBI neighbourhood correlated with poorer prognosis (Figure 5F, Extended Data Figure 11; Supplementary Table 8). Similar trends were observed for progression-free survival (PFS) and in analyses stratified by primary and recurrent tumours, with AC-like enrichment showing consistent associations with favourable outcomes across analyses (Extended Data Figure 11). We next sought to validate these prognostic associations in independent cohorts. Our study represents one of the first large-scale, single-cell-resolution spatial transcriptomic characterisations of gliomas, and comparable spatial datasets remain scarce. Transcriptional signatures have previously been shown to carry prognostic significance across tumour types 32 . Spatial transcriptomics extends this approach by enabling the derivation of signatures which capture combined transcriptional programs of multiple cell types within spatially defined neighbourhoods. We therefore derived transcriptional signatures from aggregated neighbourhood expression profiles and quantified their expression in two independent bulk RNA-seq cohorts of a total of 555 patients (Methods; Extended Data Figure 12; Supplementary Tables 8, 9) 33, 34 . Stratification of patients into tertiles based on neighbourhood signature scores revealed that higher AC-like scores were associated with superior OS, whereas higher TBI scores were associated with poorer outcomes (Figure 5G). These results indicate that the prognostic value of the AC-like and TBI neighbourhoods extends beyond our own cohort, supporting their potential as generalizable biomarkers of clinical outcome in IDH-mutant astrocytoma. Discussion

Despite their distinct molecular and histopathological characteristics, adult-type glial tumours share therapeutic challenges, with many treatments applied across tumour types with variable efficacy 1, 19 . This overlap underscores the need to understand the commonalities among these tumours. By combining spatially resolved molecular profiling with histopathological analysis across IDH-wildtype and IDH-mutant gliomas and ependymomas, we show that tissue architecture is an integral component of glioma heterogeneity, encompassing both shared and entity-specific patterns of spatial organisation. Tissue architecture, as assessed through MRI and histopathology, has long been recognised as an important feature of glioma biology https://paperpile.com/c/B39PCokq/sFTh . Our findings provide molecular and cellular correlates of this architecture, resolving 11 recurrent neighbourhoods that differ in their immune and glial landscapes and in the tumour states they comprise. Proliferating and AClike neighbourhoods were present across gliomas, whereas the remaining neighbourhoods aligned with diagnosis, methylation class and WHO grade. For instance, hypoxia/MES2 neighbourhood is enriched in glioblastoma and high-grade astrocytoma but is also detected in myxopapillary ependymoma, a WHO grade 2 tumour, suggesting that hypoxia-related programs can arise independently of tumour grade. Although mesenchymal signatures are associated with poor radiation response and reduced survival in glioblastoma 35 https://paperpile.com/c/B39PCokq/1ZdY and hypoxia with poor prognosis across solid cancers 36https://paperpile.com/c/B39PCokq/SAee+YOHQ 37 , its presence in myxopapillary ependymoma suggests that this spatial program is not restricted to high-grade tumours. The relevance of spatial architecture extends beyond tumour classification to patient outcomes. In astrocytoma, AC-like neighbourhood enrichment is associated with longer survival, whereas tumour-brain interface (TBI) enrichment is linked to poorer outcomes. These associations highlight spatial architecture as a complementary source of prognostic information beyond WHO grade. The AC-like neighbourhood is particularly notable for its recurrence across entities and proximity to multiple tumour states, placing it at the center of the tumour-state landscape. AClike programs have also been described in pediatric ependymomas 22 https://paperpile.com/c/B39PCokq/emke and we extend this to adult ependymoma subtypes. This broad distribution is compatible with AC-like representing a shared feature of glioma biology, and it raises the question of whether additional factors, such as immune cell recruitment, drive its diversification into other tumour states. Beyond its broad occurrence, AC-like tumour state has been associated with favorable response to IDHmutant inhibition 38 . Together with our findings on improved survival, this suggests that AClike programs may have broader clinical relevance, although their prognostic significance beyond astrocytomas remains to be determined. In contrast, the TBI neighbourhood was associated with poorer prognosis and comprised OPC-like and AC-like tumour cells interspersed among oligodendrocytes. Given the diffuse infiltrative growth of astrocytomas and the role of oligodendrocytes in axonal myelinationhttps://paperpile.com/c/B39PCokq/we0F this organisation may reflect direct interactions between oligodendrocytes and tumour cells 39 , or tumour cells positioned along axons 40, 41 . These associations may be related to the infiltrative growth pattern of astrocytomas, although direct tumour-axon interactions cannot be resolved through transcriptomic profiling alone. Incorporating neighbourhood prevalence could refine existing classification systems. While spatial transcriptomics offers high resolution, its cost and complexity currently prevent routine clinical use, whereas H&E histopathology remains rapid, inexpensive and universally available. Advances in foundation models for digital pathology now enable extraction of highdimensional morphological features from standard H&E slides 42, 43 . Consistent with this, we find parallel trends between spatial neighbourhoods and histopathology-derived features, a first step toward integrating molecularly informed tissue architecture into routine diagnostic workflows.

Our study has limitations. First, spatial sampling was based on tumour cores, which may not capture the full architecture of higher-grade tumours, where heterogeneity is greater 16 . Second, the transcriptional signature of the AC-like tumour state closely resembles that of non-malignant astrocytes, so cell type assignments in these compartments may partly reflect both malignant and normal populations. Lastly, since the TBI neighbourhood comprises AClike tumour, OPC-like tumour and oligodendrocytes, the signature extracted for validation in external cohorts reflects a combination of OPC-like and TBI signals. This may limit the attribution of the prognostic association to a specific neighbourhood, although both are associated with poorer outcomes. Together, our findings show that glioma heterogeneity is organised in space: tumour states are not distributed uniformly but assemble into recurrent neighbourhoods, some shared and others specific to diagnosis and grade. This organisation adds an axis to classification that genetics and bulk profiling do not capture, and it links molecular programs to the tissue architecture that pathologists and radiologists already recognise. By scaling spatial profiling to hundreds of patients and linking it to routine histology, this work shows how high-throughput tissue mapping can move from describing glioma biology to informing glioma classification in the clinic.

Extended Data

Methods

Ethics and inclusion statement

This study complied with ethics regulations as approved by the Ethics Committee Heidelberg (S-318/2022). Samples were collected from adult patients after obtaining written informed consent in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and patients were not compensated for participation. Patient sex was self-reported. No exclusions were made based on race, ethnicity, sex, age, gender or other social factors.

NGS panel sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted exclusively from formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue blocks. Targeted DNA sequencing was performed at the Department of Neuropathology, University Hospital Heidelberg, using a custom-designed hybrid capture next-generation sequencing (NGS) panel. This panel encompasses the complete coding regions (all exons with flanking ±25 bp) as well as selected intronic and promoter regions of 130 genes, enabling detection of single nucleotide variants (SNVs), small insertions and deletions (indels), exonic rearrangements, and recurrent gene fusions.

DNA methylation profiling

Genome-wide DNA methylation profiling was carried out using the Illumina Infinium MethylationEPIC BeadChip (v.1.0). Raw IDAT files were processed with the minfi Bioconductor package, including background and dye-bias correction prior to beta value calculation. Probes were filtered to exclude those located on sex chromosomes, containing single-nucleotide polymorphisms near or at the interrogated CpG site, non-uniquely mapping probes, and those not represented on the EPIC array v.2 design, ensuring compatibility with cross-cohort datasets. Classification of methylation profiles was performed using the Heidelberg brain tumour classifier (MNP) v.12.8 (https://www.molecularneuropathology.org/mnp/). Uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) based on the DNA methylation profiles was created using only the 32,000 probes with the highest variance across the cohort consisting of 304 profiles. The selected probes were each z-score normalised using sklearn's StandardScaler and PCA dimensionality reduction was performed. The 94 leading principal components were used as input for the UMAP with parameters min_idst=0.4. The number of high variance probes and the number of principal components as well as the decision to standardise the probes was adopted from Capper et al. 2.

Single nucleus RNA sequencing data production, preprocessing and annotation Single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) was performed on formalin-fixed paraffinembedded (FFPE) tissue from 14 glioblastoma patients. Nuclei were isolated using the 10x Genomics Nuclei Isolation Kit (PN-1000494) following the manufacturer's protocol. Data preprocessing was performed using CellRanger (version 7.0.0). Downstream analysis, quality control and cell-type annotation were using Seurat (v4.3) in R (v4.3.1). Copy number variants (CNVs) were inferred using the inferCNV package (version 1.3.3; https://github.com/broadinstitute/infercnv). A mix of all healthy cells was used as reference. The adjusted expressions of the genes in the inferCNV object were used to calculate the gains and losses of the chromosomes. For the inference a subset of roughly 10,000 healthy cells was used.

Xenium spatial transcriptomics (custom targeted panel)

Five-micrometer sections were cut from FFPE tissue microarray (TMA) blocks of CNS tumours and prepared for 10x Genomics Xenium analysis following the FFPE tissue preparation guide (CG000578 Rev C) and Xenium in situ gene expression User Guide (CG000582, 10x Genomics). Sections were placed within the imaging area of Xenium slides, dried on a thermocycler with a dedicated adapter at 42°C for 3 h, and then dried further in a desiccator for one day. In situ expression profiling with a custom [344]-gene panel (Supplementary Table 2) was performed on the Xenium Analyzer (instrument software v3.1.0.0, analysis software xenium-3.1.0.4) by the scOpenLab.

Spatial transcriptomics preprocessing and TME annotation

Cells were assigned to individual TMA cores using core masks generated with Segment Anything 44 . Downstream analysis was restricted to nuclear transcripts, which were extracted separately from the output of each Xenium run. Nucleus locations were taken from the nucleus boundaries file (nucleus_boundaries.csv.gz), and transcript quality (qv column) and nuclear overlap (overlaps_nucleus column) were taken from the transcripts file (transcripts.parquet). Only transcripts with a Phred-scaled quality value (QV) above 20 that overlapped a nucleus were retained. Cell-type labels were first assigned to a subset of high-quality cells (more than 50 transcripts per cell). Raw counts were normalised to the median total count across cells (sc.pp.normalize_total with default settings), log-transformed (sc.pp.log1p) and scaled to zero mean and unit variance per gene (sc.pp.scale) using scanpy 45 . Principal component analysis was performed on all genes in the panel, and the first 30 components were retained. A 15 nearest-neighbour graph was constructed from these components using Euclidean distance. The Leiden community detection algorithm was run on this graph at resolution 2.0 and where needed subclustering was performed 46 . The same graph was used to compute the twodimensional UMAP visualisation. To transfer these annotations to all cells, we trained an scVI model with a Poisson likelihood on a stratified subsample of the annotated cells, which prevents abundant populations from dominating the training 25 . The scVI model was used to initialise an scANVI model 47 https://paperpile.com/c/B39PCokq/X8wX which was trained with the annotated cell types as labels. The trained scANVI model was then applied to the raw counts of all nuclei, and each cell was assigned the cell type with the highest predicted probability. Cells with fewer than 10 transcripts were excluded from downstream analysis.

Sampling consistency analysis

To assess how representative a single core is of its tumour, we compared the transcriptomic profiles of cores from the same patient. Cores with fewer than 200 cells were excluded. For each core, raw nuclear counts were summed across all cells into a pseudobulk profile and converted to log-transformed counts per million (log1p(CPM)). Each gene was then standardised to zero mean and unit variance across cores. Similarity between two cores was measured as the Pearson correlation of their standardised profiles. Core pairs were grouped into three categories: (I) cores from the same patient; (II) cores from the same patient and different primary/recurrent status (III) cores from different patients with the same diagnosis and primary/recurrent status; (IV) cores from different patients that differed in diagnosis or status. Pairs that share a core are not independent, so differences between groups were assessed with permutation tests (2,000 permutations) on the difference in median correlation. For the comparison of same-patient pairs with different-patient pairs of matching diagnosis and status, patient labels were permuted among cores within each diagnosis-status stratum. For the comparison of matching with non-matching different-patient pairs, diagnosis-status labels were permuted between patients, keeping each patient's cores together. One-sided p-values were calculated as (number of permuted differences ≥ observed difference + 1)/(number of permutations + 1). For each same-patient pair, we also report the percentile of its correlation among different-patient pairs with the same diagnosis and status.

Tumour states annotation

To identify recurrent transcriptional programs across tumour cells, we used a linearly decoded variational autoencoder (LDVAE; scvi-tools LinearSCVI) 24 with a modified, NMF-style decoder. In this decoder, each program is a non-negative distribution over genes, and each cell's expression is a non-negative combination of program activities. Methylation class was included as the batch variable and modeled as an additional program with a fixed logit offset (batch_logit = 1.0). The model used 9 latent dimensions and a Poisson likelihood. It was trained for 400 epochs on a 1% subsample of tumour cells, stratified by diagnosis, and then applied to all tumour cells. The XIST gene was excluded.

Spatial graph-based identification of cellular neighbourhoods

To characterise the spatial organisation of tumour states within the tumour microenvironment, we identified cellular neighbourhoods. A spatial neighbor graph of all cells was constructed from cell centroids as vertices using Delaunay triangulation from squidpy (v1.6.1) 48 https://paperpile.com/c/B39PCokq/uwVI with each TMA placed at a separate offset in coordinate space, and edges longer than 50 µm were removed. For each cell, we computed a neighbourhood profile from the predictions of the cell-type scANVI model, consisting of the cell-type composition of the cell and its first-degree neighbors, concatenated with that of its second-degree neighbors. Two versions of this profile were used. The probability-based version averages the predicted cell-type and tumour state probabilities, and the count-based version counts cells by their highest-probability cell type. To define neighbourhoods, we sampled up to 100 cells from each core, built a k-nearest-neighbor graph between these cells on the probability-based profiles (scanpy defaults, k = 15) and clustered it with the Leiden algorithm at resolution 0.5 46 . The resulting clusters were then used as labels to train an scVI model with a Poisson likelihood on the count-based profiles, followed by an scANVI model 47 . The trained scANVI model then predicted the neighbourhood of every cell in the full dataset from its count-based profile.

Spatial neighbourhood interaction analysis

Spatially contiguous cells assigned to the same neighbourhood were grouped into connected regions within each tissue core, similarly to our previous work 49 . Regions containing fewer than 15 cells were excluded. Spatial contacts between regions of different neighbourhoods were quantified using the spatial connectivity Delaunay graph, with each unique region-region contact counted once. Expected neighbourhood-neighbourhood contact frequencies were estimated independently for each core using 10,000 spatial permutations that preserved the core-specific spatial organisation. Enrichment or depletion of observed contacts relative to permutation-derived expectations was assessed using Poisson generalised linear models with the log expected contact count included as an offset. Exponentiated model coefficients were reported as rate ratios, with values >1 indicating enrichment and <1 indicating depletion. Diagnosis-specific interaction profiles were obtained by fitting the same model separately to the cores of each diagnostic group, using the core-level expected counts described above. To compare neighbourhood-pair repertoires between diagnoses, a neighbourhood pair was considered present in a diagnosis if it was assessable (non-zero expected contact count) in at least two cores of that diagnosis and yielded a finite rate-ratio estimate. Similarity between two diagnoses was quantified as the Jaccard index, defined as the number of neighbourhood pairs present in both diagnoses divided by the number of pairs present in either.

Association of genetic and molecular alterations with neighbourhood abundance

For each patient, the fraction of cells assigned to each spatial neighbourhood was calculated. Associations between neighbourhood fractions and genetic or molecular alterations were tested separately for each neighbourhood, comparing tumours with and without each alteration using two-sided Welch's t-tests. Before testing, neighbourhood fractions were logittransformed. To avoid undefined values, a pseudocount ε was applied: fractions of exactly 0 or 1 were replaced by ε or 1 -ε, respectively, where ε was half of the smallest non-zero fraction of that neighbourhood among the tumours included in the comparison. P-values were corrected for multiple testing across neighbourhoods within each comparison using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure, and q < 0.1 was considered significant. Comparisons were performed only when both groups contained at least 3 tumours.

Histology feature extraction and clustering

Consecutive H&E-stained sections corresponding to the Xenium samples were scanned using a Leica Aperio AT2 scanner at 0.25mpp (40x magnification). The images were segmented and tiled using the TRIDENT library (v. 0.2.0; https://github.com/mahmoodlab/TRIDENT) 50 . The feature files that were produced for each slide were separated into files for each individual TMA core. The TITAN 31 slide-level pathology foundation model was used to generate a 768dimensional vector representation of each TMA core's H&E image. The two-dimensional UMAP visualisation of these features shown in Figure 4 was generated using the settings `metric="cosine"` (umap-learn 0.5.12) from the 768-dimensional TITAN features without prior standardisation or dimensionality reduction. The histology clusters were derived from the TITAN features by creating a 15-nearest neighbour graph based on cosine similarity on which the Leiden community detection 46 algorithm was run at resolution 0.5 through the scanpy package (1.12.1) 45.

Association of histology clusters with diagnosis and neighbourhood composition

Analyses were restricted to astrocytoma low-grade, astrocytoma high-grade and glioblastoma, in which histology clusters spanned multiple diagnoses (206 cores, 177 patients). neighbourhoods present at >1% in fewer than 5% of cores were excluded. Histology cluster membership was predicted by multinomial logistic regression (scikit-learn v. 1.7.2; L2 penalty, C = 1) using either diagnosis (one-hot encoded) or neighbourhood fractions as predictors, z-scored within each training fold. Performance was estimated by 5-fold cross-validation stratified by cluster, with all cores of a patient in the same fold, repeated 20 times. One-vs-rest AUCs were computed per cluster from pooled out-of-fold predictions and averaged across clusters (macro-AUC). To test whether neighbourhoods are associated with clusters independently of diagnosis, the improvement in cross-validated log loss from adding neighbourhoods to diagnosis was compared with a null distribution obtained by permuting neighbourhood profiles among cores of the same diagnosis (200 permutations); p = (1 + number of permutations ≥ observed) / (1 + 200). To compare the contributions of diagnosis and neighbourhoods, a joint multinomial logistic regression including both (all predictors z-scored) was fitted on all cores. Exponentiated coefficients give the odds ratio of each cluster, relative to the average across clusters, per SD increase of each predictor. 95% confidence intervals and two-sided p-values were obtained from 500 patient-level bootstrap resamples and corrected across all clusterpredictor pairs using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure.

Same-slide spatial transcriptomics and multiplexed immunofluorescence

Same-slide spatial transcriptomics and multiplexed immunofluorescence were performed as previously described 51 . Five-micrometer sections of 16 low-grade astrocytoma tissue cores were profiled using the Xenium Prime 5K gene panel with a 96-gene custom add-on, also used for profiling of lymphoma https://paperpile.com/c/B39PCokq/1cfH (Supplementary Table 5; 10x Genomics; instrument software v3.1.0.0, analysis software xenium-3.1.0.4). Sections were prepared as described for the custom-panel Xenium analysis above. After Xenium data acquisition, the same slides were used for multiplexed immunofluorescence, according to Sivajothi et al. 2024. Tissue was kept in 50% glycerol in PBS storage solution at 4 °C for up to 2 weeks. Heat-induced antigen retrieval was performed for 20 min at 155-160 °C in Tris-EDTA buffer (pH 9). Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with a cocktail of DNA oligonucleotide-conjugated antibodies (Supplementary Table 6), conjugated using previously published oligonucleotide sequences 53 . After incubation, tissue was fixed in three steps using 1.6% paraformaldehyde (PFA), 100% methanol and bis(sulfosuccinimidyl) suberate (BS3). Imaging was performed on the PhenoCycler-Fusion system (Akoya Biosciences) according to the manufacturer's instructions, using Atto550-and Atto647N-labeled complementary DNA barcodes for cyclic imaging. Raw files were processed into a single qpTIFF file with the Fusion software (v2.4.0). For each cycle, the nuclear stain was compared with the reference cycle (by default the DAPI signal of the second cycle), and the resulting offsets were used to align all cycles by rigid translation. Background was subtracted using blank cycles at the beginning and end of each run, deconvolution was carried out with the Microvolution package, and shading was corrected with BaSiC. Adjacent tiles were registered using the Microscopy Image Stitching Tool library. Further details are given in the manufacturer's technical documentation (https://help.codex.bio/codex/processor/technical-notes).

Multimodal image registration and spatial transcriptomics annotation

Individual TMA cores were segmented using Segment Anything (ViT-Hhttps://paperpile.com/c/B39PCokq/RBY5 sam_vit_h_4b8939 checkpoint) 44 , and alignment was performed at the core level. Xenium and multiplexed immunofluorescence images were registered at the pixel level on the basis of the DAPI signal. Scale-invariant feature transform (SIFT) keypoints were detected and matched with OpenCV (v4.11.0; FLANN matcher, Lowe's ratio test), and the matched landmarks were used to estimate an affine transformation by least squares (scikit-image v0.25.2, via SpatialData v0.3.0). The aligned data were stored in the SpatialData format 54 and analyzed as described for the custom-panel dataset. Cell types and tumour states in the high-plex spatial transcriptomics cohort were annotated as described above. Spatial neighbourhood annotations were transferred from the discovery cohort using multinomial logistic regression implemented in scikit-learn (v1.7.2). Cell-type annotations were harmonised between datasets, and local neighbourhood composition was quantified using cell-type counts derived from Delaunay connectivity graphs. These profiles were used to train the classifier on annotated low-grade astrocytomas from the discovery cohort and predict neighbourhood identities in the independent cohort.

Neighbourhood gene signatures and scoring in bulk transcriptomics

To create gene expression signatures for each neighbourhood which could be used as surrogates in bulk expression measurements, pseudobulk profiles were generated from spatial transcriptomics cells. For each neighbourhood and each core the expression counts were added together (decoupler v. 2.1.6; http://decoupler.pp/.pseudobulk with sample_col='core', groups_col='nhood') 55 . Each core might thus give rise to as many pseudobulks as there are neighbourhoods. Pseudobulk profiles that consist of the expression of fewer than 30 cells were excluded. The remaining profiles were normalised to each sum to 100 (http://scanpy.pp/.normalize_total with target_sum=100). For each neighbourhood a Wilcoxon's rank-sum test was performed to identify genes that differ in expression between pseudobulks from that neighbourhood as compared to all other neighbourhoods (http://sc.tl/.rank_gene_groups method='wilcoxon', reference='rest'). The 10 genes with the lowest p-values that show an increase in expression were included in the signatures. The table of signature genes is Supplementary Table 8. Signatures were derived from the entire discovery cohort. Scoring the signatures in bulk transcriptomics samples was done using singscore (R 4.3.3, singscore v. 1.22.0) 56.

Validation survival analysis

For validation of spatial transcriptomics-derived features, we assembled two previously published, clinically annotated bulk transcriptomics datasets of IDH-mutant astrocytomas from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) 57, 58 and the Chinese Glioma Genome Atlas (CGGA) 34 . TCGA: The TCGA-GBM and TCGA-LGG cohorts were combined and restricted to IDH-mutant astrocytomas using the revised sample annotation in accordance with the WHO 2021 CNS tumour classification guidelines provided by Leiria de Mendonça et al. 33. https://github.com/sysbiomed/MONET/blob/master/Final-outputs/SIMPLIFIED_CLASSIFICATION_TCGA_2016_2021.csv; commit 4361ed6. Gene expression data (log2(TPM + 1); v. 05-10-2024) were downloaded from the UCSC Xena Browser (https://xenabrowser.net/datapages/) on 9 February 2026. Signatures were scored as described above. Where several samples were available per patient, one sample was used, with the primary tumour preferred. Curated overall survival (OS) data were taken from the TCGA Pan-Cancer Clinical Data Resource 59 . CGGA: The mRNAseq_693 and mRNAseq_325 datasets (CGGA.mRNAseq_693. RSEMgenes.20200506.txt and CGGA.mRNAseq_325. RSEM-genes.20200506.txt) and the corresponding clinical data were downloaded from https://www.cgga.org.cn/download.jsp on 24 June 2026. Expression values were converted to TPM and limited to genes shared by both datasets. The expression counts for genes with the same gene symbol were added together. IDH-mutant astrocytomas were selected based on the provided clinical annotation (IDH mutation status "Mutant" and 1p/19q status "Non-codel").

Gene signatures for the AC-like and TBI neighbourhoods were derived from the discovery spatial transcriptomics dataset as described above. Scores were z-scored within each cohort, and a balance score was calculated as z(AC-like) -z(TBI), with positive values indicating relatively higher AC-like than TBI expression. tumours were divided into tertiles of the balance score within each cohort (T1, TBI-leaning; T2, intermediate; T3, AC-like-leaning). Survival across these tertiles was compared using Kaplan-Meier estimates and a two-sided log-rank test, with pairwise log-rank tests adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg method. Signature scores were compared between tertiles using pairwise two-sided Wilcoxon ranksum tests with Benjamini-Hochberg adjustment. Analyses were performed in R (v. 4.5.1) using the survival (v. 3.8.3) and survminer (v. 0.5.0) packages. Survival times were converted from days to months (days/30.4375), and the full follow-up was used.
