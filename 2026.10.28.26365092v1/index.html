Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background and Purpose: Spreading depolarizations (SDs) are waves of near-complete breakdown of the transmembrane ion gradients that underlie migraine aura and occur in patients with moderate-to-severe traumatic, ischemic or hemorrhagic brain injury. SDs are involved in primary and secondary brain injury. They are associated with poor neurological outcome and represent a modifiable therapeutic target. However, despite decades of investigation, SD detection still requires invasive subdural electrocorticography (ECoG) using an implanted subdural electrode strip, restricting monitoring to a small subset of neurosurgical patients.

We developed WAVEFRONT, a noninvasive scalp electroencephalography (EEG) biomarker of SD based on spatiotemporal propagation characteristics of delta-band EEG activity. The biomarker was trained and validated using concurrent, multi-day scalp EEG and invasive subdural electrocorticography recordings from patients with traumatic brain injury (TBI), subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH), or malignant hemispheric stroke (MHS) across three institutions. We assessed out-of-sample performance across institutions, diagnosis, and surgical approaches, as well as calibration, temporal synchrony, and spatial specificity relative to gold-standard SDs annotated on ECoG. Clinical utility was further evaluated in 607 patients comatose after cardiac arrest from the multicenter International Cardiac Arrest REsearch (I-CARE) consortium, a population in whom invasive monitoring is not performed. Outcome analyses excluded patients who died (Cerebral Performance Category [CPC] 5) because death may reflect decisions to withdraw life-sustaining treatment in addition to neurological injury.

Results: A total of 1,119 gold-standard SDs were annotated in 32 patients (TBI, n=17; SAH, n=12; MHS, n=3; range 0-187 SDs per patient). Across exhaustive cross-validation and institution-, diagnosis-, and surgical approach-based hold-out analyses, WAVEFRONT models trained on one set of patients consistently maintained high performance when evaluated in previously unseen out-of-group cohorts. At the optimal operating point, WAVEFRONT achieved a mean cross-validated true positive rate of 97.3±1% with a false positive rate of 6.0±0.5%, calculated over 2-min sliding windows with 30-s overlaps. The cumulative duration of WAVEFRONT detections within 30-hour windows strongly correlated with the number of SDs measured by invasive ECoG (R² =0.7411). Scalpdetected depressions were spatially consistent with the location of the invasive recording strip, and visual inspection demonstrated close temporal correspondence with invasive detections, including during SD clusters. External validation in the independent I-CARE cohort using WAVEFRONT trained on the invasive-monitoring cohort demonstrated significantly greater SD burden on day 3 of post-arrest EEG monitoring among patients with poor neurological outcome (CPC 3-4, n=29) than those with good outcome (CPC 1-2, n=225; p=0.007).

Conclusions: Eight decades after Leão's description of SD in 1944, we propose and validate a noninvasive scalp EEG biomarker of SD. Consistent performance across institution-, diagnosis-, and surgical approach-based hold-out analyses, together with burden calibration, spatial specificity, and temporal synchrony with ECoG detections, supports the validity of the WAVEFRONT framework. The association of biomarker burden with neurologic outcome after cardiac arrest suggests that noninvasive monitoring may enable investigation of spreading depolarizations in populations where invasive monitoring is not feasible. Future studies should evaluate prospective performance, treatment responsiveness, and disease-specific associations of the SD biomarker with clinical outcome.

Introduction

Here, we validate a noninvasive scalp electroencephalography (EEG) biomarker of spreading depolarizations (SDs) against simultaneous intracranial recordings in a multicenter cohort of craniotomy and craniectomy patients. First defined in 1944 by Leão [1], SDs are pathological waves of abrupt, near-complete breakdown of transmembrane ion gradients that propagate slowly (1-8 mm/min) across the cortex and disrupt cortical activity [2] [3] [4] [5]. SDs can cause acute injury in the presence of metabolic stress in the tissue through which they propagate [6] [7] [8]. Over the past two decades, invasive human recordings have established SDs as fundamental mechanisms of primary and secondary injury in major acute brain disorders, including traumatic brain injury (TBI), subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH), and ischemic and hemorrhagic stroke [3, [9] [10] [11] [12] [13] [14]. SDs are strongly associated with progressive lesion expansion, delayed cerebral ischemia, and poor neurological outcomes [9, [15] [16] [17] [18] [19]. Preclinical and clinical data suggest that anesthetics (e.g., ketamine [20] [21] [22] [23] [24] [25] [26]), vasodilators (e.g., cilostazol [27, 28]), and perhaps even neurostimulation techniques (e.g., vagus nerve stimulation [29], and transcranial direct current stimulation [30]) can modify SDs in the brain. Therefore, detection and continuous monitoring of SDs as potentially treatable mechanisms of worsening brain damage may help improve outcome and pave the way towards precision health. Brain injury is heterogeneous, which makes diagnosis and targeted therapy challenging [26, 31, 32]. Intracranial pressure (ICP) monitoring remains the cornerstone of neurocritical care for patients with severe TBI, yet it is an incomplete surrogate for the tissue-level pathophysiology driving secondary injury. Cerebral microdialysis studies have shown that metabolic crisis, marked by an elevated lactate/pyruvate ratio, persists in pericontusional tissue after TBI independent of cerebral perfusion pressure [33]. Multimodal monitoring cohorts have reinforced this discordance, finding that brain hypoxia and metabolic crisis are common in patients with acute brain injury despite a normal ICP [34]. SDs have emerged as a key driver of this hidden metabolic burden, with excitotoxicity and metabolic crisis accompanying SD events even when standard intensive care unit (ICU) parameters appear unremarkable [35]. Building on this mechanistic link, a prospective multicenter study from the Co-Operative Study on Brain Injury Depolarizations (COSBID) has demonstrated that SDs occur in over half of patients undergoing neurosurgery for TBI and are independently associated with unfavorable outcome after accounting for conventional prognostic covariates [36]. A subsequent, larger COSBID cohort confirmed that repetitive SDs, or clusters, are independently associated with poor neurologic recovery at six months, even when adjusted for other clinical and monitoring variables [19]. The DISCHARGE-1 study found that SD variables, in contrast to ICP, contributed to every multiple regression model of early, delayed, and total brain damage, patient outcome and death after SAH, strongly suggesting that SDs are also an independent biomarker of progressive brain injury in this disease [17]. Together, these findings indicate that ICP and other ICU measures alone are insufficient to capture the trajectory of secondary brain injury, motivating detection of SDs as a complementary monitoring modality that can provide earlier and more direct insight into ongoing cortical dysfunction.

There is a need for noninvasive monitoring of SDs. Currently, gold-standard detection of SDs in clinical practice requires placement of subdural electrode strips during neurosurgical procedures. However, this monitoring method is inherently invasive, offers limited spatial coverage of the brain, and is available only to the small subset of patients who undergo invasive surgery. This subset represents a small fraction of the overall acute brain injury population: in the United States alone, an estimated 2.8 million TBI-related emergency department visits, hospitalizations, and deaths occur annually [37], yet only about 10% of cases are classified as severe [38]. The vast majority of TBI, roughly 70-90%, is mild [39], and even among moderate-to-severe cases managed in the hospital, most are treated conservatively without a craniotomy or decompressive procedure. A major goal in the field has therefore been to develop methods for noninvasive SD detection that can be deployed broadly across the neurocritical care population, to include the much larger population of acute cases with an intact skull, such as mild to moderate brain injury, concussion, and stroke. Scalp EEG is noninvasive, widely available in ICUs worldwide, and offers global cortical coverage, making it an attractive candidate for noninvasive SD detection. However, its physics work against this goal: strong volume conduction through the skull acts as a spatial low-pass filter, blurring focal cortical activity and yielding poor spatial resolution, particularly for sources within sulci or deep cortex [40]. This blurring is reduced after decompressive hemicraniectomy, but the resulting skull defect introduces its own confound, breach rhythm, a high-amplitude, sharply contoured artifact arising from the loss of the skull's filtering effect [41]. Scalp recordings are further limited by low signal-tonoise ratio, slow baseline drift, and non-propagating depressions, making visual identification of SDs difficult and time-consuming. These same distributed, subtle patterns, however, may be more tractable for automated detection algorithms than for human reviewers. To date, no clinically validated algorithm exists for reliable, noninvasive SD detection.

Previous attempts to detect SDs using EEG have had limited success. Early feasibility work relied on visual inspection of scalp EEG, with mixed and sometimes contradictory results. Hartings et al. [42] reported amplitude depressions associated with 81% of intracranially detected SDs through visual inspection in severe TBI patients, and Drenckhahn et al. [43] reported similarly positive results in malignant stroke and SAH patients, both using invasive electrocorticography (ECoG) as ground truth. Bastany et al. [44] described brief direct-current (DC) deflections in the scalp EEG of patients with epilepsy that they attributed to SD; however, these events were not confirmed invasively, and their short duration (approximately 23 s on average) and morphology differ from the scalp correlates of SD previously characterized against concurrent ECoG [42, 43]. By contrast, Hofmeijer et al. [45] monitored 18 stroke and 18 TBI patients with 21-electrode EEG and found no SDs on visual inspection; because these patients neither underwent craniotomy nor had invasive recordings, it remains unclear whether SDs were even present. In a commentary on this negative result, Hartings et al. [46] cited the absence of established scalp-EEG detection criteria, spatial low-pass filtering by the intact skull, and short review epochs as possible confounds; using longer epochs instead, they identified candidate SD-like patterns in a TBI patient, again without invasive confirmation. In a separate cohort, Sivakumar et al. [14] monitored 15 TBI and 20 aneurysmal SAH (aSAH) patients with invasive ECoG and found no continuous scalp EEG pattern reliably associated with invasively confirmed SDs. In parallel, simulation-based studies explored automated, noninvasive SD detection independent of visual inspection, including an early forward-modeling and detection framework [47], a scalable deep-learning tracker [48] and graph attention network [49] for simulated SDs, and finite-element simulations of concussive SDs used to optimize DC-EEG electrode spacing [50].

More recently, related automated approaches have been tested on real human data. Wu et al. [51] reframed single-channel SD detection as a spectrogram-imaging task, adding a spectral dimension to the feature set, though validated only on augmented data from two patients (12 SDs total) and, by design, forgoing the spatial dynamics essential to tracking a traveling wave like SD. Brown et al. [52] introduced a dedicated high-resolution DC-EEG array (CerebroPatch) and reported proof-of-concept detection of SD-associated DC shifts against invasive ECoG in neuro-ICU patients, though usable, artifact-free data were obtained from only one of fifteen enrolled subjects. We developed WAVEFRONT [47, 53], an automated, explainable algorithm, and used it to establish, for the first time, the feasibility of noninvasive SD detection in a single-center cohort of 12 severe TBI patients with decompressive hemicraniectomy, using simultaneous scalp and intracranial recordings as ground truth. Collectively, these efforts illustrate steady but incremental progress; however, it remains uncertain whether noninvasive SD detection generalizes beyond single-center cohorts, let alone whether it is sufficiently reliable for clinical use.

Prior noninvasive detection efforts, including our own single-center report [53], have been limited to small, homogeneous, single-institution cohorts, to visual inspection, or to simulation, leaving open whether a noninvasive SD biomarker can generalize across the clinical and anatomical heterogeneity of real-world acute brain injury, or extend meaningfully to the much larger population of patients with an intact skull. Here, we address these gaps directly. We train and rigorously validate WAVEFRONT, our automated, explainable scalp-EEG biomarker of SD, on a multicenter cohort of 32 patients spanning three institutions, three etiologies (TBI, SAH, and malignant hemispheric stroke [MHS]), and both skull-present and skull-absent conditions, using more than 1,100 intracranially confirmed SDs as ground truth. Using an exhaustive leave-four-out cross-validation framework, we quantify sensitivity, specificity, spatial specificity relative to the groundtruth electrode strip, temporal synchrony with individual and clustered SD events, and the ability to noninvasively estimate cumulative SD burden from continuous EEG.

Critically, we then test whether this biomarker, without retraining, generalizes to the far larger population lacking invasive ground truth: applying pre-trained WAVEFRONT to an independent, multicenter cohort of 607 intact-skull cardiac arrest patients from the International Cardiac Arrest REsearch (I-CARE) consortium, we show that noninvasively estimated SD burden is associated with neurological outcome. Together, these results provide multicenter, out-of-sample validation of a quantifiable noninvasive SD biomarker, motivating further studies to extend SD monitoring beyond the invasively monitored minority to the broader intact-skull population previously inaccessible to it.

Methods

We first provide some details on the multi-center cohort used in this study, which includes 32 patients with mild to severe acquired brain injuries who underwent craniectomy or craniotomy (17 skull-present, 15 skull-absent patients) and intracranial ECoG strip electrode placement. We then describe the intracranial SD ground truth labeling, and introduce our automated SD detection method, emphasizing the explainability of WAVEFRONT by providing intuition for its main steps. Finally, we describe the external validation cohort of patients after cardiac arrest.

Dataset Overview

The study dataset comprised 32 patients drawn from three neuromonitoring centers (referred to as Centers A, B, and C). These patients underwent simultaneous invasive ECoG and noninvasive scalp EEG recording. Across centers, recordings captured diverse pathologies, including aSAH, TBI, MHS, and acute subdural or epidural hematomas. Demographic and clinical characteristics, including age, sex, diagnosis, electrode location, skull condition, and the total number of spreading depolarizations (SDs), are included in Tables I-III.

In Center C (ClinicalTrials.gov ID: NCT00803036), continuous EEG (cEEG) was recorded (95±42.2 hours on average) following craniectomy using a DC-coupled Moberg amplifier (CNS Advanced ICU EEG Amplifier from MOBERG ICU Solutions), with standard ICU montage of 19 individually placed electrodes on scalp and a sampling frequency of 256Hz. ECoG was recorded using the same amplifier. In Center B, ECoG recordings were acquired using DC-coupled Moberg amplifier and used for SD ground truth annotations (see ECoG SD annotations), while cEEG was recorded using either the same amplifier (four TBI patients), or separate amplifier (Natus Xltek Brain Monitor, one TBI and seven SAH patients). For these eight patients, we synchronized the cEEG and ECoG amplifiers using the common High-Frequency (HF)-ECoG (>0.5Hz) channels recorded using both amplifiers. This was done by power-envelope extraction and cross-correlation of a representative channel segment with good recording quality, identified through visual inspection of the ECoG signals. This amplifier synchronization technique enables utilization of a larger cohort of ICU data for non-invasive SD detection studies in the future. In Center A, ECoG data was recorded using GT205 amplifier using Powerlab A/D converter (ADInstruments), while scalp EEG was recorded using DC-coupled BrainAmp (BrainAmp MR plus, Brain Products). ECoG and scalp EEG recording started on separate laptops with manual synchronization (person starting the recording had to press a key on both systems simultaneously). Thus, the synchronization is not perfect. However, since SD is a slow event, with the speed of mm/min, a synchronization error of a few seconds or less is adequate for the SD detection task, where the temporal resolution for calculation of performance metrics is in the order of minutes (see Results).

ECoG SD annotations.

In all centers, during the craniectomy/craniotomy procedure, or through a burr hole in the skull, a strip of six monopolar ECoG electrodes, with an interelectrode distance of 1cm, was placed on the ipsilateral hemisphere, close to the site of injury. The recorded ECoG signals were visually assessed by clinical experts to identify and annotate the SD episodes in the dataset.

SDs were identified and annotated following recommendations by the Co-Operative Studies on Brain Injury Depolarizations (COSBID): In this study, an episode of SD refers to a unique SD propagation, while considering all electrodes of the ECoG strip. For each episode, the starting time point of SD was annotated based on a chosen ECoG electrode, which is not necessarily the same across episodes/patients. This temporal annotation may not reflect the actual starting time point of the SD wave because of (i) the limited spatial coverage of a single ECoG strip, as SDs may originate outside this coverage, and (ii) inherent variability in visual inspection of the episodes. Limited strip coverage also implies that some SDs are not captured by ECoG at all: many SDs in the human cortex are spatially restricted [5], whereas scalp EEG samples a much larger cortical area. Because detection performance was scored in time windows against strip annotations (see Results), any WAVEFRONT detection without a concurrent ECoG-annotated SD was counted as a false positive, regardless of where on the scalp it occurred. Some of these apparent false alarms may therefore correspond to genuine SDs outside the strip coverage, in which case the reported false positive rates overestimate the true false-alarm rate.

Demographics. The cohort included 19 males and 13 females, with an average age of 55.216.4 years, reflecting the typical age span of neurocritical care populations monitored for SD activity. Mean age was 59.8, 58.9, and 48.5 years in Centers A, B, and C, respectively, with Center A contributing primarily aneurysmal SAH cases, Center B predominantly additional SAH patients, and Center C consisting entirely of TBI patients, predominantly with acute subdural hematomas.

The cohort exhibited a broad distribution of neurological etiologies associated with susceptibility to spreading depolarizations. Preprocessing pipeline. As noted in Dataset Overview, EEG recordings in this cohort originate from two different acquisition systems: the CNS Advanced ICU EEG Amplifier (MOBERG ICU Solutions), used with or without continuous electrode-impedance logging, and the Natus Xltek system. Because these two setups differ in what electrode-level quality information is available, we adapted a multi-step preprocessing pipeline, built on the framework we introduced in [53], separately to each recording condition and data format, as detailed below.

Band-pass filtering and downsampling: Our earlier work [53], consistent with independent findings in the field [42], identified the Delta band (0.5-4 Hz) as the frequency range yielding the strongest non-invasive SD detection performance. Guided by this, scalp EEG signals here were band-pass filtered to isolate the Delta band using a Hamming-windowed sinc finite impulse response (FIR) filter (filter order 1000; transition bandwidth 0.02 Hz), then downsampled to 10 Hz, comfortably above the Nyquist rate for this band, to reduce the computational load of the downstream analysis.

Identifying and excluding poor-quality segments of EEG signal: Continuous ICU recordings inevitably contain stretches degraded by environmental and procedural noise, for instance amplifier disconnection while a patient is moved for imaging or a bathroom break, or transient loss of electrode-scalp contact from patient movement. For the 12 patients whose recordings included continuous impedance logging (CNS MOBERG system), we adapted the impedance-based masking approach from [53]: at each electrode, intervals whose recorded impedance exceeded twice the electrode's own median impedance (2Mch) across the full recording were flagged and excluded from the subsequent SD-detection steps. For the remaining 20 patients, no impedance channel was available, so poor-quality segments were identified solely through the amplitude-outlier step described next. Retaining both types of recordings lets us test WAVEFRONT's robustness under the dataquality conditions most ICUs actually have (typically without impedance monitoring), rather than only under best-case conditions.

Outlier removal:

The final step of preprocessing addresses residual amplitude artifacts. We flagged abnormally large positive or negative excursions using Tukey's fences [55]: a sample is treated as an outlier if it falls below Q1 -k(Q3 -Q1) or above Q3 + k(Q3 -Q1), where Q1 and Q3 are the signal's first and third quartiles and k sets the stringency of the criterion. We used k = 3, the conventional threshold for 'far-out' extreme values, and masked the corresponding segments at each electrode. Supplementary Fig. 2 in [53] illustrates this preprocessing sequence applied to ipsilateral EEG from a representative skull-absent TBI patient over a four-hour window. The cleaned signals were then passed to WAVEFRONT for SD detection.

Noninvasive WAVEFRONT biomarker detection on scalp.

We applied an enhanced version of the WAVEFRONT algorithm, originally introduced in [47] and clinically validated in [53], to detect and characterize noninvasive SD signatures, which we refer to as WAVEFRONT biomarkers, in scalp EEG from all 32 patients in this cohort (Tables I-III). WAVEFRONT is built to be explainable: every intermediate representation and the final detection output are interpretable, in contrast to end-to-end deep learning approaches whose internal decision process is not explainable. Rather than learning SD signatures purely from data, WAVEFRONT's architecture is constructed around established physical properties of SD waves (their propagation speed, the spatial width of the associated suppression, and their characteristic spread pattern), so that the detection logic reflects known SD biophysics rather than an unconstrained pattern-matching process. This design directly targets the core difficulty of scalp-based detection: SD signals arriving at the scalp are weak, noisy, and spatially blurred by the intervening tissue. WAVEFRONT addresses this by breaking the detection problem into a sequence of smaller, more tractable subproblems, each aimed at isolating and classifying individual SD wavefronts rather than attempting to detect the whole propagating event at once. Concretely, power envelopes are first computed from each electrode's filtered signal, and intervals of sustained power suppression are extracted; these suppressed intervals are then mapped onto a twodimensional representation of the electrode array, producing discrete candidate wavefronts. WAVEFRONT then tests each candidate for genuine propagation by estimating its velocity vector using optical flow, a standard computer-vision technique that infers displacement from frame-to-frame brightness changes; here, the 'frames' are successive snapshots of the spatial suppression maps rather than conventional video. Linking these local propagation estimates across time and space lets the algorithm reconstruct the larger-scale trajectory of SD activity across the cortex, a strategy that also helps absorb distortions introduced by cortical folding (sulci and gyri) and supports detection of more complex, multidirectional spread patterns.

We closely follow the steps of the WAVEFRONT algorithm, proposed in [53]: 1.

Epoching, Envelope Extraction, and Depression Identification: Cleaned EEG recordings are divided into fixed-length epochs, and a power envelope is computed for each electrode's band-limited signal within each epoch. Within these envelopes, we flag depression events (transient drops in amplitude consistent with an SD) using a correlationbased detector tuned to recognize the characteristic first-derivative-like decline at the onset of a depression. The result is a time-resolved catalog of candidate depressions across the electrode array, which feeds the spatial reconstruction step that follows.

2. Spatial Projection and Reconstruction of Depression Wavefronts:

Candidate depression events, initially defined on the three-dimensional scalp electrode layout, are mapped onto a two-dimensional cylindrical projection of the scalp. The resulting sparse spatial map is interpolated, thresholded, and downsampled into a binary image in which connected depressed regions represent candidate wavefronts. Working in this flattened 2D projection removes distortions caused by the irregular electrode geometry and makes the subsequent characterization of spatial spread more tractable.

3. Optical Flow-Based Motion Estimation:

For each pair of consecutive binary images, WAVEFRONT computes a dense optical-flow field to characterize how candidate wavefronts move between frames. The resulting local velocity vectors are pooled into spatially contiguous groups ('bounding boxes', or BBoxes), and a directional histogram is built for each BBox to identify its dominant direction(s) of motion. This pooling step is what lets the algorithm recover a stable, mesoscale estimate of propagation direction while averaging out pixel-level noise in the flow field.

4. Orientation Bounding Boxes, Effective Propagation Measure, and Consistency Scoring:

BBoxes sharing a common dominant orientation are merged into Orientation Bounding Boxes (OBBoxes). Before further scoring, each OBBox is tested against the Effective Propagation Measure (EPM), a metric that separates genuine translational movement from static fluctuations or artifactual 'pop-up'/'fade' patterns that mimic movement without true spatial spread. OBBoxes falling below a learned EPM threshold are discarded. We normalize the resultant vector by the average flow magnitude in the box; this rescales EPM to a fixed [0, 1] range, with 0 indicating no net translational movement and 1 indicating fully coherent, unidirectional propagation, which in turn makes it possible to learn a single generalizable EPM threshold across boxes of different overall motion magnitude (see Results). OBBoxes that pass the EPM test then undergo spatiotemporal consistency scoring against their neighbors, checked for agreement in direction, speed, and timing, and candidates whose implied propagation speed falls outside physiologically plausible bounds are excluded, so that only biologically credible propagation patterns are carried forward.

OBBoxes do not meet minimum coherence criteria are dropped, based on frame-level scores derived from the underlying OBBox statistics. The remaining positive frames are then linked across time with a sliding temporal window to form a continuous detection trace. A given frame is ultimately labeled as containing an SD when it shows sustained, directionally coherent, spatially contiguous wavefront propagation supported by OBBoxes that have passed both the EPM and consistency checks. The output of this step is a timeresolved trace of SD detections that can be directly compared against the intracranial ground truth (see Results).

External validation cohort (I-CARE).

To assess the biomarker in patients with an intact skull, we analyzed continuous EEG from the 607 patients in the publicly released portion of the multicenter I-CARE database [56], which includes comatose patients aged 16 years or older who underwent continuous EEG monitoring after return of spontaneous circulation following cardiac arrest. No invasive recordings are available in this cohort. The WAVEFRONT model trained on the full 32-patient invasive-monitoring cohort was applied without retraining to the full bilateral montage of 19 scalp electrodes at standard 10-20 locations, so that SD burden reflects detections across the whole scalp. SD burden was defined as the percentage of time with WAVEFRONT-detected SD; it was computed in 12-h windows advanced in 1-h steps, with bootstrap 95% confidence intervals, and summarized over consecutive 24-h periods measured from the start of each EEG recording (0-24, 24-48, and 48-72 h) and the cumulative 0-72 h period. Patients who died (CPC 5) were excluded from outcome comparisons because death after cardiac arrest may reflect decisions to withdraw life-sustaining treatment in addition to neurological injury. Group differences in SD burden were summarized with Cohen's d, and odds ratios (OR) for poor outcome were expressed per 10% increase in SD burden.

Generalization Across Centers, Etiologies, and Skull Conditions

We evaluated algorithmic stability across clinical sites, diagnostic categories, and skull configurations. WAVEFRONT maintained consistent performance under within-sample and out-of-sample evaluations (Fig. 3a). When trained on patients from one center and tested on another, detection performance remained stable, demonstrating resilience to differences in acquisition hardware, ICU noise environments, and montage variations.

Stratification by etiology (TBI vs. SAH; MHS was not analyzed separately because only three patients were available) showed comparable performance (Fig. 3b). Similarly, performance was preserved in both skull-present and skull-absent patients (Fig. 3c), indicating that major anatomical differences in cranial filtering did not degrade detection quality. These results indicate that WAVEFRONT captures cortical depression dynamics that generalize across key clinical, anatomical, and technical sources of variability. WAVEFRONT achieved robust noninvasive SD detection using a stringent leave-four-out cross-validation framework, demonstrating consistent performance on entirely unseen patients. The method showed close spatial correspondence between scalp-derived depressions and invasive ground truth in representative skull-present and skull-absent cases and exhibited strong generalization across centers and etiologies indicating stability across diverse clinical environments. Scalp detections maintained high temporal fidelity with individual SDs, accurately tracking both isolated and clustered events. Finally, WAVEFRONT provided reliable quantitative estimation of SD burden over long ICU recordings, paving the way towards noninvasive measurement of cumulative SD activity.

External Validation of WAVEFRONT SD Burden After Cardiac Arrest

All analyses above were performed in neurosurgical patients with concurrent ECoG. We next asked whether the biomarker carries clinically relevant information in patients with an intact skull, in whom SDs cannot be recorded invasively. To this end, the WAVEFRONT model trained on the 32patient invasive-monitoring cohort was applied, without retraining, to continuous EEG from 607 patients who were comatose after cardiac arrest, drawn from the multicenter I-CARE consortium (see Methods). The primary comparison was between survivors with good (CPC 1-2, n=225) and poor (CPC 3-4, n=29) outcome. SD burden in the first 24 hours of post-arrest EEG recording did not differ between these groups (Cohen's d = -0.012; OR 0.995; p=0.95). Separation emerged after the first day (Fig. 5): on day 2 (24-48 h), SD burden tended to be higher in the poor-outcome group (OR 1.106; p=0.068), and on day 3 (48-72 h) the difference was largest and statistically significant (Cohen's d = 0.556; OR 1.151 per 10% increase; 95% CI 1.040-1.275; p=0.007). SD burden accumulated over the full 0-72 h window was likewise higher in the poor-outcome group (Cohen's d = 0.457; p=0.024). Patients who died (CPC 5) had consistently lower SD burden throughout monitoring. Two factors may contribute to this finding. First, death in this population may reflect decisions to withdraw life-sustaining treatment, not only the severity of neurological injury. Second, WAVEFRONT detects depressions of ongoing activity, which cannot be resolved in maximally suppressed cortex; low burden in this group may therefore reflect a detection ceiling rather than a true absence of SDs. This consideration motivates including EEG suppression ratio as a covariate when SD burden is used for prognostication.

Discussion

In a multicenter cohort spanning three institutions, three etiologies, and both skull-present and skull-absent conditions, we validated a quantifiable noninvasive biomarker of SD in out-of-sample analyses. Using more than 1,100 intracranially confirmed events as ground truth, WAVEFRONT achieved a true positive rate of 0.973 with a false positive rate of 0.060 under stringent leave-four-out cross-validation, with performance preserved across centers, diagnoses, and skull conditions, addressing the central limitation of prior noninvasive SD efforts, which were confined to single institutions, small or homogeneous cohorts, or simulation, and had not been tested for generalization across the clinical and anatomical heterogeneity of real-world neurocritical care.

Beyond detection accuracy, WAVEFRONT's scalp-derived depressions were spatially consistent with the location of the underlying ECoG strip in representative cases and were absent during intervals without ground-truth SDs, arguing against the biomarker simply tracking generic cortical slowing or artifact. Its explainable, biophysics-constrained architecture, in which detection stages map onto known SD propagation speed, spatial width, and directional coherence, distinguishes it from end-to-end deep-learning approaches whose decision process is opaque, a property likely to matter for clinical adoption and regulatory scrutiny of a novel neuromonitoring biomarker. WAVEFRONT also provided a quantitative estimate of cumulative SD burden that correlated strongly with intracranially confirmed SD frequency, suggesting that continuous, noninvasive burden estimation, rather than binary event detection alone, may be clinically actionable given the established relationship between SD burden and secondary injury.

The extension to the I-CARE cardiac arrest cohort is, to our knowledge, the first demonstration that a scalp-EEG SD biomarker trained exclusively on invasively confirmed, surgically treated patients can be applied, without retraining, to a much larger population for whom invasive confirmation is never obtained. That SD burden on day 3 of post-arrest EEG monitoring was associated with neurological outcome in this entirely intact-skull cohort supports the premise motivating this work, that SD monitoring need not remain confined to the small, surgically selected minority of acute brain injury patients, but may generalize to the broader population, including mild-to-moderate TBI, stroke, and other intact-skull etiologies, for whom no other means of SD detection currently exists. The consistently lower SD burden observed in patients who ultimately died (CPC 5) may reflect withdrawal of life-sustaining treatment and a detection ceiling in maximally suppressed cortex rather than a true absence of SD activity, underscoring that suppression ratio should be considered alongside SD burden in future prognostic models.

Several limitations warrant consideration. Ground-truth SD annotation relied on a single ECoG strip with limited spatial coverage; SDs originating outside this coverage could be missed or misattributed, and, because many SDs are spatially restricted [5], some scalp detections counted as false alarms may reflect genuine SDs beyond the reach of the strip. Region-resolved analyses of detections remote from the strip, ideally with multiple strips as ground truth, will be needed to determine how many apparent false alarms represent true SDs. The training cohort, while larger and more heterogeneous than prior single-center efforts, remains modest in absolute size, and cross-institutional differences in amplifier hardware and montage, though shown not to degrade performance here, warrant confirmation in additional independent centers. Critically, the I-CARE validation could not be benchmarked against invasive ground truth by design, since intact-skull patients do not undergo ECoG monitoring; the outcome association we observe is therefore prognostic evidence of biological relevance rather than direct confirmation of detection accuracy in this population. Additionally, our training and validation cohorts consisted exclusively of adults, and I-CARE included only patients aged 16 years or older, so the biomarker's performance in pediatric populations, who differ in skull thickness, cortical maturation, and SD susceptibility, remains untested.

A particularly timely application is SD-targeted pharmacotherapy. The ongoing INDICT trial (Improving Neurotrauma by Depolarization Inhibition with Combination Therapy), a phase 2 randomized feasibility trial led by Hartings and colleagues across three US centers [26], uses a tiered treatment protocol, including ketamine, to suppress SDs in patients with severe TBI, but by design requires surgical placement of a subdural strip electrode for both enrollment and treatment guidance, restricting the trial to patients who undergo neurosurgery. A validated noninvasive biomarker like WAVEFRONT could extend this paradigm in two ways: first, by enabling noninvasive screening or confirmation of SD activity to complement or reduce reliance on invasive monitoring within trials structured like INDICT, and second, and more broadly, by opening SD-guided ketamine titration to the far larger population of TBI, stroke, and other acute brain injury patients who never undergo strip placement and are therefore categorically excluded from current SD-targeted trials. Realizing this will require prospective studies directly comparing noninvasive and invasive SD burden as triggers for ketamine dosing; the present results provide a foundation for such studies.

Future work should also prioritize larger, prospective multicenter validation; extension to additional intact-skull etiologies such as mild TBI, migraine, and non-surgical stroke; and extension to pediatric cohorts, where noninvasive SD monitoring could be particularly valuable given the added ethical and practical barriers to invasive neuromonitoring in children.

Results

We validate the performance of our WAVEFRONT technique on this multicenter cohort across three centers, three neurological conditions (TBI, SAH, and MHS), and two neurosurgical procedures (craniectomy or skull-absent, and craniotomy or skull-present). Intracranial recordings identified 1,119 SDs, providing a unique dataset with broad representation of SD morphology, frequency, and recurrence patterns. This heterogeneous SD dataset allows for within-and out-of-sample validations, assessing the spatiotemporal specificity of the detected events, and quantification of SD burden in long time intervals.

Cross-Validation performance across all patients.

Algorithmic performance is quantified using a leave-four-out cross-validation framework: in each fold, four patients are held out for validation, and the remaining 28 patients are used to train WAVEFRONT. We perform exhaustive cross-validation over all possible train-validation sets and obtain average receiver-operating characteristic (ROC) curves (Fig. 1). The optimal operating point is found by minimizing a validation error which is defined as the distance to the ideal operating point (true positive rate [TPR] = 1; false positive rate [FPR] = 0), i.e., 𝜖 𝐶𝑉 = √𝐹𝑃𝑅 2 + (1 -𝑇𝑃𝑅) 2 . This cross-validation error 𝜖 𝐶𝑉 provides a tradeoff between low false-alarm rate and high detection rate. The resulting optimal operating point is a "knee" point on the validation curve (Fig. 1, right inset). Temporal Synchrony Between Scalp and Intracranial SDs

We examined temporal alignment between scalp-derived depression detections and intracranial SDs using representative examples (Fig. 4b). WAVEFRONT detections showed close temporal correspondence with the onset, evolution, and recovery phases of SDs recorded on ECoG. This synchrony was preserved across centers, across skull conditions, and across etiologies.

During clustered SD activity, scalp EEG depressions tracked prolonged suppression periods corresponding to multiple, closely spaced SDs, while occasional undersegmentation occurred only during the densest clusters, consistent with known challenges even in invasive recordings (Fig. 4b).

Quantification of SD Burden From Continuous EEG

To determine whether WAVEFRONT could provide a quantitative measure of SD burden, we compared the total duration of scalp-detected depression episodes with the number of ECoG-confirmed SDs in overlapping 30-hour windows (Fig. 4a). The resulting relationship was strongly linear, yielding an R² of 0.7411, demonstrating that cumulative depression duration reflects underlying SD load. Because SD burden correlates with metabolic crisis and lesion progression, the ability to estimate this measure noninvasively from standard EEG may represent a clinically meaningful advance for continuous neuromonitoring. Based on the within-sample validation results, WAVEFRONT, once trained on large enough data, performs well on each of the patient groups. The out-of-sample validation performance is obtained by training the model on one group, e.g., Center A, finding the optimal set of thresholds (𝑇ℎ𝑟 1 𝑜𝑝𝑡 , 𝑇ℎ𝑟 2 𝑜𝑝𝑡 ), and applying the trained WAVEFRONT on the other group, e.g., Center B.

Validation curves are obtained across a predefined range of thresholds (𝑇ℎ𝑟 3 𝑜𝑝𝑡 , 𝑇ℎ𝑟 4 𝑜𝑝𝑡 ). Strong generalizability was observed from skull-present to skull-absent patients (93.8% TPR, 5.0% FPR out-of-sample average performance). More importantly, the out-of-sample validation results show that WAVEFRONT, once trained on all groups of patients with different conditions, performs well on each of the subgroups.
