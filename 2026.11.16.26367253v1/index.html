To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 2 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Text Sentence
p = 1.01 The lead SNPs within the ARL14EP and MMPED1 gene body also show association with circulating FSH at p = 1.01x10⁻⁷⁹ and p = 2.39x10⁻⁹ respectively.
p = 2.39 The lead SNPs within the ARL14EP and MMPED1 gene body also show association with circulating FSH at p = 1.01x10⁻⁷⁹ and p = 2.39x10⁻⁹ respectively.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

To leverage independent genetic evidence from multiple species, we developed a cross-species gene-based GWAS meta-analysis framework and applied this to Dizygotic Twinning (DZT) in humans and cattle. After meta-analysis within species, we converted human and cattle GWAS summary statistics to gene-level association signals within each species, mapped genes across species through human-cattle orthology, and then combined gene-level evidence for orthologous genes across the two species. DZT is defined as giving birth to multiple offspring after a single pregnancy resulting from spontaneous multiple ovulation. In humans, DZT occurs in about 1% of pregnancies with heritability estimates of 20% to 30% whilst in cattle DZT occurs in 1-5% of pregnancies and heritability is estimated at about 11%. Here, we present the largest human GWAS meta-analysis including data from 1,395,285 participants. We identified 16 genetic regions of interest, including 12 independent loci from the SNP based analyses and 4 genes identified in the gene-based MAGMA analyses. Among the prioritized genes are follicle stimulation hormone receptor (FSHR) and growth differentiation factor 9 (GDF9), that both are involved in ovarian folliculogenesis. Cattle GWAS summary statistics were based on data from 2,656 Irish Holstein bulls with estimated breeding values for predisposition to twinning based on 2,238,936 birth records, and 86,391 German Holstein dams. After combining the gene-level analyses for 13,225 genes mapped as orthologous across the human and cattle data we identify FSHR as a central locus for DZT in both humans and cattle. We find evidence of additional associations, including ACVR2A, which plays a role in embryonic implantation and TUFM, which has previously been implicated in premature ovarian insufficiency. Our approach provides a promising framework for future cross-species comparative GWAS studies. It shows benefits for gene discovery and gene prioritization and in our application expands available functional follow-up data, allowing us to leverage cattle eQTL data to resolve human GWAS loci. The method and underlying philosophy presented here are transferable to other shared phenotypes across several species.

Introduction

Dizygotic twinning (DZT) is defined as giving birth to multiple offspring after a single pregnancy resulting from a spontaneous multiple ovulation. 1, 2 DZT has been observed as a heritable trait in both humans and other mono-ovulatory mammals. [3] [4] [5] [6] Currently, well-powered genome-wide association studies (GWAS) of DZT exist for humans and cattle. 2, 7, 8 We therefore decided to explore to what extent the genetic regulation of DZT is shared across species, and whether a combined meta-analysis could help solve some of the challenges in interpreting the results from GWAS. To this end, we perform the largest human GWAS for DZT to date and explore the feasibility of cross-species meta-analysis by combining human and cattle data.

The trait of multiple ovulation is regulated through complex interactions among endocrine signals and intra-ovarian signaling pathways that govern follicular development. Key components of these regulatory networks were identified through early endocrine studies, mouse knockout models, and genetic analyses in sheep, cattle and humans. 5, 6, 9 Central to this process are variations in circulating follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) concentrations during follicular selection, oocyte-derived signaling mediated by growth differentiation factor 9 (GDF9) and bone morphogenetic protein 15 (BMP15), and the temporal dynamics of FSH receptor (FSHR) and luteinizing hormone receptor (LHCGR) expression during follicular maturation. 6, 9 Collectively, these mechanisms determine the number of follicles that survive the selection process and proceed to ovulation. GWASs into human DZT provided support for the central role of hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian signaling in the regulation of DZT and ovulation. 2, 10 Our 2024 GWAS including mothers of DZ twins and DZ twins as cases identified four loci associated with DZT: FSHB, SMAD3, GNRH, and ZMFP1. 2, 11 Gene-based testing and functional follow-up also indicated roles for FSHR and IPO8. These genes are involved in the follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) and transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β) pathways, which regulate circulating FSH levels, sensitivity to FSH and ovulation. 12, 13 The SNP-based heritability (h 2 ) was estimated at 2.4%, 2 while heritability estimates from pedigree studies for human DZT range between 20-30%. 14 In Holstein dairy cattle, the SNP-based heritability estimates for multiple births are similar to those in humans (1.9-2.6%). 7 There is a specific interest in DZT in dairy cattle as DZT is economically disadvantageous for farmers. 15 For example, in opposite-sex twin offspring, freemartinism as characterized by underdeveloped reproductive organs and masculinized features due to in-utero hormonal exchange between the twins, often results in infertile female offspring. 16 A previous GWAS identified a locus on bovine chromosome 11 containing the FSH receptor (FSHR) and Luteinizing Hormone/Choriogonadotropin Receptor (LHCGR) as associated with DZT in an Irish sample of 2,656 Holstein cattle. 7, 17 These parallels-spanning both genetic architecture and highly similar phenotypes across humans and cattle-motivated a cross-species GWAS meta-analysis. Meta-analysis typically relies on overlapping SNP markers, which are rare across species. For instance between chimpanzees and humans, which share a relatively recent common ancestor, only ~0.2-0.3% of variants are shared, 18 and the overlap between humans and cattle can be expected to be smaller still. The number of conserved orthologous genes, i.e. conserved genes shared with species inherited from a common ancestor is much larger, with about 72-76% of genes having a direct one-to-one ortholog. 19 The extensive conservation of orthologous genes across these species opens the possibility of gene-level meta-analysis as a viable and powerful alternative.

Earlier studies have investigated the overlap between gene sets associated with fertility and body size in humans, dogs and cattle. [20] [21] [22] [23] These were not gene discovery designs, but rather focused on cross-species overlap functions as candidate-gene prioritization and pathway-convergence tools, clearly establishing that multi-species GWAS comparisons can help identify conserved growth mechanisms underlying stature/height and fertility. For instance, an 87-gene set from cattle was tested for cross-species convergence with results from a human reproductive-timing GWAS, identifying significant enrichment of shared genes. 20 Eight of the cattle fertility-related genes that were associated with age at natural menopause and four with age at menarche. This overlap exceeded chance expectations and supports a shared pool of genes influencing fertilityrelated biology across mammals.

To our knowledge, no formal full cross-species meta-analyses of GWAS data have been conducted previously. Integrating GWAS results from humans and cattle has the potential to enhance genetic discovery in both species. Human populations generally exhibit shorter linkage disequilibrium (LD) blocks than commercial cattle populations, where extensive LD can hinder fine-mapping efforts. 24 In addition, combining evidence across species is expected to increase statistical power to detect loci involved in conserved biological pathways. Cross-species analyses may also facilitate downstream functional interpretation. Extensive expression quantitative trait locus (eQTL) resources are available for a diverse range of tissues in cattle and other livestock species, including tissues that are difficult or impossible to obtain from living humans. 25 Access to reproductive tissues and experimental populations in cattle enables functional validation of candidate genes and regulatory mechanisms. 17 Therefore, we propose a cross-species gene-level GWAS framework that could leverage complementary genomic and transcriptomic resources across species to accelerate the identification and characterization of genes and apply this framework to DZT and ovulation rate.

In our study we take a stepwise approach by first presenting the GWAS meta-analysis in humans and secondly perform cross-species genome-wide gene-based association tests (using MAGMA) 26 based on the newly presented GWAS results of DZT in humans and two cattle GWASs. In the human GWAS we increased discovery sample to 1,395,285 participants from European ancestry (Table 1), which included 13,554 mothers of naturally conceived DZ twin cases, 41,550 DZ twin cases and 1,340,181 controls, leading to an effective sample size of 131,893 (Methods). We include data from 10 different countries and 15 separate cohorts. The two cattle GWASs were performed in 2,656 Irish Holstein bulls with estimates of genetic merit for twinning from 2,238,936 calving records, and a cohort of 86,391 German Holstein dams with twinning records. 7 We meta-analyze the cattle MAGMA Z-scores using a Stouffer's Z, weighted based on attenuation ratio, and subsequently meta-analyze the cattle and human MAGMA Zscores via an unweighted Stouffer's Z, restricting to genes that are orthologous between the two species. Results

Updated meta-analysis of human DZT identifies novel DZ twinning loci

We performed a GWAS meta-analysis of 15 human cohorts with DZT data, based on 1,395,285 participants from 10 countries (Table 1). The effective sample size was estimated at 131,893 (Methods). In total, we identified 12 independent genetic loci that reach genome-wide significance (Table 2; Fig. 1; Supplementary Fig. 1). Our results include the four previously identified loci, linked to FSHB, SMAD3, GNRH, and ZFPM1. 2 We performed gene-based testing using MAGMA (Supplementary Table 1; Supplementary Fig. 1), 26 which highlighted four other genes in additional genetic regions. Two of these genes, IPO8 (z = 5.09; p = 1.80×10 -7 ) and SHBG (z = 4.69; p= 1.36×10 -6 ) have been identified for DZT before and two of these genes, REXO1 (z = 4.65; p = 1.67x10 -6 ) and SGMS2 (z = 4.72; p = 1.16x10 -6 ) are new findings. An overview of the 16 genetic regions, with the 12 genome-wide significant loci and the 4 additional MAGMA significant genes, and the most likely causal gene within this region, is provided in Table 3.

For each of the 12 significant loci in the human DZT GWAS, we performed gene prioritization with the FLAMES software. 29 For each locus, FLAMES annotates fine-mapped credible sets and applies a machine learning classifier to score each gene, where this score denotes the level of biological evidence for that gene being regulated by a set of credible causal SNPs in the locus.

For most loci, FLAMES was able to predict a likely causal gene (Table 2; Supplementary Table 2) above the confidence threshold, except for the rs182900232 locus on chromosome 5, rs1611747/rs1362104 locus on chromosome 6 and rs4074376 on chromosome 11. The locus zoom plots and the FLAMES decision trees for these three loci are provided in Supplementary Fig. 2 3 4. Rs182900232 on chromosome 5 is located within the AFF4 gene, even though as explained below, the nearby GDF9 gene is a more likely candidate based on previous work (Finemapping in the Cross-species analysis). 30, 31 The gene-based tests indicate that the most likely candidates for the rs1611747/rs1362104 locus on chromosome 6 are HLA-G and TRIM31. The large peak on chromosome 11 seems to include two independent signals: FSHB (rs11031006; p = 1.08×10 -32 ) and WT1 (rs5030179; p = 5.48×10 -9 ). WT1 is located >2MB downstream of FSHB and the two SNPs (rs11031006 and rs5030179) are in low LD (<0.001) with each other, implying that these are two independent signals. This is further confirmed by the Locus zoom plots and FLAMES decision trees in Supplement Fig. 5 and 6 and the gene prioritization from FLAMES (Table 2). Although the FSHB locus was already associated with DZT in previous GWASs, WT1 is a new finding (Table 2). WT1 has been previously implicated in testicular cancer and ovarian cancer and is reported to play an important role in tumor invasion and metastasis. [32] [33] [34]

Transcriptome-wide association study

To investigate functional effects of the genetic signals identified, we conducted a transcriptomewide association study (TWAShttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?broken=tg2jrC with FUSION. 35 We derived expression weights from tissue that is relevant for the hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian signaling; hypothalamus, pituitary gland, ovary, testis, vagina, breast, uterus and whole blood from GTEx v8 (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?broken=tg2jrC 3 4 5 6 7 8 9 10 https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?broken=tg2jrC. 7 8 9 10 11 12 13 14 ). 36 These analyses both confirm several previous GWAS findings and our presented new findings in the SNP-based tests. IPO8 surpassed the tissue-specific Bonferroni correction in the hypothalamus, breast, testis and whole blood, while SHBG expression did so in the pituitary, and DEAF1 expression in the hypothalamus. All three genes also were previously identified in the MAGMA or FLAMES analyses. FSHB was prioritized by FLAMES as the most likely gene but could not be included in the TWAS as FSHB's predicted gene expression did not exhibit sufficient heritability to be considered. ARL14EP is located 92kb from FSHB and has been previously associated with other fertility traits such as PMOS/PCOS. 2, 37 A significant association was found between DZT and expression of ARL14EP in the pituitary gland, hypothalamus, ovary, and testis.

Proteomic convergence of twinning and circulating FSHB levels

Given the FSHB prediction resulting from the FLAMES analysis and the well-established role of FSH in folliculogenesis, we aimed to verify the likely pathway implicated by this locus by testing the lead SNP of this locus for cis-protein quantitative trait loci (pQTL) signals. The lead SNP for DZT, rs11031006, is a fine-mapped cis-pQTL for plasma level FSHB in the FinnGen analysis of the UK Biobank Pharma Proteomics Project (ppQTL = 9.5×10⁻¹⁰⁴, βpQTL = -0.232, PIP = 0.54). 38 Colocalization analysis with the GWAS locus finds a high chance of a shared causal variant (posterior probability shared causal variant (H4) = 0.997). The pQTL effect direction aligns with carriers of the minor allele having lower circulating levels of FSH and decreased incidence of DZT. The lead SNPs within the ARL14EP and MMPED1 gene body also show association with circulating FSH at p = 1.01x10⁻⁷⁹ and p = 2.39x10⁻⁹ respectively. Effectively this indicates that some of the associations of DZT to ARL14EP and MMPED1 in the TWAS could be the result from their association to circulating FSHB, rather than ARL14EP and MMPED1 transcript levels.

Heritability and polygenicity

We estimated SNP-based heritability from the updated human DZT GWAS with SBayesRC, as implemented in gctb, 39 both on the complete GWAS summary statistics and on the summary statistics without genome-wide significantly associated regions (Methods) for an effective sample size of 131,893. The heritability was estimated at 3.85% (SD = 0.004), while 2.91% (SD = 0.008) heritability remained after removing the LD blocks containing the 12 significant loci from the summary statistics. This indicates that the 12 loci account for 0.94% overall heritability, which is approximately 24.4% of the total estimated SNP based heritability. The SBayesRC method also provides the so-called S estimate, which indicates the polygenicity of traits by estimating the percentage of SNPs that are expected to have a nonzero effect on a trait. The S estimate for human DZT was 0.03% (sd = 0.004%), an order of magnitude lower than for example height (2%, sd = 0.04%) or schizophrenia (5%, sd = 0.3%). 40

Evolutionary selection of DZT

We investigated the burden of selection of DZT in ancient DNA with the ancient genome selection (AGES) tool, 41 on the PGS for DZT as well as the 12 leading SNPs. Overall, we show a slight negative selection on the genetic basis of DZT, as indicated by the negative genome-wide genetic correlation between the DZT and AGES summary statistics (rg = -0.12, p = 0.044) (Supplementary Table 11). Of the 12 lead SNPs associated with DZT, 10 were available on the AGES website. Among these, rs11678857, located in the FSHR gene on chromosome 2, showed evidence of negative selection (Supplementary Fig. 15). Our estimates of negative selection for the DZT genes, is in line with previous estimates included in Mbarek et al (2024). 2

Cross-species gene-based meta-analysis implicates additional genes in twinning

To leverage independent genetic evidence from multiple species, we performed cross-species MAGMA meta-analysis, by meta-analyzing the MAGMA Z-scores of orthologous genes tagged by SNPs in both cattle and human GWAS, using the Stouffer's Z-score method 27, 28 (Fig. 2, Methods 42 ). We compute MAGMA Z-scores for 21,361 cattle genes and 20,011 human genes.

We subsequently subset to 15,184 genes mapped as orthologous with high confidence from the Ensembl database and further subsetted to 13,225 orthologous genes with a MAGMA Z-score for both humans and cattle. 43 To verify whether we were capturing the same genetic signal we performed correlation analysis on the gene-level Z-scores. To ensure the Z-scores are on a similar scale, and have an SD of 1, Z-scores were robustly recalibrated using median-centering and MAD-based scaling. To reduce the larger noise component stemming from null-effect genes, the calibrated Z-scores were shrunk using adaptive Bayesian shrinkage to stabilize per-gene estimates. Pairwise concordance between cohorts was assessed via Pearson correlation computed on the shrunk Z-scores (Fig. 3). We found significant correlations between the two cattle GWASs (rho = 0.458; p < 1x10 -300 ), and the human and cattle GWASs (human and Irish bulls: rho = 0.137, p = 8.22x10 -56 and human and German dams: rho = 0.214; p = 2.7x10 -136 ). A leave-onechromosome-out analysis shows that this correlation is driven by genes in the FSHR peak, where leaving human chromosome 2 or cattle chromosome 11out leaves all correlations positive, but near zero and non-significant (rho = 0.007, 0.009 and 0.002), respectively. This is not entirely unexpected, given the low observed correlation (0.12) between unshrunk gene effect sizes for stature, a much more well-powered and polygenic phenotype, between cattle and humans 23 . Next, we performed attenuation weighted meta-analysis of two cattle cohorts, followed by an unweighted meta-analysis across human and cattle results (Methods).

In the meta-analysis, many associated loci were not common to both the human and cattle data, except for the implication of FSHR/LHCGR in DZT. Additionally, we find significant associations (after FDR correction) for 14 genes, 8 of which have not been implicated in either of the human and cattle GWAS before (Table 4). We report the genes with the highest MAGMA Zscore per locus.

We find that TUFM reaches significance (Fig. 2; pCattle = 0.0029, pHuman = 0.00015, pMeta = 3.2x10 - 06 ). This gene has previously been implicated in a consanguineous family suffering from premature ovarian insufficiency, and was subsequently validated in mouse models. 44 SOX15 passes the significance threshold (pCattle = 0.0028, pHuman = 9.3x10 -05 , pMeta = 2.1x10 -06 ).

No previously established relationships between SOX15 and twinning or any fertility phenotypes have been established in the literature. SOX15 is located close to SHBG which is the top hit in human data and seems the more likely effector gene.

Interestingly, GDF9 reaches significance in the meta-analysis (pCattle = 4.2x10 -05 , pHuman = 0.013, pMeta = 6.8x10 -06 ). A human genome-wide significant SNP, rs182900232, is located closely to GDF9, but GDF9 itself does not reach genome-wide significance in the human MAGMA analysis.

Still, a role for GDF9 has previously been established in DZT in humans and in sheep, 30, 31, 45 and a GWAS for DZT in a Nigerian sample found one significant locus, with the lead SNP residing within the GDF9 gene body (Hur et al., in preparation). The involvement of GDF9 in DZT findings is consistent with the known roles of GDF9 in promoting primordial follicle development and granulosa cell proliferation. 46 ACVR2A (pCattle = 3.0x10 -03 , pHuman = 2.3x10 -03 , pMeta = 3.9x10 -05 ) plays a central role in the TGFβ pathway as one of its main receptors and in interacting with SMAD proteins. In mice loss of function knock out of ACVR2A has resulted in issues with embryonic implantation, and ACVR2A has been implicated for preeclampsia in humans. [46] [47] [48] Other novel genes that surpass the significance threshold are TIGAR (pMeta = 5.0x10 -06 ), REV3L (pMeta = 2.1x10 -05 ), GNL1 (pMeta = 6.6x10 -05 ), and COASY (pMeta = 1.4x10 -04 ) several of which have a known relation with fertility phenotypes. Loss of TIGAR has been implicated in meiotic defects and reduced quality of mice oocytes. 49 REV3L variants have previously been associated with a GWAS for age at natural menopause. 50 No previous association has been found between GNL1 and twinning or reproductive phenotypes. However, given that GNL1 is located on human chromosome 6 in the MHC region and most genes from this region are not orthologous and correlated through complex LD structure, we advise caution in interpreting this finding.

Fine-mapping in the Cross-species analysis

Fine-mapping the likely causal variants in a locus is greatly affected by the strength of LD in that locus. Cross-species analyses can help fine-map regions likely to contain causal variants in conserved regions. Although larger haplotype regions are generally conserved, the size and fine structure of haplotype blocks differ between species. 51 This is particularly relevant for the study of domesticated species, as their LD structures tend to be much larger as a result of selective breeding. 24 An example of a difficult locus to fine-map is the FSHR/LHCGR locus and the cross-species analyses seem to help identify the relevant gene for DZT. The FSHR/LHCGR locus is the only region with significant GWAS variants in the separate cattle and human meta-analyses and contains genes with biologically plausible roles in DZT due to their link with the ovarian growth pathways. Fine-mapping this locus is particularly difficult in cattle, as the signal is strongest near the transcription start site of LHCGR but the LD block continues into the FSHR gene body, providing a plausible biological mechanism for both genes (Fig. 4a). The overlap between the human and cattle signal is mainly located within the FSHR gene body. Previous research in cattle has shown the lead SNP of the FSHR/LHCGR locus in cattle is an eQTL for FSHR expression but not for LHCGR expression. 17 Combining these lines of evidence, we find that across species, the signal covers the full FSHR gene body; FSHR function strongly converges with the implication of FSHB as the strongest signal in the human GWAS, and eQTL follow up in cattle implicates FSHR even though the lead SNP in cattle is closer to the LHCGR transcription start side (Fig. 4).

This triangulates to FSHR being the most likely major effector gene for DZT in the FSHR/LHCGR locus.

Fine-mapping is also challenging at the GDF9/AFF4 locus. In the human GWAS, the genomewide significant GDF9/AFF4 locus has a lead SNP located in AFF4 and has significant SNPs in GDF9. FLAMES is unable to resolve this locus, likely due to multiple strong signals being present within both the GDF9 and AFF4 gene bodies. Neither gene reaches significance in the human gene-based MAGMA tests while the cattle-human meta-analysis finds GDF9 to be significant, the signal of AFF4 nearly completely disappears. When overlaying the cattle and human locus plots, we see that in cattle the genetic signal appears to be strongest in GDF9. The significance in the meta-analysis and the genome-wide significant locus strongly suggest GDF9 is the effector gene of this locus. This converges with earlier work showing rare variants in GDF9 to be associated with DZT. 30, 31 A recently conducted GWAS in a smaller sample in Nigeria (Hur et al, in preparation), with high DZT rates, confirms this result as a SNP within the GDF9 gene is the only genome-wide significant locus. This specific locus is undetectable in the European-ancestry samples included in this meta-analysis due to too low allele frequencies.

Discussion

Here, we present a GWAS meta-analysis of human DZT, including data from 1,395,285 participants of European descent and a novel cross-species gene-based meta-analysis approach, investigating conserved mammalian DZT genes based on GWA meta-analyses in human and cattle. In the human GWAS meta-analysis, we identified 16 genetic regions of interest, including 12 independent loci and 4 additional genes in the gene-based analyses, and estimated a SNP-based heritability of 3.85% on the underlying scale of liability. Our results are in line with previous estimations that that DZT in humans appears to be not highly polygenic, 52 since the 12 genomewide significant loci explain 24.4% of the SNP based heritability and only 0.03% (sd = 0.004%) of all common SNPs are predicted to have an effect on DZT. When combining the human GWAS with cattle data in a cross-species gene-based meta-analysis framework, we identify shared genes associated with DZT. The results contain strong evidence for FSHR as key genes in both human and cattle and our findings of GDF9 and ACVR2A further underline the influence of FSH/ovulation pathways.

The cross-species meta-analysis highlights shared, e.g. FSHR association in both species, as well as divergent genes, e.g. FSHB association in human data only, that still implicates a shared conservative biological mechanism of DZT in humans and cattle. FSHR is the strongest finding in the human-cattle meta-analysis. It plays a key role in folliculogenesis by encoding the receptor for follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), a major regulator of ovarian follicle development. 8, 53 In contrast to evidence for the FSH receptor in both species, the FSHB association is human specific. FSHB has been implicated for DZT in previous GWASs, 2, 10 as well as in several other human fertility phenotypes including PMOS/PCOS, endometriosis, and affects FSH levels in blood. 5,54- 56 The difference around FSHB might be due to missing effect variants in the target populations, different environments and breeding strategies / reproductive behaviors of cattle and humans, or a contrast between cattle breeds selected for production traits and an outbreeding human population. Both of FSHR and FSHB, however, point to conserved TGF-β signaling as a central pathway underlying twinning in humans and cattle. This suggests that, although the core biological process involving FSH signaling and ovulation appears to be shared across species, the relative contributions of individual genes may differ.

Our findings concerning FSHR, GDF9 and ACVR2A further emphasize the central role of the TGF-β pathway in DZT. The TGF-β pathway has various roles in uterine processes critical for reproductive success and that influence FSH levels. 46 FSHR and ACVR2A are both targeted receptors of this pathway while GDF9 is one of the ligands. 46, 57 In sheep, oocyte-derived GDF9 (growth differentiation factor 9 or FecG) is essential for normal folliculogenesis, with variants in GDF9 displaying strong associations with twinning rate. The growth factor is present in oocytes from the primary stage of follicular development until ovulation. 45, 58 ACVR2A, identified in the cross-species analyses, plays a central role in the TGF-β pathway as one of its main receptors and in interacting with SMAD proteins. Knock out mutations in mice in ACVR2A also show impairment of decidualization, the process of cell differentiation to support embryo implantation.

These findings further support our previous hypothesis that the TGF-β pathway is central in DZT, both for influencing ovulation rate and implantation and pregnancy success.

Our cross-species gene-based GWAS meta-analysis shows two main benefits for gene discovery, gene prioritization and fine-mapping. We show that it can boost the power of gene-based analyses for genes influencing DZT liability in both species. The strongest signal for FSHR is observed in the meta-analysis of cattle and human data, but the locus was also identified in human and cattle analyzed separately. Importantly, the meta-analysis identifies genes at FDR significance that have moderate signal in both species, but never reach significance alone, i.e. GDF9 from which we would infer that the gene (but not necessarily the same variants) affects DZT liability in both species.

Our method adds a new source of in gene prioritization and functional annotation data for GWAS loci: cattle eQTL data. For example, in the locus on chromosome 2 in the human GWAS, FLAMES prioritized FSHR as the most likely causal gene, but LHCGR located within the locus, also has a plausible contribution to DZT. 5 The prioritization of FSHR is further supported by recent research in cattle which demonstrated that the lead SNP at the FSHR/LHCGR locus is an eQTL for FSHR expression, but not for LHCGR expression. 17 In that study, granulosa cells were collected from 41 Holstein cows, and gene expression levels were tested for associations with QTL genotypes. Even though this results does not completely rule out LHCGR, it provides strong support for the role of FSHR. Comparable analysis in humans would likely rely on a less biologically relevant, but more readily available, tissue (e.g. whole blood), thereby reducing the direct interpretability of the findings. 25, 36 Our methodology therefore enables the identification of target genes shared across species, which could subsequently be investigated through QTL analyses in other species using live and biologically relevant tissues.

Our study includes limitations and challenges that should be addressed in future studies. While our analysis incorporates a large share of available human biobank data it is limited to European ancestries. Increases in diversity and sample size for human gene discovery is limited by phenotypic information on DZT and more general reproductive history in women. 59, 60 Our metaanalyses show that it is vital to remove any twin pregnancy that was conceived through assisted reproductive technology (ART) as these strongly ablate the DZT signal. In most biobanks, however, crucial information on twinning, being a twin or mode of conception often is lacking. 10, 52 It therefore remains crucial to increase the effort of collecting both genotyping and in-depth phenotyping information concerning twinning and female fertility in more and in more diverse populations. These more diverse studies are not just important for understanding DZT, but are relevant for understanding for healthy pregnancies and overall female fertility. 5, 52 We see the approach as presented in this paper as promising for comparative cross-species studies, and as a valuable addition to ongoing research in this field. Whereas we presented results based on pooling gene-level association evidence through meta-analysis, other approaches treat GWAS hits as starting points for a network-based integration. In a recent study of human obsessivecompulsive disorder (OCD) and dog compulsive disorder (OD), 61 cross-species colocalization yielded gene communities and enriched processes that were not apparent from the, relatively small, human OCD GWAS alone, and GO enrichments were more numerous/stronger in the cross-species network than in the human-only OCD network. The method was successful, even when the implicated seed genes in the two species did not overlap.

Extending the comparative framework we have presented for DZT to other phenotypes requires several considerations. Firstly, trait definitions should be aligned at the mechanistic rather than nominal level 62 , as we demonstrate here by comparing DZT driven by multiple ovulation in human and cattle or based on careful psychometric analyses of traits as was done for human OCD and dog OD. Superficially matched labels (e.g., "depression," "aggression") can describe or subsume genetically distinct constructs in different species. Secondly, potential phenotypes will vary substantially in the availability of well-powered GWAS summary statistics. In cattle, genomic data exist at sufficient scale for plausible phenotypes including temperament/handling reactivity, [63] [64] [65] whilst for other potential phenotypes such as cattle maternal separation response and human postpartum depression GWAS currently lack comparable power. 66, 67 Given these considerations, candidate phenotypes for future cross-species analysis as presented here may be prioritized according to (i) construct specificity, (ii) availability of adequately powered GWAS in both/all species, and (iii) plausibility of shared genetic architecture at the pathway level, rather than a nominal-level phenotype description.

Overall, we present the largest GWAS of DZT in humans to date, identifying 16 genetic regions of interest with a SNP-based heritability h 2 of 3.85%. We provide a framework for cross-species gene-based GWAS meta-analysis and demonstrate its usefulness, which we leverage based on human and cattle data to strengthen evidence for human DZT loci and to identify additional genetic regions of interest. Our results support a central role for FSH/ovulation pathways influenced by the TGF-β signaling pathway for DZT in both human and cattle.

Methods

Participants human GWAS

We included a total of 1,395,285 participants of European descent from 10 countries across 15 samples (Table 1). Six samples performed a GWAS with mothers of twins (MODZT) as cases and seven cohorts performed a GWAS with DZ twins (DZT) as cases and two cohorts performed two GWASs in independent samples, one with MODZT as cases and one with DZT as cases (Table 1). MODZT and DZT samples excluded cases where assisted reproductive technology (ART) had been used and controls were removed if there was any knowledge of DZ twins in the family. Details on genotyping, imputation and QC are provided in Supplementary Table 13 and Supplementary data 2. Here we describe the included participants and the phenotyping.

NTR

The Netherlands Twin Register (NTR) provided samples for both the MODZT and DZT GWAS. 68 The MODZT sample consisted of 1,808 cases and 6,373 controls, and the NTR DZT sample consisted of 3,810 cases and 7,695 controls. This sample also included data that was collected for the PROSPECT study cohort. The samples between the two analyses were selected to be independent of each other based on pedigree information (e.g. no overlapping families between the two samples). In case of overlap (e.g. both mother and twins genotype), priority was given to the MODZT GWAS. Zygosity was confirmed by DNA genotyping. Data on the mode of pregnancy were available from surveys sent out to mothers of twins or to parents upon registration of young twins, and from telephone interviews as part of a project on DZ twinning. 69, 70 Cases were excluded if they reported ART. If no reports on the mode of pregnancy were available, cases were excluded unless the twins were born prior to 1970.

QIMR

We included 4,942 cases and 29,327 controls from Australia and New Zealand. These data were collected in two waves: the previously analyzed sample of 3,273 cases and 24,009 controls (ANZ2023) 71 , and the newly collected sample of 1,669 MODZT cases and 5,318 controls (ANZ2026) 72 . Cases were screened for the use of fertility treatments based on questionnaire data.

MCTFR

All subjects in this sample were identified through Minnesota state birth records and recruited to establish a population-based registry of twins at the Minnesota Center for Twin and Family Research (MCFTR). 73 The current study included 568 mothers of DZ twins and 1,862 control subjects who were the parents of MZ twins from 1,062 families, including 800 complete parental pairs, 203 mothers, and 59 fathers. Most twins were born in the 1970s or early 1980s, when only few had access to fertility treatments.

WGHS

The Women's Genome Health Study (WGHS) is a population-based, prospective cohort of initially healthy, female North American health care professionals at least 45 years old at baseline, representing participants in the Women's Health Study (WHS) who provided a blood sample at baseline and consented for blood-based analyses. 74 The WGHS cohort has been described previously. 74 Additional information related to health and lifestyle were collected by questionnaire throughout the WHS trial and continuing observational follow-up. A total of 361 mothers of DZ twins and 20,183 controls from the WGHS were included in the current study.

MODZT were of reproductive age before the common use of ART (born at the latest in 1951).

deCODE

Mothers of twins or other multiples ('twins') were selected from among those taking part in deCODE's genetic studies based on a nation-wide genealogical database. 75 Control subjects were individuals participating in deCODE's genetic research, from which both mothers of twins and the mothers' first-degree relatives had been removed. In total, the deCODE sample included in the current study consisted of 2,877 mothers of DZ twins and 212, 767 controls. Only mothers who had given birth before 1991 were included, to ensure no fertility treatments were used.

FinnGen

In total, 2,009 MODZT were included as cases and 478,349 participants as controls for MODZT GWAS from the FinnGen database release 12 (R12). 76 Cases include mothers with sex-discordant twins, excluding births involving assisted reproductive technologies. Mothers of twins with triplets or more children from a single delivery were excluded from the study. 1st and 2nd degree relatives of MODZT, and participants aged <17 yrs. were excluded from controls. Samples from the Finnish twin study were excluded.

MoBa

The Norwegian Mother, Father and Child Cohort Study (MoBa) is a population-based pregnancy cohort study conducted by the Norwegian Institute of Public Health. 77 MoBa contributed 658 MODZT and 53,046 controls and 474 DZT and 40,622 controls to the meta-analyses efforts. Data on the participating mothers were linked with data from the Medical Birth Registry of Norway (MBRN; a national health registry containing information about all births in Norway) to assess their reproductive history. Mode of conception was reported by record linkage to the MBRN, which contains information on IVF/ICSI use as reported from the fertility clinics, or by self-report in MoBa. 70, 78 Mothers who received fertility treatments were excluded from the sample.

TwinLife

The Finnish Twin Cohort (FTC) study comprises three longitudinal studies of twins born before 1958, 84 twins born 1975-1979, 85 and twins born 1983-1987. 86 A total of 8,188 twins were included in the analyses (6,021 DZ twin individuals and 2,167 MZ twin individuals used as controls).

The Twins Early Development Study (TEDS) is a longitudinal twin study that recruited over 16,000 twin pairs born between 1994 and 1996 in England and Wales through national birth records. 87 The sample consisted of 2,919 DZ twins and 2,580 MZ twins (one per pair), as controls were included from TEDS UK. DTR

A total of 1,186 DZ twins and 391 MZ twin controls were included in the analyses from the Danish twin register (DTR). 89 To ensure natural conception, only twins born before 1970 were included.

Lifelines

Lifelines is a multi-disciplinary prospective population-based cohort study examining in a unique three-generation design the health and health-related behaviours of 167,729 persons living in the North of the Netherlands. Controls consist of MZ twins, their families and singletons' families. GWAS and Meta-analyses

A genome-wide association analysis was conducted in each participating cohort by logistic regression under an additive genetic model using fastGWA-BB. 91 Analyses were adjusted for population structure using the first ten principal components (PC) of genetic ancestry and studyspecific factors such as batch and genotyping platform and the genetic relatedness matrices (GRM) as covariates. For complete MZ twin pairs, one twin per pair was randomly selected.

Before meta-analysis, we removed SNPs with low minor allele frequencies (<0.0001), low imputation quality (r 2 < 0.8) and deviations from Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium (P < 10 -5 ) from each cohort. A total of 28,162,584 variants passed these quality control steps and were kept in the meta-analysis. Next, we collated the individual GWAS results through meta-analysis. Results from individual GWAS (MODZT and DZT) were combined by the fixed-effects inverse variance method based on the regression β estimates and standard errors from each study implemented in METAL software. 92 DZ twins carry half of the genetic information of MODZT. Genotyped DZ twins for whom no maternal genotype data were available were included in the meta-analysis, taking account of the differential genetic information from mothers of twins and twin offspring as described in the supplementary data of Mbarek et al 2024. 2 Briefly, the regression coefficient from the DZ twin GWAS is multiplied by 2 to convert it to the same scale as the coefficient from the MODZT GWAS and the standard error is scaled accordingly using the delta method, whereby multiplying the coefficient by 2 also multiplies its standard error by 2, allowing the two estimates to be combined in the meta-analysis. The genome-wide significance threshold was set at the level of P < 5.0×10 -8 . Functional Mapping and Annotation (FUMA) was used to provide genome-wide, gene-wide and regional visualization of the results. 93 Before the results were visualized by FUMA, we filtered SNPs on effect allele frequency (EAF) <0.01 and SNPs had to be measured in at least the mean (561,706) of participants included per SNP, retaining 11,370,680 SNPs. Full details of participants, cohorts and genotyping can be found in Table 1 and Supplementary Table 8. Full details of the individual GWASs and the meta-analysis plan can be found in the standard operating procedure circulated to the consortium study cohorts and in Supplementary Data 1, as well as the earlier papers of the TGC. 2, 10, 83

Fine-mapping and Gene prioritization

For fine-mapping, FUMA was employed to identify the independent significant SNPs that showed a genetic signal. 93 Multi-marker Analysis of GenoMic Annotation (MAGMA) analysis was performed to run the gene-based test and to look at tissue enrichments. 26 In MAGMA, input SNPs were mapped to 19,171 protein coding genes, so genome-wide significance was defined at P = 0.05/19,171 = 2.61 × 10 -6 . MAGMA was run with the standard settings. Gene prioritization software, fine-mapped locus assessment model of effector genes (FLAMES), 29 was used to identify the most likely causal genes for each of the independent SNPs. FLAMES utilizes the standard output of FUMA and MAGMA as starting points for the analyses and creates machine learning predictions from biological data linking SNPs to genes. It then evaluates these scores together with gene-centric evidence of convergence of the GWAS signal in functional networks. All six steps of FLAMES were performed as described in the documentation (https://github.com/Marijn-Schipper/FLAMES/tree/master). The FLAMES score of a gene denotes the combined evidence of functional convergence with other GWAS-implicated genes and the evidence from biological studies that link genes to fine-mapped SNPs in the locus of interest. The inputs for the classifier are created with the FLAMES annotation step, which annotates a credible set with various SNP-to-gene annotations. Credible sets were generated by fine-mapping the FUMA loci with the PolyFun implementation of SuSiE, 94, 95 under a single causal variant assumption. This single-variant assumption might not be true for each locus. Under the single-causal variant assumption, in the presence of multiple causal variants, depending on their strength of association, the signals will be aggregated into one credible set on which to make a FLAMES call on. However, given the complex LD association pattern of the meta-analysis and data-sharing preventing from using in-sample LD, multiple-causal variant fine-mapping is unlikely to be reasonably calibrated. 96 Network convergence-based PoPS scores were generated using the features provided by the FLAMES study, using the previously described MAGMA scores as input. The convergence-based scores and the locus-based SNP-to-gene scores are scaled and combined into a final FLAMES score for each gene in the locus. We prioritized genes above the recommended FLAMES threshold of a scaled FLAMES score larger than 0.248 and a raw FLAMES score larger than 0.136, ensuring that the set of predicted genes will cumulatively have an estimated precision of at least 75%.

Heritability

SNP-based heritability was estimated using SBayesRC, which is part of the gctb software. 39, 97 The results were first filtered on the effect allele frequency (EAF) of0.01 ≤ EAF ≤ 0.99. Variant EAF and the effect sizes were aligned with the NTR reference for the 1000 genomes variants. Discovery variants that were not part of this reference were discarded. Two heritability estimates were obtained: 1) based on all SNPs included in the meta-analyses, and 2) based on all genomewide SNPs minus the independent significant SNPs and SNPs in LD with the significant SNPs.

All SNPs within a 200kb window were removed. The independent significant SNPs were based on the information provided by FUMA. The heritability was estimated on the liability scale, with an estimated prevalence of 1%. For the effective sample size was calculated using ( 4x cases x controls)/ (cases + controls), whereby the DZT counted as half cases, leading to an effective sample size of 131,893.

Transcriptome-wide association study

We conducted FUSION TWAS 35 applied to the human meta-analyzed GWAS summary statistics to identify genes whose cis-regulated, predicted gene expression is associated with DZT. We used FUSION expression weights derived from hypothalamus, pituitary gland, ovary, testis, vagina, breast, uterus and whole blood GTExv8 tissue libraries 25, 98 of European genetic ancestry. We used the 1000 Genomes European linkage disequilibrium reference panel for all analyses. FUSION weights were only included for genes whose expression exhibited significant heritability by Genome-wide Complex Trait Analysis (GCTA). Within-tissue Bonferroni-correction was used to establish TWAS p-value significance and was based on the number of TWAS p-values generated per tissue. Genes within the major histocompatibility complex were not included, due to extensive linkage disequilibrium.

Cross-species gene-based meta-analysis

We performed MAGMA gene-based analysis on the human and two cattle GWAS. Human MAGMA analysis was performed using FUMA, using the European 1000Genomes individuals as a reference panel. For all three MAGMA analyses we used a window of 0, meaning only SNPs within the gene body were annotated to genes, and used the default --snp-wise=mean model.

Cattle gene annotations were generated using the Ensembl (v.110) gene annotations of the bovine ARS-UCD1.2 reference genome. MAGMA 26 gene-based testing was performed using Holstein cattle included in the run8 1000Bulls reference genotypes 103 for the Irish Holsteins, 104 for which only summary statistics were available. The summary statistics of the 86,391 German Holsteins were analyzed using the full in-sample PLINK files as the LD reference. 105 We meta-analyzed the cattle MAGMA Z-scores using Stouffer's Z-score method 106 . Although we also performed SNP-level inverse-variance weighted meta-analysis between the two cohorts for plotting, it is preferable to run MAGMA on the two cohorts separately, and meta-analyze the gene-based results due to the better matching of LD reference panels. Weighted Stouffer's Z should improve statistical efficiency and power relative to an unweighted meta-analysis, 28 but proper inverse-variance weighting (IVW) is not achievable here. First, postGSf90 imposes MAFdependent shrinkage on effect sizes, so the reported standard errors do not have the same scale interpretation as frequentist standard errors from SAIGE. 107 Second, postGSf90's standard errors themselves rely on a diagonal approximation of the prediction error variance, which is a further departure from the properties assumed by IVW. Together, these mean the postGSf90 and SAIGE SEs are not comparable variance estimates and cannot be combined directly. A sample-size-based weighting scheme is similarly not viable: the cattle and human phenotypes differ in scale (quantitative vs. case-control), and relatedness among the cattle samples further complicates any effective sample size calculation. Instead, to obtain an approximate measure of relative precision between the two GWAS, given the reasonable assumption that both studies are measuring the same underlying phenotype and identify SNPs that reach genome-wide significance in both, we calculate the attenuation coefficient between the Z-scores of variants that are significant in both studies (p < 5e-8). We use the median as an empirical attenuation factor to weight the studies in the gene-based meta-analysis. The attenuation factor of 1.1 gives that the German Holstein Dams are upweighted compared to the Irish Holstein Bulls. As a sensitivity analysis, relaxing the threshold to nominally significant SNPs (p < 0.05) yielded a similar coefficient (1.102 instead of 1.1).
