Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

What we found

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

The All of Us (AoU) Research Program multi-omics cohort was developed to expand discovery of the molecular mechanisms underlying health and disease. Here, we report on the AoU multi-omics cohort comprising proteomic (N = 10,056) and transcriptomic (N = 9,130) sequencing data generated from ancestrally diverse AoU participants. We provide comprehensive expression, splicing, and protein quantitative trait locus (e/s/pQTL) analyses and detected 182,246 QTLs, of which 54,255 finemapped to rare variants and 59.4% (32,235) resolve to a single causal variant. We provide multiple insights into rare variant effects and demonstrate that they are more likely to be ancestrally-restricted, replicate across molecular layers and impact known Mendelian disease genes (OR = 1.4) and approved drug targets (OR = 1.3). Rare variant QTLs from AoU further provided evidence for 5,186 variants, many absent from ClinVar, affecting established disease genes. Combined, the availability of multi-omics in AoU provides a key resource to identify the genetic and molecular mechanisms impacting health and disease across multiple ancestry groups in the United States.

Introduction

A major challenge in human genomics is mapping the molecular effects of genetic variation (1, 2). Previous studies have associated genetic variants to molecular phenotypes, such as gene expression, splicing, and protein-levels, to uncover functional effects impacting the majority of human genes (3) (4) (5) (6) (7) (8) (9). However, increasingly large studies have indicated that discovery of independent effects and rarer variant effects are far from saturated (8, 10). To overcome this, existing molecular reference datasets have traditionally encountered a trade-off between scale and diversity: large cohorts have been disproportionately European-ancestry (https://paperpile.com/c/jBx665Sr/rpjF+aJtE whereas more ancestrally diverse cohorts have often been too small to provide adequate power to identify rare variants effects (6, 7). As a consequence, much of the molecular landscape of rare genetic variation has remained uncharacterized, especially within diverse ancestry groups, limiting both biological discovery and clinical use.

Here, we report on biobank-scale genome sequencing and multi-omic profiling from 10,000 ancestrally diverse participants in the All of Us (AoU) Research Program (11) to deeply characterize the molecular effects of both common and rare genetic variation. We observe that ancestral diversity of AoU multi-omics substantially improves fine-mapping variants to a single likely-causal variant. The AoU multi-omics cohort also provides an unprecedented opportunity to study molecular effects across both transcriptome and proteome. Using this design, we reveal a vast repertoire of rare variants with large molecular effects that propagate across multi-omic layers. Further, we observe that rare variant effects were predominantly discovered in a single ancestry group and contained a subset of thousands of uncharacterized variants in well-established disease genes. We further show that variant effects can combine to produce dosage-like effects in every individual's genome. Together, these findings uncover a previously inaccessible set of rare and ancestry-enriched molecular effects and demonstrate the essential role of biobank-scale multi-omics to enable their discovery and interpretation in health and disease.

Results

Ancestral diversity improves fine-mapping of molecular trait loci

The AoU multi-omics cohort was designed to maximize representation of diverse computed genetic ancestry groups within the United States (N = 10,056; Fig. 1A) (Supplemental Table S1), providing an unprecedented opportunity to characterize the molecular effects of human genetic variation. Whole-genome sequencing and plasma proteomics were generated for all participants, with total RNA sequencing available for 9,130 individuals. The use of total RNA sequencing substantially expanded quantification of noncoding transcripts and genes involved in transcriptional regulation relative to poly(A)-selected whole-blood datasets (10) (Supplementary Figure 1). After quality control, 8,895 participants with total RNA-sequencing data and 9,581 participants with plasma proteomic data were retained for QTL mapping (Methods; Fig. 1A). The use of Olink Explore HT quantified 5,415 circulating proteins with high precision (Methods) (Supplemental Figure 2), with strongest coverage among extracellular and secreted proteins (Supplementary Fig. 3). Together, these complementary molecular measurements provide a foundation for systematic characterization of molecular traits across several diverse ancestry groups.

We mapped expression (e), splicing (s), and protein (p) quantitative trait loci (QTL) across all samples (minor allele frequency; MAF > 0.05%) and within individual genetic similarity groups (Methods) (Supplemental Tables 2 3 4), identifying eQTLs, sQTLs, and pQTLs for 96.8%, 100%, and 58.7% of genes, respectively (Supplemental Figure 4 ). We observed that 93.8% of QTLs had reproducible effects in at least three computed genetic ancestry groups (Methods) (Supplemental Figure 5) and strongly replicated QTLs from external efforts (https://paperpile.com/c/jBx665Sr/bP7P+aJtE+4pBM) (Supplemental Figure 6 7). Lower pQTL discovery rates were predominantly attributable to protein detection rates (Supplemental Figure 8). Comparison against existing molecular reference resources such as GTEx, TOPMed, INTERVAL, and UK Biobank (4, 5, 8, 10) demonstrated that the AoU QTLs expand the catalog of molecular trait associations, identifying 10,148, 2,450, and 1,143 previously unreported expression, splicing, and proteomics QTLs, respectively (Fig. 1B). Notably, 88% of novel eGenes and 16.9% of novel sGenes corresponded to lncRNAs and other noncoding RNAs (Fig 1C), indicating the increased sensitivity of total RNA sequencing relative to poly(A)-selected protocols used in most studies (Supplementary Figure 1, Supplementary Figure 9-10). Of the novel protein coding QTLs we identified, 159 were cancer drivers, 541 were Mendelian disease genes, 741 were genes under strong loss of function constraint, 475 were transcription and chromatin regulators, 199 were involved in RNA processing and splicing and 304 were drug targets (Fig 1C).

We performed statistical fine-mapping using SuSiE (https://paperpile.com/c/jBx665Sr/VoKU) in population-specific and combined analyses (Methods). Across all three QTL classes, the combined analysis identified more credible sets per molecular trait than the population-specific analyses (Supplemental Fig. S11), and achieved better fine-mapping resolution, with smaller credible sets and higher lead-variant posterior inclusion probabilities (PIPs) (Supplemental Figures S12-S13). The combined analysis identified 143,512, 17,674, and 11,922 credible sets for expression, splicing, and protein abundance, respectively, with 41-132% more eQTL credible sets than two other large blood eQTL studies when each dataset was restricted to protein-coding genes (Supplemental Figure S14). Molecular QTLs exhibited extensive allelic heterogeneity, with nearly half of eGenes harboring at least five independent eQTL credible sets (Fig. 1D; Supplemental Table 5). Across molecular phenotypes, 48.9%, 47%, and 52.7% of eQTL, sQTL, and pQTL credible sets, respectively, contained a variant with PIP > 0.9 (Fig. 1E; Supplemental Figure S13), yielding 84,831 high-confidence putative causal variants. Despite its smaller sample size, AoU also produced smaller pQTL credible sets than UK Biobank (mean sizes: https://paperpile.com/c/jBx665Sr/rpjF.1 versus 9.7; (5); Supplemental Figure S12). Fine-mapped variants were enriched for genomic features consistent with their expected molecular mechanisms (Supplemental Figure S15; Supplemental Table 6). eQTLs were strongly enriched within promoter and enhancer elements as well as splice regions. sQTLs showed pronounced enrichment at splice donor and acceptor sites, whereas pQTLs were enriched for protein-coding variants and other coding-disrupting annotations.

Biobank-scale multi-omics reveals the landscape of rare molecular effects Multi-omics in a large, biobank-scale cohort enabled more systematic characterization of increasingly rare variation. Of 30,719,753 variants with MAF > 0.05% we identified 47,572 unique rare fine-mapped variants (max ancestry group MAF <1%) across eQTLs, sQTLs and pQTLs (Supplemental Table 7). When restricted to single variant credible sets with high posterior support (PIP > 0.9) we identified 9,523 rare variants, which represent 15.7% of all putative causal variants across the molecular phenotypes (Fig 2A).

Rare variants and, likewise rare functional variants are typically enriched within specific computed genetic similarity groups (13). The AoU multi-omics cohort provided a unique opportunity to identify rare functional variants that were enriched within a similarity group. To examine this, we compared group enrichment between rare and common variants within our combined fine-mapped analyses across QTL modalities (Methods). As expected, group-enrichment of QTLs increased dramatically with decreasing allele frequency: 78% of variants with global allele frequency below 0.5% were group-enriched, compared to 16% of variants with global allele frequency greater than 1% (Supplemental Figure 16). The AFR-like similarity group contributed the largest fraction of group-enriched variants (44.7%), followed by EAS-like (22.2%) and SAS-like (15.5%) ancestries (Methods). Quantifying the magnitude of this enrichment further demonstrated that rare causal QTL variants showed greater ancestry enrichment than common variants, with a mean difference in enrichment odds ratio of 39.6 after adjusting for ancestry (95% CI, 38.1-41.0; P < 2.2 × 10⁻¹⁶). The level of enrichment varied dramatically across the similarity groups with the greatest enrichment in our cohort seen in the rare QTL variants in the EAS-like (median rare variant enrichment OR = 104) and SAS-like similarity groups (mean rare variant enrichment OR = 29.9) (Fig 2B). Rare QTL variants also showed large allelic effects (|log2 aFC|) across all genomic contexts with the majority of the large effect variants being found in promoter-like regions, proximal and distal enhancers and coding disrupting variants (stop-gain, frameshift) (Fig 2C) (Supplemental Table 8).

Cumulative genetic effects drive extreme molecular phenotypes in individual genomes

The presence of multiple independent eQTLs enabled high resolution analysis of how regulatory effects combine within individual genomes. Using an additive model, we calculated the expected heterozygous expression level resulting from increasing numbers of independent eQTLs affecting the same gene (Methods). Under this system, increasing the number of directionally consistent expression-decreasing alleles produced progressively larger cumulative effects, with the proportion of genes whose modeled expression reached a deletion-like range rising from 19%, when only the strongest allele was considered, to 72% when four or more alleles were considered (Fig. 2D) (Supplemental Table 9). Similarly, for expression-increasing alleles, the proportion of genes whose cumulative effects reached a duplication-like range increased from 34% when one allele was considered to 77% when four or more alleles were considered. Notably, these dosage-like expression states were markedly less prevalent when only common variants were considered, indicating the disproportionately large effects of rare variants and their contribution to cumulative molecular burden (Supplemental Figure 17). These results show that relatively small numbers of SNVs can combine to produce expression changes comparable in magnitude to those caused by gene deletions or duplications.

To quantify the impact of cumulative burden at the individual level, we estimated predicted gene expression using allelic fold changes (https://paperpile.com/c/jBx665Sr/QTYP) from high-confidence causal variants (PIP > 0.9) (Methods). Across all protein-coding genes, individuals carried a median of five genes with predicted expression decreases greater than 50% and 108 genes with predicted expression increases greater than 150% (Fig 2E) (Supplemental Table 10). Further, we directly observed that individuals with expected dosage-like effects were enriched among expression and protein underliers (Fig 2F; Supplemental Figure S18).

Genes that are under strong selective constraint were significantly depleted among the largest predicted perturbations in both deletions and duplications, with 18.9-fold and 30.7-fold reductions (Methods), respectively (p = 3.79 × 10⁻⁴ and p = 0.0033; Supplemental Figure S19 ). ClinGen annotations were also significantly depleted in both deletions and duplications (1.4-fold and 1.5-fold reductions, respectively; p = 3.95 × 10⁻⁴ and p = 0.0017). Burden and drug-interaction genes were significantly depleted only among deletions (1.4-fold and 1.2-fold reductions, respectively; p = 0.0149 and p = 0.0480). Despite this relative depletion, we identified thousands of strong cumulative QTL effects involving constrained genes.

Coordinated effects of rare variants across the regulatory cascade

The cumulative effects observed at the level of gene expression suggested that rare variants may perturb multiple stages of gene regulation. We therefore tested for coordinated effects across expression, splicing, and protein abundance. To characterize the propagation of cis-regulatory effects across molecular layers (expression, splicing and protein), we performed pairwise colocalization of fine-mapped molecular QTLs (15). Across 755 genes with detectable eQTLs, sQTLs, and pQTLs (Supplemental Tables 11 12), 86.2% had at least one regulatory effect shared across molecular layers (Fig. 3A). Cross-omic colocalization rates varied across molecular phenotype pairs, ranging from 39.4% to 60.2% (Supplemental Fig S21), and approximately 25% of assayed proteins showed evidence of colocalization with blood eQTL or sQTL signals, substantially higher than previous estimates of transcript-protein sharing (16). These patterns were robust across a range of colocalization probability thresholds (Supplemental Figure S20).

Despite extensive sharing found at the gene level, sharing between specific credible sets was limited. We identified 7,132 colocalized credible sets, representing 13% of all possible overlapping credible sets. Colocalization rates also differed across molecular phenotype pairs, with sQTL-pQTL signals showing the highest sharing rate (50.6%), compared with eQTL-pQTL (16.8%) and eQTL-sQTL (12.0%) signals (Supplemental Figure S22). Consistent with this, most genes contained only one or two colocalized signals despite frequently containing multiple independent QTL credible sets (Fig. 3B).

We next evaluated features that distinguish effects that propagate across the molecular layers. Allele frequency was a major driver of cross-omic sharing with colocalization rates between eQTL-pQTL and sQTL-pQTL both increasing markedly as allele frequency decreased; the most pronounced effect was seen for the sQTL-pQTL signals (Supplemental Figure S23). Consistent with this, the molecular effects of the colocalizing variants became increasingly concordant across the assays as allele frequency decreased and effect size increased (Fig 3C,D). Rare colocalizing variants were also enriched for coding and splice disrupting annotations (frameshift, stop-gained, splice region variants) whereas common colocalizing variants were comparatively enriched in distal enhancer elements (Fig 3E) (Supplemental Table 13). Altogether this suggests that rare variants are more likely to give rise to effects that propagate across the molecular layers.

Fine-mapped molecular QTLs reveal convergent trait associations and improve effector-gene prioritization

To investigate the phenotypic consequences of fine-mapped QTL variants, we first integrated our molecular QTL fine-mapping with fine-mapped GWAS for 102 complex traits (Supplemental Table 14). Colocalization identified 9,222 gene-trait associations across many physiological systems (Fig. 4A). The molecular signatures of these colocalizations varied across trait categories. eQTLs provided the broadest coverage, with an average of 25-30% of GWAS (Supplemental Table 15) credible sets per trait colocalizing with an eQTL for neurodegenerative, dermatological, autoimmune, and allergic-respiratory traits. pQTL colocalization rates were highest for autoimmune and cardiometabolic traits (8%) and neurodegenerative traits (7%), whereas sQTL rates were highest for neurodegenerative traits (7%), followed by allergic-respiratory and autoimmune traits (5% each). Both pQTLs and sQTLs provided nonredundant information relative to eQTLs: 68% of pQTL and 54% of sQTL gene-trait associations lacked a corresponding eQTL association.

Importantly, colocalizations often converged across molecular layers. We identified 1,617 gene-trait pairs with colocalization support from multiple QTL classes, including 100 supported by all three molecular QTL types (Supplemental Figure 24,Supplemental Table 16). CD40 provided an illustrative example: distinct eQTL and pQTL credible sets supported colocalization of CD40 across seven GWAS involving inflammatory bowel disease, multiple sclerosis, rheumatoid arthritis, systemic lupus erythematosus, LDL cholesterol, and monocyte count (Supplemental Figure S25).

To complement our colocalization analyses, we next used AoU phenotype data (https://paperpile.com/c/jBx665Sr/Mqa7) in a transcriptome-wide association study (TWAS) framework (https://paperpile.com/c/jBx665Sr/CwE3) (Methods). The major advantage of TWAS is the ability to integrate both common and rare QTLs. We first asked whether incorporating rare eQTL variants improves the prediction of genetically regulated expression. Across 7,226 genes harboring both common and rare eQTLs, models including rare variation exhibited improved cross-validated prediction accuracy relative to models restricted to common variants (mean ΔR² = 4%; Fig 4B). Further, prediction accuracy increased with increasing expression extremity, indicating that rare variation increases prediction of extreme molecular phenotypes.

We next asked whether the rare-variant component of molecular prediction improves prioritization of trait-associated genes beyond common-variant TWAS. Across 22 traits, we partitioned fine-mapped molecular predictors into common-and rare-variant components and tested the rare component conditional on the common component (Methods, Supplemental Table 17). Conditional rare-variant association signals were significantly enriched among colocalized gene-trait pairs relative to matched non-colocalized pairs (OR = 3.29, p-value = 4.1e-9) and significantly enriched for druggable genes (OR = 1.21, p-value = 0.004), approved drug targets (OR = 1.19, p-value = 0.013) from the Drug-Gene Interaction Database (https://paperpile.com/c/jBx665Sr/dX7l and Mendelian disease genes (https://paperpile.com/c/jBx665Sr/Qp0V) (OR = 1.47, p-value = 0.013). Together, these results showed that rare QTLs supplement common-variant TWAS to prioritize biologically and clinically-relevant effector genes.

Molecular consequences of rare variation in known disease genes

Rare genetic variation is abundant in clinically relevant genes, yet the molecular consequences of most variants remain unresolved (21). Given the multiple genetic ancestry design of the AoU multi-omics cohort, we evaluated if there were rare QTLs that impact known disease genes. Across 13,547 credible sets (46% resolved to single-variant credible sets), we identified 5,186 rare molecular QTL variants spanning 944 disease-associated genes annotated in ClinGen (22) (Fig. 5A top).

Strikingly 94.9% of these variants were either unclassified (92.1%) or annotated as variants of uncertain significance (2.8%) in ClinVar (23) https://paperpile.com/c/jBx665Sr/cNrv with only 1.1% (42 variants) having an existing pathogenic classification (Fig 5A bottom). ClinVar annotations were also significantly less frequent in non-European genetic similarity groups for variants in autosomal dominant and recessive genes (OR = 0.33 and 0.38; P = 4.4 × 10⁻⁶ and 6.5 × 10⁻¹² respectively; two-sided Fisher's exact tests; Fig 5B), demonstrating the lack of representation of group-enriched variation in current clinical variant databases. Despite the lack of annotation, known pathogenic variants had significantly greater molecular effects than benign variants (p-value = 0.00576) (Supplemental Figure S26). We also observed that the magnitude of the molecular effect varied systematically according to disease inheritance mode with the largest effects seen in the autosomal recessive genes (Supplemental Figure S27). We then asked whether rare causal eQTLs could produce loss-of-function-like effects. Of the 574 high confidence rare causal eQTLs (PIP > 0.9) affecting ClinGen disease genes, 18.3% had log₂(aFC) < -0.5 (7.8% with log₂(aFC) < -1), identifying candidate loss-of-function alleles (Supplemental Figure S28).

We next examined if specific high-effect variants could recover known pathogenic alleles and provide functional evidence for uncharacterized or VUS variants. As a proof of principle, we recovered a well-established pathogenic recessive Tay-Sachs indel which resulted in a strong reduction in HEXA expression(log2 aFC = -0.85) (Fig 5D ,Supplemental Figure 29). Starting with 2,928 rare variants at autosomal recessive ClinGen genes with either no ClinVar annotation, a VUS annotation or a pathogenic/likely pathogenic assertion we applied a series of filters to prioritize the unclassified and VUS variants that were most likely to be pathogenic. First, restricting to likely causal molecular QTL variants (PIP > 0.9) yielded 526 variants across 375 genes and increased the proportion of variants with a pathogenic/likely pathogenic assertion by 3.7 fold compared to the original set. Further restricting to variants with a posterior mean QTL effect < -0.3 resulted in 230 variants across 186 genes and increased the enrichment to 7.9 fold compared to the original set (Fig 5C). Finally, within the 186 genes that remained we examined all of the rare high-PIP variants (PIP > 0.9) with either effect direction. This identified nine genes which contained both a variant with a pathogenic/likely pathogenic ClinVar assertion and a distinct unclassified or VUS variant (Fig 5C).

Comparison to the pathogenic references revealed different molecular effects for these nine genes. At HEXA, GALM, and PMM2 the unclassified/VUS variants were associated with increased expression or protein abundance (in contrast to the pathogenic references) and were more in line with the benign/likely benign counterparts(Fig. 5D). In contrast the unclassified or VUS variants at GAA, SMPD1, CTH, PLG, CC2D1A and ACY1 all had negative effects matching the direction of the pathogenic references (Fig 5E). Several of them had effect magnitudes comparable to or greater than the pathogenic references, including an unclassified GAA eQTL and a SMPD1 VUS pQTL.

For the genes where there was no known pathogenic variant amongst the fine-mapped signals we further refined the prioritisation using trait effects from AoU and predicted regulatory consequences. Examples included a VUS in F10 with an INR (blood clotting time) effect in the top percentile of all variant effects and an unclassified variant in ANGPTL3 which had a triglyceride effect in the bottom percentile (Supplemental Figure S30). These were in line with the bleeding phenotype of factor X deficiency and the low lipid levels seen in ANGPTL3 loss of function. At ACVRL1, a gene associated with hereditary haemorrhagic telangiectasia, PromoterAI predicted strongly reduced activity of a fine-mapped promoter variant (Supplemental Figure S31). SPR deficiency is known to impair brain dopamine and serotonin synthesis and result in dystonia so the neural regulatory predictions are particularly pertinent. Borzoi predicted 96% reduction in CTCF signal and 76% reduction in chromatin accessibility at the SPR deletion in the neural contexts (Supplemental Figure S32).

Discussion

The AoU multi-omics cohort provides the scientific community with new avenues to study molecular mechanisms involved in human health and disease. Prior efforts in the UK Biobank have demonstrated the key value of multi-omics data when coupled to diverse phenotypic information from identifying the molecular effects of genetic variants (https://paperpile.com/c/jBx665Sr/rpjF) to discovering novel biomarkers (24) (25) (26). Further, such data provides multiple avenues to derisk drug targets by aiding in gene prioritization and establishing effect directionality (27). Here, the AoU multi-omics cohort expands on these opportunities by focusing on an ancestrally-diverse cohort in which both transcriptome and proteome data were generated to enable deeper insights into the effects of genetic variants across multiple major US ancestries.

The design of the AoU multi-omics cohort enables multiple facets of study from the expanded quantifications using Total RNA-sequencing, increased repertoire of 5,415 proteins to evaluating ancestry-enriched effects and cross-omic effects. We demonstrate several key insights from these data. First, we provide a comprehensive set of e/p/sQTL data expanding the discovery of independent QTLs for each gene. We also leverage the multi-ancestry design, which provides increased resolution of fine-mapped alleles (28, 29) , to provide credible sets for each QTL.

The expansive set of independent QTLs in the AoU multi-omics cohort enabled a deeper survey of the genetic architecture of molecular effects. By combining QTLs, we were able to identify dosage effects carried by each individual suggesting that locus-level allelic heterogeneity can achieve effects on par with gene deletions or duplications. The cohort of ~10,000 individuals also allowed us to report on rare QTLs down to MAFs of 0.05%. The map of these rare effects provides a resource of larger effect variants influencing known disease genes and drug targets. Further, we demonstrate that these effects are more likely to replicate across transcriptome and proteome and increase power for gene prioritization approaches through TWAS analyses.

By focusing on rare eQTLs, we also provide a compendium of variants that may have imminent clinical utility. We observe 5,186 rare eQTLs impacting 922 disease-associated genes in ClinGen. While AoU is not a rare disease cohort, many healthy individuals may be carriers of Mendelian disease alleles. We demonstrate the ability of biobank-scale multi-omics to identify large-effect rare variants within rare disease genes that may support classification of pathogenicity for variants of uncertain significance or identify new potentially pathogenic alleles enriched in specific ancestry groups but absent from ClinVar.

The ancestry enrichment patterns reported here should be interpreted in the context of the genetic ancestry represented in the All of Us cohort. These data do not capture the full global or local spectrum of genetic ancestry, and a variant enriched in one ancestry group in this study may also occur in populations not represented or adequately sampled here. Thus, "ancestry enriched" describes relative frequency in the studied cohort and does not imply that a variant is restricted to, or exclusive to, that group.

Collectively, the AoU multi-omics cohort enables ongoing mechanistic investigations of health and disease. We demonstrate the significant value of generating multi-omics within an ancestrally diverse cohort. We expect that future investigators will benefit from use of these data in several ways from specific disease studies, exploration of omics relationships with diverse AoU demographic variables, through to accelerating biomarker discovery and therapeutic development.

Proteomics Processing

The proteomics processing workflow is designed to generate protein expression profiles from 10,056 blood plasma samples using the Olink Explore HT platform. These samples were systematically organized into 61 individual Olink batches, each consisting of two plates, and were processed across two primary rounds. To address potential batch effects and ensure longitudinal consistency, the workflow incorporated approximately 127 replicate samples, including 25 specific bridge samples designated for software-based bridging analysis.

Reference median-based normalization

To remove technical artefacts we used a reference median based normalisation approach. Control samples were excluded prior to normalisation. A single plate was selected as the reference and assay specific median NPX values were calculated from that plate. The NPX values from all other plates were then adjusted to these reference medians using the Olink normalisation procedure, resulting in a harmonised set of values across plates with the relative biological variation being preserved. The choice of reference plate had minimal impact on downstream analysis.

Outlier sample removal

Sample-level proteomic quality control was performed using a network connectivity approach applied to the reference median normalized Olink NPX matrix. Briefly, pairwise biweight midcorrelations were calculated across all samples, and the resulting adjacency matrix was used to estimate sample connectivity (30). Connectivity values were converted to z scores, and samples with connectivity z scores less than -3 were designated as outliers and removed prior to downstream analysis.

RNA-sequencing Processing

Read alignment and expression quantification

Reads were extracted from input CRAM files using Picard SamToFastq v2.27.1 and aligned to the hg38 reference genome using STAR v2.7.11b (31). The reference genome used did not include the ALT, HLA or decoy contigs and gene annotations were based on GENCODE v48 (32). WASP (https://paperpile.com/c/jBx665Sr/Xjsl) filtering was enabled during alignment to reduce reference mapping bias. Duplicate reads were marked using Picard MarkDuplicates v2.27.1. Gene level raw counts and RNA-seq QC metrics were generated using RNA-SeQC 2 v2.4.3 (34) with a collapsed GENCODE v48 GTF annotation.

Expression normalization

Raw count data were transposed into a sample by gene matrix and filtered to keep only those genes which had a count >6 in at least 20% of samples. The filtered counts were then normalised using the TMM method (in edgeR (35)) and converted to counts per million (CPM).

To stabilize variance and approximate normality, expression values for each gene were rank-based inverse normal transformed across samples. The normalized expression matrix was then merged with gene-level TSS coordinates to generate a BED-formatted file containing genomic positions and normalized expression values for each gene across samples.

Splicing Quantification

Per sample splice junction files generated with regtools were jointly clustered across samples using LeafCutter v0.2.9 (36). Junctions were clustered into intron clusters using the leafcutter_cluster_regtools.py workflow with a minimum of 30 reads per cluster, a minimum junction to cluster read ratio of 0.001 and a maximum intron length of 500kb. This generated the per-intron count tables across all samples for downstream splicing quantification.

Intron clusters were then assigned to genes by intersecting the intron boundaries with the exon annotations. Under a strict assignment scheme a cluster was only assigned to a gene if both splice sites fell at annotated exon boundaries. Introns with insufficient representation or low information content were removed prior to the QTL analysis (those with zero usage in more than 50% of samples, low variability across individuals and those on the Y chromosome).

QTL identification and covariate calculation

Genotype Principal Component Calculation

Joint srWGS variants were filtered to biallelic variants, an AC threshold of 10 and a call rate of 95% was applied, and the VCFs were converted to PLINK(https://paperpile.com/c/jBx665Sr/CCSV) for downstream QTL analysis. Genetic PCs were calculated using SNPRelate after pruning with SNPs with an LD threshold = 0.2 and MAF = 0.01. The number of components to retain was determined using the Gavish-Donoho(https://paperpile.com/c/jBx665Sr/QxV7) method for optimal hard thresholding, which estimates the effective dimensionality of the data based on the singular value spectrum.

Phenotype Principal Component Calculation

Phenotype PCs for the covariates were calculated via PCA on the sample by molecular phenotype matrix using PCAtools v2.22.4. The number of components to retain was determined using the Gavish-Donoho method for optimal hard thresholding (this method estimates the effective dimensionality of the data from the singular value spectrum). The requisite number of PCs were then extracted from the rotated PCA matrix (each PC representing a major axis of variation in expression across the samples).

cis-QTL Identification cis-QTL mapping was performed using TensorQTL (39). The mapping window was defined as 1 Mb up and down-stream of the transcription start site for each feature. All variants with a MAC >= 10 were used as input within each cohort. Phenotype and genotype principal components were used as covariates. A gene was considered to have a significant QTL if the corresponding FDR value was less than 0.05. QTL identification was performed within each ancestry and in a pooled fashion using all samples.

Ancestry Group Definitions

Ancestry group assignment was defined using measures of genetic similarity of each participant to global reference populations using the Human Genome Diversity Project(https://paperpile.com/c/jBx665Sr/UCJB+VhkZ+HW9n 1000 Genome Project, and gnomAD (https://support.researchallofus.org/hc/en-us/article_attachments/52524243515540 Appendix G). The following acronyms are used to describe genetic similarity to a reference population AFR (1KGP-HGDP-AFR-like); AMR (1KGP-HGDP-AMR-like); EAS (1KGP-HGDP-EAS-like); EUR (1KGP-HGDP-EUR-like); MID (1KGP-HGDP-MID-like); SAS (1KGP-HGDP-SAS-like); and REM, not belonging to one of the other ancestries or is an admixture. Due to uneven sampling across global populations within the HGDP and 1000 Genomes reference panels, inferences regarding genetic background and admixed ancestry may exhibit reduced precision for participants originating from underrepresented groups, warranting careful interpretation of these estimates.

QTL cross-ancestry meta analysis and replication

Cross-ancestry meta-analysis

To assess the consistency of molecular QTL signals across ancestry groups, we performed cross-ancestry meta-analysis using METASOFT (https://paperpile.com/c/jBx665Sr/Qf6j excluding the pooled and admixed cohorts. For each molecular trait and ancestry group, we extracted the effect size estimate and standard error for the lead QTL variant and used these as input to METASOFT to assess the consistency of genetic effects across ancestries and estimate the posterior probability that an effect was present within each population. METASOFT estimates ancestry-specific M-values, which represent the posterior probability that a genetic effect exists within a given ancestry group. Associations were considered present when the corresponding M-value exceeded 0.9. We applied this analysis to eQTLs, sQTLs, and pQTLs and summarized both the number of ancestry groups supporting each association (M-value > 0.9) and the distribution of ancestry-specific M-values across populations.

Fine-mapping

To further pinpoint causal genetic variants, we applied Bayesian fine-mapping with SusieR (https://paperpile.com/c/jBx665Sr/VoKU) to significant cis-QTLs (FDR < 0.05). For each QTL, we extracted variants within a cis-window of +/-1 Mb of the transcription start site for that gene. Inverse-normal-transformed phenotypes were used for fine-mapping. Both the phenotype and genotype matrices were residualized with respect to the covariate matrix. Variants with no alternate alleles were removed, and missing genotype dosages were imputed to the variant mean. SuSiE was run with a maximum of 10 single-effect components (L=10), with residual and prior variances estimated from the data (estimate_residual_variance=TRUE, estimate_prior_variance=TRUE), a scaled prior variance of 0.1, and univariate z-scores computed (compute_univariate_zscore=TRUE). Credible set purity was assessed using a minimum absolute correlation threshold of 0.5 (min_abs_corr=0.5). In downstream reporting, we kept non-overlapping credible sets and excluded low-purity credible sets (minimum absolute correlation < 0.5).

Ancestry Enrichment Calculation

To quantify the level of ancestry enrichment amongst the fine-mapped variants we limited the analysis to variants with strong posterior evidence of causality (PIP > 0.9). For each variant we identified the ancestry group with the highest allele frequency and then compared the number of alleles and the number of allele counts in that group to the number of alleles and number of allele counts in all the other ancestry groups combined within the greater AoU genome sequencing cohort. An ancestry enrichment odds ratio was then calculated using Fisher's exact test on the resulting contingency table, giving a measure of the degree to which the variant was enriched in its most frequent ancestry relative to all the other ancestry groups. Odds ratios > 5 were considered to indicate ancestry-specific enrichment.

Allelic Fold Change

Allelic fold changes (14) were estimated for all variants identified as causal (PIP > 0.9) through eQTL fine-mapping using log2(CPM) expression values rather than inverse normal-transformed expression values, as the latter do not preserve an interpretable expression scale. Principal components were recalculated from the log2(CPM) expression matrix using the same procedure employed for QTL mapping. Allelic fold changes were then estimated using the aFC software package https://github.com/secastel/aFC.

Cumulative eQTL Burden effects

To estimate the cumulative effect of multiple independent regulatory variants on expression we assumed additive effects in log2 expression space. For each gene the independently fine-mapped variants were split into expression increasing and expression decreasing sets based on the sign of their estimated allelic fold change. Within each direction the allelic fold changes of all causal variants were then summed in sequence, beginning with the variant of highest effect, to give an estimate of the cumulative log2 expression change. This gives an estimate of the expected heterozygous expression level under an additive model and across sequential number of variants.

eQTL Burden Model To quantify the regulatory burden carried by each individual, we first estimated the expected expression effect of fine-mapped eQTL variants on expression for each eGene. Because allele frequencies differ across ancestry groups, expected population-specific expression shifts calculated separately for each gene and ancestry using the observed allele frequencies and allelic fold change estimates under an additive model. Specifically, the expected expression shift was computed as ∑ᵢ 2pᵢβᵢ, where pᵢ is the ancestry-specific allele frequency and βᵢ is the estimated log₂ allelic fold change.

For each individual-gene pair, the predicted expression effect was centered by subtracting the ancestry-specific expected population shift. Centered effects were converted to percent expression change relative to the population expectation as (2^Δ -1) × 100, where Δ denotes the centered log2 expression effect. Cross-ome colocalization

Using independently fine-mapped signals from each molecular assay, we performed pairwise colocalization analysis with fastENLOC(https://paperpile.com/c/jBx665Sr/jFGz) to identify genes and variants with evidence of shared causal effects across transcriptional and post-transcriptional processes. Unlike other Bayesian colocalization methods, fastENLOC estimates colocalization priors directly from the fine-mapping results based on the total number of signals and variants analyzed. Because molecular features were not perfectly shared across QTL assays, we used only variants tested in cis-windows of the intersecting set of features for each pairwise comparison. fastENLOC reports evidence for colocalisation at multiple levels: gene level colocalisation probability (GLCP), region level (credible set) colocalisation probability (RCP) and signal level (variant) colocalisation probability (SCP). For each pairwise comparison and each of the colocalisation metrics we have applied Bayesian false discovery rate correction and only events passing the appropriate significance threshold were retained.

Trait colocalization

GWAS credible sets were analyzed against eQTL, sQTL, and pQTL datasets using fastENLOC (15) and a complementary CLPP-based approach(https://paperpile.com/c/jBx665Sr/6sDo.

FastENLOC was run independently for each GWAS analysis and QTL layer. Signal-level regional colocalization probabilities (RCP) and locus colocalization probabilities (LCP), together with gene-level regional and locus colocalization probabilities (GRCP and GLCP), were retained for downstream analysis.

To prevent duplicate counting of loci represented in multiple GWAS studies of the same trait, credible sets were merged across studies using variant-membership similarity. Credible sets were assigned to the same consensus locus when the number of shared variants divided by the total number of unique variants across both credible sets was at least 0.90. Only credible sets from different studies of the same trait were merged.

CLPP was calculated for each GWAS credible-set-QTL-signal pair by multiplying the GWAS PIP and QTL PIP for each variant shared between the two signals, then summing these products across all shared variants.

FastENLOC and CLPP evidence was harmonized at the signal, credible-set, and gene levels. Bayesian false-discovery-rate thresholds were calculated separately for each GWAS-QTL analysis pair at an FDR level of 0.05. RCP thresholds were estimated over signal-level probabilities, whereas GRCP and GLCP thresholds were estimated over gene-level probabilities. Probabilities were considered significant when they exceeded the threshold corresponding to a cumulative Bayesian FDR of 0.05. Stringent colocalization evidence was defined by the union of RCP ≥ 0.5, CLPP ≥ 0.05, RCP Bayesian-FDR significance, or GLCP Bayesian-FDR significance. Colocalization was evaluated separately for each QTL layer and across QTL layers. For each gene individuals were split into cross-validation folds (10 folds), molecular phenotypes were rank normalised separately within each training and test set to prevent information leakage across folds. Expression principal components were estimated from the training samples within each fold and projected onto the test samples (the held out samples). The fold specific expression PCs were then combined with the genetic PCs and used as covariates.

Within each training fold we fine-mapped the cis effects using SuSiE and estimated the posterior mean effect sizes for variants in the credible sets. We trained two models per gene: one using all the cis variants and one using only those variants with an allele frequency >1%. Genetic prediction in the held out samples was the dosage weighted sum of the SuSiE posterior mean effect sizes. Expression in the held out samples was residualised using the covariate effects estimated from the corresponding training set and performance was assessed by comparing predicted to observed residualised expression across folds.

TWAS association analysis

TWAS scores were calculated by combining the GWAS summary statistics with the SuSiE fine-mapping results for each molecular trait. For each gene, the GWAS variants that overlapped the genomic interval defined by the fine-mapped variants were pulled from the tabix-indexed summary statistics. Variants were kept if the chromosome, position, reference allele and alternate allele all matched between the two datasets; duplicate variant records and any variants that had a mismatch in allele were removed. Variant level GWAS Z-scores were then calculated by taking the GWAS effect estimate and dividing it by its standard error.

The TWAS score was calculated as the sum across variants of each variant's SuSiE posterior mean weight multiplied by its GWAS Z-score. Its variance was calculated by combining the variant weights with the LD correlation matrix, such that each pair of weights was multiplied by the LD correlation between the corresponding variants and these products were summed. The TWAS Z-score was then calculated by dividing the TWAS score by the square root of its variance. Two-sided association P-values were obtained from the squared TWAS Z-score using a chi-squared distribution with one degree of freedom. LD matrices were computed from standardized genotype dosages after mean imputation of missing values.
