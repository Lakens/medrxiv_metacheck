Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is a major cause of cancer-related mortality (3) and usually develops in the context of chronic liver disease, most often cirrhosis (4). Surveillance seeks to detect HCC at a treatable stage, but patients with cirrhosis do not share the same near-term risk: some develop HCC within months, whereas others face a more immediate risk of hepatic decompensation or death (5). Shortterm prediction in cirrhosis is therefore not simply a cancer-versus-non-cancer problem; it requires distinguishing HCC risk from competing mortality risk within the same clinical time window (6).

Recent clinical prediction and machine-learning studies show that routinely available data can support HCC risk stratification across diverse clinical settings. Conventional models, including aMAP (7), the Toronto HCC Risk Index (THRI) (8) and the Kanwal cirrhosis score (9), use routinely measured clinical and laboratory variables to stratify HCC risk in chronic liver disease and cirrhosis, while deeplearning models built on longitudinal electronic health records (EHRs) have identified patients with cirrhosis at high HCC risk (10). Machine-learning models often outperform these scores in development data, but are typically binary, evaluated without competing risks, or built on inputs not routinely available; in prospective French cohorts analysed under competing risks, machine-learning models did not outperform aMAP (2).

Population-level frameworks such as PRE-Screen-HCC (11) extend this approach upstream, using large cohorts to identify individuals at increased HCC risk before chronic liver disease or cirrhosis is consistently recognised. This addresses an important screening gap because cirrhosis is often unrecognised, or recognised only shortly, before HCC diagnosis (12), and some HCC arises without cirrhosis (13). However, patients with established cirrhosis are already candidates for surveillance, unless competing mortality is high or curative treatment is not feasible (4), and present a different decision problem: their short-term HCC risk must be interpreted alongside competing risks of decompensation and death, since binary HCC-versus-non-HCC modelling may obscure clinically distinct trajectories by grouping patients who die without HCC with those who remain alive and event-free (5, 6).

Region-wide real-world data allow this problem to be studied at the scale required for health-system deployment. The Andalusian Population Health Database (BPS) is well suited to this task because it integrates longitudinal EHRs from more than 15 million patients in the Andalusian public health system (14), including diagnoses, laboratory results, imaging, prescriptions, hospital activity, and mortality-related information (15). Its secondary use is enabled through PAGEM, a secure processing environment embedded within the healthcare network, where pseudonymised data are analysed under GDPR-compliant and EHDS-aligned governance, and only non-identifiable outputs are released (14). This framework supports longitudinal studies of incidence (16), treatment use and outcomes (17), survival (18), drug repurposing (19) (20) (21), synthetic EHR generation (22), and predictive modelling across the regional public health system (23).

In such high-stakes settings, interpretability is central to clinical translation (24). A short-term HCC prediction model should not only estimate risk, but also show whether abnormal biomarkers support cancer risk, hepatic deterioration, or eventfree survival. Explainable Boosting Machines are well suited to this task because they learn feature-specific response functions that can be inspected directly (25). This allows clinicians to assess whether the model recovers plausible biomarker patterns, whether abnormal values reflect HCC risk or competing mortality, and whether clinically familiar thresholds emerge directly from real-world data rather than being imposed during model development.

To this end, we developed an interpretable machine-learning model for 12-month HCC prediction in patients with cirrhosis using routine public health data from Andalusia. We framed the task as a three-class prediction problem: incident HCC, death or liver transplantation without HCC, and event-free survival. Using an Explainable Boosting Machine trained on a curated clinical feature set, we evaluated discrimination, precision-recall performance and class-specific featureresponse profiles in repeated stratified cross-validation. We compared the model, on the same cross-validation folds, with a binary HCC model, a model with pairwise interaction terms, and a model using the full health record (3,000+ features), with variance-corrected paired tests and Bayesian equivalence testing (26, 27). We then compared it with aMAP and THRI at the first routine encounter at which each score could be computed, under competing risks, and recalibrated it to absolute risk at routine laboratory visits. Because HCC risk and its predictors differ by sex and etiology, we report performance within these groups. Crucially, we evaluated the clinical plausibility of learned biomarker shape functions: standard binary formulations group deaths without HCC with event-free survivors, so a low predicted risk cannot distinguish a healthy patient from one about to die of liver failure, whereas a three-class model shows the cancer and the competing-death signals separately.

Finally, to support reproducibility and external validation, we transformed the data pipeline to OMOP-compatible structures and provide code for feature extraction, preprocessing, model training, and evaluation, enabling other institutions to reproduce the feature definitions and port the model to harmonised EHR environments.

Methods

Data source and ethics

Real-world longitudinal data were extracted from the Population Health Database (15), known in Spanish as Base Poblacional de Salud (BPS), which aggregates electronic health records (EHRs) from all users within the Andalusian public health system. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee for the Coordination of Biomedical Research in Andalusia (CCEIBA; Acta 10/24). All data were transferred from the BPS systems (pseudonymised in origin) to the PAGEM trusted research environment (14), where all analyses were performed.

Study design

We designed this retrospective observational cohort study using routinely collected healthcare data from the BPS: one cohort, analysed in two designs that differ only in their time zero (Fig. 1 a). In the development design, the model was trained and internally validated by repeated cross-validation on prediction dates anchored on each patient's outcome: a sampling of time zero that is efficient for learning how the predictors relate to the three outcomes, but that does not reproduce the case mix met in practice. In the evaluation design, the same cross-validated models, without retraining, were applied at routine encounters, dates that do not depend on the outcome, and patients were followed forward under competing risks. The evaluation estimates, not the cross-validated ones, describe performance at the point of care; they underlie the comparison with aMAP and THRI and the calibration of the deployment model. Cohort definition

We used records from 1 January 2019 to 31 December 2024, together with chronic-disease diagnoses of any date from the BPS chronic-condition registry. Adults with cirrhosis were identified by ICD-10-CM codes K70.3, alcoholic cirrhosis; K71.7, toxic liver disease with fibrosis and cirrhosis; or K74, fibrosis and cirrhosis of the liver (and their ICD-9-CM equivalents, translated with the CMS General Equivalence Mappings). We defined incident HCC as a diagnosis coded as ICD-10-CM C22.0. Of 37,702 patients, we excluded 4,926 without a cirrhosis code, 1,821 whose HCC was diagnosed within 180 days of the cirrhosis record, 83 with a pregnancy-related code and 136 who were under 18 at the cirrhosis record or had an undated liver-transplant complication, leaving 30,736 (Fig. 1 b). Both designs applied the same eligibility rule at time zero: at least 180 days past the first cirrhosis record, alive, free of HCC, and without a previous cancer, a transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt (TIPS) or a liver transplant. No sample size calculation was needed: both designs draw on the whole population with a recorded cirrhosis diagnosis in the Andalusian public health system who met the eligibility criteria, not a sample of it. The precision of each estimate is given by its spread over the repeated cross-validation or by its confidence interval.

Development design: outcome-anchored prediction dates

Each patient received an index date: the date of HCC for patients who developed it, the date of death or liver transplantation for those who died or were transplanted without HCC, and for the rest a date drawn at random from the cases' index dates. Index dates ran from 2021 to 2024. The prediction date lay 12 months before the index date, and features came from the 12 months before it, so no record from the prediction window was used (Fig. 1 a). Of the 30,736, 3,696 had HCC, died or were transplanted before 2021, and 9,863 were not eligible at any prediction date (cirrhosis recorded less than 6 months before it, or a previous cancer, TIPS or liver transplant), leaving 17,177 patients with an eligible prediction date (the development set; Fig. 1 b), each with a 12-month three-class outcome.

Evaluation design: landmarks at routine encounters

For each score, the landmark was the first date, from 2 January 2020, on which all of the score's laboratory components were measured with the patient eligible: 14,928 patients had an aMAP landmark and 18,848 a THRI landmark (the landmark sets; Supplementary Table 1). They were followed to HCC, death or liver transplantation, or to 31 December 2024 (Comparison with established risk scores). The recalibration of the deployment model used routine laboratory visits, the first eligible visit per patient in each 6-month window from 2020 to 2024 (82,134 visits; Deployment model and absolute-risk recalibration). The landmark sets are largely the same patients as the development set (13,048 of the 14,928 with an aMAP landmark and 16,013 of the 18,848 with a THRI landmark also had a prediction date), seen at a different time zero. The evaluation is therefore internal, in the same population and period, not a validation in new patients; each patient was scored by a cross-validation model that had not been trained on them.

Predictor variables

We extracted demographic, chronic condition, and laboratory variables from the electronic health record. All variables were retrieved from the 12 months before time zero.

The sociodemographic variables were date of birth and sex.

We used chronic disease records curated by BPS from ICD-10 codes and free-text information documented in the EHR. ICD-9-CM diagnoses were translated to ICD-10-CM before grouping. Supplementary Table 7 lists the features.

The laboratory variables include eight key liver function and haematological biomarkers specifically selected for their clinical relevance to hepatocellular carcinoma risk assessment: platelet count (OMOP concept_id: 3024929), platelet mean volume (OMOP concept_id: 3001123), alanine aminotransferase (ALT, OMOP concept_id: 3006923), total bilirubin (OMOP concept_id: 3006140), prothrombin time (PT, OMOP concept_id: 3034426), aspartate aminotransferase (AST, OMOP concept_id: 3013721), albumin (OMOP concept_id: 3024561) and alpha-1-fetoprotein (OMOP concept_id: 3039783).

The model therefore needs only data already held in the health record: age, sex, the coded chronic diagnoses and eight routine laboratory tests. From these, the pipeline derives 110 features automatically: age, sex, 68 diagnosis-group flags, and five summary statistics (mean, median, SD, maximum, minimum) of each of the eight tests. We restricted the feature set to variables with prior use in HCC risk models. Nevertheless, we report a complementary model using the full set of variables extracted from the electronic health record (see Comparison of model variants).

Feature engineering

We converted the recorded variables into model features using only information recorded in the 12 months before time zero. This ensured that no data from after time zero (the prediction window in development, the follow-up in evaluation) contributed to feature construction. Predictors are routine laboratory results and coded diagnoses, extracted automatically, so no subjective interpretation was involved.

We used the date of birth to calculate age in days at time zero: the prediction date in development, and the landmark, the laboratory visit or the date of use in evaluation and deployment. Sex was encoded as a binary variable (1 indicating male). For diagnostic variables, ICD-10 codes were grouped into blocks, except for conditions in block K70-K77 (Diseases of the Liver), which were further expanded to the category level, i.e., the first three characters of each code. This approach reduced dimensionality while preserving clinically interpretable diagnostic categories. For each grouped ICD-10 category, we created a binary indicator reflecting whether the patient had at least one corresponding diagnosis recorded before time zero.

For laboratory variables, we summarised all available measurements recorded in the 12 months before time zero by the mean, median, standard deviation, minimum, and maximum value. A missing value took the training median.

Outcome definition

We modelled the prediction task as a three-class outcome to account for the competing risk of death. In development, each patient was assigned to one of three mutually exclusive outcome groups over the 12 months after the prediction date: HCC; death or liver transplantation without HCC; event-free. In evaluation, the same outcomes were analysed as times from time zero to the first of HCC or death or liver transplantation without HCC (competing events), censored at 31 December 2024 and followed up to 36 months. A liver transplant without previous HCC ends the natural history of the liver disease as death does, so it is counted with death. Outcomes were derived automatically from coded records, independently of the predictors, so no subjective assessment or blinding of assessors was involved.

Model development

We trained the main model as a three-class classifier using the structured feature set derived from the EHR. We used an Explainable Boosting Machine (EBM) (25) as the primary modelling approach. We used the default EBM hyperparameters except that pairwise interaction terms were disabled (interactions = 0), so that the model is a sum of one shape function per feature and its global explanation is complete. (For three classes, the interpret library does not display pairwise terms, so a model with them would be only partly visible.)

We addressed outcome imbalance using class weights estimated within each training subset. For each cross-validation iteration, we calculated class weights according to the training-specific outcome prevalence, so that less frequent classes received larger weights during model fitting.

We implemented preprocessing and model training within a single reproducible pipeline. The pipeline applied standard scaling to age; sex and diagnostic indicators entered as binary flags. For laboratory measurements, we applied robust scaling based on the interquartile range to reduce the influence of extreme values, then median imputation. Supplementary Table 8 lists every preprocessing step and hyperparameter with its value.

We evaluated model performance using 20 repeats of 5-fold stratified crossvalidation, yielding 100 train-test evaluations. Folds were stratified by the threeclass outcome and seeded, so every model below was trained and tested on the same 100 folds of the same patients. Within each iteration, we held out one fold as the test set and split the remaining data internally, reserving 20% as a validation subset for threshold selection. We trained the model on the training subset, selected the operating threshold on the validation subset, and evaluated performance on the held-out test fold. The preprocessing parameters learned on the training subset were applied unchanged to the validation and test subsets.

The model produced class-specific predicted probabilities for HCC, death or transplantation without HCC, and event-free survival. For the HCC class, we selected the operating threshold to target a false-positive rate of 10% in the validation subset. We then applied the selected threshold to the corresponding test fold.

We assessed discrimination for HCC prediction using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) and the area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC), treating HCC as the target class against the remaining outcome classes. Because every patient's 12-month outcome class is known, these are ordinary, time-fixed AUROC and AUPRC, without censoring weights; we keep the term AUROC for them, and AUC() for the time-dependent AUC of the comparison with the scores. We also evaluated threshold-dependent performance using sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, F1 score, balanced accuracy, Matthews correlation coefficient, and the likelihood ratios. We calculated all threshold-dependent metrics in each test fold using the threshold selected in the corresponding validation subset. We reported the mean and standard deviation of each metric across the 100 test evaluations.

Comparison of model variants

We compared the reference model (three classes, curated features, no interactions) with three variants, each changing one element: a binary model (HCC against the rest, on the same patients and folds), the same model with interpret's default pairwise interactions, and a model using the full health record. The full record comprised every diagnosis (ICD-10-CM truncated to three characters, registry and other sources separately), procedure (ICD-10-PCS, four characters), laboratory test (five summary statistics and the slope), active drug ingredient and visit type: more than 3,000 features in all, without interactions.

To compare model performance while accounting for the dependence between cross-validation folds, we used Nadeau and Bengio's corrected paired t-test (26); this variance-corrected approach addresses the issue of non-independent samples arising from models being evaluated on the same data partitions. For multiple comparisons, we applied Bonferroni correction over the three comparisons to control the family-wise error rate.

In addition to the frequentist approach, we conducted Bayesian analysis to compute the posterior distribution of performance differences between models. This allowed us to calculate the probability that one model is better than another, as well as the probability that models are practically equivalent within a region of practical equivalence (ROPE) of 0.01 (27).

Comparison with established risk scores

We compared the model with aMAP (7) and THRI (8), computed from routine BPS data with the published formulas and categories (aMAP: low < 50 / medium < 60 / high; THRI: low < 120 / intermediate / 240 < high). Sensitivity analyses

Four further analyses addressed the main threats to these conclusions. First, to approximate validation in a new region, we used internal-external cross-validation by province (37): the reference model was refitted eight times, each on the development patients of seven of the eight Andalusian provinces (assigned from the patient's primary-care health district), with the same pipeline, hyperparameters and class weights as the deployment model, and applied to the eighth. Each heldout province was evaluated in the development design, against the cross-validated predictions for the same patients, and at the score landmarks, where the provincelevel differences in concordance were pooled with a DerSimonian-Laird randomeffects model (38). Second, because laboratory tests are ordered by clinicians, we compared the model with the scores separately in patients with all four key tests (AFP, platelets, albumin and bilirubin) measured in the history window and in those with at least one missing, and by calendar year of the landmark. Third, the visitbased recalibration has no sex term; we refitted it with a female indicator, crossfitted by fold, and compared observed/expected ratios by sex. Fourth, because the recalibration was fitted at laboratory visits, we applied it at time zeros that need not be visits: each anniversary of the study start (2 January 2020 to 2024), to every patient eligible on that date, with features rebuilt from the previous 12 months and out-of-fold predictions, and compared observed/expected ratios by whether a laboratory visit fell in the preceding 6 months.

OMOP standardisation

Source data were transformed into the Observational Medical Outcomes Partnership (OMOP) Common Data Model (CDM) (39, 40). Because the entire machine-learning pipeline was built against the OMOP standard rather than against dataset-specific logic, other researchers can reproduce the same process on their own data once it is likewise standardised to OMOP CDM, improving the reproducibility of the overall methodology.

Unlike OMOP, which organises data by clinical domain (conditions, drugs, measurements, etc.), the BPS source tables were organised by their originating system or department. To bridge this difference, table column names were first harmonised: identifier columns were mapped to OMOP's person_id, start and end date columns were renamed to generic names, and a type_concept_id was defined for each table. After this preprocessing, each OMOP clinical table could be built on top of this pattern, supplemented with any additional records specific to that table.

The Person and Death tables were directly derived from patients' sociodemographic information. Location and Provider tables were also generated for completeness, although they were not used in the training or evaluation.

The Condition Occurrence and Procedure Occurrence tables were generated from condition-related and procedure-related tables, respectively. These tables contained exclusively ICD-9 and ICD-10 codes, which can be systematically mapped to standard codes in the OMOP Vocabularies ecosystem.

Drug occurrence was built from hospitals and outpatient pharmacy records. These identify each medication with a Spanish National Code, assigned by the Spanish Agency for Medicines and Health Products (AEMPS). Each code maps to an ATC code and a Virtual Therapeutic Moiety (VTM) code, both of which are represented within the OMOP Vocabularies repository, allowing these codes to be mapped directly.

Measurement records use the Andalusian CLC (Código de Laboratorio Clínico) system, a standardised nomenclature designed by the "Grupo de Nomenclatura y Codificación" (GNC) of the Andalusian Public Health Service to uniquely identify clinical laboratory tests and measurements. Most CLC codes map to LOINC codes, present in the OMOP Vocabularies, or to IUPAC nomenclature.

The local vocabularies employed by the source data were registered as custom codes in the Concept table, with their corresponding vocabularies added to the Vocabulary table. Whenever possible, a plausible code was manually assigned to any code that could not directly be mapped to a standard OMOP code, registering the mapping in both the Concept Relationship and Source To Concept Map OMOP tables. This ensured that a record for every mapping decision was kept in case a future revision is needed.

Finally, the Visit Occurrence and Observation Period tables were derived from all the other tables combined, since each record of the source tables reflects an interaction between the patient and the health system.

Results

Study population

Of 30,736 adults with cirrhosis, 17,177 had an eligible prediction date between 2020 and 2023 and formed the development set (Fig. 1 b). Within 12 months of the prediction date, 771 (4.5%) developed HCC, 3,314 (19.3%) died or underwent liver transplantation without HCC (3,061 deaths and 253 transplants) and 13,092 (76.2%) remained event-free (Table 1). Women accounted for 34.9% of the development set but 17.3% of HCC cases. Patients who died without HCC were older and had lower albumin concentrations, those who underwent transplantation were younger and had the most advanced liver disease, and those who developed HCC had lower platelet counts and the highest AFP concentrations. In the 12month history window, 79% of patients had a platelet count and 41% an AFP measurement. In repeated cross-validation on outcome-anchored prediction dates, the reference model discriminated 12-month HCC with an AUROC of 0.734 0.018 and an AUPRC of 0.148, approximately three times the outcome prevalence (0.045), and death or transplantation with an AUROC of 0.759 (Table 2). The training AUROC was moderately higher (0.794), indicating limited overfitting. At a threshold set for a 10% false-positive rate, the model identified approximately one third of the patients who developed HCC within a year (sensitivity 0.31; PPV 0.13; NPV 0.97).

None of the three variants improved HCC discrimination. The binary model and the model with pairwise interactions were practically equivalent to the reference (posterior probability of equivalence 0.75 and 1.00), and the shape functions of the model with interactions were indistinguishable from those of the reference, as main effects are fitted before interactions. The full health record, comprising more than 3,000 features, reduced HCC AUROC by 0.024 while increasing the AUROC for death or transplantation from 0.759 to 0.773, consistent with comorbidity and frailty features that predict mortality diluting the HCC-specific signal.

The model ranked HCC better than aMAP and THRI at routine encounters

To estimate performance at the point of care, the cross-validated models were applied without retraining at the first routine encounter at which each score could be computed. The aMAP landmark set comprised 14,928 patients, of whom 791 developed HCC during follow-up, and the THRI landmark set 18,848 patients, with 975 HCC cases. The model discriminated HCC better than both scores at all horizons from 6 to 36 months (Fig. 2 a, d; Table 3). At 12 months, AUC(12 mo) was 0.790 for the model and 0.688 for aMAP (ΔAUC(12 mo) +0.102, 95% CI +0.071 to +0.132), and 0.761 and 0.703 for THRI (+0.057, +0.032 to +0.081). Because some early diagnoses may follow investigations prompted by suspicion of HCC, the analyses were repeated after a 6-month washout; the advantage was attenuated but persisted, with differences in concordance to 36 months of +0.055 against aMAP and +0.040 against THRI, both with confidence intervals excluding zero. When each marker flagged 10% of patients, the model identified approximately twice as many of the HCC cases diagnosed within 12 months (44% vs 20% for aMAP; 39% vs 22% for THRI; Fig. 2 b, e).

After identical recalibration, the index of prediction accuracy for 12-month HCC was 9.5% (aMAP landmark set) and 6.3% (THRI landmark set) for the model, compared with approximately 1% for either score (Supplementary Fig. 5), and the visit-based recalibration remained close to calibrated at both landmarks (observed/expected ratios 1.12 and 1.05). Results were consistent when hepatitis C was scored as cured in THRI, which reduced the score's discrimination (Supplementary Fig. 2). The advantage was evident in men and largest in viral hepatitis, but was not significant in women (ΔC +0.029 against aMAP, 95% CI -0.022 to +0.080), in whom the recalibration also overestimated risk, and was absent in MASLD (Supplementary Fig. 3; Supplementary Table 3).

At the level of feature groups, predictors fell on either side of the line of equal contribution (Fig. 3 a). AFP, platelet count, male sex and viral hepatitis contributed more to the log-odds of HCC, whereas albumin, prothrombin time, ALT, alcoholrelated liver disease (K70), heart disease and chronic airway disease contributed more to the log-odds of death or transplantation; age, bilirubin and diabetes contributed to both. AFP was the most HCC-specific variable: it increased the log-odds of HCC steeply up to 20 µg/L (+0.31), without affecting the death contrast, and its within-year variability contributed an additional HCC-specific signal (Supplementary Fig. 6). Low platelet counts increased both contrasts, the HCC contrast more, with the effect changing sign near 165 10 /L. The HCC contrast increased sharply between 48 and 50 years of age and plateaued between 82 and 88 years before declining, whereas the death contrast increased continuously and exceeded the HCC contrast from about 80 years.

Markers of hepatic reserve were associated predominantly with death. Albumin below 35 g/L increased the death contrast approximately three times more than the HCC contrast, and prolonged prothrombin time increased mainly the death contrast. Bilirubin increased the death contrast monotonically, whereas the HCC contrast decreased in a step between 51 and 55 µmol/L, at the boundary of the highest Child-Pugh bilirubin category (Fig. 3 g). This step was sharpest in the median term, the mean term declining gradually (Supplementary Fig. 6), and was learned from the data; it may reflect clinical practice at this threshold as well as biology (see Discussion). Very low ALT increased the death contrast, and AST above approximately 100 IU/L decreased the HCC contrast (Fig. 3 h, i). Male sex and viral hepatitis increased only the HCC contrast (+0.57 and +0.34), whereas K70, kidney failure and organic mental disorders increased the death contrast (Supplementary Fig. 8).

Sensitivity analyses

Leaving each province out of training in turn did not lower discrimination. The heldout provinces' AUROC for HCC was 0.738 (0.712 to 0.791 across provinces), against 0.733 for the cross-validated models in the same patients, and the advantage over the scores held, with pooled concordance differences of +0.091 (95% CI +0.068 to +0.114; I # 23%) against aMAP and +0.057 (+0.040 to +0.073; I # 0%) against THRI (Supplementary Table 10). The advantage was confined to patients with all four key laboratory tests measured in the history window (ΔC +0.090 against aMAP and +0.087 against THRI); with any missing, it was not significant (+0.029, 95% CI -0.015 to +0.074, and +0.013, -0.011 to +0.036). It was similar across landmark years (Supplementary Fig. 3; Supplementary Table 3).

At laboratory visits, the recalibration overestimated short-term risk in women (observed/expected 0.89 at 12 months, against 1.03 in men), converging by 36 months; a female term barely changed this (0.91), with a subdistribution hazard ratio of 0.98 (range over the refits 0.81 to 1.11; Supplementary Table 11). At calendar time zeros, the model still ranked patients (AUC(12 mo) 0.75, against 0.78 at visits; calibration slope 0.96), but the visit-based recalibration overestimated risk (observed/expected 0.79 at 12 months and 0.94 at 36 months), most in patients without a laboratory visit in the previous 6 months (0.68, against 0.85 with one; Supplementary Table 12).

Discussion

Principal findings

An interpretable three-class model, in which the effect of each variable is displayed as a curve, discriminated short-term HCC better than aMAP and THRI at routine encounters, at every horizon up to three years and after excluding HCC diagnosed within six months. It used only data already held in the health record (age, sex, coded diagnoses and eight routine laboratory tests). At the same group size, its high-risk group held as much HCC as the scores' high-risk categories with 20-28% fewer patients who died or underwent transplantation first, and after recalibration at routine visits its absolute risks agreed with observed incidence. The advantage held when each province was left out of training. Neither interaction terms, nor the full health record, nor a binary formulation improved discrimination, and the binary formulation concealed the competing-death signal within its single curve.

Clinical interpretation of the shape functions

Interpretable modelling allowed us to examine clinical hepatology heuristics directly in routine data. Instead of producing only opaque risk probabilities, the model showed how each biomarker shifted prediction toward HCC, death or transplantation without HCC, or event-free survival. This made it possible to separate signals of oncogenesis from signals of hepatic deterioration and competing mortality.

The shape functions followed clinically plausible patterns. We describe each as two curves: its contribution to the log-odds of HCC, and to the log-odds of death or transplantation, each relative to remaining event-free (the HCC and death contrasts). Low albumin, prolonged prothrombin time, and higher bilirubin increased the model contribution toward death without HCC, consistent with impaired liver reserve and with how albumin and bilirubin anchor the ALBI liver function grade (53). The bilirubin function also recovered a clinical threshold without being given it: the HCC contrast dropped in a step at 51 µmol/L, the boundary of the highest Child-Pugh bilirubin band, while the death contrast kept rising. This step may reflect disease biology, but also care: Child-Pugh C prompts transplant evaluation and, outside the transplant list, surveillance is no longer recommended (4), and in routine records the ordering and timing of tests carry information beyond their values (54). We therefore read the recovered thresholds as the joint footprint of disease and clinical practice, which is what a model deployed in the same health system will encounter.

AFP showed the most HCC-specific response among all evaluated biomarkers. The learned function preserved a continuous risk gradient rather than imposing a binary threshold: the HCC contribution rose steeply from 2 to 20 µg/L (+0.31 at 20 µg/L) and more slowly above it, with no death signal. This gradient spans the conventional threshold of 20 ng/mL (20 µg/L) used in HCC surveillance and recall strategies (55, 56), and is compatible with recent proposals of a lower threshold (57). Longitudinal surveillance data also support this graded interpretation, showing that AFP can discriminate HCC before diagnosis, with reported AUROC values of 0.792 at 12 months, 0.823 at 6 months, and 0.867 at diagnosis (58). Its within-year variability added an HCC-only signal, so a rising or fluctuating AFP below conventional thresholds carried information. Overall, the model recovered AFP risk regions directly from real-world data while retaining clinically relevant subthreshold information.

Platelet count added a clinically coherent marker of portal hypertension and cirrhosis severity. The HCC contribution rose as platelets fell below about 165 10 /L, consistent with thrombocytopenia as a marker of the cirrhotic substrate in which HCC risk emerges, and the death contribution moved the same way but less. This pattern aligns with the clinical use of 150 10 /L in portal-hypertension risk stratification, including Baveno VII criteria (59, 60), and with the inclusion of platelets in aMAP for HCC risk prediction (7). Unlike AFP, platelet count did not behave as a cancer-specific biomarker. Instead, it contextualised cirrhosis severity and competing risk.

Although some recent models use the AST/ALT ratio as a single feature (61), entering both separately let the model give them different roles. AST above about 100 IU/L lowered the HCC contrast, in line with the AST/ALT ratio tracking fibrosis and impaired liver function (62) and short-term mortality in viral cirrhosis (63). Very low ALT raised the death contrast, compatible with low aminotransferases as a marker of frailty. Unlike a population cohort in which elevated ALT predicted HCC (64) and Child A HCV cirrhosis, in which persistently elevated ALT did (65), ALT showed no clear HCC signal once the other markers were in the model.

Finally, age added a strong demographic gradient to the model interpretation. Younger patients were shifted toward event-free survival and away from HCC and death without HCC, consistent with age being an independent risk factor for HCC in cirrhosis, overall and in HCV-related cirrhosis (66), while with increasing age the contribution toward death or transplantation rose, in line with age predicting decompensation in compensated cirrhosis (67). The HCC contribution rose steeply between 48 and 50 and plateaued between 82 and 88, whereas the death contribution kept rising and exceeded it from about 80, which reflects competing mortality rather than a decline in intrinsic HCC risk. The three-class model thus separated age-related oncological risk from age-related mortality, a distinction that a binary formulation partly loses, since competing outcomes are naturally described as transitions between the clinical states of cirrhosis (68).

Liver-disease and comorbidity code profiles, encoded as binary features, behaved as clinical knowledge predicts. Viral hepatitis shifted prediction toward HCC with no death signal, consistent with viral cirrhosis being a principal HCC surveillance risk state (56). K70, alcohol-related liver disease, shifted prediction toward death rather than HCC: alcohol-associated cirrhosis carries clinically relevant HCC incidence (69) and alcohol-related HCC is often diagnosed late, with a worse prognosis (70); in our 12-month window the competing outcome dominated. Kidney failure, organic mental disorders, heart disease and chronic airway disease were associated with death. The model did not treat all codes as equivalent: it assigned cancer signal to viral etiology and male sex, and competing-death signal to alcohol and comorbidity.

Value of the three-class formulation Discrimination alone does not justify the three-class formulation, as the binary model ranked HCC almost as well. Its value lies in how it handles patients who die before developing HCC. Both scores lean on platelets and, in aMAP's case, the albumin-bilirubin grade, which predict decompensation as much as cancer, so their high-risk categories fill with patients who die without HCC (aMAP: four deaths for every HCC). At the same size, the model's high-risk group held as much HCC with 20-28% fewer competing deaths, the separation a surveillance programme needs, since a patient likely to die within months gains least from intensified screening. The binary model performed in between: its high-risk group contained more competing deaths (26.7% vs 23.3%), its recalibrated risks were less accurate (IPA 7.8% vs 9.5%), and its curves display only the cancer signal. The three-class model provides a similar ranking and displays both signals.

Limits of the binary formulation

A binary model predicts HCC against all other patients, so patients who died without HCC are grouped with those who remained event-free. Its shape functions are therefore a blend: approximately the HCC curve of the three-class model minus one fifth of its death curve, one fifth being the average proportion of deaths among patients without HCC (eq. 4). Two consequences follow. First, the binary curves resemble the three-class HCC curves and discrimination is similar, so a binary model is not wrong about cancer. Second, the death signal is hidden within the same curve: where it is strong (low albumin, prolonged prothrombin time, old age), the binary curve bends downwards, so that a low binary risk may reflect a healthy liver or one about to fail, and the curve cannot show which (Supplementary Fig. 7). Interpretable models have exposed such mixtures before. In a widely cited example, a model of pneumonia mortality learned that asthma lowered the risk of death, because patients with asthma were admitted directly to intensive care and treated more aggressively (71); inspecting the model revealed the artefact. Here, modelling competing events as a separate outcome removes the mixture rather than only revealing it.

Comparison with established scores and machine-learning models Few HCC models, machine learning or regression, have been developed or compared under competing risks (1). In HCV cirrhosis, a random survival forest built under competing risks discriminated better than a Fine-Gray model (72). Nahon et al., in 4,867 patients from three prospective French cohorts under semiannual ultrasound surveillance, analysed under competing risks, found no advantage of a decision tree, a random survival forest or a deep neural network over aMAP and FASTRAK in C-index, Brier score or decision curves (2). Our setting differs in three ways that may explain the contrast. Their patients had compensated, biopsy-proven cirrhosis, three quarters of it after HCV cure and the rest controlled HBV or non-viral, under protocolised surveillance; ours are an unselected population of all etiologies and stages, with routine, irregular testing, where a model can use the full laboratory trajectory: the advantage was confined to patients whose key tests had all been measured in the preceding year (Supplementary Table 3). Their models used about fifteen demographic and laboratory variables measured once, at inclusion or at viral cure; ours used five summary statistics of each test over a year, and coded comorbidity. And our largest gain was in viral hepatitis, which they excluded unless controlled. They also censored liver transplantation, whereas we count it with death as the competing outcome. Part of our advantage lies in the first months after the landmark, when some diagnoses may follow tests ordered on suspicion; the 6-month washout is the conservative estimate, and it still favoured the model against both scores. Both scores were built for longer horizons (aMAP 5 years, THRI 10) than our three years; THRI's etiology points depend on whether hepatitis C has been cured, which is not recorded, but scoring hepatitis C as active or as cured led to the same conclusion. aMAP and THRI performed worse here than in most curated cohorts. aMAP's pooled C-statistic is 0.81 in non-viral liver disease (1); after HCV cure, its C-index was 0.77 in a Scottish cohort and lower in a second UK cohort (77); and THRI's external C was 0.77 (8). In Nahon et al.'s validation set, however, aMAP's timedependent AUC was 0.73 at 1 year and 0.70 at 3 years (2), close to ours. Our AUC() values for the scores, 0.66-0.70, come from an unselected routine-care population, with each score evaluated at the first encounter where it could be computed, over 6 to 36 months, and outcomes from coded records rather than protocolised surveillance. The comparison is paired on the same patients and the same follow-up, so these conditions apply to both markers.

A relevant recent machine-learning comparator using routine clinical data is PRE-Screen-HCC (11). However, the clinical target differs from ours. PRE-Screen-HCC was designed as a broad population-level prescreening tool, developed in UK Biobank (78) and externally tested in All of Us (79), with models evaluated in the full population and in a broader patients-at-risk subgroup that was not restricted to cirrhosis. It used random forest models explained post hoc with SHAP values, which approximate the contribution of each variable to a model that cannot itself be inspected, for a binary HCC versus no-HCC task; such explanations do not show whether a biomarker signals cancer risk or competing mortality. Our model instead targets a narrower surveillance problem: short-term HCC prediction among patients with established cirrhosis, while explicitly separating death or transplantation without HCC from event-free survival. This makes our three-class model less suited to broad population prescreening than PRE-Screen-HCC, but more informative for understanding short-term HCC risk, liver failure risk, and competing mortality in established cirrhosis.

Implementation considerations

The model's inputs are extracted automatically from the electronic health record: age, sex, coded chronic diagnoses and the results of eight laboratory tests over the previous 12 months. A test not performed in that window takes the training median, so it contributes as a typical value; age is required, and the reference scorer rejects a record without it. The model adds most where AFP, platelets, albumin and bilirubin have all been measured in the previous year (63% of the aMAP and 42% of the THRI landmark set; Supplementary Table 3), and its absolute risks are calibrated for use at a laboratory visit; at other dates they overestimate (Supplementary Table 12). No manual data entry or tuning is needed. The output, the risk of HCC by 6 to 36 months together with each feature's contribution, is meant for the clinicians who decide on surveillance; a strong competing-death signal is a prompt to review liver function and goals of care, not an instruction.

Limitations

This study has limitations. First, all evaluations were internal, in one regional health system and period. The landmark comparison applied the cross-validated models at routine encounters without retraining, and leaving each province out of training did not lower discrimination (Supplementary Table 10), but the provinces share one health service, laboratory network and coding practice. External validation, with local recalibration of at least the baseline risk, is needed before use elsewhere; the OMOP-standardised pipeline is meant to make it possible. Second, routine data limit what is measured: HCC was ascertained from diagnosis codes without review of imaging or histology, and sustained virological response after hepatitis C is not recorded, so THRI was computed under both assumptions. Testing is cliniciandriven, and the advantage over the scores was confined to patients with the key tests measured (Supplementary Table 3): where testing is sparse, the model adds little, and a missing test enters as a median value. Third, the model showed no advantage in women, who were 34.9% of the development set but 17.3% of HCC cases, nor in MASLD, with few cases. In women the recalibration overestimated risk at short horizons, and a proportional sex term did not correct it (Supplementary Table 11). Fourth, the recalibration was fitted at laboratory visits and overestimated risk at time zeros that were not visits (observed/expected 0.79 at 12 months, 0.68 without a visit in the previous 6 months; Supplementary Table 12), so its absolute risks apply at a laboratory visit.

Conclusion

An interpretable three-class model built on the routine records of a whole region ranked short-term HCC risk in cirrhosis better than aMAP and THRI under competing risks, and neither interaction terms, a binary outcome nor the full health record improved its discrimination. Modelling death or transplantation without HCC as a separate outcome kept patients who die first out of the high-risk group and gave shape functions that separate cancer from liver failure, a distinction that a binary model folds into one curve. Because the pipeline is standardised to the OMOP Common Data Model, the model can be recalibrated and validated in other health systems, the next step before it informs surveillance decisions.
