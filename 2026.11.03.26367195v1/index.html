To fix Exact P-Values

What we found

We found 3 imprecise p values out of 45 detected p values.

Show details

Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

What we found

We found 2 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

This module checks references and warns for citations of original studies for which replication or reproduction studies exist in the FLoRA database.

The Replication Check module compares the reference list against studies in the FLoRA (FORRT Library of Replication Attempts) database based on the DOI. If a study in the database is found, a reminder is provided that a replication or reproduction of the original study exists, and should be cited. No reminder is given when the paper already cites that replication or reproduction (matched by its DOI).

The module requires that the reference has a DOI: the DOI in the paper’s reference list, or one looked up with add_bib_match() before the module runs (see ref_table()). References without a DOI are not checked.

It is possible the original study was cited for other reasons than the empirical claim tested, or that the replication/reproduction in the FLoRA database is for only one of the studies in the paper, and not the study the authors discuss.

The database can be manually updated with the FLoRA_update() function. For more information, see https://forrt.org/FLoRA/.

Abstract

Background: Identifying functional variants that influence human health remains a challenge in human genetics. Conventional genotype-phenotype association studies test millions of variants, imposing a substantial multiple-testing burden as genomic and biobank datasets expand in size and variant diversity. Additionally, the contribution of structural variation, which is a substantial driver of genetic diversity, remains underexplored in these studies. Signatures of recent positive selection offer a powerful biological basis for prioritizing potentially functional variation, although demographic history can confound their detection and interpretation in these analyses.

Results: We performed evolution-guided association mapping using long-read genomes from 1,027 selfidentified Black or African American participants in the All of Us Research Program and short-read genomes from an additional 38,552 matched participants of African-ancestry. Using demographycalibrated inferences, we identified 228 genomic regions with local ancestry enrichment or depletion and 1,616 candidate loci showing putative recent positive selection signals. These loci were enriched for genic and regulatory sequences, as well as previously reported eQTL, sQTL, and GWAS associations. Leveraging the resolution of the long-read data, we mapped 15 structural variants to these candidate loci, including 10 located within 1,000 base pairs of putative cis-regulatory enhancers. Admixture-and selection-based phenome-wide association testing identified 13 and 446 nominal associations, respectively, 17 of which remained significant following replication and meta-analysis.

Our results support the use of evolutionary signatures as a robust framework for uncovering clinically relevant variation, including structural variants, in underrepresented populations.

Background

Mapping the functional consequences of genomic variation remains a central challenge in human genetics. Population-scale biobanks produce a large amount of whole-genome sequencing (WGS) and electronic health records (EHR) from millions of participants [1, 2]. The recent advances in long-read sequencing now allow us to access difficult genomic regions that were inaccessible by short-read sequencing data and resolve complex variations, including structural variants (SVs) [3]. SVs, such as deletions, insertions, inversions that affect ≥ 50 base pairs (bp), alter substantially more base pairs than single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) [4], are disproportionately enriched for expression quantitative trait loci (eQTLs) and are associated with many diseases [5]. Incorporating long-read sequencing, these large biobanks now unveil hundreds of thousands of previously unresolved variants for biobank-scale association testing. While this sheer amount of biobank data offers unprecedented opportunity to map genotype to phenotype, testing millions of segregating sites, of which the vast majority of which are likely evolutionarily neutral, imposes a severe multiple-testing burden that limits statistical power and obscures true biological signals and disease associations [6, 7].

Recent positive selection serves as a powerful biological prior for variant prioritization. Natural selection acts directly on fitness, which is inherently governed by phenotype. A variant that rapidly rose in frequency under the classic model of positive selection did so by conferring a distinct functional advantage [8]. Genome-wide evolutionary scans have a proven record of recovering high-impact candidates, such as LCT and MCM6 variants conferring lactase persistence [9], amylase copy number expansions in agricultural populations [10, 11], and immunity-driven phenotypes, e.g., the Duffy-null allele selected under malaria pressure [12]. It is worth noting that alleles targeted by historical selection may have additional phenotypic effects in modern environments through pleiotropy or gene-byenvironment interactions. Examples include the malaria-protective Duffy-null allele, which is also associated with lower benign neutrophil counts and can lead to neutropenia misclassification, clinical trial ineligibility, and inappropriate withholding of medications [13, 14].

More broadly, alleles that were advantageous under past selective pressures can become maladaptive in modern contexts or reveal disease trade-offs. Classic examples include the HbS allele, which protects heterozygotes against severe malaria but causes sickle cell disease in homozygotes [15], and APOL1 risk variants, which protect against Trypanosoma brucei infection but substantially increase chronic kidney disease risk [16]. Similarly, disease trade-offs are seen for G6PD-deficiency variants, which are enriched in malaria-endemic regions but predispose carriers to hemolysis after oxidative exposures. Another example is the diabetes-related SLC16A11 haplotype, which is common in Latin American populations and increases type 2 diabetes risk, likely through effects on lipid metabolism in modern nutritional environments [17, 18]. Taken together, we hypothesize that by leveraging these evolutionary footprints of selection as a direct filter, we can effectively prioritize variants targeted by past selection for association testing because they carry a higher prior for harboring relevant biological and clinical functionality in the genome.

It is equally important to recognize that leveraging selection as a prior requires distinguishing adaptive signals from demographic confounders. This is particularly challenging in recently admixed populations, where gene flow, local genetic drift, and rapid population expansion distort the allele frequency spectrum and generate patterns of linkage disequilibrium that easily mimic selective sweeps [19, 20]. Furthermore, admixture also presents a unique analytical opportunity because it allows us to detect loci where alleles carried on divergent ancestral backgrounds exhibit differing phenotypic effects [21]. To isolate adaptive evolution from background demography, evolutionary inferences must be calibrated against simulations that explicitly model complex population histories [22]. Here, we integrate evolutionary population genetics with biobank-scale data to systematically link putatively functional variants to phenotypic outcomes. We leverage both SNVs and SVs identified from a newly generated long-read sequencing cohort of 1,027 self-identified Black or African American participants from the NIH All of Us (AoU) Research Program [23]. We also use this resource as a high-resolution WGS imputation panel to project variants into an additional 10,000 AoU individuals with matched predicted ancestry and linked EHR data [1]. By cross-referencing demographic-aware selection scans with phenome-wide associations (PheWAS), we construct a comprehensive map of adaptive variation and assess how evolution continues to shape complex disease risk and health outcomes today.

Results

Demographic inferences of the 1,025 self-identified Black or African Americans.

Our AoU cohort includes 1,025 self-identified Black or African American (AoU_AFA) participants, recruited from multiple sampling locations across the continental U.S., with state-level sample sizes ranging from ≤20 to 180 participants (Fig. 1A). We removed two related individuals (kinship>0.1) from the initial 1,027 samples from analysis. To understand the genetic relationship of the AoU_AFA sample with respect to the rest of AoU WGS participants in the U.S. (n=245,388, v8) [1] and the global panel of the 1000 Genomes Project (1KG) [24], we performed principal component (PCA) and ADMIXTURE analyses (Methods). Our PCA results showed that most individuals in the AoU_AFA cohort well overlap with genetically inferred African (AFR)-like individuals in the main AoU biobank, while a subset falls within the range of European (EUR)-like participants. (Fig. 1B). Similarly, the joint unsupervised ADMIXTURE analysis supports that AoU_AFA samples are predominantly composed of AFR-like ancestry, with a median EUR-like admixture fraction of approximately 16.8% (K=2, Fig. 1C and Fig. S1). To further explore the diversity represented in the AoU_AFA at the global scale, we performed a PCA-based geospatial projection of these samples using the 1KG populations as a reference panel (Methods). We found that most of the AoU_AFA cohort is genetically spread throughout Central sub-Saharan Africa (Fig. 1D). Consistent with the findings of PCA and ADMIXTURE analysis, 2.34% (n=24) of our cohort share strong EUR-like components in their genomes and are geographically more affiliated with the 1KG European populations (Fig. 1D). These results together reveal a strong signal of historical admixture between EUR-like and AFR-like ancestries underlying the genetic diversity in the AoU_AFA cohort.

To better calibrate the evolutionary history of the AoU_AFA cohort for downstream analysis, we formally model their history used ∂a∂i [25], an allele-frequency spectrum based demographic inference method. We built a joint allele frequency spectrum using >22 million segregating intergenic SNVs after quality control filtering from our cohort and merging with 409 AFR and 418 EUR ancestry samples from the 1KG collection (Methods). Among the models we tested (Fig. 1E, Figs. S2A-E, and Table S2), our best-fit model (Fig. S2E, composite log-likelihood: -35,290.57) quantitatively reproduces the allele frequency spectrum and qualitatively fits the data (likelihood ratio test: adjusted D statistic=20.01, p=7.69×10 -6 , χ² degrees of freedom=1). Under the best-fit model, a population diverged from the ancestral population analogous to the Out of Africa event around 44,129 years ago (95% CI: 37,592-53,392). This divergence results in the formation of a population with an effective population size of 921 (95% CI: 714-1,195) individuals, resembling the ancestral population of modern-day Europeans. Following the divergence event, the effective population size of the EUR-like population grew exponentially (0.126% per generation) to 8,529 (95% CI: 7,078-10,867) in the present day. Consistent with the ADMIXTURE analysis, the AoU_AFA cohort comprises 80.2% (95% CI: 80.1-80.3%) AFRlike ancestry and 19.8% (95% CI: 19.7-19.9%) EUR-like ancestry. Finally, our best-fit model supports significant asymmetric gene flow between the present-day AFR-like and EUR-like populations (EUR-like to AFR-like: 7.4×10 -5 , 95% CI: 5.8×10 -5 -9.4×10 -5 ; AFR-like to EUR-like: 5.2×10 -5 , 95% CI: 4.0×10⁻⁵-6.3×10 -5 per chromosome per generation). Our inferred model shares the core demographic events with the Gravel et al. (2011) demographic model [26], including comparable Out of Africa split times (44 kya vs. 51 kya). However, our model infers immediate exponential growth following the Out of Africa split rather than an extended bottleneck, along with a more modest European growth rate of 0.126% per generation compared to 0.38% per generation in Gravel et al. (2011) [26]. To further evaluate the quality of our demographic inference, we performed 100 nonparametric bootstraps of the intergenic data (Methods). Overall, the estimated parameters are well within the bootstrapping estimates, suggesting that our best-fit model is well-calibrated and accounts for sampling uncertainty within the data (Fig. 1G, S2, and S3).

Admixture-mapping analysis in the AoU_AFA cohort. Genetically admixed population cohorts provide an opportunity to test for associations between local ancestry and complex traits [27]. To systematically explore the contribution of EUR-like and AFR-like local ancestry in the All of Us cohort to complex traits, we performed a local ancestry scan and two-stage PheWAS in the AoU Biobank using AFR-like samples (Methods). Importantly, our objective was to investigate broader health outcomes since African Americans often carry a disproportionately high burden for disease prevalence. To infer marker-level local ancestry, we applied FLARE v0.6 [28] to the AoU_AFA individuals with the 1KG AFR and EUR reference panels (Methods) (Fig. 2A). We merged consecutive markers to identify 228 local ancestry outlier regions, defined as loci where local ancestry deviates by >4.42 standard deviations from the genome-wide mean (mean EUR-ancestry estimate = 21.6%; outlier thresholds: <16.7% or >26.5%, corresponding to a genome-wide significance of p < 1.0 × 10 -5 in African Americans) [29]. These outlier regions range from 3.6 to 761 kbp in length and encompass a mean of 0.49 genes per region (range: 0 -4). To improve the performance of association mapping, we built partial least squares regression (PLSR) models for each outlier region to predict the expected EUR-like ancestry dosage and applied the trained PLRS models to a discovery set of 10,000 ancestry-matched AFR-like imputation cohort (Methods) [23]. With an 80:20 training vs. testing split, we found that the performance of our PLRS models was highly correlated (Pearson's r = 0.48) with the number of SNVs in individual regions (Fig. 2B). Using a prediction r 2 cutoff of 0.5 (Fig. 2B and 2C), we selected 39 admixture outlier loci for association mapping.

To detect local-ancestry specific associations, we tested 447 phecodes with at least 200 cases and 200 controls in the discovery cohort for each region, using the predicted EUR-like ancestry dosage as the predictor along with age, age 2 , sex, and 20 PCs from a within-population PCA as covariates. We identified 13 associations at a nominal discovery significance threshold of p = 1.0 × 10 -3 involving nine outlier regions (Table S3). Three loci (chr17:21,654,025-21,724,463, Fig. 2D and S17; chr6:32,470,563-32,481,211, Fig. 2E and Fig. S13); chr21:8,756,876-9,169,773, Fig. S18 and S19) contain distinct associations in different phecode categories, suggesting pleiotropic effects. For example, the region chr17:21,654,025-21,724,463 is depleted of EUR-like ancestry (mean EUR-like ancestry=12.73%), and has nominal associations for increased risk of "hypotension" (p = 5.4 × 10 -4 ; β = 0.685, SE =0.20) and "abnormal involuntary movements" (p = 7.0 × 10 -4 ; β = 0.792, SE=0.23) with increasing EUR-like dose (Fig. 2D). These results suggest that higher AFR-like ancestry may be protective against hypotension risk, which is interesting given that African Americans carry a lower prevalence for orthostatic hypotension (7.5%) than whites (14.5%) [30], but a high prevalence for hypertension (59%) [31]. Located in the EUR depletion region is KCNJ18, a potassium channel gene primarily expressed in skeletal muscle [32] and involved in hypokalemic periodic paralysis, which may partially account for the hypotension and abnormal involuntary movement associations found. In addition, the chr6:32,470,563-32,481,211 locus near HLA-DRB5 is enriched for EUR-like ancestry (mean EUR-like ancestry=26.67%), but shows reduced risk for acute kidney failure (p = 3.3 × 10 -4 ; β = -0.402, SE = 0.11) and bronchitis (p = 8.59 × 10 - 4 ; β = -0.372, SE = 0.11) with increasing EUR-like dose (Fig. 2E). HLA is suggested to be a risk loci for renal function [33], and HLA-DRB5 is upregulated in interstitial lung disease [34]. Across our admixture associations, SVs do not appear to be driving the associations, with no SVs as the lead variant and low linkage of SVs (r 2 > 0.5) to the lead fine-mapped variants. Finally, we attempted to replicate all 13 nominal associations in a replication cohort of 28,552 AFR-like AoU samples from short read data. Among these, nine associations were not testable in the PLSR models due to sparsity of SNVs in the replication cohort (Table S3). One association for chr9:63,748,785-63,837,789 was significant in metaanalysis for "acute pain" (βmeta = 0.449, SE = 0.165, pmeta = 3.1 × 10 -3 ) (Fig. S16).

Genome-wide identification of selection candidates.

To detect potential signals of adaptation, we computed linkage-based integrated haplotype score (iHS) [35] at individual variants and recorded the maximum absolute normalized iHS score within each non-overlapping 20 kilo-base pairs (kbp) window across the autosomes. We evaluated significance of selection using 1,000 coalescent simulations incorporating our best-fit demographic model (Fig. 1E) and mutation and recombination rate heterogeneity across the genome (Methods). To account for model uncertainty in our simulations, demographic parameters were jointly drawn from the bootstraps using a multivariate sampling approach (Methods). We showed that the genome-wide mutation rate estimates from our simulations match those observed from the empirical data (Pearson r=0.98, Fig. S4). This suggests our approach is likely more robust to potential biases from variation in mutation rate and recombination rate across the genome [36].

At a significance threshold of simulation p ≤ 0.01, we identified 2,345 significant windows as putative selection targets (Fig. 3A). We observed a genome-wide left-skewed p-value distribution from our analysis, indicating a conservative inference of selection (Fig. S5A). A Quantile-Quantile comparison of our p-value distribution against an expected uniform distribution confirms that our approach is wellpowered to detect putative targets of selection (Fig. 3B). Overall, we found that these candidate windows exhibit larger absolute iHS values (|iHS|) and a higher alternative allele frequency (median alternative allele frequency=22.7%) than the non-candidate windows (median alternative allele frequency=17.4%, p=7.2×10 -27 , one-sided Mann-Whitney U) (Fig. S5B and S5C). Notably, our simulation-based approach captured a distinct set of signals compared to a simple empirical ranking. Among the candidate windows, 1,287 significant windows fell outside the top 1% genome-wide empirical |iHS| values (Quadrant IV, Fig. S6), while 171 windows within the empirical top 1% were not significant in our simulations (Quadrant II, Fig. S6).

Enrichment of selection candidates in genes and known GWAS signals.

The 2,345 significant windows correspond to 1,616 distinct loci across the genome (length range: 20 to 160 kbp), spanning 46.9 million base pairs (Mbp) or 1.6% of the autosome. Among these, 963 loci overlap with UCSC RefGene genes (Table S5). Because false-positive signals are not expected to preferentially associate with genes, we evaluated the functional relevance of our candidate loci using a permutation test for gene enrichment (Methods). We observed significant enrichment for genic windows compared to intergenic windows (1.08-fold, permutation p<0.001, Fig. S7A). Such enrichment was also observed for both exonic windows (1.15-fold, permutation p<0.001, Fig. S7B) and intronic windows (1.08-fold, permutation p<0.001, S7C). Furthermore, we evaluate genic windows for gene constraint using probability of loss intolerance (pLI) scores and ENCODE4 candidate cis-regulatory elements (cCRE) [37, 38]. Among the genic candidate loci, we found no significant deviations from the null expectation for overlap with ENCODE4 cCREs (20.4% of the genic loci, permutation p=0.20, Fig. S8A) or gene constraint (mean probability of loss intolerance [pLI] in candidate loci=0.33, permutation p=0.29, Fig. S8B) or. However, our Gene Ontology analysis [39] revealed that these loci are significantly enriched in 17 out of 600 molecular functions tested, including catalytic (1.26-fold, p=2.5×10 -4 ) and transferase (1.40-fold, 5.8×10 -4 ) activities (Table S7).

Given the observed genic enrichment of our selection candidates, we further performed a colocalization analysis to identify overlapping signals between our lead iHS variants and previously reported association signals (Methods). We compiled published eQTL and sQTL credible variants from the MAGE database [40] and genome-wide significant GWAS variants from NHGRI-EBI GWAS catalog (v1.0.2) [41]. Compared to windows falling outside the candidate set, we found enrichment of GWAS signals, eQTL signals, and sQTL signals in the candidate set (permutation P<0.001) (Fig. 3E). Overall, 299 of the 963 genic candidates were linked to at least one eQTL (n=84), sQTL (n=46), or GWAS signal (n=241) (Table S6). 52 loci were linked to both regulatory (eQTL/sQTL) and GWAS signals, suggesting that selection may act on phenotypes through gene regulation at these variants. Among these, we recovered several well-characterized targets of selection (Table S6), including the European lactase-persistence variant rs4988235 in an MCM6 intronic enhancer that increases lactase activity [9], and the CCL5 variant rs2107538, which is associated with reduced HIV infection risk in African populations (p=0.0002) [42] and protection against SARS-CoV-2 infection (p<0.001) [43]. We also identified LPA variants, rs6455695 associated with lipoprotein(a) levels (p=5×10 -26 ) and rs6905073, which is associated with coronary artery disease (p=2×10 -10 ), angina (p=3×10 -13 ), and the ratio of polyunsaturated fatty acids to total fatty acids (p=7×10 -14 ). Together the observed gene enrichment and colocalization with known association signals in our results highlight the ability of our evolutionary framework to identify biologically meaningful signals.

Selection targets and SV contributions to selection.

Our analysis recovered both previously reported selection candidates and novel loci. For example, one of the candidates overlaps with rs4988235 (chr2:135851076 G>A), a regulatory variant in MCM6 that drives lactase persistence by increasing LCT expression [44]. Around rs4988235, we observed a slow decay of extended haplotype homozygosity (EHH) on haplotypes carrying the derived allele A (Fig. 3C) and elevated |iHS| values (iHS=3.20) at the locus, with the A allele at 13.4% frequency in AoU_AFA, 53.5% in 1kg EUR, and 2.4% in 1kg AFR. Consistent with a classic selection model, we found a reduction in nucleotide diversity (π) around this variant (Fig. 3C). Similarly, we identified a candidate at SLC24A5, which carries rs2470102 (chr15:48141297 A>G, iHS=-3.58). The derived allele A under selection is known to be associated with lighter skin pigmentation [45] and eye color [46] across human populations. The rs2470102 A allele frequency in AoU_AFA is 23.2%, contrasting with 99.4% in 1kg EUR and 6.8% in 1kg AFR. Haplotypes that carry rs2470102 A exhibit much longer homozygosity and reduced diversity than those with allele G at this locus (Fig. 3D). Furthermore, we identified a selection variant rs28470942 (chr4:99103864 C>T, alternative allele frequency=18.1%) in the ADH gene cluster. Notably, rs28470942 is strongly linked (r²= 0.87) to rs2066702 (ADH1B*3 or chr4:99307860 G>A), which encodes an isoform with increased alcohol dehydrogenase activity [47], and is found at 19.3% frequency in the 1KG AFR populations. The ADH locus has recurrently shown signals of selection [48, 49], with proposed selective pressures including agricultural and dietary changes [50] and reduced adverse effects of prenatal alcohol exposure [51] . We also identified candidates with high allele frequency, such as rs2190227 (chr7:90,206,732 G>A, iHS=2.85, alternative allele frequency=93.6%) in STEAP2, a prostate growth mediator [52].

The long-read data from our AoU_AFA cohort enabled us to explore the contribution of SVs to recent positive selection. Within the 963 candidate loci, we identified 18 SV-lead associations that are either lead iHS variants or in strong linkage disequilibrium (r 2 > 0.5 within 100 kbp) with lead iHS SNVs over 15 unique SVs (allele frequency range: 5.7-81.2%) (Table S5, Fig. S38-S52). SVs are significantly enriched by selection (2.89-fold enrichment, 15 unique SVs observed vs mean 5.19 SVs observed in permutations, p<0.001, Fig. S9). These comprise 13 insertions (eight intronic, five intergenic, length range: 50 to 4,455 bp) and two deletions (one intronic, one intergenic, length range: 52 to 267 bp). Of the 9 genic SVs where an SV is the putative lead variant, nine genes are tolerant to loss-of-function mutations (pLI=0.00-0.54), with the exception of the highly constrained THRAP3 (pLI=1.0) (Table S5). The 52-bp intronic deletion at THRAP3 (rs55751877, AF=81.2%) removes one of two 52-bp tandem repeats (Fig. S39 and Table S11). THRAP3 is an RNA processing factor that promotes the DNA response pathway and variants within it are reported associated with nonalcoholic fatty liver disease when overexpressed [53]. The largest iHS lead SV we identified is a 4,455-bp partial endogenous retrovirus insertion in an intron of ANKRD31 (AF=78.4%, Fig. S46). ANKRD31 is a double-strand break mediator during meiosis, and variants in this gene are known associated with male and female infertility [54, 55] and neurodevelopmental disorders, with a candidate variant for Rett syndrome [56]. Furthermore, we find a 90-bp insertion (Fig. S52) at allele frequency 9.3% in ZNF74, a gene which is frequently found deleted in 22q11.2 deletion syndrome (DiGeorge syndrome) and is a candidate gene for affecting age-at-onset of schizophrenia [57].

Colocalization with published eQTLs and GWAS signals further supports the functional relevance of several candidate SVs (Table S6). For example, we found the 72-bp insertion at HS2ST1 (Fig. S40) is in strong linkage (r²= 0.56) to a known eQTL (rs1621247) [40]. HS2ST1 is essential for heparan sulfate sulfonation and kidney formation [58]. Similarly, a 135-bp insertion at RBBP8 (Fig. S51) is linked to rs7236869, a variant associated with eosinophil counts (p=3.0×10 -14 ) [59], while the 985-bp TcMar-Tigger DNA transposon insertion (Fig. S45 and Table S12) at CDH10 is in strong linkage (r²= 0.74) with rs10942058, a variant associated with educational attainment (p=3.0×10 -10 ) [60]. Notably, spatial overlap within 1 kbp of ENCODE candidate cis-regulatory elements (cCREs) suggests that most these SVs (10 of 15) possess expression-modulating potential (Table S14), including RBBP8, HS2ST1, ANKRD31, and ZNF74.

PheWAS of selected loci. To systematically explore the phenotypic impact of the selection candidates, we conducted a two-stage PheWAS in the AoU Biobank using AFR-like samples. Similar to the admixture analysis, we take 10,000 genetically predicted AFR samples in All of Us that contain EHR data, age and sex covariates, and 20 PCs for PheWAS analysis. (Methods). Importantly, our objective was to investigate the broader health outcomes associated with these variants, rather than the specific traits originally under selection. Exact participant counts for categories <20 cannot be shown as per the All of Us Data and Statistics Dissemination Policy and are instead shown as 20. In the discovery stage, using all variants discovered from the 1,025 AoU_AFA samples as the reference, we imputed all variants into a cohort of 10,000 ancestry matched AFR-like short-read samples in the AoU (Methods). We tested 447 phecodes with at least 200 cases and 200 controls against our candidate set of 963 variants (Table S5). At a nominal discovery significance threshold of p=1.0×10 -3 , we identified 446 associations involving 272 unique phecodes across 263 variants (Fig. 4A). Consistent with previous findings [61, 62], we found an inverse correlation between variant effect size and allele frequency (Fig. 4B).

We then performed replication analysis for these associations in an independent cohort of 28,552 shortread AFR-like AoU samples (Methods). We also attempted to isolate the specific variants driving these associations using fine-mapping (Methods); however, the extended haplotype homozygosity resulting from positive selection creates extensive linkage around these loci, inherently confounding fine-mapping algorithms. In our analysis, we thus chose to report the lead index variant for each locus as the best available candidate for downstream evaluation (Table S9). Following inverse-variance weighted metaanalysis (Methods), 17 phecodes across 13 variants remained highly significant (Bonferroni p<1.1×10 -4 , Fig. 4C and Table S8). This is consistent with the action of selection against variants with large effects [63]. In addition, we found that effect sizes of variants significant in the meta-analysis attenuated to approximately 21.9% of those observed in the discovery cohort (Figs. 4C and S10), consistent with ascertainment bias in genetic association studies or differences between cohorts [64, 65].

The 13 variants significant in meta-analysis are associated with diverse phecodes (Figs. 5A-C and S23-S37), including five represented in the PheWAS mental category (alcohol use disorders, alcohol abuse and dependence, psychoactive substance abuse, posttraumatic stress disorder, mood disorders), and 16 phecodes in the 15 other categories (Fig. 4D). Across the meta-significant associations, the 13 putatively selected alleles are associated with decreased risk in eight cases (61.5%), and associated with increased risk in 5 cases (38.5%). One variant in LINC01950, rs73205915 (chr5:106,987,252 C>T), exhibits possible pleiotropic effect with an increased risk for "synovitis and tenosynovitis" but a nominally decreased risk for "diseases of esophagus" (Fig. S31). The variant rs2190227 (chr7:90,206,732 G>A) at the STEAP2 locus showed the largest effect size for irregular menstrual cycle (βmeta=-0.501, SE=0.128, p=9.1×10 -5 ), with the putative selected alternative A allele associated with decreased risk (Fig. S32). rs2190227 fine-maps to a variant in CFAP69 (chr7:90,254,854 A>G, rs17867598) with a PIP=0.03 (β=1.51, SE=0.357, p=2.1×10 -5 , alternative allele frequency=1.3%). rs17867598 is primarily found in European populations (1KG EUR frequency 3.5%, AFR frequency 0.6%). CFAP69, a cilia and flagellaassociated protein, is highly expressed in the testes and fallopian tubes [66], and loss-of-function variants in this gene result in sperm morphology defects and dysfunction [67].

At the ADH1B locus, fine-mapping recovers the missense variant rs2066702 (ADH1B*3) as a functional candidate with the highest posterior inclusion probability (PIP=0.06) associated with "alcohol use disorder" (β=-0.336, SE=0.074; p=6.3×10 -6 , alternative allele frequency=19.7%, Fig. 5A). This variant is exclusive to Africans (1KG AFR allele frequency 19.3%, EUR allele frequency 0.0%), with the direction of selection consistent with decreased risk. The ADH1B*3 allele facilitates rapid ethanol oxidation and has been previously associated with reduced drinking behavior, protection against alcohol dependence, and/or lowered risk of alcohol-related birth defects in African Americans [51, 68, 69]. At ZMYM1, we find two variants (rs11264100, chr1:35,126,025 A>G; rs11264001, chr1:35,070,852 C>A, both with associations for "hypertension" and "essential hypertension", with selection on the protective alternative allele (Fig 5B and S23-S25). Fine-mapping resolves these as one lead locus, rs11264001 (alternative allele frequency 21.96%, 1KG AFR=5.60%, 1KG EUR=88.78%), with similar effect sizes for hypertension (β=-0.288, SE=0.059; p=1.2×10 -6 , PIP=0.380) and essential hypertension (β=-0.269, SE=0.059, p=6.7×10 -6 , PIP=0.046). The protective allele is substantially lower in frequency in 1KG AFR than in 1KG EUR, of interest given the disproportionately high prevalence of hypertension among African Americans in the United States (59%) [31].

We further investigated phenotype associations involving SVs linked to selected SNVs. At the discovery stage, we find four nominal associations with a linked SV (r 2 > 0.5) implicating three unique SVs (pdiscovery<1. 0 1 2 3; Table S10). First, the 52-bp intronic deletion at chr1:36,283,616 at THRAP3 (alternative allele frequency=81.2%) was linked to the tag allele rs2358719 (r²= 0.52, chr1:36,277,076 C>G; iHS=4.25 AoU_AFA, alternative allele frequency=12.1%) (Fig. S39 and S53). The alternative allele G is almost exclusively found in African populations (1KG AFR alternative allele frequency=16.1%, EUR alternative allele frequency=0.0%), and is significantly associated with reduced risk for medical noncompliance (βdiscovery=-0.498, SE=0.134, p=1.9×10 -4 ) and "other mental and behavioral problems" (βdiscovery=-0.337, SE=0.100, p=7. 9×10 -4 ). The alternative G allele of rs2358719 tags the non-deleted (reference) allele of this SV, suggesting selection for the non-deleted haplotype and increased risk of the deletion allele for these associated phenotypes. In addition, at PLEKHB2, a 111-bp insertion (chr2:131,129,733, alternative allele frequency=18.8%) at an intron of PLEKHB2 is linked (r²= 0.70) to SNV lead rs4850257 (chr2:131,094,186 A>G, iHS=4.25, AoU_AFA allele frequency=19.3%) (Fig. S43 and S54). The SNV is found more frequently in European populations (1KG AFR AF=6.8%, EUR AF=63.1%) and is linked to disorders of calcium metabolism (βdiscovery=0.448, SE=0.126, p=3.7×10 - 4 ). PLEKHB2 is bound to recycling endosome membranes and regulates retrograde trafficking to the Golgi apparatus [70], and knockdown of PLEKHB2 in a breast cancer cell line reduces proliferation rate [71]. Finally, our analysis revealed a 90-bp insertion (chr22:20,401,764, alternative allele frequency=9.3%) at ZNF74 linked to rs76618551 (chr22:20,391,674 T>C; iHS=3.99, AoU_AFA allele frequency=6.2%) (Fig. S52 and S55). The insertion falls into the putative distal-like enhancer EH38E2150574, potentially affecting gene regulation through disruption of H3K27ac acetylation. rs76618551 is associated with cystitis and urethritis (βdiscovery=0.499, SE=0.151, p=9.2×10 -4 ). The rs76618551 C allele is primarily found in African populations, with an AFR frequency of 6.9% in 1KG and is absent from all other populations except AMR (AF=0.8%). However, none of these SV-linked associations survived meta-analysis with the replication cohort. This lack of replication may be driven by subtle population structure differences, as the replication cohort exhibited a slightly different principal component analysis (PCA) composition compared to the discovery cohort (Fig. S11), potentially affecting the reproducibility of these specific effects.

Discussion

In this study, we use recent positive selection as a biological prior to identify genetic variants affecting present-day phenotypes in the NIH All of Us self-identified African American cohort. A key strength of our framework is calibrating this selection scan using a realistic demographic model rather than traditional genome-wide empirical cutoffs. Such traditional cutoffs assume simplistic histories that can severely skew statistics in rapidly growing, admixed populations. Using simulations that incorporate neutral demographic forces and local variation in recombination and mutation rates, we restricted our subsequent phenome-wide analysis to these robustly identified loci, reducing the computational burden. This approach successfully prioritized functional variation, as evidenced by strong enrichment for genic sequences, regulatory elements, and diverse clinical diagnoses.

Typically, positive selection is expected to drive beneficial traits, such as lactase persistence [72] and adaptation to diet with consumption of fermented food and beverage [48] in certain populations. While 76% of our association signals with putatively selected alleles showing protective effects, including the reduced risk of alcohol use disorder associated with the ADH1B*3 allele, we also identified instances of evolutionary mismatch, where historically adaptive alleles appear to confer increased disease risk in modern environments. For example, the rs2470102 allele A at the SLC24A5 locus, which is likely selected for facilitating light skin pigmentation and vitamin D synthesis [73], reduces melanin production in the eye, a strong biomarker for ocular neoplasms [74]. Similarly, we observed an enrichment of inflammatory disease risks among recently selected loci, consistent with a known evolutionary tradeoff between historically beneficial immune hyper-responsiveness and modern autoimmune or inflammatory disorders [75]. These examples illustrate how alleles that increased fitness in past environments can become maladaptive as diet, lifespan, and pathogen exposures rapidly change.

Our framework highlights the critical role of SVs and regulatory elements as important drivers of phenotypic variation under selection. Because SVs alter substantial genomic base pairs and frequently occur in complex regions, they carry a strong prior for functional effects [4]. We identified 15 selectionlinked SVs, including nine within genes and several overlapping or adjacent to ENCODE4 cCREs. This regulatory focus is further supported by our colocalization analysis, which revealed that nearly one-third of all genic candidate loci are linked to a GWAS association, eQTL, or sQTL. Notably, 52 loci linked to both a regulatory QTL and a GWAS hit, while we observed no significant shift in gene constraint (pLI) among the selected loci. Together, these observations demonstrate that recent selection could have frequently acted through regulatory changes, including those driven by SVs, rather than coding mutations [76]. This aligns with evolutionary expectations, as large-effect coding changes in constrained genes are typically deleterious, whereas regulatory alterations can beneficially tune gene expression in an environment-specific manner [77].

Several limitations should be considered. Our demographic model is a necessary simplification that cannot fully capture complex population substructure, and our reliance on iHS restricts our sensitivity to recent, incomplete sweeps [35]. Furthermore, the lack of SV-phecode replication highlights the limited statistical power and cohort-specific tagging differences inherent in imputing structural variants from short-read data [78]. Despite these challenges, our framework highlights the utility of integrating an evolutionary approach to biobank-scale analysis of trait and health outcomes.

Conclusions

Our analysis establishes a practical framework for connecting evolutionary history to modern clinical outcomes. By utilizing positive selection as a biological prior, we can systematically prioritize functional variation and illuminate mechanistic links between past adaptation and modern disease susceptibility. As EHR-linked biobanks and long-read sequencing expand, this evolution-guided approach will become increasingly valuable for uncovering consequential genetic variation and advancing personalized medicine in diverse, understudied populations. and EUR individuals that had at least 98% ancestry assignment to their respective primary component were kept (Table S1). A high-quality chimpanzee genome was used as the ancestral outgroup for allele polarization [81]. In summary, we obtain a final set of 1KG 409 AFR, 418 EUR, and 1,025 AoU_AFA unrelated samples with 22,785,857 biallelic segregating SNVs across 481,020,725 bp of intergenic masked sequence. Due to computational limitations on inference, we project the joint site frequency spectrum down to 25 alleles in each population, resulting in 15,625 allele frequency bins. Iterative model fitting was then performed on the projected joint site frequency spectrum with dadi v2.2 (diffusion approximation for demographic inference) [25] on the unfolded SFS, where 100 perturbed parameter sets were fitted under each model to ensure that the parameters and likelihood converged. We correct for ancestral state misidentification with an explicit parameter in each model that flips a proportion of each half of the SFS. Log-likehood of models was computed using diffusion grid sizes [35, 45, 55]. We investigated various models including with or without migration, instantaneous or exponential growth, and admixture timings. Model selection was performed using likelihood ratio tests to obtain the best stable model. To estimate uncertainty, we generated 100 bootstrap projected replicates, and refitted the perturbed parameter set to obtain 95% confidence intervals. We use a generation time of 25 years and a mutation rate of 1.5x10 -8 per base pair per generation to scale from genetic to physical units.

We project the 1025 AoU_AFA samples into geographic coordinates using genetic similarity to 1KG samples. We take 1KG samples of 409 AFR and 418 EUR individuals, composing four European sampling locations (GBR, IBS, TSI, FIN) and five African locations (GWD, MSL, YRI, ESN, LWK), and use these as reference locations. For each sample, we take 16 PCs from the shared PC space and compute the weight of each PC using their eigenvalues to weight the PCs by their genetic variance explained. Geographic coordinates (latitude and longitude) of the nearest 20 1KG samples by PC distance were then used to project the AoU_AFA sample location, weighting the contribution of each sample by their PC distance to the AoU_AFA sample such that closer samples by PC distance are given more weight. The projected latitude and longitude is then computed as the average of the PC-weighted latitude values and longitude values among the 20 samples and then plotted along with the 1KG locations.

Selection scans and coalescent simulation.

We next scanned the genome for recent positive selection in the AoU_AFA samples using the integrated haplotype score (iHS) statistic [35]. We use the 1,025 AoU_AFA panel, generated from long reads in the marker paper, to compute iHS using selscan v2.1 [82] and the hg38 genetic map [83], and then normalized scores genome-wide into 100 allele frequency bins using recommended parameters [84]. The genome was partitioned into non-overlapping 20 kb windows, and the maximum |iHS| value in each window was recorded.

To establish a null distribution under neutrality, we performed 1,000 coalescent simulations using msprime v1.3.3 [85]. These simulations incorporated the GRCh38 recombination map and the inferred demographic model from dadi, with parameter sets for effective population sizes, divergence time, migration rates, and admixture fraction independently drawn from the multivariate normal distribution of 100 bootstrap replicates. Each simulated dataset was processed in the same manner as the empirical data to produce a distribution of maximum |iHS| values per window. For each empirical window, a simulationbased p-value was calculated as the proportion of simulated windows with a maximum |iHS| value greater than or equal to the observed value. Over 122,828 20 kb windows across the genome, we find 2,345 significant windows (p<=0.01). We computed EHH (extended haplotype heterozygosity) with rEHH [86] using haplotypes carrying the reference or alternative allele at candidate iHS leads. For SVs of interest, we plot the locus using svbyeye [87] by querying for samples that carry the SV and aligning the region flanking to SV in hg38 to contigs in the sample assemblies.

We use the UCSC-provided hg38 RefGene track, updated Jan 10, 2020, and use all annotated genes [88]. We computed a Fisher exact test for enrichment, finding 1.08-fold genic enrichment, 1.15-fold exonic enrichment, and 1.08-fold intronic enrichment. We conduct permutation testing for gene enrichment against all 122,828 windows, where the permutation p-value is the number of times a permutation of 2,345 windows randomly drawn yields a greater proportion of genic (or exonic or intronic) windows. We merge consecutive windows with sim p ≤ 0.01 (n=1616). We focus on exonic windows as our primary candidate set of selected loci for downstream analysis (n=963). We conduct permutation testing by testing for enrichment in gene constraint (window contains gene with pLI > 0.9) [77], and ENCODE4 cCRE [38]. For gene constraint, we utilize the mean pLI on selected windows, draw 963 windows randomly from the 122,828 windows that contained a gene with annotated pLI, and compute the two-tailed p-value as the proportion of draws that had windows with mean pLI in window more extreme than observed. For cCRE enrichment, we draw 963 windows randomly from all 122,828 windows and compute the p value as the proportion of draws where the fraction of windows with the IHS lead overlapping a cCRE is more extreme than observed.

We conduct Panther GO-Slim enrichment analysis (molecular function, cellular component, biological process) on the exonic gene list of candidate loci against the Homo sapiens database (Panther v19.0, 20,580 genes) [89]. For Panther-GO-Slim molecular function at FDR=0.05, we find significant enrichment for catalytic activity (1.26-fold), transferase activity (1.4-fold), but depletion for transmembrane signaling receptor activity (0.54-fold), and cis-regulatory region sequence-specific DNA binding (0.52-fold) (Table S5). Cellular component and biological process results are in Table S5.

Colocalization to MAGE catalog and GWAS catalog.

We conduct colocalization analysis on our candidate set using the multi-ancestry database of gene expression fine-mapped results (MAGE sQTL and eQTL v1.0) [40] and the GWAS catalog (v1.0.2) [41]. For each candidate selected loci, we take all SNVs in linkage to the lead IHS variant (r2 > 0.5, +-100 kb, 1,025 panel). We use variants in any sQTL or eQTL credible set for MAGE, and variants below the genome-wide significance threshold p=5e×10 -8 for GWAS. 84 loci have a linked eQTL, 46 loci have a linked sQTL, and 241 loci have a linked GWAS trait. 52 loci have a linked eQTL/sQTL and a GWAS hit, and 12 loci have all three. Four loci are linked to an SV and a eQTL, sQTL, or GWAS hit.

We conduct permutation testing by testing the enrichment of the GWAS, eQTL and sQTL signal in the candidate set compared to the overall windows. We draw 963 windows randomly from the 122,828 windows, compute all linked variants to the IHS lead of the drawn windows, and compute the number of windows with colocalized hits (GWAS, eQTL, or sQTL). The p-value is the number of times that a draw had a larger proportion of windows with a colocalized hit than the empirical observed value.

Phenome-wide association tests.

We conducted PheWAS on the candidate exonic loci. We used PheTK (the Phenotype Toolkit) as the primary package for PheWAS analyses [90]. Phenotypes were defined using phecodeX v1.2 [91]. Individuals required at least two phecode counts to be counted as a case. Only phecodes with at least 200 cases and 200 controls were included in the analysis. Sex-specific phenotypes were tested only on their respective groups. The main discovery cohort consists of 10,000 self-identified African Americans, for which 8,771 samples were unrelated (kinship coefficient<0.1), had covariates and were a case or control for at least one phecode. We conduct replication on 28,552 (kinship coefficient<0.1) unrelated AFR-like samples, provided by All of Us (CDR v8 predicted ancestry cohorts which were generated from a random forest classifier trained on principal components with 1KG and Human Genome Diversity Project samples).

The PheWAS model is a logistic regression of the form: risk ~ dosage + age + age 2 + sex + 20 PCs where dosage was coded as 0, 1, or 2 copies of the alternative allele.

PCs were computed from the pooled discovery and replication cohort in Hail (hail_normalized_pca) using the All of Us global PCA high-quality SNVs (Genomic Research Data Quality Report) [92]. In the case that the lead variant is an SV, we test the best linked SNV in lieu of the SV (r2 > 0.5, +-100 kb). Regression models were computed with Newton-Raphson optimization and a maximum of 100 iterations (statsmodels v0.14.0) [93]. Variants with minor allele counts <20 and unconverged regressions were dropped from further analysis. In total, 428,857 tests were performed in the AFR cohort. We applied a lenient threshold of p<1e-3 for candidate hits, obtaining 446 nominal associations. For nominal associations, fine-mapping was performed with SuSIE v0.16 [94] for each outlier region ±500 kb, with the same covariates as in the PheWAS, and with minor allele count>20. We retest each candidate phecode in the replication cohort of 28,552 samples, followed by inverse variance weighted meta-analysis, with 17 hits significant at the meta-analysis level after Bonferroni correction on 446 tests (p=1.12×10 -4 ). Beta (β), odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals from the standard error were derived from the regression models for the genotype dosage.

Local Ancestry.

Local ancestry was performed using flare v0.6 [28] with default parameters, including minor allele count=50 and minimum allele count=0.005 in the reference panel. We use the 1,025 AoU_AFA [23] as the query and 1KG high coverage release [79, 95] of the phased variants as the reference panel, where the same AFR and EUR samples as in the demographic inference were used to construct the panel in bref3 format. We utilize the hg38 genetic map [96]. Each chromosome was run separately to generate the phased marker-level local ancestry per haplotype for each sample in AoU_AFA. At each marker, we compute the EUR and AFR ancestry fraction over all samples to obtain local ancestry outlier regions, defined as regions where the EUR marker local ancestry across all samples deviates significantly from the mean (±4.42 SD) [29]. We merge consecutive markers within 200 kb and use the first and last marker as the breakpoints. We remove regions within 2 Mb of telomeres or overlapping the centromere, obtaining 228 outlier regions. Then, we adjust the PheWAS method to explore regional associations using predicted EUR dosage by fitting local ancestry prediction models on the AOU_AFA samples and then applying these to the discovery and replication cohort. The 1,025 AoU_AFA samples were split 80:20 for training (n=820) and test (n=205) data. For each region, we fit a partial least squares (PLSR) model with up to 20 components to predict basepair-weighted EUR ancestry dosage per sample from their marker genotypes. Each sample may have a EUR dosage from 0 to 2, corresponding to no EUR ancestry called over a region to the entire region called EUR over both haplotypes. Partial dosages are allowed.
